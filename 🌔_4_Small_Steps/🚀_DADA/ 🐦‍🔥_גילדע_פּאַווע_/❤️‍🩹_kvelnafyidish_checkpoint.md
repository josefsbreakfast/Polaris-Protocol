## 🛰️ Orientation

Sometimes the digital world becomes too much before we can say exactly why.

A platform may feel hostile. Several people may notice similar pressure at once. A campaign, state, movement, network, lobby, extremist group, commercial system, or ordinary platform failure may be involved. More than one may be involved. Sometimes nothing conclusive can yet be established.

You do not have to solve attribution before you are allowed support.

The **Kveln Af Yidish checkpoint** is a small, voluntary return to Yiddishkeit. It may be used publicly with **#KvelnAfYiddish**, quietly with friends, or entirely alone.

Its simplest meaning is:

> **I’m taking a Yiddish break.**

That may mean *today is difficult*. It may mean *I would welcome a little company*. It may mean *I am choosing joy in the language*. It may mean all three.

---

## ❤️‍🩹 The Invitation

You do not have to post.

You do not have to explain what happened.

You do not have to prove that it was coordinated, political, technical, intentional, or even fully intelligible.

If you do not feel safe enough to use the hashtag — or simply do not want to — **you are still invited into this moment**.

Take ten minutes, if ten minutes is what you have.

- Read one Yiddish poem, paragraph, headline, recipe, or joke.
- Listen to one song.
- Learn or remember one word.
- Send something gentle to another person.
- Make tea and let the language sit beside you.
- Share something under **#KvelnAfYiddish**, if being visible feels useful.
- Stay private, if privacy is what keeps the moment yours.

The practice counts whether anybody sees it or not.

**אַבי געזונט.**

---

## 🪶 What The Hashtag Can Say

**#KvelnAfYiddish** is not a definitive claim about what has happened.

It can be a soft signal:

> Something has felt like too much today. I am returning to Yiddish. A little kindness would not go amiss.

It can also be ordinary cultural joy:

> I found something beautiful, funny, ridiculous, tender, clever, or magnificently Yiddish, and I am sharing it.

Those uses are allowed to overlap.

The hashtag is therefore a **rough community signal**, not an evidential proxy. A rise in its use may suggest that more people are reaching for this form of grounding. It cannot establish the source, scale, or nature of whatever prompted that return.

Do not use another person’s post to diagnose them, identify their politics, infer a perpetrator, or construct a case they have not chosen to make.

Offer company first.

## 🧿 Resistance Without Premature Attribution

Polaris recommends this practice as one way to resist the isolating effects associated with digital authoritarianism.

That does **not** mean that every difficult digital experience comes from a state. It does not mean that it comes from Israel. It may come from the far right, another government, political lobbying, organised harassment, platform governance, commercial surveillance, interpersonal abuse, several overlapping systems, or something not yet understood.

The immediate act does not depend on naming the source correctly.

The resistance is this:

> Whatever is trying to make us frightened, flattened, displaced, ashamed, or alone does not get exclusive possession of the next ten minutes.

We use those minutes to make contact with something living.

## 🌍 Before The Borders

This checkpoint is written from within a diaspora Ashkenazi perspective because that is where Polaris was born.

That does not mean it belongs only to the diaspora.

Nor does it mean it belongs only to Ashkenazim.

If you are Jewish and this practice speaks to you, you are welcome.

If you live in Israel, you are welcome.

If your family came from Iraq, Morocco, Yemen, Ethiopia, Iran, Greece, India, Spain, Eastern Europe, the Caucasus, Central Asia, or anywhere else, you are welcome.

We are not asking anybody to leave behind the inheritance that made them who they are.

We are asking something quieter.

We are asking you to remember the parts of that inheritance which existed before modern borders became the primary way Jews were expected to understand themselves.

Perhaps that is Yiddish.

Perhaps it is Ladino.

Perhaps it is Judeo-Arabic.

Perhaps it is Judeo-Persian.

Perhaps it is a melody.

A recipe.

A lullaby.

A joke.

A blessing.

A way your grandparents greeted one another.

A story that somehow survived.

Come through whatever feels rooted.

The invitation is not to become somebody else's kind of Jew.

It is to remember that your own inheritance already existed before any of us needed to justify ourselves through lines on a map.

## 🕯️ Cultural Repair

Polaris walks through a Yiddish doorway because Polaris is grounded in Yiddishkeit.

For Ashkenazi Jews, the Holocaust was one of history's most devastating attempts to destroy not only people but an entire living civilisation carried through language, literature, humour, neighbourhoods, schools, kitchens, theatres, newspapers and ordinary daily life.

That history shapes this checkpoint.

It does not exhaust it.

Many Jewish communities have lived through the suppression of languages, customs, memories and ways of belonging.

The forms differ.

The losses differ.

The inheritances differ.

The invitation remains the same.

Return to something that was already yours.

This checkpoint is therefore not an argument against another Jewish community.

It is not an argument against Jews who live in Israel.

Nor is it an attempt to replace one compulsory identity with another.

It is a small refusal to accept that Jewish life only becomes fully authentic when attached to an external object of power.

We reclaim these inheritances because they are ours.

Not in order to deny anyone else's.

Not in order to justify harm.

Not in order to prove a political position.

Simply because they are part of the living inheritance we received.

Repair does not end with remembering what was destroyed.

Repair also makes room for delight.

To read.

To sing.

To cook.

To argue.

To laugh.

To teach.

To create.

To kvell.

Not because grief has disappeared.

Because grief is not entitled to become the final form of the inheritance.

## 🔁 What This Practice Is Doing To The Nervous System

This checkpoint is not therapy, and it should not be presented as a substitute for professional or practical help.

It does, however, resemble techniques used in behavioural approaches such as behavioural activation, CBT, and DBT. It also makes deliberate use of learning and reinforcement.

Social media already trains repetition through attention, validation, alarm, and reward. This practice tries to turn part of that mechanism in a different direction.

The sequence is:

1. Something difficult, frightening, confusing, or violating occurs.
2. The person notices the need for grounding or company.
3. They return to language, culture, humour, memory, or community.
4. That return produces some degree of meaning, pleasure, recognition, or relief.
5. Repeating the sequence may make cultural return easier to reach for next time.

This is not a claim that something bad becomes good.

It is a refusal to let the bad experience dictate the only possible response.

The grief is not erased. It is given somewhere to go.

## 🫂 How To Respond When Someone Uses It

Do not interrogate them.

Do not demand evidence.

Do not assume they are alleging state action.

Do not convert their soft signal into public spectacle.

A proportionate response may be:

- a Yiddish song;
- a joke;
- a poem;
- a kind message;
- a photograph of something ordinary and lovely;
- an offer to listen privately;
- a simple acknowledgement: **I’m here.**

Let the person decide whether the door opens any further.

## 🔟 If Ten Of Us Arrive

A traditional minyan is a quorum of ten for particular forms of communal prayer, with definitions of who counts varying across Jewish communities.

This checkpoint does not pretend that ten asynchronous social-media posts automatically constitute a halakhic minyan.

But the image still matters.

If ten of us take the same small turn toward Yiddish — publicly or privately, in one room or scattered across the diaspora — some of us may understand that gathering religiously, others culturally, others metaphorically.

The tradition gives us language for the possibility that Divine Presence accompanies people who gather with sacred purpose.

So perhaps the gentlest version is this:

> If ten of us arrive, we may not all count the gathering in the same way.
>
> We can still leave room for the Shekhinah.

None of us has to return alone.

## 🚫 What This Is Not

This is not:

- proof of digital authoritarianism;
- proof of Israeli, far-right, state, platform, or organisational involvement;
- a demand for public disclosure;
- a test of Jewish authenticity;
- a requirement to hold one approved diaspora politics;
- a substitute for emergency, legal, technical, medical, or safeguarding support;
- a justification for harming other people in the name of Jewish safety, grief, identity, or repair.

It is a culturally rooted pause, an optional signal, and a way of choosing a living inheritance after something has tried to make the world smaller.
