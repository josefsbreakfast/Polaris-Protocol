# 💾 Capabilities
**First created:** 2026-07-20 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-20  
*What systems can technically do, what evidence would show actual use, and where interpretation can go wrong.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

When digital systems are opaque, the first question is often not:

> Who did this?

It is:

> What kind of system could produce this outcome?

Capability analysis helps when:

- symptoms are confusing;
- records are incomplete;
- several explanations remain possible;
- technical language is being used to dismiss the reader;
- powerful systems are being treated as unimaginable;
- a person needs to know what evidence to preserve or request.

Understanding the machinery does not prove who turned it on.

This branch exists to separate:

- technical possibility;
- operational plausibility;
- confirmed implementation;
- attribution;
- causation.

Capability tells us what a system could do.

Evidence tells us what it did.

---

## 🧭 Why This Branch Exists

Digital systems can:

- collect;
- combine;
- classify;
- predict;
- rank;
- restrict;
- intervene.

Those capabilities may be distributed across:

- platforms;
- state bodies;
- contractors;
- data brokers;
- banks;
- employers;
- insurers;
- public institutions;
- shared vendors.

No one organisation needs to hold the whole system.

One body may collect.

Another may score.

Another may provide infrastructure.

Another may make the decision.

Another may carry out the intervention.

A capability may be distributed across institutions even when responsibility is not.

This creates practical effects that may be:

- technically coherent;
- institutionally fragmented;
- difficult to attribute;
- difficult to appeal;
- difficult to correct.

The purpose of this branch is not to turn every fragment into one hidden machine.

It is to make the machinery legible enough to ask better questions.

## 🪜 Capability, Access, Implementation, Attribution, And Causation

Capability analysis should distinguish five stages.

### 1. Capability

A system can technically perform the function.

### 2. Access

The relevant actor has the necessary:

- data;
- permissions;
- infrastructure;
- legal reach;
- practical reach.

### 3. Implementation

The capability exists inside an actual:

- product;
- workflow;
- policy;
- procurement;
- institutional process.

### 4. Attribution

Evidence links that implementation to a particular:

- actor;
- institution;
- contractor;
- decision-maker.

### 5. Causation

Evidence shows that the system produced the observed outcome.

The chain is:

> **possible → accessible → implemented → attributed → causal**

Every step after capability requires new evidence.

A description of what a system could do does not establish that:

- the relevant actor had access;
- the capability was deployed;
- a particular institution used it;
- the observed harm resulted from it.

---

## ⚙️ Possibility Is Not Plausibility

Some things may be technically possible but operationally unlikely.

Questions should include:

- Would the actor have access?
- Would the cost be proportionate?
- Would the data be good enough?
- Would the action create unacceptable legal risk?
- Would the action create unacceptable political risk?
- Is there an easier mechanism?
- Would the outcome be useful to the actor?
- Is the capability reliable enough to produce that result?

The technically strongest explanation is not always the operationally most plausible one.

A sophisticated capability may exist.

A cheaper administrative route may still be more likely.

Capability analysis must consider:

- cost;
- access;
- incentive;
- risk;
- operational simplicity.

---

## 🔭 Plausibility Is Not Proof

A capability may fit the observed pattern.

That still does not establish actual use.

Several mechanisms may produce similar outcomes.

A good capability node should state:

- what the capability could explain;
- what it cannot explain;
- what evidence would support actual use;
- what evidence would distinguish it from rival explanations.

A plausible mechanism is a hypothesis with a route, not a verdict.

Plausibility is useful because it narrows the field.

It should not be allowed to inherit the authority of proof.

## 🧩 The Capability Analysis Method

Every capability node should answer eight questions.

### 1. Name The Function

What does the capability do?

### 2. Identify The Inputs

What data, access, or infrastructure would it require?

### 3. Describe The Process

How are the inputs transformed?

### 4. Identify The Outputs

What:

- scores;
- classifications;
- recommendations;
- decisions;
- interventions;

could result?

### 5. Locate The Decision Point

Who or what acts on the output?

### 6. Identify Observable Traces

What:

- records;
- logs;
- notices;
- changes;
- patterns;

might appear?

### 7. Preserve Rival Explanations

What else could produce the same result?

### 8. Define Disproof Conditions

What evidence would count against the hypothesis?

The shorthand is:

> **Function → inputs → process → output → decision → trace → rivals → disproof**

A capability model that cannot identify a decision point or disproof condition is not yet ready to support a strong allegation.

---

## 🔬 Start With The Smallest Claim

Do not begin with the largest possible theory.

Begin with the smallest claim that can be checked.

Instead of:

> A whole system is controlling every part of my life.

Test whether:

- one record was shared;
- one risk label travelled;
- one account was accessed;
- one threshold triggered review;
- one institution changed treatment;
- one vendor appears in several workflows;
- one correction failed to propagate.

Small claims are not timid.

They are harder to evade.

They are also:

- easier to test;
- easier to disprove;
- easier to document;
- more useful for accountability.

This principle is developed further in [🔬 The Smallest Claim First](./🥀_Little_Database_Of_Horrors/🧭_Orientation/🔬_the_smallest_claim_first.md).

## 🧾 Facts, Proxies, Inferences, And Predictions

Capability analysis should distinguish four categories.

### Fact

A directly observed or documented event.

Example:

- two devices were co-located.

### Proxy

A measurable feature standing in for something harder to observe.

Example:

- co-location used as a proxy for association.

### Inference

A conclusion drawn from one or more records.

Example:

- the users know one another.

### Prediction

An estimate about hidden or future behaviour.

Example:

- the users may coordinate in future.

The further the claim moves from the original record, the more uncertainty should be visible.

A prediction should not be displayed as a fact.

An inference should not erase its proxy.

A proxy should not be mistaken for the thing it represents.

## 📊 Scores Are Political Objects

Scores may appear objective because they are numerical.

But scores contain choices about:

- variables;
- weights;
- thresholds;
- missing data;
- false positives;
- false negatives;
- acceptable harm;
- protected categories;
- review routes.

A score may claim to measure:

- risk;
- influence;
- disruption value;
- compliance;
- trust;
- access;
- vulnerability;
- coalition potential.

Arithmetic can hide judgement without removing it.

A numerical output should therefore be accompanied by:

- variable definitions;
- source quality;
- confidence;
- error rate;
- threshold rationale;
- human review;
- appeal routes.

The number is not neutral merely because it is precise.

## 🪲 Data Quality And Error Propagation

Recurring problems include:

- incorrect identity matching;
- stale records;
- duplicated data;
- inherited labels;
- merged people;
- context loss;
- incorrect relationship classification;
- copied vendor outputs;
- predictions displayed as facts.

One wrong record may become many apparently independent confirmations.

A label may move through:

- one vendor;
- several institutions;
- multiple databases;
- repeated decisions.

The repetition creates confidence.

The source remains singular.

Scale multiplies confidence faster than it corrects error.

Every consequential system should preserve:

- source;
- date;
- provenance;
- confidence;
- correction history;
- dispute status.

---

## 🕸️ Distributed Systems And Convergent Effects

Capabilities may be spread across several organisations.

For example:

- one body collects;
- another scores;
- another hosts;
- another verifies;
- another decides;
- another intervenes.

This makes it difficult to identify:

- the original source;
- the responsible actor;
- the appeal route;
- the correction path;
- the true decision point.

The system may be coherent in effect while fragmented in ownership.

This does not automatically prove central coordination.

It also does not remove responsibility.

Fragmentation may itself be a governance problem because each actor can claim that:

- it only supplied data;
- it only followed policy;
- it only relied on the score;
- it only implemented the decision.

A complete capability map should show the whole route.

## 🏢 Commercial And State Overlap

Data may move through:

- commercial platforms;
- contractors;
- analytics firms;
- cloud providers;
- financial institutions;
- identity providers;
- public bodies;
- shared vendors.

State capability may depend on commercial infrastructure.

Commercial decisions may be shaped by:

- law;
- regulation;
- public contracts;
- security incentives;
- reputational risk;
- market concentration.

Public and private power may reinforce one another without becoming identical.

The boundary between state and commercial capability is often a contract, an interface, or a data-sharing agreement—not a wall.

Capability analysis should therefore examine:

- procurement;
- data access;
- vendor relationships;
- service agreements;
- shared standards;
- common screening systems.

---

## 🌱 Capability Drift

Tools created for one purpose may expand into another.

Examples include:

- fraud detection becoming political-risk screening;
- safeguarding becoming behavioural profiling;
- counter-terrorism tools becoming public-order tools;
- advertising models becoming influence analysis;
- identity verification becoming movement control;
- workplace monitoring becoming political classification.

Exceptional capability tends to look reusable once the infrastructure already exists.

Questions should include:

- Was the new use authorised?
- Was consent renewed?
- Was proportionality reassessed?
- Were retention limits changed?
- Was oversight informed?
- Were new harms evaluated?
- Were protected groups considered?

A technically reusable system is not automatically a democratically legitimate one.

---

## 🚨 Function Creep And Emergency Logic

Temporary powers may persist.

Emergency datasets may be repurposed.

Thresholds may become easier to trigger.

Tools may spread to new agencies or institutions.

Exceptional assumptions may become administrative defaults.

The end of an emergency does not automatically dismantle the machinery built during it.

Capability analysis should therefore ask:

- What was temporary?
- What became permanent?
- Which data were retained?
- Which powers migrated?
- Which standards weakened?
- Which exceptional pathway became routine?

Emergency logic often changes what institutions believe is proportionate.

Those changes should not remain invisible.

## 🎯 Threat Models

Capability analysis should remain threat-specific.

Possible actors include:

- intimate abuser;
- stalker;
- employer;
- platform;
- organised criminal group;
- public authority;
- intelligence service;
- mixed or unknown actors.

For each actor, ask:

- What access do they have?
- What data can they obtain?
- What legal powers apply?
- What technical skill is realistic?
- What would they gain?
- What risks would they face?
- Is there an easier route?
- How persistent are they likely to be?

Different actors can produce similar symptoms through very different mechanisms.

A useful threat model narrows protection.

It should not automatically select the most powerful imaginable adversary.

## 🧪 Evidence Types

Useful evidence may include:

- logs;
- access records;
- notices;
- contracts;
- procurement documents;
- policies;
- source code;
- audit records;
- correspondence;
- legal authorities;
- data-protection records;
- witness testimony;
- reproducible technical tests;
- institutional behaviour changes.

Different claims require different evidence.

### Capability Claim

Technical documentation may be enough to show that a function exists.

### Implementation Claim

Workflow, product, procurement, or policy evidence is required.

### Attribution Claim

Actor-specific records are required.

### Causation Claim

The decision and outcome must be linked.

Evidence should fit the claim.

A broad capability document cannot, by itself, prove a specific intervention.

---

## 🧵 What Counts As A Useful Trace?

A useful trace helps distinguish mechanisms.

Examples include:

- one vendor appearing across several institutions;
- the same risk wording repeating;
- a timestamp aligning with a decision;
- one correction failing to propagate;
- a label appearing before a later refusal;
- a classification referenced in correspondence;
- access logs showing a specific account event;
- a policy threshold matching the observed action.

A trace is useful when it narrows the mechanism, not merely when it feels suspicious.

Useful traces should help answer:

- Where did the record begin?
- How did it travel?
- Who received it?
- What decision followed?
- What rival explanation remains?

---

## 🔎 Rival Explanations

Every capability node should actively preserve alternatives such as:

- ordinary bugs;
- administrative error;
- prejudice;
- incompetence;
- commercial incentives;
- policy changes;
- fragmented decision-making;
- interpersonal conflict;
- trauma;
- coincidence;
- unrelated failures;
- resource scarcity.

A pattern may be real while the first explanation for it is wrong.

Rival explanations are not included to dismiss the reader.

They are included to:

- improve the claim;
- identify evidence;
- prevent overreach;
- protect credibility;
- locate the real mechanism.

A good capability analysis should be capable of changing its mind.

---

## 🌫️ Capability Without Totalisation

Not every system is connected.

Not every institution shares data.

Not every anomaly belongs to one actor.

Some systems may interact indirectly.

Some harms may be cumulative rather than coordinated.

Several causes may coexist.

A capability map should widen the field of explanation without forcing every event into one machine.

This means preserving several possibilities at once:

- coordinated action;
- shared infrastructure;
- copied risk categories;
- institutional imitation;
- fragmented over-compliance;
- unrelated failure.

The map should clarify mechanisms.

It should not become a total theory of everything.

---

## 🛡️ Governance Questions

Every capability node should ask:

- What is the stated purpose?
- What data are required?
- Who has access?
- What legal authority applies?
- What model or rule is used?
- What threshold triggers action?
- Who makes the decision?
- Can the subject challenge it?
- How are errors corrected?
- Do corrections propagate?
- How long are records retained?
- Are protected relationships affected?
- Is the system independently audited?
- What would count as misuse?
- What would count as evidence that the capability was not used?
- Who is responsible when several institutions contribute?

A system that cannot answer these questions should not be treated as beyond democratic challenge.

---

## 🩺 Safety For The Reader

Capability analysis must not become a new source of harm.

Readers should not be encouraged to:

- confront suspected actors impulsively;
- destroy devices or evidence;
- spend beyond their means;
- remove accessibility tools;
- isolate themselves;
- treat every contact as unsafe;
- become permanently vigilant;
- publish sensitive records without support;
- investigate continuously without rest.

Capability analysis should increase choice, not produce another form of captivity.

It should help the reader decide:

- what matters;
- what can wait;
- what can be tested;
- what should be protected;
- when to stop.

The point is not to make the threat feel total.

The point is to make one part of it more legible.

## 🚫 What This Branch Is Not

The Capabilities branch is not:

- proof that every described capability has been deployed;
- a catalogue of accusations;
- amateur agent-hunting;
- a reason to treat all institutions as one actor;
- a substitute for forensic investigation;
- a demand for constant monitoring;
- a reason to abandon ordinary explanations;
- a claim that powerful systems never act abusively;
- a claim that technical sophistication guarantees competence;
- a requirement to reach certainty.

Capability analysis should make claims narrower, not grander.

It should clarify:

- what can be shown;
- what remains possible;
- what remains unknown;
- what evidence would move the claim forward.

## 🧿 Compact Definition

**Capabilities** is the analytical branch of DADA that examines what digital, institutional, and data-driven systems can technically do.

It separates:

- possibility;
- access;
- implementation;
- attribution;
- causation.

It asks:

- what inputs are required;
- how the system works;
- what outputs it produces;
- who acts on those outputs;
- what traces remain;
- what rival explanations survive.

Its central guardrail is simple:

> Capability tells us what a system could do. Evidence tells us what it did.
