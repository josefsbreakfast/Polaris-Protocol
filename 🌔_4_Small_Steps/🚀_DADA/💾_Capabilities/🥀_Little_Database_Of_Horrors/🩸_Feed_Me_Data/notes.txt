Cluster Purpose

🩸_Feed_Me_Data/ examines the information required to construct a Little Database of Horrors.

The cluster should explain:

* what kinds of data reveal human relationships;
* how ordinary administrative records become politically revealing when combined;
* how incomplete data generate further institutional appetite;
* which records reveal identity, movement, trust, dependency, influence, and leverage;
* why data collected for one purpose may become dangerous when reused for another;
* how commercial, institutional, platform, and state datasets can converge.

The emphasis is not simply on surveillance volume.

It is on relational meaning.

A list of phone numbers is one thing.

A model claiming to know who trusts whom, who can protect whom, who can fund whom, and who may organise together is something else.

The cluster should preserve the Orientation guardrails:

Capability is not allegation.

A dataset does not explain itself.

Data collected lawfully can still become dangerous when connected, reinterpreted, or retained beyond its original purpose.

The model’s hunger is not a validation metric.

💾 what_the_database_eats.md

Purpose

* Introduce the full data-input model.
* Explain that the Little Database does not need one perfect source.
* Show how fragments from separate systems can be joined.
* Establish the difference between:
    * raw records;
    * inferred relationships;
    * political interpretations;
    * operational decisions.
* Act as the opening node for the cluster.

Core Argument

The database eats fragments.

It may consume:

* identity records;
* communications data;
* location histories;
* travel records;
* financial transfers;
* employment links;
* organisational affiliations;
* family records;
* intimate associations;
* political activity;
* purchased commercial data.

None of these alone necessarily reveals a person’s social world.

The danger emerges when the system connects them and assigns meaning.

Suggested Sections

The Menu

* Provide an overview of all major data categories.
* Explain how each contributes a different kind of relational visibility.

From Record To Relationship

* Name on a payroll becomes an employment link.
* Shared location becomes an inferred meeting.
* Repeated communication becomes estimated trust.
* Transfer of money becomes inferred support.
* Shared institution becomes potential affiliation.

Facts, Proxies, And Guesses

Distinguish:

* direct fact;
* administrative category;
* probabilistic inference;
* political interpretation.

Example:

* Fact: two phones were near the same venue.
* Inference: the owners attended the same event.
* Interpretation: they are political collaborators.
* Decision: increase monitoring.

More Data Does Not Mean More Truth

* Missing context.
* Wrong identity matching.
* Shared devices.
* Group accounts.
* Public events.
* Coincidental co-location.
* Cultural and family practices unfamiliar to the analyst.

Data Appetite

* Error creates demand for additional data.
* Uncertainty is treated as an information deficit.
* More intimate records are requested.
* The system becomes larger without necessarily becoming more accurate.

Key Lines

The database does not eat people. It eats traces of people and mistakes the resulting pattern for the person.

A relationship graph is built from records that were rarely created to answer relationship questions.

More data can make a wrong interpretation more elaborate without making it more true.

🕸️ social_graphs_and_network_analysis.md

Purpose

* Explain how social graphs represent people and relationships.
* Introduce nodes, edges, clusters, bridges, centrality, and weak ties in accessible language.
* Show why network analysis is useful.
* Show how descriptive analysis can become political ranking.
* Connect directly to 🕸️_who_may_safely_know_whom.md.

Core Argument

A social graph turns people into nodes and relationships into edges.

This can reveal:

* communities;
* bridge figures;
* influential connectors;
* isolated people;
* routes through which information or resources travel.

The same tools used for legitimate research, fraud detection, public health, or organisational planning can also be used to identify relationships that create political capacity.

Suggested Sections

People As Nodes, Relationships As Edges

* Explain directed and undirected relationships.
* Explain strong, weak, confirmed, and inferred ties.

What A Graph Can Reveal

* central people;
* dense communities;
* bridges;
* brokers;
* peripheral actors;
* potential routes of diffusion.

Bridge Figures

* Explain why connectors may matter more than public leaders.
* Connect to journalism, law, unions, funders, and diaspora institutions.

Centrality Is Not Influence

* High connectivity may reflect:
    * employment;
    * care work;
    * community service;
    * event administration;
    * popularity;
    * surveillance artefacts.
* A highly connected person is not automatically politically powerful.

Weak Ties

* Occasional contact can bridge communities.
* Weak ties may create access to new information or institutions.
* Systems may overestimate or underestimate their importance.

From Description To Intervention

Progression:

1. map;
2. classify;
3. rank;
4. prioritise;
5. disrupt.

Graph Errors

* identity merging;
* missed aliases;
* shared households;
* shared devices;
* public-event attendance;
* organisational mailing lists;
* mistaken direction of influence.

Key Lines

A social graph can show that two people are connected. It cannot, by itself, explain what the connection means.

The bridge may matter more than either endpoint.

Centrality is a mathematical property, not a moral or political verdict.

The graph becomes coercive when description begins deciding which edges should survive.

📱 device_access_and_communications_data.md

Purpose

* Examine what devices and communications records can reveal.
* Distinguish content from metadata.
* Explain how contact patterns can expose social structure even without message content.
* Cover direct device access, cloud data, account access, spyware, and communications records.
* Preserve technical and evidentiary caution.

Core Argument

A phone is not merely a container of messages.

It may hold:

* contact lists;
* call histories;
* message metadata;
* group memberships;
* photographs;
* calendars;
* notes;
* location histories;
* authentication tokens;
* cloud backups;
* records of institutional contact.

Even metadata can reveal:

* who communicates;
* how often;
* when;
* for how long;
* during which political events;
* through which intermediaries.

Suggested Sections

Content And Metadata

* Message content.
* Sender and recipient.
* Time.
* duration.
* frequency.
* group membership.
* device identifiers.

Direct Device Access

* seizure;
* forensic extraction;
* unlocked devices;
* backup access;
* malicious access;
* commercial spyware.

Avoid presenting all access routes as equivalent.

Contact Lists As Social Maps

* Stored contact does not prove current relationship.
* Absence does not prove no relationship.
* Names may conceal role or intimacy.
* Shared contacts can reveal network overlap.

Group Communications

* messaging groups;
* mailing lists;
* channels;
* broadcast lists;
* administrator roles.

Membership does not automatically establish endorsement.

Temporal Patterns

* communication before and after:
    * protests;
    * publication;
    * legal action;
    * travel;
    * arrest;
    * crisis.

Communication Silence

* Silence can reflect:
    * fear;
    * illness;
    * platform change;
    * ordinary drift;
    * deliberate security practice.
* It should not automatically be interpreted as covert conduct.

Device Data Errors

* shared phones;
* old backups;
* recycled numbers;
* synced accounts;
* workplace devices;
* family plans;
* duplicated contacts.

Key Lines

Metadata can reveal the shape of a relationship without revealing its substance.

A stored contact is evidence of a record, not proof of trust.

The phone may remember relationships the person has already left behind.

Communications data become politically powerful when timing, identity, and network position are interpreted together.

📍 location_travel_and_event_attendance.md

Purpose

* Examine how movement data can reveal association.
* Cover device location, transport records, borders, travel bookings, accommodation, and event attendance.
* Show how co-location becomes inferred contact.
* Explain the limits of those inferences.

Core Argument

Location data can show where devices, accounts, vehicles, or travel documents appear.

It cannot automatically establish:

* who was physically present;
* whether two people met;
* what occurred;
* whether attendance implied support;
* whether travel was political.

The danger begins when co-location is converted into relational or political meaning without sufficient context.

Suggested Sections

Sources Of Movement Data

* mobile location;
* app permissions;
* Wi-Fi and Bluetooth;
* transport cards;
* licence-plate systems;
* airline and rail bookings;
* border records;
* hotel and accommodation records;
* event registration;
* ticketing;
* access-control systems.

Co-Location

* Same venue.
* Same neighbourhood.
* Same journey.
* Same event.
* Repeated geographic overlap.

Explain confidence levels.

Event Attendance

* demonstrations;
* religious services;
* conferences;
* union meetings;
* community events;
* cultural gatherings;
* legal hearings.

Attendance does not equal ideology, leadership, or active participation.

Travel As Relationship Evidence

* visiting relatives;
* accompanying a person;
* shared bookings;
* repeated routes;
* cross-border meetings.

Temporal Inference

* movement before:
    * publication;
    * protest;
    * funding;
    * political action.
* Risk of retrospective storytelling.

Location Errors

* inaccurate GPS;
* shared vehicles;
* shared devices;
* account logins;
* nearby venues;
* delayed uploads;
* VPN or network-location mismatch.

Borders And Leverage

* travel records can support:
    * monitoring;
    * restrictions;
    * questioning;
    * pressure;
    * identification of diaspora links.

Key Lines

Co-location is not conversation.

Attendance is not allegiance.

A map may show that two devices crossed paths. It cannot tell us what passed between the people carrying them.

Movement data become dangerous when geography is treated as intent.

🧬 family_friendship_and_intimate_relationships.md

Purpose

* Examine the most sensitive relational data.
* Explain what family, friendship, care, and intimate ties can reveal.
* Show why these relationships matter for protection, resilience, dependency, identity, and leverage.
* Place strong boundaries around overinterpretation.
* Connect to transnational repression and family coercion.

Core Argument

Intimate relationships are politically relevant because they determine:

* who provides care;
* who notices harm;
* who offers housing;
* who shares resources;
* who can be pressured;
* who makes someone harder to isolate.

They are also the relationships most easily misunderstood by data systems.

    * financial support;
    * repeated attendance;
    * caregiving.

Intimate Relationships

* partnership;
* dating;
* sexual relationships;
* cohabitation;
* former partners;
* private support.

The system may misclassify:

* intimacy as influence;
* conflict as risk;
* care as dependency;
* proximity as partnership.

Care Networks

* disability support;
* elder care;
* childcare;
* emotional support;
* emergency assistance.

Family As Leverage

* relatives in origin state;
* property;
* documents;
* travel;
* shame;
* employment;
* legal status.

Transnational Repression

* threats to relatives;
* coerced calls;
* pressure through community institutions;
* passport or consular leverage.

Strong Guardrails

* relationship failure is not evidence of state action;
* attraction cannot be inferred reliably;

Key Lines

The relationships most important to survival are often the least legible to institutions and the most dangerous to expose.

Family data can reveal protection and leverage at the same time.

A system may detect proximity. It cannot reliably measure love, trust, obligation, or fear.

Intimacy should not become an administrative variable merely because it can be inferred.

📣 political_sentiment_and_influence_mapping.md

Purpose

* Explain how systems attempt to classify political belief, sentiment, influence, and mobilisation capacity.
* Cover public speech, social media, event participation, donations, petitions, organisational roles, and inferred ideology.
* Distinguish belief, expression, affiliation, reach, persuasion, and action.

Core Argument

Political sentiment is difficult to measure.

Systems may rely on proxies such as:

* words;
* follows;
* shares;
* attendance;
* donations;
* social connections;
* engagement patterns.

These proxies may reveal activity.

They do not reliably reveal:

* motive;
* private belief;
* seriousness;
* irony;
* persuasion;
* future conduct.

Political Inference By Association

From Mapping To Prioritisation

Political sentiment is not a stable substance waiting to be extracted from a person.

Reach, trust, influence, and authority are different properties.

Association may reveal access. It does not prove agreement.

The political map becomes authoritarian when lawful belief and association are converted into intervention targets.

🧩 data_brokerage_and_capability_convergence.md

Purpose

* Close the cluster by showing how separate datasets become a capability.
* Examine commercial data brokers, contractors, platform data, public records, state databases, and institutional sharing.
* Explain interoperability, identity resolution, enrichment, and data fusion.
* Show why responsibility becomes difficult to trace.
* Prepare the reader for later capability and dashboard clusters.

Core Argument

The Little Database does not need to collect everything itself.

It can purchase, request, inherit, exchange, or infer.

Capability convergence occurs when:

* separate datasets can be linked;
* identities can be resolved across systems;
* contractors enrich public records;
* platforms provide behavioural signals;
* institutions share risk categories;
* outputs influence decisions.

Shared Errors

Governance Questions

* Who sourced the data?
* What consent existed?
* What purpose applied?
* Who corrected errors?
* Who audited joins?
* Which decisions used the output?

Key Lines

The Little Database does not need to own every dataset. It only needs to be able to ask them questions together.

Data fusion turns administrative fragments into political capability.

The same system that enriches a record may also enrich an error.

Distributed supply chains create power without creating clear responsibility.

Capability convergence is the point at which the plant stops eating separate scraps and begins digesting a whole social world.

Cluster-Level Guardrails

Every node should distinguish:

* raw data;
* verified fact;
* administrative category;
* inferred relationship;
* political interpretation;
* operational use.

Every node should ask:

* What can this data actually establish?
* What is merely inferred?
* What errors are likely?
* What legitimate purpose produced the record?
* What changes when datasets are connected?
* Who can correct the result?
* What decision can the output influence?

The cluster should repeatedly preserve:

Data are not knowledge merely because they have been joined.

Association is not allegiance.

Proximity is not coordination.

Frequency is not trust.

A score is not an explanation.

The database’s appetite is not evidence that the model is becoming accurate.
