# 🕸️ Social Graphs And Network Analysis
**First created:** 2026-07-19 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-19  
*How lists become networks, relationships become edges, and descriptive analysis can become political ranking.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

A list records people.

A graph records what the system believes exists between them.

That distinction matters.

A list may contain:

- names;
- phone numbers;
- addresses;
- organisations;
- event attendees;
- employees;
- donors;
- family members.

A graph connects those records.

It represents:

- who contacted whom;
- who paid whom;
- who attended with whom;
- who belongs where;
- who introduced whom;
- who appears between otherwise separate groups.

The graph begins by drawing lines.

The danger begins when it forgets who drew them and why.

A line may represent:

- a documented relationship;
- one observed interaction;
- an administrative association;
- a statistical inference;
- a model’s prediction.

Those are not the same thing.

A graph can represent connection.

It cannot, by itself, explain meaning.

---

## 🧩 What Is A Social Graph?

A social graph is a model made from:

- **nodes**;
- **edges**.

Nodes represent entities.

They may include:

- people;
- organisations;
- accounts;
- devices;
- addresses;
- events;
- institutions;
- locations.

Edges represent connections between nodes.

They may include:

- communication;
- family;
- employment;
- payment;
- membership;
- co-location;
- shared device use;
- introduction;
- event attendance.

Different edge types should not be treated as equivalent.

A family relationship is not the same as:

- following an account;
- sending one email;
- attending one public event;
- working in the same institution.

The graph may make them all look like lines.

The investigator must preserve the differences.

---

## 🔎 Confirmed, Observed, Inferred, And Predicted Edges

Not every edge rests on the same kind of evidence.

### Confirmed Edge

A relationship is directly documented.

Examples:

- legal family relationship;
- formal employment;
- declared membership;
- signed contract.

### Observed Interaction

A specific contact occurred.

Examples:

- one call;
- one payment;
- one shared event;
- one meeting.

### Inferred Edge

Repeated traces suggest a relationship.

Examples:

- frequent communication;
- recurring co-location;
- repeated financial support;
- consistent shared attendance.

### Predicted Edge

The model estimates that a relationship probably exists despite no direct record.

Examples:

- shared contacts;
- similar behaviour;
- repeated appearance near the same network;
- statistical likelihood.

A system becomes misleading when:

- inferred ties look identical to confirmed ones;
- predicted ties are displayed as fact;
- old relationships appear current;
- disputed classifications remain visually authoritative.

A line on a graph may represent a fact, a proxy, or a guess.

The display should not conceal which one it is.

---

## ➡️ Directed And Undirected Relationships

Some relationships have direction.

Examples:

- one person follows another;
- one institution funds another;
- one person contacts another;
- one actor issues an instruction;
- one organisation refers a person elsewhere.

Other relationships are usually treated as undirected.

Examples:

- family;
- shared household;
- co-membership;
- mutual communication;
- co-attendance.

But apparent reciprocity may be false.

Being contacted does not mean responding.

Being followed does not mean influencing.

Receiving money does not establish control.

Belonging to the same organisation does not mean knowing one another.

Direction matters because political interpretation often depends on who is believed to act upon whom.

A graph that flattens direction may invent influence.

---

## ⚖️ Weighted Relationships

Systems often assign weight to relationships.

Weight may be based on:

- frequency;
- recency;
- duration;
- financial value;
- shared locations;
- number of communication channels;
- estimated trust;
- institutional importance.

But the weight depends on the proxy chosen.

High-frequency contact may reflect:

- family care;
- professional duty;
- crisis;
- conflict;
- automated communication;
- administrative necessity.

Low-frequency contact may still be politically consequential.

One introduction may matter more than hundreds of routine messages.

Frequency is measurable.

Importance is not the same thing.

---

## 🕸️ What Network Analysis Can Reveal

Network analysis can identify:

- densely connected groups;
- people linking separate clusters;
- frequently contacted actors;
- possible routes of information flow;
- isolated or peripheral nodes;
- relationships shared across institutions;
- changes in network structure over time;
- potential dependencies or bottlenecks.

These methods have legitimate uses.

They may support:

- fraud detection;
- public health;
- cyber defence;
- organisational planning;
- infrastructure resilience;
- emergency coordination.

Social-graph analysis is not inherently abusive.

The capability becomes dangerous through:

- the data used;
- the meaning assigned;
- the purpose pursued;
- the decisions made;
- the consequences imposed.

---

## 🌐 Clusters And Communities

Graph systems may divide networks into clusters.

A cluster may reflect:

- family;
- workplace;
- religion;
- language;
- geography;
- political activity;
- platform behaviour;
- community organisation.

The grouping may be mathematically useful.

The label attached to it may not be.

A cluster is a mathematical grouping before it becomes a political category.

Risks arise when:

- one member’s label spreads to others;
- diverse groups are flattened;
- overlapping identities disappear;
- temporary event attendance looks like durable membership;
- a public gathering is mistaken for a coordinated organisation.

The algorithm may identify density.

It cannot automatically explain what creates it.

---

## 🌉 Bridge Figures

A bridge figure links otherwise separate groups.

Examples include:

- survivor to lawyer;
- activist to journalist;
- diaspora group to parliament;
- technical researcher to witnesses;
- religious body to civil society;
- local organiser to international funder.

Bridge figures may matter more than visible leaders.

They may:

- transfer trust;
- translate information;
- make introductions;
- connect resources;
- verify claims;
- move evidence;
- make private harm institutionally legible.

The bridge may matter more than either endpoint.

Power often enters a network through the person who knows where the doors are.

This is why relationship-governance systems may pay special attention to people who repeatedly connect separate worlds.

---

## 🪢 Weak Ties

Weak ties are lower-frequency or less intimate connections.

They may seem unimportant.

But they can:

- connect separate communities;
- introduce new information;
- create access to institutions;
- provide routes out of closed networks;
- enable unexpected coalitions.

A system focused on message frequency may overlook them.

A system focused on coalition risk may prioritise them.

A weak tie can carry a strong consequence.

The politically important feature may not be closeness.

It may be reach across boundaries.

---

## 📐 Centrality

Centrality measures describe structural position.

They do not describe virtue, guilt, leadership, or intent.

### Degree Centrality

How many direct connections a node has.

A person with many contacts may appear important.

### Betweenness Centrality

How often a node lies on routes between others.

This may identify brokers or bridges.

### Closeness Centrality

How efficiently a node could reach the rest of the network.

### Eigenvector-Style Centrality

Whether a node is connected to other highly connected nodes.

These measures can be useful.

They can also be overinterpreted.

Centrality is a mathematical property, not a moral, political, or security judgement.

---

## 🧍 Centrality Is Not Influence

A highly connected person may be:

- a receptionist;
- a community administrator;
- a carer;
- an event organiser;
- a journalist;
- a shared service;
- an automated account.

A person with few recorded connections may possess:

- confidential authority;
- specialist expertise;
- financial power;
- unusual credibility;
- access to a decisive institution.

Influence depends on:

- trust;
- role;
- context;
- timing;
- legitimacy;
- resources.

Network position is only one proxy.

A high centrality score does not establish leadership.

A low one does not establish irrelevance.

---

## 🚪 Brokers, Gatekeepers, And Bottlenecks

A **broker** connects people, information, or resources.

A **gatekeeper** controls or filters access.

A **bottleneck** is a route through which many connections must pass.

One person or institution may occupy several roles.

These roles may be:

- formal;
- informal;
- protective;
- exploitative;
- administrative;
- accidental.

Intervening in these positions can have large effects.

Removing a bridge may fragment support.

Forcing contact through one gatekeeper may centralise control.

A bottleneck may become a point of surveillance.

A network can be weakened without banning any of its members.

---

## 🧵 Multiplex Relationships

Two people may be connected in several ways.

They may be:

- colleagues;
- relatives;
- neighbours;
- political collaborators;
- members of the same institution;
- friends.

Multiple edge types may strengthen confidence that a relationship exists.

They do not automatically reveal its dominant meaning.

One relationship may generate several traces.

Colleagues may:

- communicate;
- co-locate;
- share calendar entries;
- appear in the same institution;
- receive payments from the same employer.

These are not necessarily independent pieces of corroboration.

They may all arise from one cause.

A system that counts them separately may inflate confidence.

---

## 🕰️ Temporal Networks

Relationships change.

A responsible graph should distinguish:

- active;
- historical;
- intermittent;
- one-off;
- disputed;
- ended.

A static graph may:

- preserve former partners;
- retain old employment;
- show past membership as current;
- make a temporary crisis look like a stable coalition;
- treat an old phone contact as an active relationship.

The graph may remember a relationship long after the people have left it.

Time matters.

A connection without a date may be worse than no connection at all.

---

## 🌱 Network Growth And Change

Graphs can track:

- new nodes;
- new bridges;
- cluster merging;
- increased cross-community contact;
- disappearance of central actors;
- changes after crisis;
- movement between groups.

The same change may reflect:

- mobilisation;
- influence;
- vulnerability;
- ordinary life.

A growing network may result from:

- a wedding;
- a new job;
- a protest;
- a support campaign;
- a public controversy;
- a religious festival;
- a crisis response.

Change is observable.

Meaning still requires context.

---

## 📊 From Description To Ranking

The escalation may look like this:

1. identify connections;
2. classify relationship types;
3. measure network position;
4. infer influence or trust;
5. rank nodes and relationships;
6. prioritise monitoring or intervention.

The dangerous transition is from:

> This person connects two groups.

to:

> This connection should be disrupted.

The graph becomes coercive when description begins deciding which edges should survive.

At that point, network analysis is no longer simply explaining a social structure.

It is shaping one.

---

## 🌡️ Relationship-Risk Scoring

A hypothetical system might score relationships according to:

- strength of tie;
- bridge value;
- institutional access;
- resource transfer;
- credibility;
- coalition potential;
- likelihood of disclosure;
- resistance to pressure;
- capacity to mobilise others.

These are plausible design variables.

They do not prove that a particular system is using them.

A relationship may be ranked as more consequential than either person alone.

That is the central concern developed in [🕸️ Who May Safely Know Whom?](../🧭_Orientation/🕸️_who_may_safely_know_whom.md).

Relationship risk is not merely the sum of two personal risk scores.

The edge can create new capacity.

---

## 🦷 Network Disruption

A system seeking to weaken a network might attempt to:

- remove a node;
- isolate a node;
- reduce visibility;
- sever an edge;
- overload a bridge;
- insert an intermediary;
- redirect resources;
- damage trust;
- centralise access through a gatekeeper.

This node does not provide instructions for disrupting networks.

Its purpose is to make the capability legible.

Network disruption may occur without formal prohibition.

People may remain technically free to associate while:

- introductions disappear;
- trust is damaged;
- access narrows;
- costs rise;
- key bridges become exhausted.

That is why network governance may operate through friction rather than bans.

---

## 🪲 Graph Errors

Common graph errors include:

- identity merging;
- duplicate nodes;
- missing aliases;
- shared devices;
- shared addresses;
- household accounts;
- public-event attendance;
- organisational mailing lists;
- automated accounts;
- historical contacts;
- mistaken direction of influence.

One false edge may distort:

- cluster membership;
- centrality;
- bridge scores;
- inferred risk;
- relationship strength.

More calculations do not automatically correct a bad input.

A sophisticated metric built on a false edge remains wrong.

---

## 👁️ Visual Authority

Network diagrams look persuasive.

Dense lines and highlighted nodes create an impression of:

- completeness;
- precision;
- hidden insight;
- structural truth.

But layout choices affect perception.

The designer decides:

- which nodes appear central;
- which clusters look separate;
- which edges remain visible;
- which thresholds remove weaker ties;
- which categories receive colour or emphasis.

A graph is not a window onto the network.

It is an argument about what the network contains.

Visual clarity can conceal evidentiary uncertainty.

A neat diagram may make weak inferences look settled.

---

## 🕳️ Unknown And Hidden Relationships

Absence of an edge may mean:

- no relationship;
- no observed trace;
- communication outside the dataset;
- incomplete identity matching;
- privacy;
- missing records.

Systems may use link prediction to estimate unobserved relationships.

Predicted connections should never be displayed as established facts.

The graph’s empty spaces are unknown, not automatically concealed.

A missing edge is not proof of secret contact.

A private relationship is not a hostile one merely because the system cannot observe it.

---

## ⚖️ Political And Ethical Risks

Social-graph analysis can create risks including:

- guilt by association;
- political profiling;
- discrimination by network position;
- exposure of confidential relationships;
- chilling lawful association;
- misidentifying journalists, lawyers, doctors, or carers as political organisers;
- family and community harm;
- disproportionate targeting of bridge figures.

Association is democratic infrastructure.

It is not a suspicious externality.

A graph that treats lawful connection as risk can convert ordinary social life into a basis for intervention.

---

## 🛡️ Minimum Governance Questions

Any system using social graphs should answer:

- What entities count as nodes?
- What evidence creates an edge?
- Are inferred and confirmed edges visually distinct?
- What time period does the graph cover?
- What confidence applies to each link?
- Which relationships are excluded?
- How are shared devices and households handled?
- What centrality measures are used?
- What interpretation is attached to those measures?
- Which decisions use the graph?
- Can subjects inspect or challenge links?
- Are sensitive professional relationships protected?
- What happens when an edge is wrong?
- When are historical edges removed?

A system that cannot answer these questions should not use graph outputs to guide coercive decisions.

---

## ⚠️ What This Node Is Not Claiming

This node does not claim that:

- a graph proves political coordination;
- high centrality proves leadership;
- a bridge figure is automatically an operative;
- repeated contact proves trust;
- co-location proves a relationship;
- shared affiliation proves direct contact;
- link prediction establishes hidden conduct;
- social-network analysis is inherently abusive.

Abuse depends on:

- data;
- interpretation;
- purpose;
- intervention;
- consequence.

The graph is a capability.

What matters is how it is governed and used.

---

## 🧿 Compact Definition

**Social Graphs And Network Analysis** describes the methods by which systems turn records into models of connection.

Those models may identify:

- clusters;
- bridges;
- brokers;
- weak ties;
- central actors;
- possible routes of influence.

The danger begins when uncertain or context-poor relationships are treated as reliable political facts, and when descriptive analysis begins deciding which connections should be tolerated, monitored, or disrupted.
