# 💾 What The Database Eats
**First created:** 2026-07-19 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-19  
*How ordinary records become relational intelligence when systems begin joining, interpreting, and acting on them.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

> Feed me data.

The Little Database does not begin by eating whole lives.

It begins by eating fragments.

A contact list.

A payroll record.

A border crossing.

A mailing-list subscription.

A shared address.

A protest registration.

A consular note.

A family relationship.

A location ping.

A payment.

A risk flag.

Each record may have been created for an ordinary reason.

Some may have been collected lawfully.

Some may have been gathered to provide a service.

Some may be accurate.

Some may be necessary.

The danger begins when these fragments are joined and asked to answer questions they were never created to answer.

Not merely:

> Who is this person?

But:

> Who do they know?

> Who do they rely on?

> Who can protect them?

> Who can give them access?

> Who might act with them?

> Which relationships make them harder to isolate?

The database does not eat people.

It eats traces of people and mistakes the resulting pattern for the person.

---

## 🩸 What Counts As Food?

The Little Database does not need one complete master record.

It may consume information from many domains:

- identity;
- devices and communications;
- location and travel;
- money and employment;
- institutional affiliation;
- religious and cultural belonging;
- family and intimate relationships;
- political expression;
- commercial profiling;
- administrative risk systems.

Each category reveals something different.

Identity data help decide who the system thinks a person is.

Communications data reveal contact.

Location data reveal proximity.

Financial data reveal support and dependency.

Institutional records reveal access and formal belonging.

Family records reveal care and leverage.

Political data reveal expression, reach, and possible mobilisation.

Commercial data fill gaps.

Administrative labels tell other institutions what they are expected to think.

The political capability does not emerge from collection alone.

It emerges from connection, interpretation, and use.

## 🪪 Identity Data

Before the system can map relationships, it must decide who it thinks each person is.

Identity data may include:

- names;
- aliases;
- dates of birth;
- addresses;
- photographs;
- biometrics;
- passport details;
- visa and immigration records;
- telephone numbers;
- email addresses;
- device identifiers;
- account handles;
- professional registrations.

Identity resolution allows records from different systems to be joined.

That process is not neutral.

It can:

- merge two people with similar names;
- split one person across several records;
- attach another person’s history;
- preserve an outdated address or alias;
- mistake a shared account for an individual one;
- connect a recycled phone number to the wrong person.

A system may look sophisticated while resting on one early mistake about identity.

Every later inference then inherits the error.

The first question is therefore not only:

> What does the system know?

It is:

> Has the system correctly identified the person it is talking about?

---

## 📱 Communications Data

Communications records may include:

- contact lists;
- call records;
- message metadata;
- group memberships;
- email headers;
- account interactions;
- cloud backups;
- login histories;
- device synchronisation records.

Message content is not always required.

Metadata may reveal:

- who contacted whom;
- how often;
- at what time;
- for how long;
- through which account;
- before or after which event.

From those traces, a system may infer:

- familiarity;
- urgency;
- hierarchy;
- trust;
- coordination;
- influence.

But repeated contact does not explain the relationship.

A lawyer may speak frequently to a client.

A doctor may contact a patient.

A journalist may contact a source.

A carer may repeatedly call someone in crisis.

A family group may generate hundreds of messages without producing any political coordination at all.

Metadata can reveal the shape of contact.

It cannot reliably reveal its meaning.

---

## 📍 Location And Movement

Movement data may come from:

- mobile devices;
- application permissions;
- Wi-Fi connections;
- Bluetooth proximity;
- transport cards;
- vehicle records;
- airline bookings;
- rail bookings;
- border systems;
- hotel records;
- event registration;
- access-control systems.

These records may show that:

- two devices were near one another;
- two people travelled on the same route;
- someone entered a building;
- someone attended an event;
- a person repeatedly visited a neighbourhood.

From that, a system may infer:

- contact;
- association;
- routine;
- political activity;
- shared planning.

But:

> Co-location is not conversation.

> Attendance is not allegiance.

> Shared travel is not automatically coordination.

A person may attend an opponent’s event.

A device may remain in a vehicle.

A shared household may produce repeated proximity.

A location estimate may be wrong.

The record shows geography.

The political meaning is added later.

## 💸 Financial And Employment Data

Financial and employment records may include:

- wages;
- contracts;
- donations;
- grants;
- loans;
- gifts;
- remittances;
- rent;
- household transfers;
- shared accounts;
- board memberships;
- procurement records;
- professional sponsorship.

These data may reveal:

- dependency;
- support;
- hierarchy;
- access;
- resource transfer;
- possible leverage.

A payment can be interpreted as:

- funding;
- reimbursement;
- care;
- obligation;
- debt;
- household support;
- political sponsorship.

The record does not explain which.

Money reveals connection.

It does not explain loyalty.

Employment data may also reveal where pressure can be applied:

- salary;
- references;
- professional licensing;
- visa sponsorship;
- contracts;
- institutional reputation;
- housing tied to work.

Material relationships matter politically because they shape what people can continue doing.

That makes them useful to a protective system.

It also makes them useful to a coercive one.

## 🏛️ Institutional, Religious, And Cultural Affiliation

Affiliation records may include:

- university enrolment;
- employment;
- professional membership;
- trade-union membership;
- party membership;
- charity involvement;
- religious participation;
- cultural organisations;
- alumni groups;
- mailing lists;
- volunteering.

These records may reveal:

- where people gather;
- who holds formal authority;
- who can make introductions;
- which institutions confer legitimacy;
- who has access to policymakers or vulnerable groups.

But affiliation is not allegiance.

A person may:

- attend without agreeing;
- remain on an old list;
- belong professionally rather than politically;
- participate critically;
- enter a building for a public event;
- inherit a religious or cultural connection without endorsing leadership.

Formal representatives are not automatically representative.

Institutional records become dangerous when belonging is treated as obedience.

## 🧬 Family, Friendship, And Intimacy

Relational data may include:

- birth records;
- marriage records;
- household composition;
- dependants;
- emergency contacts;
- shared property;
- immigration sponsorship;
- care arrangements;
- repeated communication;
- repeated co-location;
- shared finances.

These data may reveal:

- care;
- protection;
- resilience;
- dependency;
- obligation;
- possible leverage.

Family and intimate ties may determine:

- who provides housing;
- who pays for travel;
- who notices harm;
- who stores evidence;
- who can be pressured;
- who can help someone leave.

Chosen family and friendship may be less visible in formal records.

The system may try to infer them from:

- communication frequency;
- location;
- money;
- event attendance;
- shared contacts.

That inference is fragile.

A system may detect proximity.

It cannot reliably measure:

- love;
- trust;
- estrangement;
- fear;
- duty;
- coercion;
- grief;
- obligation.

The relationships most important to survival may be both poorly represented and dangerously exposed.

## 📣 Political And Influence Data

Political data may include:

- public posts;
- private messages where accessed;
- petitions;
- donations;
- protest attendance;
- organisational roles;
- media appearances;
- event participation;
- contact with journalists;
- contact with lawyers;
- contact with politicians;
- search and engagement behaviour.

A system may attempt to infer:

- sentiment;
- ideology;
- influence;
- mobilisation capacity;
- hostility;
- susceptibility;
- credibility;
- network reach.

These categories are unstable.

Political expression may involve:

- sarcasm;
- quotation;
- criticism;
- humour;
- code-switching;
- multilingual language;
- strategic ambiguity;
- changed belief.

Association does not prove agreement.

Reach is not the same as influence.

Popularity is not the same as authority.

Political sentiment is not a stable substance waiting to be extracted from a person.

## 🏷️ Administrative And Risk Records

Administrative records may include:

- police intelligence;
- safeguarding notes;
- immigration classifications;
- counter-extremism flags;
- consular records;
- professional concerns;
- institutional complaints;
- reputational warnings;
- risk scores.

These records may carry disproportionate weight because they appear official.

A later decision-maker may assume that:

- the information was verified;
- the category was justified;
- the person had a route to challenge it;
- the original context remains accurate.

Those assumptions may be false.

The record may be:

- unverified;
- stale;
- copied;
- context-free;
- impossible to correct;
- based on another institution’s error.

An administrative label can travel farther than the evidence beneath it.

The warning is repeated.

The source disappears.

The repetition itself begins to look like corroboration.

## 🧩 Commercial And Brokered Data

The Little Database does not need to collect everything itself.

It may:

- buy;
- request;
- exchange;
- inherit;
- scrape;
- infer.

Commercial and brokered data may include:

- consumer profiles;
- location histories;
- household information;
- device graphs;
- inferred interests;
- wealth estimates;
- employment information;
- online behaviour;
- professional records;
- likely associations.

A commercial record may have been created for:

- advertising;
- fraud prevention;
- market research;
- credit assessment;
- audience segmentation.

It may later be reused for:

- public-sector risk analysis;
- political profiling;
- intelligence enrichment;
- relationship mapping.

The original purpose does not travel automatically with the data.

Neither do the original safeguards.

## 🔄 From Record To Relationship

The database’s most important work happens between the raw record and the final decision.

### Example One: Event Attendance

**Record:** two devices appear at the same event.

**Inference:** the owners attended together.

**Interpretation:** they are political collaborators.

**Decision:** increase scrutiny.

---

### Example Two: Financial Transfer

**Record:** one person transfers money to another.

**Inference:** one person funds the other.

**Interpretation:** the relationship supports political activity.

**Decision:** classify the pair as higher risk.

---

### Example Three: Professional Contact

**Record:** a lawyer contacts several affected people.

**Inference:** the lawyer is a bridge figure.

**Interpretation:** the network may produce litigation.

**Decision:** monitor, reroute, or obstruct access.

---

Each stage adds assumptions.

By the time the decision is made, the output may be described as data-driven.

But the crucial steps were interpretive.

The system becomes most dangerous in the steps it presents as calculation but which are actually judgement.

## 🪜 Facts, Proxies, And Guesses

A useful internal ladder is:

### 1. Direct Record

A call occurred.

### 2. Administrative Fact

Two identifiers were linked.

### 3. Proxy

Frequency is used as a stand-in for closeness.

### 4. Inference

The relationship is classified as trusted.

### 5. Political Interpretation

The relationship is assessed as capable of enabling organisation.

### 6. Operational Decision

The system intervenes.

These levels must not be collapsed.

A record can be accurate while the proxy is poor.

A proxy can be reasonable while the inference is wrong.

An inference can be plausible while the intervention is unlawful or disproportionate.

## 🫥 Context Collapse

Datasets often preserve the record while losing the reason for the record.

A lawyer speaks to a client.

A doctor contacts a patient.

A family member sends rent.

A journalist contacts a source.

An activist attends an opponent’s event.

A person remains on an old mailing list.

Once detached from context, each trace can be reinterpreted.

The record survives.

The reason for the record disappears.

Context collapse is especially dangerous when:

- one body collects the data;
- another enriches it;
- a third scores it;
- a fourth acts on the result.

Each step moves farther from the original relationship.

## 🪲 Errors That Travel

Common data-quality problems include:

- misspellings;
- duplicate identities;
- stale addresses;
- shared phones;
- recycled numbers;
- family accounts;
- common names;
- incorrect relationship labels;
- delayed updates;
- historical contacts;
- imported errors.

Joining more datasets can expose an error.

It can also amplify it.

A wrong address may be matched to a household.

The household may be linked to an organisation.

The organisation may be assigned a political category.

The person may then inherit the category.

Shared errors may reveal shared infrastructure.

But corrections often travel less efficiently than accusations.

One system updates the record.

Another keeps the old version.

A third treats the discrepancy as further uncertainty.

The plant is fed again.

## 🕳️ Absence As Data

Systems may also interpret what they cannot see.

No recent communication may be read as:

- relationship breakdown;
- covert communication;
- operational security;
- disengagement;
- hidden coordination.

Refusal to provide data may be treated as suspicious.

Encryption may be interpreted as intent.

Silence may be assigned meaning.

But:

> Silence is not a confession.

Missing data should remain unknown.

A gap is not proof of concealment.

The inability to observe a relationship does not make the relationship hostile.

## 🩸 The Appetite Problem

The model produces uncertainty.

Officials ask for more data.

More data produce:

- more contradictions;
- more ambiguous relationships;
- more anomalies;
- more pressure to widen collection.

The cycle may look like this:

1. insufficient confidence;
2. additional collection;
3. new categories;
4. more contradictions;
5. expanded intervention;
6. community reaction;
7. reaction treated as confirmation.

The system begins assuming that every failure reflects insufficient access.

It rarely asks whether the question itself can be answered reliably.

The database keeps demanding more intimate data because the last batch did not make human beings predictable enough.

The model’s hunger is not a validation metric.

---

## 🌫️ What More Data Cannot Solve

More data cannot automatically resolve:

- ambiguity;
- context;
- irony;
- love;
- fear;
- duty;
- coercion;
- estrangement;
- political change;
- human inconsistency.

The problem may not be insufficient data.

The problem may be that the system is trying to quantify something unstable, contextual, or fundamentally human.

More data can make a wrong interpretation more elaborate without making it more true.

A detailed social graph can still misunderstand the relationships it depicts.

A high-confidence score can still rest on a bad proxy.

A large database can still be wrong at scale.

## ⚖️ Legitimate Collection, Dangerous Reuse

A record may be collected legitimately for:

- healthcare;
- welfare;
- travel;
- employment;
- safeguarding;
- taxation;
- education;
- event administration.

The harm may arise later through:

- retention;
- sharing;
- enrichment;
- political reinterpretation;
- automated scoring;
- use beyond the original purpose.

Important questions include:

- Was the person told?
- Was the new purpose compatible with the original one?
- Could they refuse?
- Could they correct the record?
- Did the original safeguards travel with the data?
- Could the new system infer relationships never disclosed directly?
- What decisions could the enriched record affect?

Data do not remain innocent merely because their first purpose was legitimate.

---

## 🛡️ Minimum Governance Questions

Every dataset should be forced to answer:

- Who collected it?
- Why was it collected?
- Under what authority?
- How accurate is it?
- What context has been lost?
- Who can access it?
- What other data can it be joined with?
- What proxies are used?
- What relationships are inferred?
- What decisions can the output influence?
- How can the person correct it?
- When is it deleted?
- What happens when the system is wrong?

A system that cannot answer these questions safely should not be fed more data.

## ⚠️ What This Node Is Not Claiming

This node does not claim that:

- every dataset forms part of a political system;
- every data join is abusive;
- every administrative record is inaccurate;
- every inference is illegitimate;
- every missing record indicates concealment;
- every institution shares the same infrastructure;
- one complete database has already ingested every category described here.

- possible inputs;
- transformation risks;
- inference problems;
- governance questions;
- testable mechanisms.

The existence of the food does not prove that one plant has eaten it all.

## 🧿 Compact Definition

**What The Database Eats** is the input model for the Little Database of Horrors.

It describes how ordinary records from different domains may be:

- identified;
- joined;
- enriched;
- interpreted;
- scored;
- used to infer relationships and political capacity.

The central danger is not that records exist.

It is that traces created for separate purposes are combined and treated as a reliable model of a person’s social world.
