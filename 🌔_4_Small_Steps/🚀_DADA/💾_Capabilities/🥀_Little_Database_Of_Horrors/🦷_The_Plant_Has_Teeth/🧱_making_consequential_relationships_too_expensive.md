# 🧱 Making Consequential Relationships Too Expensive
**First created:** 2026-07-21 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-21  
*How the cumulative costs of lawful closeness can be distributed across targets, supporters, families, professionals, and institutions until relationships capable of producing material change become unsustainable.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Nobody prohibited the relationship.

It simply began charging for every ordinary thing:

- a meeting;
- a phone call;
- a journey;
- a public word of support;
- a place to stay;
- a legal letter;
- one more attempt to repair the misunderstanding;
- one more form proving why contact should remain possible.

At first the cost was manageable.

Then the relationship required travel, legal advice, repeated reassurance, secrecy, professional risk, family negotiation, administrative labour, money neither person could safely spare, and health neither person could afford to lose.

Neither person necessarily decided that the relationship did not matter.

They may have decided they could no longer keep paying what it had begun to cost.

> The relationship is not forbidden. It is priced beyond the capacity of the people trying to sustain it.

This node closes *🦷 The Plant Has Teeth* by gathering the shared logic beneath the preceding mechanisms.

The governing question is:

> Who can afford to remain close once closeness creates recurring costs for everyone involved?

The central propositions are:

> The decisive cost may not be paid by the target, but by everyone whose help could change the target’s position.

> A relationship becomes containable when support requires exceptional labour and withdrawal restores ordinary life.

> The system need not make connection impossible. It needs to make disconnection cheaper.

This node does not reduce love, friendship, solidarity, professional commitment, or chosen family to transactions.

Every serious relationship requires time, compromise, emotional labour, money, care, and limits.

The concern is not that a relationship has costs.

It is whether lawful and potentially protective relationships repeatedly acquire exceptional, externally imposed, selectively transferred, or foreseeably unmanageable costs that diminish when the relationship loses practical consequence.

---

## 🧭 What Makes A Relationship Consequential

A relationship is consequential where it creates capacity.

It may produce:

- evidence;
- protection;
- money;
- housing;
- transport;
- legal action;
- publication;
- political access;
- relocation;
- professional legitimacy;
- public corroboration;
- cross-community connection;
- durable care;
- a credible route out.

It may be intimate, familial, professional, legal, political, communal, journalistic, or practical.

> Consequence lies not in the label attached to the relationship, but in what the people become able to do together.

A relationship becomes institutionally consequential when affection, trust, contact, or shared knowledge begins converting into action, access, shelter, testimony, money, coalition, or institutional ownership.

---

## 🧾 The Invoice Nobody Issued

The costs may never appear on one statement.

They may be distributed across:

- fares;
- missed work;
- legal consultations;
- childcare;
- accommodation;
- inaccessible transport;
- complaint responses;
- damaged reputation;
- family conflict;
- professional caution;
- disrupted sleep;
- medical deterioration;
- lost opportunity.

> The absence of one invoice does not mean nobody is paying.

A useful audit asks:

- What did the relationship require before pressure appeared?
- What does it require now?
- Who pays each cost?
- Which costs disappear if the relationship becomes less consequential?
- Which costs are ordinary?
- Which are externally created?
- Which are transferred from institutions onto private people?

---

## 🪙 Ordinary Cost, Structural Burden, And Imposed Cost

Three categories should remain distinct.

### Ordinary relationship cost

The time, care, compromise, conflict, and money that real relationships naturally require.

### Structural burden

Costs produced by disability, poverty, geography, prejudice, inaccessible services, immigration systems, or scarcity.

### Imposed or selectively intensified cost

Costs deliberately created, knowingly transferred, or foreseeably allowed to accumulate around a particular relationship or class of relationship.

> Some relationships are expensive because life is hard. The model matters where the cost repeatedly tracks consequence rather than ordinary complexity.

Mixed causes are common.

An inaccessible journey may become more damaging because a document is delayed. A difficult family may become more coercive because employment or travel risk is introduced. An ordinary professional boundary may become containment when every alternative route has already disappeared.

---

## 🧱 The Cost Stack

A consequential relationship may accumulate several costs at once.

### Emotional

Fear, guilt, repeated reassurance, trauma activation, uncertainty, conflict, and dread of causing harm.

### Reputational

Warnings, guilt by association, public scrutiny, community judgement, and loss of standing.

### Professional

Complaints, boundary allegations, conflict-of-interest claims, disciplinary risk, funding concern, and lost promotion.

### Financial

Travel, legal fees, missed work, housing, document costs, care, and account disruption.

### Administrative

Forms, evidence demands, reviews, appointments, record correction, and repeated explanation.

### Familial

Pressure on relatives, childcare, caregiving, immigration, inheritance, and family conflict.

### Physical

Pain, fatigue, inaccessible travel, health deterioration, and inability to recover between demands.

### Political And Security

Surveillance anxiety, public exposure, institutional suspicion, cross-border pressure, and foreign-state risk.

> No single cost needs to end the relationship if enough costs arrive together.

---

## 🔄 From Classification To Cost

The earlier nodes describe the cost stack as it forms.

- **Suppressed introductions:** the relationship never begins, so trust and shared capacity cannot accumulate.
- **Visibility reduction:** the person remains present but is rerouted away from people able to act.
- **Reputation warnings:** association acquires perceived risk before direct experience can contradict it.
- **Wedge creation:** the relationship spends energy verifying, repairing, and surviving ambiguity.
- **Family pressure:** third parties begin paying for the relationship’s existence.
- **Travel leverage:** every meeting acquires geographic, document, and return risk.
- **Legal and administrative pressure:** time, money, and health are consumed by procedure.
- **Functional isolation:** the remaining network loses the people able to provide durable protection.

> The final tooth does not add one new mechanism. It shows the invoice produced when all the others arrive together.

---

## 🎚️ The Conversion Threshold

A relationship may be tolerated while it provides companionship, sympathy, occasional contact, symbolic solidarity, private concern, or low-level practical help.

Friction may rise when it begins producing documentation, legal strategy, housing, public corroboration, funding, relocation, coalition, institutional access, or durable protection.

> The threshold is crossed when connection begins converting into capacity.

A useful comparison is not merely whether the relationship was allowed.

It is which forms remained affordable, which forms attracted exceptional cost, and whether cost rose as practical consequence increased.

---

## 🧱 The Relationship Cap

A relationship cap describes the level of consequence beyond which costs begin rising.

A possible pattern is:

1. casual contact remains easy;
2. emotional closeness is tolerated;
3. practical assistance attracts caution;
4. public support attracts warning;
5. institutional action attracts formal pressure;
6. durable coalition attracts multiple forms of interference.

> The cap does not limit how much the people may care. It limits how much their care is allowed to change.

This remains a hypothesis to test, not a universal rule.

---

## 👤 Cost Imposed On The Target

The target may need to explain the situation repeatedly, reassure supporters, protect others from risk, fund travel, manage family consequences, preserve evidence, remain calm enough to appear credible, and keep the relationship alive while also needing it for support.

> The person most in need of support may be required to perform the largest share of the labour necessary to make support possible.

This burden intensifies where the person is disabled, traumatised, in pain, financially precarious, isolated, or already managing several institutions.

The target may become unable to accept help because accepting help creates more work than they can complete.

---

## 🤝 Cost Imposed On The Supporter

The supporter may face reputational warning, employer concern, professional referral, family pressure, security anxiety, financial exposure, legal threats, emotional exhaustion, demands for evidence, and fear of making matters worse.

> The supporter does not need to stop believing the person. They may only need to become unable to carry the consequences of continuing to act on that belief.

Belief may remain. Affection may remain. Solidarity may remain. Material support may still disappear.

---

## 🏛️ Cost Imposed On The Institution

An institution may perceive involvement as requiring legal review, security consultation, safeguarding work, senior approval, media management, data protection, staff time, and political explanation.

It may therefore prefer a generic referral, symbolic contact, an approved intermediary, a lower-capacity substitute, or no direct relationship.

> Institutions often describe the relationship as complicated when what they mean is that supporting it would require them to accept responsibility.

The complexity may be genuine.

The question is whether complexity repeatedly functions as justification for giving the person less.

---

## 🌐 Cost Imposed On The Wider Network

A community, campaign, charity, workplace, or informal network may absorb complaints, donor concern, insurance risk, venue difficulty, staff time, internal conflict, security questions, and media scrutiny.

> The relationship becomes too expensive when the network is made to believe that one person’s inclusion endangers the whole structure.

Withdrawal may then appear prudent, managerial, protective, or financially necessary.

No direct prohibition is required.

---

## ⏳ Time Cost

Consequential relationships need time for understanding, planning, repair, travel, advocacy, documentation, and ordinary presence.

Administrative and professional pressure may remove that time.

> A relationship starved of ordinary time may remain sincere while never becoming durable.

A relationship kept permanently busy surviving itself has little capacity left to change anything outside it.

---

## 💷 Money Cost

The relationship may require fares, accommodation, legal advice, childcare, accessibility support, secure communication, missed work, housing, medical care, and emergency expenses.

One person may pay more, creating guilt, dependency, imbalance, fear of exploitation allegations, and concern from families or employers.

> Cost does not only weaken relationships. It can deform them by making one person finance the conditions under which the relationship is allowed to continue.

The danger lies not in support itself, but in externally inflated cost and the removal of alternatives.

---

## 🏷️ Reputational Tax

Each public association may require the supporter to explain why they remain involved, whether they endorse everything, whether they understand the risk, and whether the person is safe, stable, credible, or politically acceptable.

> A reputational tax is imposed when ordinary association repeatedly requires a public defence.

The tax may deter employers, professionals, friends, organisations, potential partners, and funders.

---

## 💼 Professional Liability

Professionals may fear boundary allegations, complaints, conflict-of-interest claims, safeguarding scrutiny, regulatory referral, security concern, and reputational damage.

> Professional distance becomes a control mechanism when the cost of helping is made to look greater than the cost of abandonment.

Legitimate boundaries remain necessary.

The relevant question is whether the boundary tracks identifiable harm, consent, power imbalance, and proper role—or merely the relationship’s growing consequence.

---

## ⚖️ Legal Cost

A relationship may require advice, representation, confidentiality management, disclosure review, litigation defence, immigration expertise, and evidence preservation.

> Legal risk can drain ordinary trust from a relationship by requiring every human act to become evidence-safe.

A relationship cannot easily remain mutual and ordinary if every message must also survive disclosure, complaint, regulatory scrutiny, or public quotation.

---

## 🧾 Administrative Cost

The relationship may require approval, registration, disclosure, vetting, repeated forms, proof of role, justification of contact, recordkeeping, and permission to meet.

> Administration becomes relational control when staying connected requires constant proof that connection is legitimate.

Each requirement may appear small. Together they may replace the relationship itself with the labour of defending its existence.

---

## 🛂 Travel And Geography Cost

Distance may require visas, passports, flights, accessible transport, accommodation, carers, and uncertainty about safe return.

> A relationship cannot become ordinary when every meeting has the logistics and cost of an expedition.

The journey may be technically possible and practically unsustainable.

---

## 👪 Family And Third-Party Cost

Relatives may absorb fear, gossip, document difficulty, employment risk, financial loss, caregiving burden, and pressure from authorities or communities.

> The relationship becomes expensive when people who never chose it are made to pay for its existence.

The analytical question is whether third-party cost has been created, selectively intensified, or used as leverage.

---

## ♿ Health And Disability Cost

The same relationship may require far more from a person managing pain, limited mobility, cognitive fatigue, fluctuating capacity, medication effects, sensory barriers, care needs, or inaccessible transport.

> A relationship becomes unaffordable faster when every ordinary act of sustaining it requires the body to cross an inaccessible system.

This burden may be structural rather than deliberate. Its role in relationship loss still matters.

---

## 🔍 Verification And Repair Cost

Wedges and contradictory messages may require checking identities, confirming intent, preserving records, comparing accounts, repeated reassurance, neutral mediation, and rebuilding trust.

> A relationship can be kept busy surviving itself until it has no energy left to change anything outside it.

Repair is ordinary. Permanent verification labour is not.

---

## 🤐 Secrecy Cost

A relationship kept private may avoid some exposure. It may also lose witnesses, legitimacy, practical support, continuity, and ordinary social recognition.

> Secrecy lowers one kind of risk by making the relationship carry every other risk alone.

A relationship can remain emotionally real while becoming materially invisible.

---

## ❔ The Uncertainty Premium

Supporters may not know whether contact is permitted, whether the person is under investigation, whether association creates professional risk, whether travel will remain possible, whether a warning is credible, or whether helping will worsen the situation.

> The uncertainty premium is the extra cost people pay because nobody will tell them which risks are real.

The safest response may appear to be delay, distance, symbolic support, no public association, or referral elsewhere.

---

## 🚪 Opportunity Cost

Time and resources spent sustaining the relationship cannot be spent elsewhere.

The target may lose work, treatment, rest, other relationships, legal deadlines, and housing opportunities.

The supporter may lose promotion, family time, income, social standing, political capital, and other responsibilities.

> The relationship becomes harder to sustain when persistence requires both people to sacrifice the rest of the lives that would have made persistence possible.

---

## ⚖️ Cost Asymmetry

The supporter may often be able to leave and recover ordinary life.

Withdrawal may restore professional safety, family peace, time, money, reputation, and administrative simplicity.

The target does not receive the same restoration.

> The supporter can often exit the cost structure. The target is the cost structure’s address.

This asymmetry explains attrition without shaming those who reach their limit.

---

## 🔄 Withdrawal Restores Ordinary Life

A powerful signal may be what changes after distance.

The supporter may experience fewer warnings, less scrutiny, lower legal risk, reduced family conflict, professional normality, returned time, and financial relief.

> A relationship becomes containable when withdrawal is the first decision that makes the rest of life ordinary again.

This does not prove deliberate imposition. It may still reveal where pressure was concentrated.

---

## 🪴 The Substitute Economy

When consequential relationships become expensive, institutions may offer cheaper substitutes:

- helplines;
- generic support;
- occasional check-ins;
- consultation;
- symbolic visibility;
- approved intermediaries;
- low-authority contacts.

These may be useful.

They become containment where they consistently replace direct access, housing, legal action, durable advocacy, relocation, or institutional ownership.

> The cheaper substitute may reduce distress while leaving the material problem carefully untouched.

---

## 🕯️ Symbolic Relationships Remain Affordable

Relationships with little material consequence may attract fewer institutional costs.

> The system may tolerate connection most easily where connection cannot produce an exit.

This does not make symbolic or emotional connection false. It identifies a difference in institutional tolerance.

---

## 🎚️ Selective Affordability

The same relationship may be affordable in one form and expensive in another:

- private but not public;
- emotional but not practical;
- online but not physical;
- symbolic but not financial;
- temporary but not durable;
- consultative but not decision-making.

> The relationship is not rejected outright. It is permitted only in forms that do not accumulate power.

---

## 🩺 Professionalisation Of All Support

As informal support becomes risky, every relationship may be pushed toward case management, safeguarding, recorded appointments, formal boundaries, approved channels, and professional oversight.

Professional support can be valuable. It cannot replace friendship, chosen family, solidarity, ordinary companionship, and mutual care.

> A life becomes institutionally lonely when every safe relationship must first become a service.

---

## 🚨 Crisis-Only Economics

A relationship organised entirely around emergencies may lack time for ordinary trust, shared pleasure, future planning, mutual care, and strategic work.

> Crisis can keep people connected while preventing the relationship from becoming stable enough to become protective.

There is no surplus from which to build a future.

---

## 🪫 Burnout And Attrition

Supporters may face constant crisis, unclear role, institutional hostility, no supervision, financial strain, repeated failure to achieve change, and fear of making mistakes.

> Burnout is not evidence that the relationship lacked value. It may be evidence that too much public failure was loaded onto private care.

Withdrawal may be necessary. Limits are real.

The protective response is not endless sacrifice. It is reducing the avoidable costs that made sacrifice the only available infrastructure.

---

## ↔️ One-Way Care And Loss Of Mutuality

A relationship under pressure may become dominated by crisis management, evidence work, reassurance, practical rescue, and legal or administrative demands.

> A relationship becomes harder to sustain when the system permits one person to appear only as need and the other only as resource.

The person receiving support may lose opportunities to give care, bring pleasure, contribute skills, and be known outside the crisis.

---

## 🏷️ The “Too Much” Person

A person may acquire a reputation as resource-intensive, legally risky, demanding, complex, hard to place, difficult to support, or reputationally sensitive.

> Once the person is classified as expensive, every institution can justify giving them less while insisting that the problem is the cost of helping.

Reducing support increases crisis. Crisis increases visible need. Visible need confirms the label.

---

## 🔁 Dependency Created And Then Condemned

As alternatives disappear, one relationship may carry too much.

That concentration can produce dependency, fear, boundary strain, and vulnerability to loss.

Institutions may then cite the dependency as a reason to restrict the relationship.

> The system removes the alternatives and then treats reliance on what remains as evidence that the remaining relationship is unsafe.

Real dependency concerns should be addressed by expanding support, restoring alternatives, preserving consent, and reducing single points of failure.

---

## 🧬 Social Selection Effects

High costs do not remove everyone equally.

People with stable careers, family responsibilities, public reputations, professional regulation, and secure but risk-sensitive lives may be more easily priced out.

Those who remain may be similarly excluded, precarious, unusually risk-tolerant, dependent themselves, or unable to provide durable material support.

> Relationship pricing changes not only how many people remain, but which kinds of people are able to remain.

This identifies a distorted social ecology. It must not be used to demean those who stay.

---

## 🧊 Chilling Effects Before Contact

A person may never become a supporter because the anticipated cost arrives first.

They may hear that the situation is complicated, there are safeguarding issues, involvement could affect their work, direct contact is not advised, or there may be legal risk.

> The cheapest consequential relationship to contain is the one whose future cost is communicated before it begins.

---

## 📊 Cost As Evidence And Self-Confirmation

The fact that a relationship requires extraordinary effort may later be used as evidence that it was inappropriate, unstable, dependent, obsessive, unsustainable, or manipulative.

> The pressure creates the cost and the cost is later cited as proof that pressure was necessary.

A high-cost relationship may genuinely be unsafe or incompatible. The question is whether externally imposed conditions materially produced the features later used against it.

---

## 🧮 The Cumulative Cost Ledger

A qualitative ledger may record:

- time spent;
- money spent;
- work lost;
- travel burden;
- legal exposure;
- family impact;
- health impact;
- institutions engaged;
- repair attempts;
- practical capacity lost;
- outcomes achieved;
- costs that disappeared after withdrawal.

> The purpose is not to assign a value to the relationship. It is to identify who is being required to pay for its continuation.

The ledger must remain descriptive. It must not become another relationship score.

---

## 🧪 The Evidence Problem

Every serious relationship has costs.

High cost alone does not prove deliberate interference.

Potential evidence of structured relationship pricing may include:

- costs rising near consequential thresholds;
- supporters receiving linked warnings or processes;
- emotional support being tolerated while practical support triggers scrutiny;
- low-capacity substitutes being repeatedly offered;
- comparable relationships facing fewer burdens;
- costs reducing after withdrawal;
- the same mechanisms recurring;
- records showing intent to create distance;
- institutional knowledge of cumulative burden without corrective action.

> A difficult or expensive relationship is not proof of engineered interference. The relevant evidence lies in which costs appeared, who imposed them, when they escalated, what capacity they removed, and whether the same threshold recurs.

---

## 🧯 Rival Explanations

Possible alternatives include:

- ordinary incompatibility;
- illness;
- disability;
- poverty;
- geography;
- family opposition;
- genuine safeguarding;
- lawful regulation;
- relationship instability;
- supporter burnout;
- institutional scarcity;
- coincidence.

> A cost model is useful only if it preserves the possibility that the relationship was simply hard, unsafe, or no longer chosen.

Mixed explanations are likely.

---

## 🔍 Comparative Questions

A careful comparison may ask:

- What did the relationship make possible?
- When did costs begin rising?
- Which costs affected the target?
- Which affected the supporter?
- Were casual or symbolic forms treated differently?
- Was a lower-capacity substitute offered?
- Did cost rise near public support, funding, legal action, relocation, or institutional access?
- Did withdrawal reduce pressure?
- Did similar relationships without the same consequence face equal cost?
- What ordinary explanation remains?
- What would disconfirm deliberate pricing?

---

## 🛠️ Protective Practice

Possible protective measures include:

- distribute support across several people;
- fund legal and travel costs;
- provide professional backing to supporters;
- create respite and role clarity;
- preserve ordinary social time;
- avoid compulsory intermediaries;
- consolidate administrative processes;
- protect lawful association;
- recognise chosen family;
- support accessibility;
- correct reputational records;
- measure cumulative burden;
- provide material rather than symbolic support;
- ensure no relationship becomes the entire rescue infrastructure.

> A resilient relationship should not depend on two people privately absorbing every cost created by public institutions.

---

## ⛔ What Not To Do

Do not:

- pressure someone to remain in a relationship they have chosen to leave;
- treat persistence as a moral duty;
- dismiss legitimate boundaries;
- force supporters to accept legal, financial, or safety risks;
- shame people for burnout;
- treat dependency as romantic proof;
- assume anyone who leaves was pressured;
- make one person the whole support system;
- use cost analysis to surveil intimate life;
- reduce relationships to transactions.

> The aim is to make safe connection possible, not to make departure illegitimate.

Consent remains controlling. A supporter’s limits remain real. The person’s right to end contact remains real.

---

## 🧱 When Relationship Pricing Becomes Social Control

Warning signs include:

- costs rising near consequential thresholds;
- supporters acquiring professional, legal, family, or financial risk;
- emotional support being tolerated while material help attracts pressure;
- low-capacity substitutes being repeatedly offered;
- the person becoming classified as too difficult or expensive;
- alternatives being removed and dependency later criticised;
- withdrawal reducing pressure;
- no formal ban existing while continuation becomes practically unsustainable;
- the same pattern recurring across relationships.

> Making consequential relationships too expensive becomes social control when the burdens of connection are deliberately imposed, predictably allowed to accumulate, or selectively concentrated until lawful association remains formally possible but materially irrational.

---

## 🏛️ What Responsible Institutions Require

Responsible systems should:

- assess cumulative burden;
- protect lawful association;
- fund accessibility and advocacy;
- provide safe direct-access routes;
- support supporters without turning them into unpaid case managers;
- record and justify warnings;
- avoid serial referral;
- distinguish conduct from association;
- review professional-boundary claims;
- prevent retaliatory complaints;
- provide correction propagation;
- restore opportunities lost through error;
- recognise chosen relationships;
- avoid treating support need as evidence of unsafe dependency;
- identify who is absorbing institutional cost.

> A system should not call a relationship unsustainable after making the people inside it privately carry every public failure surrounding them.

---

## 🪜 Small-Step Audit

A reader examining one high-cost relationship can ask:

- What capacity did the relationship create?
- When did costs begin?
- What kinds of cost appeared?
- Who imposed or transmitted them?
- Who paid them?
- Did costs rise near a consequential threshold?
- Was emotional or symbolic contact treated differently from practical support?
- Was a lower-capacity substitute offered?
- Did pressure reduce after withdrawal?
- Did the relationship become dependent because alternatives disappeared?
- Were supporters given protection or backing?
- What ordinary explanation remains?
- What is the smallest supportable claim?

---

## ⚖️ Evidentiary Guardrail

Use a clear ladder:

1. ordinary relationship cost;
2. structural hardship;
3. unequal burden;
4. institutional friction;
5. repeated threshold-linked escalation;
6. selectively imposed cost;
7. deliberate relationship containment.

Do not treat intensity as proof of manipulation, expense as proof of interference, a supporter’s withdrawal as false, dependency as inherently abusive, professional caution as automatically political, persistence as proof of wrongdoing, or ordinary hardship as evidence of one controlling system.

The claim should remain tied to identifiable costs, routes, timing, and effects.

---

## 📖 Compact Definition

**Making Consequential Relationships Too Expensive** describes the accumulation or selective imposition of emotional, reputational, professional, legal, financial, administrative, familial, physical, and political costs around relationships capable of producing protection, corroboration, organisation, mobility, institutional access, or material change.

It may operate through warnings, referrals, travel barriers, family pressure, legal exposure, administrative burden, supporter risk, withdrawal of alternatives, low-capacity substitutes, exhaustion, and attrition.

The central danger is that lawful connection can remain formally available while the cost of allowing it to become consequential is raised beyond what the people involved can sustain.

## 🌒 Cluster Conclusion

*🦷 The Plant Has Teeth* began with the relationship that never received its introduction.

Then:

- the person became harder to find;
- a warning arrived before them;
- mistrust entered the relationship;
- family absorbed pressure;
- movement became uncertain;
- process consumed capacity;
- practical support disappeared;
- persistence became too expensive.

> The plant has teeth because classification can become consequence without ever becoming an order.

No prohibition was required.

The people remained free to care, believe, remain in contact, and try again.

The system merely arranged for leaving to be the first affordable decision.

> The database did not have to say whom the person was forbidden to love, trust, employ, fund, shelter, or defend. It only had to ensure that everyone who tried received a bill.
