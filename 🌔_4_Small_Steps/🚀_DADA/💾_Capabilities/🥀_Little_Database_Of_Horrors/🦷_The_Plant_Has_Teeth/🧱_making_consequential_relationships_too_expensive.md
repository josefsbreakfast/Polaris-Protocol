🧱 making_consequential_relationships_too_expensive.md — Node Plan

Core purpose

This node should close 🦷 The Plant Has Teeth by identifying the shared logic beneath every preceding mechanism.

The system does not need to eliminate every relationship.

It does not need to ensure total isolation.

It does not need to issue a formal prohibition.

It may succeed by making certain relationships expensive enough that:

* they never begin;
* they remain shallow;
* they cannot survive pressure;
* supporters withdraw;
* institutions avoid involvement;
* the people inside them lose the capacity to persist.

The governing question is:

What happens when the price of maintaining a consequential relationship rises faster than the relationship can accumulate trust, protection, and shared capacity?

This node should gather:

* suppressed opportunities;
* reduced visibility;
* reputational risk;
* relational wedges;
* family pressure;
* movement restrictions;
* legal and financial burden;
* functional isolation.

Its central proposition should be:

The system does not need to stop the relationship. It only needs to make persistence irrational for everyone except the people least able to afford the cost.

⸻

Governing thesis

Not all relationships are equally consequential.

A relationship may matter because it creates:

* evidence;
* protection;
* money;
* housing;
* political access;
* legal action;
* publication;
* relocation;
* professional legitimacy;
* durable solidarity;
* a credible route out.

These relationships may attract higher costs.

The costs may fall on:

* the target;
* the supporter;
* both people;
* their families;
* their employers;
* their institutions;
* the wider network.

Core lines

A relationship cap is enforced when the price of becoming consequential rises faster than the relationship can accumulate protection.

The system does not need to ban the relationship if it can make every ordinary act of maintaining it require exceptional courage, money, time, or risk.

The relationship may remain emotionally real while becoming materially impossible.

Containment works when connection remains available in forms too weak to alter the person’s conditions.

⸻

Opening movement — the price appears gradually

Open with accumulation.

At first, the relationship requires:

* one delayed meeting;
* one difficult explanation;
* one missed train;
* one awkward warning.

Then:

* legal advice;
* reputational defence;
* money;
* travel;
* secrecy;
* repeated reassurance;
* professional risk;
* family conflict;
* administrative labour.

Neither person may decide that the relationship does not matter.

They may decide they cannot keep paying what it now costs.

Possible opening

Nobody prohibited the relationship.

It simply began charging for every ordinary thing: a meeting, a phone call, a public word of support, a journey, an introduction, a place to stay, one more attempt to repair the misunderstanding.

Then establish:

A relationship can be destroyed economically, socially, and administratively without either person ceasing to care.

Section 1 — What makes a relationship consequential

Define consequential relationships as those capable of changing material conditions.

They may provide:

* corroboration;
* practical care;
* institutional access;
* financial support;
* strategic coordination;
* political legitimacy;
* emotional stability;
* legal protection;
* cross-community connection;
* long-term planning.

A relationship does not need to be:

* romantic;
* public;
* famous;
* politically declared.

Core line

Consequence lies not in the label attached to the relationship, but in what the people become able to do together.

This should preserve a broad understanding of relationship.

Section 2 — Why cost is a useful control mechanism

Direct prohibition attracts:

* scrutiny;
* resistance;
* records;
* litigation;
* political accountability.

Cost-shifting is quieter.

It can make withdrawal appear:

* voluntary;
* prudent;
* professional;
* financially necessary;
* protective;
* emotionally sensible.

Core line

Cost converts coercion into a sequence of apparently personal decisions.

The system may never need to say:

Leave.

It may only need to ensure that staying requires more than either person can safely provide.

Section 9 — The relationship cap

A relationship cap describes the level of consequence beyond which costs begin rising.

Possible pattern:

1. casual contact is easy;
2. emotional closeness is tolerated;
3. practical assistance attracts caution;
4. public support attracts warning;
5. institutional action attracts formal pressure;
6. durable coalition attracts multiple forms of interference.

Core line

The cap does not limit how much the people may care. It limits how much their care is allowed to change.

This is a strong Polaris line.

The node should present this as a hypothesis to test, not a universal rule.

Section 24 — The cost escalation pattern

Core line

Cost escalation works best when each new burden can be explained as a response to the burden before it.

The system becomes self-justifying.

Section 35 — The evidence problem

High-cost relationships can arise through:

* illness;
* disability;
* poverty;
* distance;
* family conflict;
* legal complexity;
* ordinary institutional failure;
* intense emotional dynamics;
* social prejudice.

Evidence of structured cost escalation may include:

* burdens appearing near consequential thresholds;
* supporters receiving linked warnings or processes;
* low-capacity alternatives being consistently offered;
* costs falling disproportionately on specific relationships;
* withdrawal following identifiable external pressure;
* the same mechanisms recurring;
* costs reducing after the relationship loses practical consequence;
* records showing intent to create distance or risk.

Essential line

A difficult or expensive relationship is not proof of engineered interference. The relevant evidence lies in which costs appeared, who imposed them, when they escalated, what capacity they removed, and whether the same threshold recurs.

Section 39 — Protective practice

Possible measures include:

* distribute support across several people;
* provide professional backing to supporters;
* fund legal and travel costs;
* protect direct communication;
* preserve ordinary social time;
* avoid compulsory intermediaries;
* create respite and role clarity;
* recognise chosen family;
* separate actual safeguarding concerns from political discomfort;
* provide accessible routes;
* assess cumulative cost;
* maintain correction and review.

Core line

A resilient relationship should not depend on two people privately absorbing every cost created by public institutions.

⸻

Section 40 — What not to do

Do not:

* pressure someone to remain in a relationship they have chosen to leave;
* treat persistence as a moral duty;
* dismiss legitimate boundaries;
* assume all cost was externally imposed;
* force supporters to accept legal, financial, or safety risks;
* make one relationship carry an entire support system;
* treat dependency as romantic proof;
* shame people for burnout;
* reinterpret explicit withdrawal as false without evidence;
* use cost analysis to surveil intimate life.

Essential line

The aim is to make safe connection possible, not to make departure illegitimate.

⸻

Section 41 — When cost becomes social control

Warning signs include:

* costs rise near consequential thresholds;
* supporters acquire professional, legal, family, or financial risk;
* emotional support is tolerated while material help triggers pressure;
* low-capacity substitutes are repeatedly offered;
* the person becomes classified as too difficult or expensive;
* alternatives are removed and dependency is later criticised;
* withdrawal reduces pressure;
* no formal ban exists, but continuation becomes practically unsustainable;
* the same pattern recurs across relationships.

Anchor line

Making consequential relationships too expensive becomes social control when the burdens of connection are deliberately imposed, predictably allowed to accumulate, or selectively concentrated until lawful association remains formally possible but materially irrational.

Section 42 — What responsible institutions require

Responsible systems should:

* assess cumulative burden;
* protect lawful association;
* fund accessibility and advocacy;
* provide safe direct-access routes;
* support supporters without converting them into unpaid case managers;
* record and justify warnings;
* avoid serial referral;
* distinguish conduct from association;
* review professional-boundary claims;
* prevent retaliatory complaints;
* provide correction propagation;
* restore opportunities lost through error;
* recognise the material value of chosen relationships;
* avoid treating support needs as evidence of unsafe dependency;
* identify who is absorbing institutional cost.

Core line

A system should not call a relationship unsustainable after making the people inside it privately carry every public failure surrounding them.

Section 43 — Small-step audit

A reader examining one high-cost relationship can ask:

* What capacity did the relationship create?
* When did costs begin?
* What kinds of cost appeared?
* Who imposed or transmitted them?
* Who paid them?
* Did the costs rise near a consequential threshold?
* Was emotional or symbolic contact treated differently from practical support?
* Was a lower-capacity substitute offered?
* Did pressure reduce after withdrawal?
* Did the relationship become dependent because alternatives disappeared?
* Were supporters given protection or backing?
* What ordinary explanation remains?
* What is the smallest supportable claim?

Possible smallest claims include:

* one supporter withdrew after a documented professional warning;
* one relationship became unaffordable because of repeated travel or legal burdens;
* one practical support route triggered scrutiny absent from symbolic contact;
* one institution shifted its responsibility onto private care;
* one low-capacity substitute replaced a materially effective relationship;
* one dependency was created by removing alternatives and then used to justify restriction.

Evidentiary guardrail

Use a clear ladder:

1. ordinary relationship cost;
2. structural hardship;
3. unequal burden;
4. institutional friction;
5. repeated threshold-linked escalation;
6. selectively imposed cost;
7. deliberate relationship containment.

Do not treat:

* intensity as proof of manipulation;
* expense as proof of interference;
* a supporter’s withdrawal as false;
* dependency as inherently abusive;
* professional caution as automatically political;
* persistence as proof of wrongdoing;
* ordinary hardship as evidence of one controlling system.

The claim should remain tied to identifiable costs, routes, timing, and effects.

⸻

Compact definition

Making Consequential Relationships Too Expensive describes the accumulation or selective imposition of emotional, reputational, professional, legal, financial, administrative, familial, physical, and political costs around relationships capable of producing protection, corroboration, organisation, mobility, institutional access, or material change.

It may operate through:

* warnings;
* referrals;
* travel barriers;
* family pressure;
* legal exposure;
* administrative burden;
* supporter risk;
* withdrawal of alternatives;
* low-capacity substitutes;
* exhaustion and attrition.

The central danger is that lawful connection can remain formally available while the cost of allowing it to become consequential is raised beyond what the people involved can sustain.
