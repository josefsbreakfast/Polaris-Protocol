# 🔭 Capability, Not Allegation
**First created:** 2026-07-19 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-19  
*How to discuss foreseeable digital-authoritarian capability without turning possibility into accusation.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

A capability can be dangerous before its use is fully proven.

It can also be discussed irresponsibly if possibility is presented as fact.

Both mistakes matter.

One mistake is to say:

> This system could exist, therefore this actor must already be using it.

The other is to say:

> No complete public proof case has been exposed, therefore there is nothing worth examining.

Neither position is good enough.

**Capability is not allegation.**

The purpose of capability analysis is to identify what existing tools, institutions, incentives, and data flows could foreseeably enable.

The purpose of allegation is different.

An allegation attributes specific conduct to a specific actor.

Those are not the same claim, and they should not be made with the same evidence.

The Little Database of Horrors cluster depends on preserving that distinction.

We do not need to pretend the plant is fully grown.

We do need to notice that the shop is stocking fertiliser.

---

## 🔭 Why Capability Analysis Matters

Governments routinely assess dangerous capability before catastrophic use has been publicly demonstrated.

They do this in:

- defence;
- cyber security;
- weapons proliferation;
- biosecurity;
- public safety;
- data protection;
- critical infrastructure;
- hostile-state activity.

Safeguards are often designed before the worst-case event occurs.

That is the point of prevention.

The same logic should apply to:

- social-graph analysis;
- diaspora mapping;
- behavioural scoring;
- relationship inference;
- influence ranking;
- platform visibility systems;
- transnational repression.

The absence of a fully exposed case is not evidence that the capability is harmless.

But foreseeability does not establish that a particular actor has used it.

A system may be:

- technically possible;
- commercially available;
- politically attractive;
- institutionally accessible;

without being deployed in the way we fear.

Capability analysis identifies the risk.

Evidence establishes the conduct.

Attribution identifies the actor.

## 🪜 The Evidence Ladder

The cluster needs a stable vocabulary for moving from possibility to proof.

### Capability

A tool, dataset, institution, or process can perform a relevant function.

Examples include the ability to:

- construct social graphs;
- identify bridge figures;
- rank influence;
- track communications;
- infer relationships;
- share consular records;
- alter visibility;
- prioritise targets.

Capability answers:

> Could this be done?

Capability does not establish that the function was used.

---

### Access

An actor can obtain or control the relevant:

- data;
- software;
- institution;
- intermediary;
- decision pathway;
- operational authority.

Access answers:

> Could this actor use the capability?

Access may be demonstrated through:

- procurement;
- ownership;
- contracts;
- legal authority;
- partnership;
- administrative control;
- technical access.

Access does not prove use against a particular person or group.

---

### Incentive

An actor has a political, security, commercial, or institutional reason to seek the outcome.

Possible incentives include:

- suppressing dissent;
- protecting regime reputation;
- preventing coalition-building;
- controlling exile politics;
- identifying whistleblowers;
- limiting access to media or law;
- avoiding institutional embarrassment;
- preserving gatekeepers.

Incentive answers:

> Why might they want to do it?

Motive does not prove conduct.

An actor can want an outcome without causing every event that resembles it.

---

### Opportunity

The circumstances allow the capability to be used.

Opportunity may arise through:

- crisis;
- emergency law;
- procurement;
- weak oversight;
- diplomatic access;
- platform cooperation;
- community partnerships;
- data-sharing agreements;
- institutional dependency.

Opportunity answers:

> When and where could it occur?

Opportunity strengthens plausibility.

It does not establish implementation.

---

### Documented Conduct

Reliable evidence shows that a relevant action occurred.

Examples may include:

- surveillance;
- family coercion;
- data sharing;
- account interference;
- contact mapping;
- institutional obstruction;
- platform suppression;
- financial or travel pressure.

Documented conduct answers:

> What happened?

The evidence may establish the act without establishing the full system behind it.

---

### Pattern

Similar conduct recurs:

- across time;
- across people;
- across institutions;
- at the same functional threshold;
- through the same intermediary;
- using the same language;
- with the same data errors.

Pattern answers:

> Is this isolated or repeated?

A pattern can strengthen an inference.

It does not automatically establish coordination.

---

### Inference

Observed facts support a possible explanation, but alternatives remain.

Inference answers:

> What mechanism best fits the evidence so far?

A responsible inference should identify:

- the facts being explained;
- the proposed mechanism;
- the rival explanations;
- the confidence level;
- the evidence that would strengthen or weaken it.

---

### Attribution

Evidence connects the conduct to:

- a named person;
- institution;
- contractor;
- state;
- network;
- policy;
- operational unit.

Attribution answers:

> Who was responsible?

Attribution may require evidence of:

- command;
- control;
- payment;
- communications;
- shared infrastructure;
- instruction;
- legal responsibility.

---

### Allegation

A specific claim is made about specific conduct by a specific actor.

A responsible allegation should be:

- precise;
- sourced;
- proportionate;
- clear about uncertainty;
- limited to what the evidence can carry.

The broader and more serious the claim, the stronger the evidence required.

---

### Proof

The relevant evidentiary threshold has been met.

That threshold differs between:

- journalism;
- civil litigation;
- criminal law;
- academic research;
- intelligence assessment;
- policy risk;
- public-interest investigation.

Proof answers:

> What can now responsibly be stated as established?

One evidentiary standard cannot simply be imported into every context.

---

## 📊 A Compact Matrix

| Evidence | What It Supports | What It Does Not Support |
|---|---|---|
| A tool exists | capability | proof of use |
| A state purchased the tool | access | proof of target or purpose |
| A state represses its diaspora | incentive and precedent | proof of this mechanism |
| Similar incidents recur | pattern | attribution by itself |
| The same data error appears across bodies | possible shared source | proof of central control |
| An internal document names the practice | strong conduct evidence | full scale or legality |
| A direct operational record exists | conduct and possible attribution | every broader inference |

The matrix exists to stop conceptual slippage.

A tool becomes a practice only when there is evidence of use.

A practice becomes a system only when there is evidence of integration, governance, or repeated operation.

A system becomes attributable only when responsibility can be tied to an actor.

## 🧩 Capability Convergence

The Little Database concern rests on convergence.

Relevant components may include:

- spyware;
- identity records;
- contact mapping;
- recommendation systems;
- political-risk scoring;
- embassy networks;
- family coercion;
- financial controls;
- travel controls;
- community-intelligence systems.

No single component proves relationship management.

A pile of capabilities is not yet a system.

Integration, governance, and use are what turn the pile into one.

The warning becomes stronger where:

- the components are interoperable;
- one actor has access to several of them;
- procurement links them;
- policy documents describe shared objectives;
- the same data are reused across purposes;
- operational records show joint use;
- people experience repeated outcomes tied to the same infrastructure.

The task is therefore to ask:

- What is connected?
- Who controls the connection?
- What decisions flow from it?
- What happens when the output is wrong?

## 📐 Possibility, Plausibility, Probability, Establishment

These words should not be used interchangeably.

### Possible

The mechanism is technically or administratively feasible.

### Plausible

The mechanism is consistent with known capabilities, incentives, and circumstances.

### Probable

The mechanism fits the available evidence better than competing explanations.

### Established

The evidence is strong enough to state the claim as fact within the relevant standard.

The cluster may responsibly discuss:

- possible systems;
- plausible escalation pathways;
- documented components;
- known precedents;
- credible indicators.

It must not automatically claim:

- probability;
- attribution;
- proven deployment;
- central coordination.

Calibrated language is not weakness.

It is how the argument remains usable.

## 🎯 Motive Is Not Enough

Authoritarian regimes may have strong motives to:

- identify dissidents;
- suppress coalitions;
- pressure families;
- control community representation;
- prevent international scrutiny;
- manage reputation;
- limit access to law and media.

Those motives matter.

They help explain why a capability might be attractive.

They do not establish:

- which tool was used;
- whether a specific incident was directed;
- whether an intermediary acted knowingly;
- whether one system explains the outcome;
- whether the event was political at all.

An actor can want an outcome without causing every event that resembles it.

Motive strengthens a case only when connected to conduct.

---

## 🛠️ Technical Possibility Is Not Enough

A system may be capable of:

- inferring relationships;
- suppressing visibility;
- ranking influence;
- predicting coalition risk;
- identifying bridge figures.

That does not prove:

- the relevant feature was enabled;
- the data were available;
- the operator used it;
- the output affected a decision;
- the target was included;
- the result was acted upon.

Useful questions include:

- Was the tool procured?
- Was it deployed in this jurisdiction?
- What data did it ingest?
- What outputs did it generate?
- Who saw those outputs?
- What decision followed?
- Can the decision be tied to the output?

Technical feasibility is the beginning of the inquiry.

It is not the conclusion.

---

## 🔁 Pattern Is Not Automatically Coordination

Repeated outcomes may arise from:

- shared prejudice;
- common platform incentives;
- standard professional risk aversion;
- copied policy;
- community norms;
- shared data;
- imitation;
- direct coordination;
- central direction.

These are different mechanisms.

Similar outcomes do not prove one hand on the controls.

Investigators should distinguish:

### Common Cause

Several actors respond independently to the same event or incentive.

### Shared Source

Several actors rely on the same data, warning, or classification.

### Imitation

One institution copies another.

### Formal Coordination

Actors exchange information or plan together.

### Central Direction

One actor instructs the others.

The evidence needed for each claim is different.

---

## 🎭 Attribution Through Layers

Attribution becomes difficult where conduct passes through:

- embassies;
- contractors;
- community organisations;
- private individuals;
- platforms;
- universities;
- host-state institutions;
- allied governments.

The inquiry should separate:

- who supplied the information;
- who interpreted it;
- who ordered action;
- who carried it out;
- who benefited;
- who could stop it.

Plausible deniability may be structural rather than improvised.

A distributed system may be designed so that:

- the state does not issue the final instruction;
- the contractor does not know the political context;
- the community intermediary does not know the original source;
- the host institution acts on a risk warning without seeing the evidence.

Lack of easy attribution must not become automatic state attribution.

It must also not become automatic dismissal.

---

## ⚠️ The Risk Of Weak Accusation

Weak accusations can:

- harm innocent people;
- fracture communities;
- destroy evidence;
- alert genuine perpetrators;
- expose relatives;
- create legal risk;
- undermine credible claims;
- make future witnesses less willing to speak.

This cluster must explicitly reject:

- public naming from pattern alone;
- loyalty tests;
- amateur agent-hunting;
- social-media pile-ons;
- treating difficult behaviour as proof of state alignment;
- cutting people off solely because they fit a theory.

A theory designed to explain wedges must not become a machine for creating them.

The model should improve investigation.

It should not become a new form of suspicion management.

---

## 🧊 The Risk Of Excessive Skepticism

The opposite error also matters.

Institutions may demand forms of proof that affected people cannot realistically obtain.

They may:

- treat secrecy as evidence that nothing occurred;
- dismiss repeated reports because each case is incomplete;
- require documentary proof held by the alleged perpetrator;
- refuse precaution until after severe harm;
- use uncertainty to erase what is already known.

Early warning often relies on:

- partial evidence;
- converging testimony;
- technical capability;
- precedent;
- repeated anomalies;
- credible indicators.

The correct response may be to:

- investigate;
- preserve evidence;
- protect people;
- audit a system;
- suspend data sharing;
- impose safeguards;

without declaring final attribution.

Uncertainty is not innocence.

It is also not proof.

## 🗣️ Calibrated Language

Wording should match confidence.

### Low Confidence

Use:

- could;
- might;
- is technically possible;
- is consistent with;
- raises the question;
- cannot be ruled out.

### Moderate Confidence

Use:

- appears;
- suggests;
- is more consistent with;
- has been reported across several cases;
- there are credible indicators;
- the pattern warrants investigation.

### High Confidence

Use:

- documents show;
- the system was procured;
- the court found;
- the agency confirmed;
- multiple independent sources establish;
- records demonstrate.

Confidence language should not be decorative.

It should reflect the evidence.

---

## 🧾 A Claim Template

Every major allegation or inference should be capable of being broken into:

1. **Claim**
2. **Observed facts**
3. **Sources**
4. **Capability required**
5. **Actor access**
6. **Possible incentive**
7. **Alternative explanations**
8. **Confidence**
9. **What would strengthen the claim**
10. **What would weaken the claim**

This structure prevents dramatic conclusions from floating free of the evidence beneath them.

It also allows claims to be revised without discarding the entire model.

---

## ❌ Negative Evidence

A serious model must be allowed to lose.

Absence of expected traces can matter.

Examples include:

- no procurement route;
- no shared actor;
- no relevant data access;
- no timing relationship;
- no recurrence;
- high-consequence relationships proceeding normally;
- interventions varying in ways inconsistent with a shared rule;
- ordinary explanations fitting better.

Negative evidence should reduce confidence.

A model that explains every possible outcome explains nothing.

---

## 🔍 What Strengthens A Capability Claim

Useful evidence includes:

- procurement records;
- product documentation;
- budget lines;
- training material;
- technical testing;
- data-sharing agreements;
- official speeches;
- internal policy;
- contractor marketing;
- patents;
- research programmes;
- admissions;
- litigation disclosure.

These sources may establish what a system can do and who can access it.

They do not necessarily establish use against a particular person.

---

## 📚 What Strengthens A Conduct Claim

Useful evidence includes:

- direct records;
- witness testimony with independent corroboration;
- repeatable technical evidence;
- shared data errors;
- access logs;
- internal messages;
- decision records;
- timing tied to private information;
- consistent cases across independent targets;
- official findings;
- judicial findings.

Conduct claims become stronger when the evidence connects:

- the act;
- the system;
- the decision;
- the consequence.

---

## 🧭 What Strengthens Attribution

Useful evidence includes:

- command records;
- payment trails;
- contracts;
- communications;
- common infrastructure;
- named operational personnel;
- legal findings;
- admissions;
- evidence that rules out independent action.

Attribution should specify the level claimed.

There is a difference between:

- a contractor acting independently;
- an embassy supplying information;
- a ministry directing an operation;
- a state being legally responsible.

---

## 🛑 Precaution Without Overclaiming

Institutions may act cautiously before final proof.

Proportionate precautions may include:

- suspending data sharing;
- conducting an independent audit;
- separating welfare and security systems;
- preserving logs;
- notifying affected people;
- restricting embassy access;
- pausing automated scoring;
- protecting relatives;
- establishing independent review;
- narrowing data retention.

Precaution should be:

- proportionate;
- reversible where possible;
- transparent;
- rights-protective;
- independently reviewed.

You do not need a criminal conviction to stop feeding a system that is showing dangerous teeth.

Protective action does not require public accusation.

---

## ✍️ Writing Rules For The Wider Cluster

Use precise verbs:

- documented;
- reported;
- alleged;
- inferred;
- could enable;
- was used;
- appears consistent with;
- remains unproven.

Name:

- date;
- jurisdiction;
- actor;
- source;
- confidence.

Avoid:

- obviously;
- clearly, where the evidence is not clear;
- everyone knows;
- must have;
- totalising language;
- regional generalisation.

Distinguish:

- one case;
- repeated practice;
- state policy;
- regional pattern;
- global capability.

The claims register should make those distinctions visible.

## 🫂 Why This Protects Survivors And Dissidents

Evidentiary discipline should not be framed as disbelief.

It can protect survivors and dissidents by:

- making claims harder to dismiss;
- separating uncertainty from institutional denial;
- supporting legal and journalistic use;
- preventing others from hijacking testimony;
- protecting people from being framed as irrational;
- preserving what is known even where the full mechanism remains hidden.

Precision is not a demand that survivors know everything.

It is a way of preventing institutions from using the unknown parts to erase what is known.

Affected people may know:

- what happened to them;
- what changed;
- who contacted them;
- what records appeared;
- what consequences followed;

without knowing the entire architecture.

That partial knowledge still matters.

---

## ⚖️ The Core Rule

This node holds two obligations together.

Do not turn possibility into fact.

Do not turn uncertainty into permission to ignore foreseeable harm.

The correct posture is:

- careful;
- curious;
- proportionate;
- testable;
- protective.

Capability analysis allows early warning without reckless attribution.

That is its value.

---

## 🧿 Compact Definition

**Capability, Not Allegation** is the evidentiary rule that separates:

- what a system could do;
- what an actor could access;
- what an actor might want;
- what conduct is documented;
- what pattern is visible;
- what can be attributed;
- what can be proved.

It allows the Little Database of Horrors to be discussed as a foreseeable governance problem without presenting every hypothesis as an established operation.
