# 🥀 What Is The Little Database Of Horrors?
**First created:** 2026-07-19 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-19  
*A capability warning about systems that move from recording relationships to scoring, shaping, and constraining them.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The **Little Database of Horrors** is a name for a possible system assembled from technologies and administrative practices that already exist in separate forms.

It does not require one secret supercomputer, one all-knowing ministry, or one immaculate master plan.

It may begin with several ordinary systems:

- an identity database;
- a consular register;
- a social-media monitoring tool;
- a list of political organisations;
- a platform recommendation system;
- a communications or location dataset;
- a safeguarding programme;
- a community-engagement map;
- a contractor selling influence analysis;
- an embassy keeping track of people it considers politically important.

Each component may have a stated purpose. Some purposes may be legitimate. Some data may be lawfully collected. Some officials may sincerely believe they are protecting people.

The danger begins when these systems are connected and asked increasingly intimate political questions.

Not only:

> Who is this person?

Not only:

> What are they doing?

But:

> Who do they know?

> Who do they trust?

> What might those people be able to do together?

> Which relationships make this person harder to isolate, discredit, frighten, detain, return, or ignore?

At that point, the system is no longer simply observing a population.

It is beginning to model the social conditions under which people can exercise agency.

That is the Little Database of Horrors.

---

## 🪴 The Little Database

The database is **little** at the beginning because frightening systems rarely arrive under frightening names.

It may be introduced as a modest attempt to:

- improve diaspora engagement;
- coordinate safeguarding;
- identify vulnerable citizens abroad;
- understand community concerns;
- detect hostile-state interference;
- prevent political violence;
- map extremist influence;
- support consular welfare;
- allocate limited resources;
- identify trusted community representatives.

The first version may contain only basic records.

Names.

Addresses.

Family relationships.

Organisations.

Travel.

Public statements.

Known contacts.

The project may appear narrow, useful, and administratively tidy.

It may even solve real problems.

Then someone asks whether two databases can be linked.

Then whether the system can identify influential people.

Then whether it can distinguish ordinary contacts from politically consequential ones.

Then whether it can predict which relationships are likely to produce organisation, evidence, embarrassment, litigation, protest, or escape.

The little database grows.

It does not need to become omniscient.

It only needs to become useful enough that institutions begin relying on it, and opaque enough that the people being classified cannot see or challenge what it says.

## 🩸 What The Database Eats

A relationship-control system would not need a single extraordinary source of information.

It could be assembled from fragments.

Those fragments might include:

- names, dates of birth, addresses, and identity documents;
- family and household records;
- passport, visa, border, and travel information;
- phone and messaging metadata;
- contact lists;
- social-media connections;
- geolocation and event attendance;
- education and employment history;
- professional and religious affiliations;
- protest participation;
- donations and financial transfers;
- immigration and consular records;
- interactions with lawyers, journalists, unions, universities, charities, or political parties;
- reports from community intermediaries;
- commercial data purchased from brokers;
- risk assessments produced by contractors;
- platform engagement and recommendation data.

No individual record needs to say:

> This friendship is dangerous.

The relationship graph emerges when the records are joined.

A phone number appears in several devices.

Two people attend the same meetings.

One person sends money to another.

A journalist repeatedly contacts a witness.

A lawyer appears beside several dissidents.

A student moves between otherwise separate political groups.

A relative abroad remains in frequent contact with someone publicly critical of the state.

The system begins by noticing connections.

It then starts assigning meaning to them.

That is where the plant begins to feed.

## 🕸️ From People To Relationships

Traditional surveillance often treats the individual as the main unit of concern.

It asks:

- Is this person influential?
- Is this person politically active?
- Is this person connected to prohibited conduct?
- Is this person likely to organise?
- Is this person vulnerable to pressure?

The Little Database adds another unit:

> the relationship.

A relationship can matter even when neither person appears especially consequential alone.

A witness may lack public reach.

A journalist may lack access to the relevant evidence.

Together, they can create a story.

A claimant may lack legal expertise.

A lawyer may lack direct knowledge of the harm.

Together, they can create a case.

A local organiser may lack money.

A funder may lack trusted local relationships.

Together, they can create durable capacity.

A technically skilled researcher may lack testimony.

An affected person may lack the tools to analyse what happened.

Together, they can make an opaque system legible.

This is why relationship risk is not merely the sum of two personal risk scores.

A state interested in containing dissent may care less about whether two people are individually troublesome than whether their relationship creates:

- corroboration;
- protection;
- legitimacy;
- resources;
- publication;
- legal consequence;
- institutional access;
- cross-community solidarity;
- practical escape routes.

The governing question becomes:

> Who may safely know whom?

## 🎶 Suddenly, Surveillance

There is an important difference between recording relationships and acting on predictions about them.

The progression may look like this:

1. **Collection**  
   The system gathers information about people and contacts.

2. **Mapping**  
   It constructs social graphs showing families, organisations, intermediaries, and bridges.

3. **Interpretation**  
   It assigns meaning to particular connections.

4. **Scoring**  
   It estimates influence, disruption, dependency, credibility, and coalition potential.

5. **Filtering**  
   It identifies relationships considered safe, useful, irrelevant, or dangerous.

6. **Shaping**  
   It changes visibility, access, timing, opportunity, and reputation.

7. **Coercion**  
   It uses family, legal, financial, immigration, employment, or physical pressure.

The decisive shift occurs when the system stops asking:

> What relationships exist?

and begins asking:

> Which relationships should be allowed to become effective?

No instruction needs to say:

> End this friendship.

The same result may be pursued through smaller interventions:

- an introduction never happens;
- an invitation disappears;
- a warning is repeated;
- a person becomes harder to find;
- an opportunity is delayed;
- an institution quietly withdraws;
- an intermediary insists on remaining in the middle;
- a relative receives pressure;
- travel becomes risky;
- a collaboration becomes exhausting to maintain.

The system does not need perfect control.

It only needs to keep altering the probabilities.

## 🪞 Safe Contacts And Managed Substitutes

A relationship-management system would not necessarily try to leave people completely alone.

Complete isolation is visible. It can generate sympathy. It can also create operational problems.

A subtler system might tolerate or encourage contact that provides:

- companionship;
- cultural familiarity;
- emotional attention;
- prestige;
- distraction;
- a sense of belonging;
- somewhere to discharge fear or anger.

At the same time, it might obstruct relationships that provide:

- institutional leverage;
- independent judgement;
- legal protection;
- journalistic reach;
- political credibility;
- practical resources;
- cross-network access;
- durable solidarity;
- willingness to act.

This creates a difference between relationships that **absorb distress** and relationships that **change material conditions**.

A person may appear socially surrounded while remaining practically unsupported.

They may be offered people who resemble the kind of person they need on the surface, but lack the qualities that would make the relationship consequential.

That possibility must be handled carefully.

It is a plausible system design, not proof that every repeated social pattern was engineered.

The point is not to reinterpret every disappointing friendship, professional contact, romance, or community interaction as an operation.

The point is to recognise what an institution might be tempted to optimise if it began treating human association as a political-risk problem.

## 🧱 Relationship Caps

A relationship cap is a functional ceiling.

The relationship may proceed while it remains:

- casual;
- private;
- decorative;
- emotionally consuming;
- institutionally minor;
- politically unproductive.

Friction may increase when it becomes:

- corroborative;
- protective;
- financially useful;
- legally consequential;
- publicly credible;
- organisationally durable;
- capable of connecting separate communities;
- capable of creating an exit route.

The cap does not need to be formally written.

It may emerge through a combination of:

- platform visibility;
- gatekeeping;
- reputational warnings;
- administrative delay;
- community pressure;
- institutional risk aversion;
- foreign-state intimidation;
- ordinary prejudice reinforced by bad data.

This is one reason the model must be investigated at the smallest supportable level.

A repeated ceiling may come from one platform rule.

One institution.

One contractor.

One embassy.

One community faction.

One corrupted record.

Several overlapping systems may create the appearance of a single controller even where no single actor sees the whole.

## 🏛️ Why A Regime Might Want It

Authoritarian states have obvious reasons to care about diaspora relationships.

Diaspora communities may contain:

- political exiles;
- opposition media;
- former officials;
- students;
- academics;
- lawyers;
- campaigners;
- religious organisations;
- donors;
- witnesses;
- survivors;
- people with relatives still living under the regime.

These communities may be safer abroad, but they are not necessarily beyond reach.

A state may retain leverage through:

- family members;
- passports;
- consular services;
- property;
- remittances;
- business interests;
- travel;
- immigration status;
- religious or cultural institutions;
- aligned community organisations;
- host-state partnerships.

The state may wish to know:

- who connects dissidents to journalists;
- who can turn testimony into litigation;
- who funds opposition organisations;
- who provides practical protection;
- who brings separate communities together;
- who is becoming credible with host-country institutions;
- who might encourage defection;
- who might expose corruption or abuse.

Embassies, foreign ministries, contractors, aligned organisations, and informal intermediaries can also create layers of plausible deniability.

The person applying pressure may not present themselves as a state official.

The institution withdrawing access may not know the full origin of the warning.

The contractor may describe its work as risk analysis.

The community intermediary may believe they are reducing conflict.

Responsibility can be distributed until nobody appears to have made the decision.

The outcome can still be coercive.

---

## 🌍 Why Diasporas Are Especially Exposed

Diaspora communities often combine host-country freedom with origin-country vulnerability.

Members may be able to speak, organise, publish, litigate, or protest in the country where they live.

At the same time, they may remain connected to:

- relatives living under the origin state;
- property or inheritance;
- passports and travel documents;
- religious authorities;
- professional networks;
- business interests;
- cultural organisations;
- communities with strong internal gatekeepers.

From an authoritarian perspective, this creates both a threat and an opportunity.

The threat is that people abroad can say things that are dangerous at home.

The opportunity is that they may remain reachable through relationships.

The Little Database therefore does not treat diaspora life as ordinary human belonging.

It risks treating it as a network of dependencies to be measured and manipulated.

A diaspora is not a foreign state’s population-management problem merely because its members retain family, cultural, religious, or political connections elsewhere.

## 🧰 Why The Model Is Plausible Now

The Little Database does not depend on speculative technology.

Relevant capabilities already exist in adjacent forms:

- social-network analysis;
- identity and biometric databases;
- spyware;
- communications metadata;
- location tracking;
- behavioural and political risk scoring;
- platform recommendation systems;
- visibility ranking;
- data brokerage;
- sentiment and influence analysis;
- transnational repression;
- family coercion;
- embassy monitoring;
- financial and travel restrictions.

The warning lies in capability convergence.

A state does not need to invent every component.

It can purchase tools, reuse existing databases, work through contractors, cooperate with allies, or connect systems originally built for different purposes.

The important governance question is not only:

> Has one complete system already been publicly proven?

It is also:

> Are institutions assembling capabilities that make this abuse foreseeable, and are there safeguards strong enough to stop the progression?

Waiting for a perfect proof case may mean waiting until the infrastructure is entrenched and the affected community has already been reorganised around it.

## 🪴 Feed Me Data

The *Little Shop of Horrors* metaphor matters because it describes how a system can grow through failure.

The project begins small.

The model produces uncertainty.

Officials ask for more data.

The model produces errors.

Officials add more categories.

People behave unpredictably.

Officials assume concealment.

The community reacts to surveillance or pressure.

The system treats that reaction as proof that its original concerns were correct.

The plant’s appetite becomes self-validating.

> Feed me data.

More intimate records are demanded because the last batch did not make people predictable enough.

More intervention is authorised because the previous intervention did not produce compliance.

More relationships are classified because the existing graph still contains ambiguity.

But the model’s hunger is not a validation metric.

Human beings are not datasets with inconvenient gaps.

A system may be failing because the task itself is impossible: love, loyalty, courage, fear, trust, grief, politics, and solidarity cannot be reliably reduced to stable variables.

The database cannot solve this by eating the shop.

---

## 🌡️ What Happens Under Pressure

The system may look relatively benign under ordinary conditions.

Its scores may affect outreach, monitoring, invitations, grants, or routine risk assessments.

The most serious danger appears when political conditions change.

Pressure may come from:

- war;
- protest;
- sanctions;
- elections;
- succession crisis;
- international litigation;
- terrorism;
- economic collapse;
- leadership scandal;
- mass defection;
- fear of revolution.

Under pressure:

- categories widen;
- confidence thresholds fall;
- more people are included;
- relationships are interpreted more aggressively;
- uncertain data become operational facts;
- dissent becomes evidence of foreign alignment;
- requests for correction become signs of deception;
- resistance becomes confirmation.

The project may progress into systematic harm without one official ever saying:

> Let us build an instrument of persecution.

Instead:

1. the system is created for a limited purpose;
2. additional data are integrated;
3. relationships receive risk meanings;
4. categories expand during crisis;
5. weak predictions guide real decisions;
6. people resist;
7. resistance confirms the score;
8. safeguards are weakened;
9. coercion becomes routine;
10. nobody wants to admit the model was wrong.

That is how an apparently innocent project can become infrastructure for severe abuse.

## ⚠️ What This Node Is Not Claiming

This node does not claim that:

- one complete global relationship-management system has been proven;
- every state operates the same architecture;
- every embassy manages personal relationships;
- every repeated social pattern is engineered;
- every intermediary knowingly acts for a state;
- every failed relationship reflects political interference;
- software can perfectly predict human behaviour;
- ordinary community politics are irrelevant;
- technical possibility equals operational fact.

The model is a reason to investigate.

It is not permission to accuse.

A strange social pattern may arise from:

- homophily;
- class sorting;
- prejudice;
- platform recommendation;
- institutional gatekeeping;
- gossip;
- factional conflict;
- exhaustion;
- ordinary human failure;
- several systems interacting without central direction.

A serious investigation must preserve those alternatives.

The purpose of the model is to make emerging capabilities legible, not to turn every confusing relationship into evidence of conspiracy.

## 🔭 Capability, Conduct, And Proof

The cluster uses several distinct evidentiary categories:

### Capability

A tool, database, institution, or administrative mechanism can perform a relevant function.

### Documented conduct

There is reliable evidence that an actor used a capability in a particular way.

### Incentive

An actor has a political or institutional reason to seek a particular outcome.

### Inference

Observed facts reasonably suggest an explanation, but do not prove it.

### Hypothesis

A proposed mechanism is being tested against alternatives.

### Allegation

A specific actor is said to have committed specific conduct.

### Proof

The relevant evidentiary threshold has been met for the claim being made.

These categories must not be collapsed.

Capability is not conduct.

Motive is not proof.

Pattern is not attribution.

A vivid metaphor is not evidence.

The plant is allowed to be funny.

The claims still need footnotes.

## 🔬 The Smallest Claim First

The correct starting question is not:

> Is there one hidden architecture controlling everything?

It is:

> What is the smallest mechanism capable of producing this particular outcome?

That mechanism may be:

- one database;
- one policy;
- one shared watchlist;
- one contractor;
- one platform;
- one embassy route;
- one risk category;
- one institution repeating a bad record;
- one community intermediary;
- several overlapping systems.

Useful questions include:

- What happened?
- Who made the decision?
- What information did they possess?
- What system did they use?
- What rule or category was applied?
- Did the same error appear elsewhere?
- Was the timing significant?
- What ordinary explanation fits?
- What evidence would weaken the hypothesis?
- What evidence would establish shared data or coordination?

Proving one repeatable mechanism is more valuable than asserting a total architecture that cannot be tested.

The smallest claim first is not timidity.

It is how the plant is actually found.

## 🚀 Why This Belongs In DADA

The Little Database of Horrors belongs in **Defence Against Digital Authoritarianism** because it concerns digital systems used to constrain the social conditions of freedom.

It engages:

- privacy;
- family life;
- freedom of association;
- expression;
- political participation;
- movement;
- community autonomy;
- access to law and media;
- protection from discrimination;
- protection from transnational repression.

Digital authoritarianism does not operate only through censorship, arrest, or visible prohibition.

It can also operate through:

- ranking;
- friction;
- delay;
- visibility;
- uncertainty;
- administrative pressure;
- social exhaustion;
- relationship disruption.

The central question is therefore larger than:

> What does the state know?

It is:

> What social world is the state trying to make possible?

A system that decides which relationships may safely become effective is not merely a database.

It is an attempt to govern collective agency.

## 🧿 Compact Definition

The **Little Database of Horrors** is:

> A capability model in which personal data, social graphs, behavioural scoring, institutional intelligence, and coercive leverage are combined to assess—and potentially shape—the relationships through which people gain trust, protection, legitimacy, resources, and political agency.

Its danger lies not only in surveillance.

It lies in the progression from:

> seeing the network

to:

> scoring the network

to:

> pruning the network.
