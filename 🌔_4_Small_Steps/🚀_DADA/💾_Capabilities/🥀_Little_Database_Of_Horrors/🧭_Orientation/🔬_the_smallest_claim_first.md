# 🔬 The Smallest Claim First
**First created:** 2026-07-19 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-19  
*How to turn a large capability warning into a small, testable, useful investigation.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Several strange events occur.

They appear connected.

No institution explains them.

The same kind of friction appears in different places.

A relationship fails at the moment it becomes useful.

An invitation disappears.

A referral is blocked.

A warning travels farther than the evidence beneath it.

A false record appears in more than one system.

The whole environment begins to look managed.

At that point, the temptation is to prove the whole architecture at once.

Do not begin by trying to prove the whole plant.

Begin with the first visible root.

The governing question is:

> What is the least elaborate mechanism that could explain the evidence in front of us?

This is the smallest-claim method.

It does not deny the possibility of a larger system.

It creates a route by which a larger system can eventually be demonstrated.

---

## 🌿 Why Large Theories Are Seductive

Large theories can be useful.

They can:

- join otherwise disconnected experiences;
- reveal common incentives;
- explain repeated institutional failure;
- make hidden power imaginable;
- identify capability convergence;
- provide language for patterns that individual incidents cannot hold.

They can also become too large to investigate.

A large theory may:

- absorb every contradiction;
- become impossible to disprove;
- blur separate actors together;
- encourage weak attribution;
- make useful evidence look insignificant;
- overwhelm readers and investigators;
- become detached from the decisions that caused actual harm.

A theory can be broadly right about the danger and still be too large to investigate.

The answer is not to abandon the wider model.

The answer is to reduce it into claims that evidence can carry.

---

## 🔬 The Smallest Mechanism Rule

The smallest mechanism is:

> The least complex identifiable process capable of producing the observed outcome.

Possible mechanisms include:

- one bad database entry;
- one shared watchlist;
- one contractor;
- one embassy warning;
- one institutional policy;
- one platform rule;
- one gatekeeper;
- one referral route;
- one copied risk category;
- one informal professional network;
- one data-sharing agreement;
- one automated threshold;
- one decision-maker relying on one false record.

Several small mechanisms may later prove to be connected.

The investigation should not assume that connection before tracing it.

“Smallest” does not mean harmless.

One category can reproduce a great deal of harm.

One false record can travel through several institutions.

One gatekeeper can block an entire route.

One data-sharing agreement can expose a whole network.

---

## 📍 Start With The Event

Begin with one concrete event.

Record:

- what happened;
- when it happened;
- where it happened;
- who was directly involved;
- what changed;
- what evidence exists;
- what consequence followed.

Separate three layers.

### Observation

What can be directly shown?

> An invitation was withdrawn.

### Inference

What explanation is supported but not yet proven?

> Someone may have received a warning.

### Larger Hypothesis

What broader mechanism might explain the event?

> A relationship-management system may have blocked the introduction.

The event may be provable.

The immediate decision may be investigable.

The larger hypothesis may remain open.

Failure to prove the whole architecture should not erase the smaller facts.

---

## 🚪 Find The Decision Before The System

Ask where the outcome became fixed.

Who:

- removed the invitation;
- declined the referral;
- changed the risk category;
- suspended the account;
- issued the warning;
- denied travel;
- redirected contact;
- withdrew institutional support;
- prevented direct communication?

The decision point may be:

- human;
- automated;
- hybrid;
- uncertain.

Find the decision before trying to find the system.

A person may have acted on a score.

A score may have relied on a bad category.

A category may have come from a contractor.

A contractor may have received state information.

But the first investigable point remains the decision that produced the immediate consequence.

---

## 🧠 Identify The Information Available

Ask what the decision-maker knew or believed.

Possible information may include:

- direct observation;
- public activity;
- institutional notes;
- police information;
- security information;
- embassy contact;
- platform data;
- community gossip;
- contractor analysis;
- a false or stale record;
- information copied from another body.

Questions include:

- Where could the information have come from?
- Was it correct?
- Was it private?
- Was it current?
- Did it contain an unusual error?
- Did another institution repeat the same error?
- Was the information independently verified?
- Could the affected person correct it?

Shared errors may be more revealing than shared conclusions.

Two institutions may independently decide that someone is risky.

That does not prove coordination.

Two institutions repeating the same strange false detail suggests a shared source.

---

## 🏷️ Identify The Rule

Information becomes action through a rule, threshold, or category.

Possible labels include:

- safeguarding concern;
- reputational risk;
- hostile-state contact;
- extremism flag;
- conflict of interest;
- unsuitable partner;
- trusted-channel requirement;
- security concern;
- platform-integrity risk;
- vulnerability;
- instability;
- non-cooperation.

Ask whether the rule was:

- written;
- informal;
- automated;
- discretionary;
- copied from elsewhere;
- temporary;
- crisis-specific;
- capable of appeal.

The mechanism may be smaller than the motive.

One category can reproduce a great deal of harm.

A system does not need an explicit instruction saying:

> Prevent this relationship.

It may only need a rule saying:

> Route all contact through an approved intermediary.

---

## 🔀 Trace The Route

Trace how information travelled.

A possible route might be:

1. origin-state body;
2. embassy;
3. contractor;
4. host institution;
5. community intermediary;
6. final decision-maker.

Another route might be:

1. platform signal;
2. recommendation change;
3. reduced visibility;
4. missed introduction.

Ask:

- Who passed the information?
- In what form?
- Was it labelled intelligence, safeguarding, risk, or engagement?
- Did the recipient verify it?
- Was it transformed on the way?
- Who could see the original source?
- Did the target have any route to correction?

The route may be direct.

It may be fragmented.

It may also be shorter than expected.

One institution may simply be repeating another institution’s classification.

---

## 🧭 Test Ordinary Explanations

Ordinary explanations must be tested.

Possible explanations include:

- scheduling failure;
- personality conflict;
- class sorting;
- professional caution;
- platform recommendation;
- prejudice;
- factional politics;
- resource shortage;
- poor communication;
- coincidence;
- human error.

The smallest-claim method does not require choosing the least sinister explanation automatically.

It requires comparing explanations according to:

- evidence;
- fit;
- repetition;
- timing;
- predictive power;
- disproof conditions.

Ordinary explanations should be tested, not assumed.

Hidden coordination should be tested, not presumed.

A documented ordinary explanation may close the question.

An ordinary explanation that fails repeatedly may strengthen a more complex one.

---

## 🪜 Build Claims In Layers

A large theory becomes manageable when claims are built in levels.

### Level One: Event Claim

Something happened.

> The invitation was withdrawn.

### Level Two: Decision Claim

A particular actor or process caused the immediate outcome.

> The institution withdrew it after an internal review.

### Level Three: Information Claim

The actor relied on specific information.

> The review cited a security warning.

### Level Four: Source Claim

The information came through an identifiable route.

> The warning came from an external contractor.

### Level Five: Rule Claim

A recurring category or threshold shaped the decision.

> The contractor classified certain associations as hostile-state risk.

### Level Six: System Claim

The same mechanism appears across several cases.

> The same warning structure affected several people.

### Level Seven: Coordination Claim

Several actors or systems share information or direction.

> Institutions relied on the same source or exchanged records.

### Level Eight: State-Attribution Claim

The conduct is connected to a state.

> Evidence links the source, instruction, or infrastructure to a state body.

Each level needs additional evidence.

Failure at one level should not erase what has been established below it.

---

## 🔁 One Mechanism, Several Cases

Once one mechanism is identified, compare cases.

Ask:

- Does the same actor recur?
- Does the same language appear?
- Does the same false detail appear?
- Does the same relationship threshold trigger action?
- Does the timing follow the same pattern?
- Is the same intermediary present?
- Does the same category produce the same consequence?

Repetition may reveal:

- shared policy;
- shared data;
- copied practice;
- common training;
- formal coordination;
- central direction.

Do not jump directly from repetition to command.

The same result may come from shared infrastructure rather than a shared operator.

---

## 🧩 Several Mechanisms, One Effect

An apparently unified pattern may arise from several systems.

For example:

- platform recommendation reduces visibility;
- community gatekeeping limits introductions;
- institutional risk aversion blocks formal access;
- embassy pressure affects relatives;
- poor records circulate between services.

Together, these may create a relationship ceiling.

No single actor needs to control the whole environment.

A system-level effect can emerge from several smaller mechanisms that do not share one controller.

This matters because the remedy may also need to be distributed.

Stopping one actor may not remove the pattern if other mechanisms continue producing it.

---

## 🧪 Natural Experiments

A natural experiment compares similar cases without manufacturing risk.

Consider two relationships that are similar in most respects.

One remains:

- social;
- private;
- emotionally important;
- institutionally minor.

The other becomes:

- legally consequential;
- publicly credible;
- financially useful;
- organisationally durable;
- connected to media or officials.

Observe whether friction rises specifically around the consequential relationship.

Track:

- timing;
- warnings;
- access;
- visibility;
- intermediary involvement;
- administrative action;
- changes in platform treatment;
- changes in institutional behaviour.

Keep as many variables similar as possible.

Do not manufacture danger.

Do not manipulate vulnerable people.

Use naturally occurring comparisons.

---

## 🌡️ Threshold Testing

Ask what changed immediately before intervention.

Possible thresholds include:

- evidence exchange;
- a formal complaint;
- publication;
- legal instruction;
- funding;
- travel;
- introduction to officials;
- cross-community collaboration;
- access to records;
- practical protection.

A repeated threshold is more informative than general bad treatment.

The question is not only whether interference occurred.

It is what the relationship had begun to make possible.

If friction reliably increases after the same functional threshold, the mechanism becomes more testable.

---

## 🔮 Predictions

A useful hypothesis should make predictions.

Possible predictions include:

- friction will increase when relationships become consequential;
- the same categories will appear across cases;
- shared errors will recur;
- low-consequence contacts will proceed normally;
- the same intermediaries will appear;
- pressure will rise during political crises;
- specific institutions will behave similarly after receiving the same data.

Predictions should be recorded before the next relevant event where possible.

Correct predictions strengthen the model.

Failed predictions should weaken it.

A hypothesis that never risks failure cannot become evidence.

---

## ❌ Disproof Conditions

Every claim should state what would count against it.

Examples include:

- similar high-consequence relationships proceed normally;
- no shared actor or source can be found;
- timing does not correspond to relevant activity;
- the event is fully explained by documented ordinary causes;
- allegedly coordinated actors hold contradictory information;
- the pattern does not recur;
- the expected threshold produces no change.

A model that survives only by explaining away disproof is not an investigative model.

The Little Database must remain a hypothesis capable of losing.

Otherwise it becomes another closed system.

---

## 🧾 The Minimum Useful Finding

An investigation does not need to prove the whole architecture to matter.

Useful findings may include:

- an inaccurate risk flag;
- unlawful data sharing;
- an unreviewable algorithm;
- an embassy warning;
- a contractor relationship;
- a platform suppression rule;
- a gatekeeper controlling access;
- a recurring threshold;
- an institution relying on unverifiable information;
- an appeal route that does not function.

Each finding can support:

- correction;
- litigation;
- safeguarding;
- journalism;
- oversight;
- reform;
- protection.

Small proof can produce real protection.

A corrected record may matter before a grand theory is resolved.

A stopped data route may prevent further harm.

A disclosed contract may reveal the next question.

## 🗂️ Documentation Template

For each incident, record:

- **Incident**
- **Date**
- **People involved**
- **Relationship type**
- **Observed change**
- **Immediate decision-maker**
- **Information apparently relied upon**
- **Possible source**
- **Apparent rule**
- **Threshold crossed**
- **Direct evidence**
- **Alternative explanations**
- **Confidence**
- **Next test**
- **Disproof condition**

This creates a record that can later connect to:

- investigation tools;
- case comparisons;
- evidence registers;
- legal review;
- journalistic verification;
- technical testing.

The format should preserve the distinction between:

- fact;
- inference;
- hypothesis;
- attribution.

## 🛡️ Safety And Community Guardrails

Do not:

- bait suspected actors;
- fabricate information;
- conduct covert confrontations;
- publicly name intermediaries without evidence;
- access devices or accounts unlawfully;
- pressure vulnerable people to participate;
- expose relatives;
- create loyalty tests;
- turn community members into amateur intelligence targets.

Preserve:

- consent;
- source safety;
- direct communication;
- legal advice;
- evidentiary integrity;
- the right to decline participation;
- the right to maintain ordinary relationships.

The search for the mechanism must not become another mechanism of harm.

A theory about relationship disruption must not produce more relationship disruption.

---

## ⚖️ Why Small Claims Are Politically Stronger

Small claims are:

- easier to verify;
- harder to dismiss;
- easier to remedy;
- safer to publish;
- more useful in court;
- more persuasive to outsiders.

They allow readers to move from:

- one error;
- to one route;
- to one practice;
- to one system;
- to wider architecture.

The large picture becomes credible because the smaller parts hold.

This is not rhetorical modesty.

It is structural strength.

---

## ⚠️ What “Smallest” Does Not Mean

The smallest-claim method does not mean:

- the wider threat is irrelevant;
- systemic harm should be reduced to isolated mistakes;
- protection must wait for perfect certainty;
- only one actor can be responsible;
- the simplest explanation is always innocent;
- institutional explanations deserve automatic trust.

“Smallest” means:

> the least elaborate mechanism consistent with the evidence.

It does not mean:

> the least politically uncomfortable explanation.

A simple mechanism may still be:

- unlawful;
- coordinated;
- discriminatory;
- state-linked;
- dangerous.

---

## 🧿 Compact Definition

**The Smallest Claim First** is an investigative rule.

It begins with:

- one event;
- one decision;
- one information source;
- one rule;
- one route;
- one threshold.

It then asks whether the same mechanism recurs.

The method turns a large capability warning into testable claims without losing sight of the possibility that several small mechanisms form a wider system.

- what the Little Database of Horrors is;
