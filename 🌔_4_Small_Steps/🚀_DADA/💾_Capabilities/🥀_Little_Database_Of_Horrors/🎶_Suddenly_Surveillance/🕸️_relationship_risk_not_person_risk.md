🕸️ relationship_risk_not_person_risk.md — Bullet-Point Plan

Purpose

* Establish the conceptual heart of the cluster.
* Explain why two individually low-risk people may form a relationship that a system considers highly consequential.
* Shift the unit of analysis from:
    * person;
    * to pair;
    * to edge;
    * to emergent capacity.
* Show how a relationship may create:
    * trust;
    * credibility;
    * care;
    * access;
    * protection;
    * coalition potential.
* Establish the central line:
    The relationship may be classified as dangerous because neither person can produce the same outcome alone.

⸻

Opening

* Begin with:
    Person-centred risk asks what each individual can do alone. Relationship risk asks what becomes possible between them.
* Explain that conventional systems often score:
    * individuals;
    * organisations;
    * devices.
* But some consequences exist only at the relational level.
* Key line:
    The politically significant unit may not be the person. It may be the possibility created between people.

⸻

What Is Person Risk?

* A person-risk model may consider:
    * conduct;
    * history;
    * resources;
    * access;
    * visibility;
    * affiliations;
    * prior classifications.
* It asks:
    * How important is this person?
    * What can they do?
    * How likely are they to act?
* Explain the limits:
    * misses complementary capacity;
    * treats people as isolated;
    * underestimates support and trust;
    * may misread dependence.

⸻

What Is Relationship Risk?

Define relationship risk as a judgement about:

* what two or more people can create together;
* what one person enables in another;
* how the relationship changes each person’s capacity;
* whether the edge connects wider networks.

Possible questions:

* Does this relationship create credibility?
* Does it create care?
* Does it create access?
* Does it connect evidence to an institution?
* Does it connect isolated groups?
* Does it make either person harder to pressure?

Core line:

Relationship risk is not the sum of two person-risk scores.

⸻

Emergent Capacity

* Define emergent capacity:
    * capability produced by the relationship itself.
* Examples:
    * one person has evidence;
    * one person has legal access;
    * together they can pursue remedy.
* Or:
    * one person has trust;
    * one person has institutional credibility;
    * together they can make testimony legible.
* Core line:
    The edge may contain capacity that neither node possesses.

⸻

Complementarity

Possible complementary pairings:

* survivor + lawyer;
* organiser + journalist;
* patient + clinician;
* local witness + national institution;
* researcher + community organiser;
* funder + trusted intermediary;
* disabled person + reliable carer;
* dissident + translator.
* Explain why complementarity matters more than similarity.
* Two similar people may add little.
* Two different people may unlock a new route.
* Key line:
    Difference can create capacity because each person supplies what the other lacks.

⸻

Trust As Infrastructure

* Trust enables:
    * disclosure;
    * coordination;
    * risk-sharing;
    * emotional regulation;
    * long-term planning;
    * mutual defence.
* Trust cannot be measured directly.
* Possible proxies:
    * frequency;
    * privacy;
    * duration;
    * shared risk;
    * emergency-contact status.
* Guardrail:
    Contact is not trust, and trust is not command.
* Explain that systems may overestimate or underestimate trust.

⸻

Care As Capacity

* Care may provide:
    * stability;
    * transport;
    * housing;
    * medication support;
    * regulation;
    * practical access.
* A person may become more capable because someone helps them remain functional.
* Core line:
    Care may become politically consequential without being political in purpose.
* Stress:
    * care is not coordination;
    * support is not allegiance;
    * dependence is not obedience.

⸻

Credibility Transfer

* One person may lend another:
    * legitimacy;
    * professional standing;
    * institutional access;
    * public trust.
* Possible examples:
    * professional validates witness;
    * established organisation supports small group;
    * journalist publishes survivor account.
* Key line:
    Credibility can travel through a relationship without either person controlling the other.
* Risk:
    * endorsement inferred where only access exists;
    * introduction treated as agreement.

⸻

Access Transfer

* Relationships may transfer access to:
    * institutions;
    * decision-makers;
    * media;
    * legal systems;
    * funding;
    * care;
    * protected spaces.
* Explain why access may be more consequential than ideology.
* Core line:
    The relationship matters because it opens a door, not because the people think alike.

⸻

Protection And Mutual Defence

* A relationship may make people harder to isolate.
* Possible forms:
    * witnesses;
    * legal advice;
    * public visibility;
    * housing;
    * financial backup;
    * emotional support;
    * documentation.
* A system may view protection as reduced intervention effectiveness.
* Key line:
    A protected person is not necessarily more dangerous. They may simply be harder to harm quietly.

⸻

Relationship Risk And The Disruption Score

* Connect directly to 🧮_the_disruption_score.md.
* Explain that relational features may include:
    * trust;
    * complementarity;
    * credibility transfer;
    * protection;
    * bridge value;
    * irreplaceability.
* A pair may score highly even if:
    * neither person is central;
    * neither has serious prior classification;
    * the relationship is lawful.
* Core line:
    The disruption score may rise because the relationship reduces vulnerability.

⸻

Pair-Level Scoring

Possible pair features:

* contact frequency;
* duration;
* shared resources;
* complementary roles;
* mutual trust;
* public legitimacy;
* ability to protect;
* institutional reach;
* substitution difficulty.
* Stress:
    * these are hypothetical variables;
    * plausible variables do not prove deployment.
* Explain that pair-level scoring may be hidden within person-level outputs.

⸻

Edge Centrality

* A relationship may be important because it connects:
    * communities;
    * institutions;
    * professions;
    * geographies;
    * identity groups.
* Explain:
    * edge centrality;
    * bridge edge;
    * bottleneck.
* Core line:
    Some relationships matter because many routes pass through them.
* Connect to social-graph node.

⸻

Relationship Risk Versus Connector Risk

* Distinguish:
    * pair creates capacity for itself;
    * connector creates capacity across groups.
* Relationship risk:
    * focus on one edge.
* Connector risk:
    * focus on wider bridging function.
* Transition toward:
    🔗_coalition_and_connector_risk.md

⸻

Relationship Risk Versus Association Risk

* Association risk says:
    * person becomes risky because they know a risky person.
* Relationship risk asks:
    * what does this specific relationship enable?
* Guardrail:
    Proximity is not capacity.
* Avoid guilt by association.
* Require:
    * specific mechanism;
    * actual relationship type;
    * evidence of effect.

⸻

Asymmetric Relationships

* One person may provide:
    * care;
    * access;
    * money;
    * legitimacy.
* The other may provide:
    * evidence;
    * lived knowledge;
    * community trust.
* Capacity may be uneven but mutual.
* Risks:
    * dependence mistaken for control;
    * influence mistaken for domination.
* Key line:
    Unequal contribution does not prove one-sided command.

⸻

One-Sided And Non-Reciprocal Edges

* Repeated contact may reflect:
    * harassment;
    * professional duty;
    * coercion;
    * unwanted attention.
* A relationship-risk model must establish:
    * consent;
    * reciprocity;
    * actual trust.
* Core line:
    An edge can exist without a relationship.
* Connect back to private-relationships node.

⸻

Historical Versus Current Relationships

* Former:
    * partner;
    * carer;
    * colleague;
    * adviser;
    * friend.
* Risk:
    * old capacity treated as current;
    * historic trust treated as live access.
* Key line:
    A relationship that once created capacity may no longer exist in practice.
* Require:
    * dates;
    * current status;
    * recency.

⸻

Latent Relationships

* A system may estimate potential relationships before they form.
* Possible basis:
    * shared contacts;
    * complementary roles;
    * co-location;
    * common institutions;
    * mutual interests.
* This is especially speculative.
* Core line:
    Compatibility is not contact, and potential is not relationship.
* Important link to relationship caps:
    * cap may be applied before the edge stabilises.

⸻

Relationship Formation As The Target

* A system may focus on:
    * whether introduction occurs;
    * whether trust forms;
    * whether contact becomes private;
    * whether resources begin to flow.
* Intervention may occur before overt activity.
* Core line:
    The target may be the formation of capacity, not the suppression of conduct.
* This is one of the node’s strongest conceptual sections.

⸻

Relationship Stabilisation

* Relationships become more consequential through:
    * repetition;
    * privacy;
    * shared routine;
    * successful mutual aid;
    * crisis survival;
    * institutional recognition.
* A system may tolerate early contact but resist stabilisation.
* Connect directly to 🧱_relationship_caps.md.
* Key line:
    The risk score may rise not when the people meet, but when the relationship begins to endure.

⸻

Protective Relationships

Examples:

* lawyer-client;
* doctor-patient;
* therapist-client;
* journalist-source;
* carer-disabled person;
* union representative-worker;
* clergy-congregant.
* Explain why relationship-risk scoring may threaten:
    * confidentiality;
    * access to remedy;
    * care;
    * rights.
* Core line:
    A relationship that protects rights should not become risky merely because protection makes intervention harder.

⸻

Coalition-Building

* A pair may create a route into a wider coalition.
* Examples:
    * one trusted introduction;
    * one shared document;
    * one public endorsement.
* The pair’s value may exceed direct output.
* Transition toward connector-risk node.
* Core line:
    The pair may matter because it is the first bridge in a much larger structure.

⸻

Relationship Substitution

* If a high-capacity relationship is disrupted, a system may prefer:
    * approved contact;
    * formal intermediary;
    * lower-trust substitute.
* Connect to:
    🪞_safe_contacts_and_managed_substitutes.md
* Key line:
    The substitute may preserve contact while removing the capacity that made the original relationship consequential.

⸻

Intervention Risks

* Disrupting relationships may cause:
    * loss of care;
    * loss of legal access;
    * trauma;
    * isolation;
    * financial harm;
    * reduced safeguarding;
    * coalition fragmentation.
* Key line:
    Relational intervention may destroy protection before it reduces any genuine threat.

⸻

False Positives

A relationship may score highly because of:

* ordinary friendship;
* care;
* professional duty;
* mutual aid;
* family;
* lawful advocacy;
* shared vulnerability.
* Consequences may be severe because the relationship itself becomes target.
* Core line:
    The model may mistake ordinary human interdependence for operational capacity.

⸻

False Negatives

* Systems may overlook:
    * hidden coercion;
    * abusive private relationships;
    * non-digital trust;
    * informal networks;
    * relationships outside institutions.
* A visible relationship may be over-scored while hidden harmful relationships are missed.
* Key line:
    The relationships easiest to map are not necessarily the relationships most capable of harm.

⸻

Self-Fulfilling Risk

* Monitoring increases pressure.
* Pressure changes communication.
* Communication becomes secretive or irregular.
* System treats this as proof of risk.
* Core line:
    The model may create the suspicious pattern it expected to find.
* Connect to observation-to-intervention node.

⸻

Evidence Ladder

1. Two people are connected.
2. The relationship type is established.
3. The relationship creates specific capacity.
4. A system scores that capacity.
5. A decision changes treatment.
6. The intervention affects the relationship.
7. Intent or coordination is separately evidenced.

* Do not leap from connection to risk.
* Key line:
    The smallest claim may be that a relationship created identifiable capacity—not that the people formed a conspiracy.

⸻

What Would Disconfirm The Hypothesis?

* Relationship is weak, historic, or non-reciprocal.
* No capacity is created.
* No treatment changes as the relationship stabilises.
* Similar pairs face the same ordinary barriers.
* No score, referral, or intervention route can be traced.
* Relationship is allowed to become:
    * private;
    * stable;
    * protective;
    * materially useful.
* Strong disconfirmation section required.

⸻

Governance Questions

* What is the relationship type?
* Is it current?
* Is it reciprocal?
* Is it consensual?
* What capacity does it actually create?
* Is that capacity lawful?
* Is care being mistaken for coordination?
* Is credibility being mistaken for control?
* Are protected relationships involved?
* What proxy is used for trust?
* Does the score attach to the person or the pair?
* What threshold triggers intervention?
* Could intervention create the observed instability?
* Can both people challenge the classification?
* Are harms to care and protection measured?
* What evidence would falsify the assessment?

⸻

What This Node Must Not Claim

* Connection does not prove relationship.
* Relationship does not prove coordination.
* Trust does not prove command.
* Care does not prove political purpose.
* Complementarity does not prove conspiracy.
* Protection does not prove danger.
* Pair-level scoring capability does not prove deployment.
* Relationship difficulty does not automatically prove intervention.
