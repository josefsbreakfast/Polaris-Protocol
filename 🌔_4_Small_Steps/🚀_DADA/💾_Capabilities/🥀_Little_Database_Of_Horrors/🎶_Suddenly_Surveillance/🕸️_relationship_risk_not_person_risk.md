# 🕸️ Relationship Risk, Not Person Risk
**First created:** 2026-07-20 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-20  
*How the consequential unit may be the relationship itself—and the capacity that emerges only when two people are allowed to know, trust, or protect one another.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Person-centred risk asks what each individual can do alone.

Relationship risk asks what becomes possible between them.

Conventional systems often score:

- individuals;
- organisations;
- devices;
- accounts;
- institutions.

But some forms of capacity exist only at the relational level.

The politically significant unit may not be the person.

It may be the possibility created between people.

A relationship may create:

- trust;
- credibility;
- care;
- access;
- protection;
- coalition potential;
- mutual resilience.

Relationship risk is not the sum of two person-risk scores.

The relationship may be classified as dangerous because neither person can produce the same outcome alone.

---

## 👤 What Is Person Risk?

A person-risk model may consider:

- conduct;
- history;
- resources;
- access;
- visibility;
- affiliations;
- prior classifications;
- professional role;
- network centrality.

It asks:

- How important is this person?
- What can they do?
- How likely are they to act?
- How difficult are they to monitor?
- How vulnerable are they to pressure?

This model may be useful for some purposes.

It also has limits.

It may:

- miss complementary capacity;
- treat people as isolated;
- underestimate support and trust;
- misunderstand care;
- mistake dependency for control;
- ignore what becomes possible through relationship.

A person may appear low-capacity alone while becoming highly capable within one specific relationship.

---

## 🕸️ What Is Relationship Risk?

Relationship risk is a judgement about:

- what two or more people can create together;
- what one person enables in another;
- how the relationship changes each person’s capacity;
- whether the edge connects wider networks;
- whether the relationship reduces vulnerability;
- whether the relationship opens access.

Possible questions include:

- Does this relationship create credibility?
- Does it create care?
- Does it create access?
- Does it connect evidence to an institution?
- Does it connect isolated groups?
- Does it make either person harder to pressure?
- Does it create a future coalition?

Relationship risk is not the sum of two person-risk scores.

It is an assessment of emergent capacity.

---

## ✨ Emergent Capacity

Emergent capacity is capability produced by the relationship itself.

Examples include:

- one person has evidence;
- one person has legal access;
- together they can pursue remedy.

Or:

- one person has trust;
- one person has institutional credibility;
- together they can make testimony legible.

Or:

- one person has mobility;
- one person has community knowledge;
- together they can reach people neither could reach alone.

The edge may contain capacity that neither node possesses.

This capacity may be:

- lawful;
- protective;
- ordinary;
- politically consequential.

The existence of capacity does not establish wrongdoing.

---

## 🧩 Complementarity

Complementarity matters because difference can create capability.

Possible pairings include:

- survivor + lawyer;
- organiser + journalist;
- patient + clinician;
- local witness + national institution;
- researcher + community organiser;
- funder + trusted intermediary;
- disabled person + reliable carer;
- dissident + translator;
- whistleblower + protected publisher.

Two similar people may add little.

Two different people may unlock a new route.

Difference can create capacity because each person supplies what the other lacks.

Complementarity should not be mistaken for conspiracy.

It may simply describe lawful cooperation.

---

## 🫂 Trust As Infrastructure

Trust enables:

- disclosure;
- coordination;
- risk-sharing;
- emotional regulation;
- long-term planning;
- mutual defence;
- evidence transfer;
- crisis support.

Trust cannot be measured directly.

Possible proxies include:

- frequency;
- privacy;
- duration;
- shared risk;
- emergency-contact status;
- repeated mutual aid.

But:

- contact is not trust;
- trust is not command;
- privacy is not concealment;
- duration is not agreement.

A system may overestimate trust.

It may also underestimate it.

Some of the deepest relationships leave very few digital traces.

---

## 🩺 Care As Capacity

Care may provide:

- stability;
- transport;
- housing;
- medication support;
- regulation;
- practical access;
- advocacy;
- survival.

A person may become more capable because someone helps them remain functional.

Care may become politically consequential without being political in purpose.

Care is not coordination.

Support is not allegiance.

Dependence is not obedience.

A system that treats care as suspicious may remove the very relationship preventing harm.

---

## 🗣️ Credibility Transfer

One person may lend another:

- legitimacy;
- professional standing;
- institutional access;
- public trust;
- evidentiary weight.

Examples include:

- a professional validating a witness;
- an established organisation supporting a small group;
- a journalist publishing a survivor account;
- a recognised institution opening a route to remedy.

Credibility can travel through a relationship without either person controlling the other.

Risks include:

- endorsement inferred where only access exists;
- introduction treated as agreement;
- professional duty treated as political alignment.

Credibility transfer is relational.

It is not necessarily ideological.

---

## 🚪 Access Transfer

Relationships may transfer access to:

- institutions;
- decision-makers;
- media;
- legal systems;
- funding;
- care;
- protected spaces;
- specialist knowledge.

Access may be more consequential than ideology.

The relationship matters because it opens a door, not because the people think alike.

One introduction may:

- make a complaint legible;
- secure legal advice;
- protect evidence;
- create public scrutiny;
- reduce isolation.

Access transfer should not be confused with command.

---

## 🛡️ Protection And Mutual Defence

A relationship may make people harder to isolate.

Possible forms of protection include:

- witnesses;
- legal advice;
- public visibility;
- housing;
- financial backup;
- emotional support;
- documentation;
- emergency planning.

A system may view protection as reduced intervention effectiveness.

A protected person is not necessarily more dangerous.

They may simply be harder to harm quietly.

This is an important distinction.

Protection may be:

- lawful;
- necessary;
- rights-preserving;
- life-saving.

---

## 🧮 Relationship Risk And The Disruption Score

Relational features may include:

- trust;
- complementarity;
- credibility transfer;
- protection;
- bridge value;
- irreplaceability;
- access creation;
- coalition potential.

A pair may score highly even if:

- neither person is central;
- neither has a serious prior classification;
- the relationship is lawful;
- the capacity is protective.

The disruption score may rise because the relationship reduces vulnerability.

This connects directly to [🧮 The Disruption Score](./🧮_the_disruption_score.md).

The system may prioritise the relationship not because it causes harm, but because it makes intervention harder.

---

## 📊 Pair-Level Scoring

Possible pair-level features include:

- contact frequency;
- duration;
- shared resources;
- complementary roles;
- inferred mutual trust;
- public legitimacy;
- ability to protect;
- institutional reach;
- substitution difficulty;
- shared access;
- crisis resilience.

These are hypothetical variables.

Plausible variables do not prove deployment.

Pair-level scoring may also be hidden inside person-level outputs.

A person’s score may rise because of:

- who they know;
- what that relationship creates;
- how hard the relationship is to replace.

---

## 🔗 Edge Centrality

A relationship may be important because it connects:

- communities;
- institutions;
- professions;
- geographies;
- identity groups;
- bodies of evidence.

Possible concepts include:

- edge centrality;
- bridge edge;
- bottleneck;
- cut edge.

Some relationships matter because many routes pass through them.

Removing one edge may:

- isolate a cluster;
- block information;
- prevent coalition;
- sever care;
- reduce legal access.

This connects directly to [🕸️ Social Graphs And Network Analysis](../🩸_Feed_Me_Data/🕸️_social_graphs_and_network_analysis.md).

## 🔗 Relationship Risk Versus Connector Risk

Relationship risk focuses on one edge.

Connector risk focuses on wider bridging function.

A relationship may create capacity for:

- two people;
- one case;
- one support route.

A connector may create capacity across:

- groups;
- institutions;
- communities;
- geographies.

The distinction matters because:

- pair-level intervention targets one relationship;
- connector intervention may affect an entire network.

The next node widens the frame into [🔗 Coalition And Connector Risk](./🔗_coalition_and_connector_risk.md).

---

## ⚠️ Relationship Risk Versus Association Risk

Association risk says:

> A person becomes risky because they know a risky person.

Relationship risk asks:

> What does this specific relationship actually enable?

Proximity is not capacity.

A meaningful assessment should require:

- specific relationship type;
- actual mechanism;
- evidence of effect;
- current status;
- reciprocity.

This protects against guilt by association.

The existence of an edge does not establish its meaning.

---

## ↔️ Asymmetric Relationships

One person may provide:

- care;
- access;
- money;
- legitimacy;
- expertise.

The other may provide:

- evidence;
- lived knowledge;
- community trust;
- public meaning;
- access to another network.

Capacity may be uneven but mutual.

Unequal contribution does not prove one-sided command.

Asymmetry may reflect:

- disability;
- professional role;
- age;
- institutional access;
- unequal resources.

It should not automatically be interpreted as domination.

---

## 🚫 One-Sided And Non-Reciprocal Edges

Repeated contact may reflect:

- harassment;
- professional duty;
- coercion;
- unwanted attention;
- administrative obligation.

A relationship-risk model must establish:

- consent;
- reciprocity;
- actual trust;
- current relationship type.

An edge can exist without a relationship.

This is especially important where:

- one person repeatedly contacts another;
- the contact is unwanted;
- the system mistakes persistence for mutual closeness.

Connection must not be mistaken for reciprocity.

---

## 🕰️ Historical Versus Current Relationships

Former relationships may include:

- partner;
- carer;
- colleague;
- adviser;
- friend;
- housemate.

Risks include:

- old capacity treated as current;
- historic trust treated as live access;
- former intimacy treated as present influence;
- ended care treated as ongoing dependency.

A relationship that once created capacity may no longer exist in practice.

Every assessment should preserve:

- dates;
- current status;
- recency;
- dispute status;
- evidence of ongoing contact.

---

## 🌱 Latent Relationships

A system may estimate potential relationships before they form.

Possible bases include:

- shared contacts;
- complementary roles;
- co-location;
- common institutions;
- mutual interests;
- overlapping networks.

This is especially speculative.

Compatibility is not contact.

Potential is not relationship.

A predicted relationship may never form because:

- there is no trust;
- there is no introduction;
- interests differ;
- timing fails;
- practical barriers remain.

Treating latent possibility as existing capacity creates a serious inference leap.

---

## 🪴 Relationship Formation As The Target

A system may focus on:

- whether introduction occurs;
- whether trust forms;
- whether contact becomes private;
- whether resources begin to flow;
- whether the relationship becomes recognised.

Intervention may occur before overt activity.

The target may be the formation of capacity, not the suppression of conduct.

This is a major conceptual shift.

The system may attempt to prevent:

- a witness reaching a lawyer;
- a survivor reaching a journalist;
- a disabled person reaching reliable care;
- two communities reaching one another.

The anticipated relationship becomes the object of governance.

---

## 🧱 Relationship Stabilisation

Relationships become more consequential through:

- repetition;
- privacy;
- shared routine;
- successful mutual aid;
- crisis survival;
- institutional recognition;
- material support.

A system may tolerate early contact but resist stabilisation.

The risk score may rise not when the people meet, but when the relationship begins to endure.

Stabilisation creates:

- memory;
- confidence;
- mutual obligation;
- reliability;
- practical capacity.

This connects directly to [🧱 Relationship Caps](./🧱_relationship_caps.md).

## ⚖️ Protective Relationships

Protective relationships may include:

- lawyer-client;
- doctor-patient;
- therapist-client;
- journalist-source;
- carer-disabled person;
- union representative-worker;
- clergy-congregant;
- advocate-survivor.

Relationship-risk scoring may threaten:

- confidentiality;
- access to remedy;
- care;
- rights;
- disclosure;
- safeguarding.

A relationship that protects rights should not become risky merely because protection makes intervention harder.

Heightened safeguards are required.

---

## 🌉 Coalition-Building

A pair may create a route into a wider coalition.

Examples include:

- one trusted introduction;
- one shared document;
- one public endorsement;
- one protected referral;
- one bridge between institutions.

The pair’s value may exceed its direct output.

The pair may matter because it is the first bridge in a much larger structure.

This is where relationship risk becomes connector risk.

The wider frame is developed in [🔗 Coalition And Connector Risk](./🔗_coalition_and_connector_risk.md).

## 🪞 Relationship Substitution

If a high-capacity relationship is disrupted, a system may prefer:

- approved contact;
- formal intermediary;
- lower-trust substitute;
- institutional liaison;
- managed channel.

The substitute may preserve contact while removing the capacity that made the original relationship consequential.

This connects directly to [🪞 Safe Contacts And Managed Substitutes](./🪞_safe_contacts_and_managed_substitutes.md).

The person remains supported on paper.

The relational capacity changes.

## 🪫 Intervention Risks

Disrupting relationships may cause:

- loss of care;
- loss of legal access;
- trauma;
- isolation;
- financial harm;
- reduced safeguarding;
- coalition fragmentation;
- loss of evidence;
- functional decline.

Relational intervention may destroy protection before it reduces any genuine threat.

Intervention should therefore assess:

- welfare;
- rights;
- care;
- collateral harm;
- reversibility.

The edge may be protective rather than harmful.

## ❌ False Positives

A relationship may score highly because of:

- ordinary friendship;
- care;
- professional duty;
- mutual aid;
- family;
- lawful advocacy;
- shared vulnerability;
- emergency support.

The model may mistake ordinary human interdependence for operational capacity.

Consequences may be severe because the relationship itself becomes the target.

A false positive may remove:

- trust;
- care;
- housing;
- access to remedy;
- emotional stability.

---

## 🕳️ False Negatives

Systems may overlook:

- hidden coercion;
- abusive private relationships;
- non-digital trust;
- informal networks;
- relationships outside institutions;
- isolated predatory relationships.

The relationships easiest to map are not necessarily the relationships most capable of harm.

A visible supportive relationship may be over-scored.

A hidden abusive relationship may be missed.

Relational visibility is not relational danger.

---

## 🔁 Self-Fulfilling Risk

Monitoring may increase pressure.

Pressure may change communication.

Communication may become:

- secretive;
- irregular;
- cautious;
- infrequent.

The system may treat this as proof of risk.

The model may create the suspicious pattern it expected to find.

This connects to [👁️ From Observation To Intervention](./👁️_from_observation_to_intervention.md).

A valid system must separate:

- baseline behaviour;
- intervention effect;
- unrelated change.

---

## 🪜 Evidence Ladder

A cautious evidence ladder might be:

1. Two people are connected.
2. The relationship type is established.
3. The relationship creates specific capacity.
4. A system scores that capacity.
5. A decision changes treatment.
6. The intervention affects the relationship.
7. Intent or coordination is separately evidenced.

Do not leap from connection to risk.

The smallest claim may be that a relationship created identifiable capacity—not that the people formed a conspiracy.

This keeps capability analysis grounded.

---

## ❎ What Would Disconfirm The Hypothesis?

Evidence against the relationship-risk hypothesis may include:

- the relationship being weak;
- the relationship being historical;
- the relationship being non-reciprocal;
- no capacity being created;
- no treatment changing as the relationship stabilises;
- similar pairs facing the same ordinary barriers;
- no score, referral, or intervention route being traceable;
- the relationship being allowed to become:
  - private;
  - stable;
  - protective;
  - materially useful.

A useful hypothesis must be capable of losing.

---

## 🛡️ Minimum Governance Questions

Any system assessing relationship risk should answer:

- What is the relationship type?
- Is it current?
- Is it reciprocal?
- Is it consensual?
- What capacity does it actually create?
- Is that capacity lawful?
- Is care being mistaken for coordination?
- Is credibility being mistaken for control?
- Are protected relationships involved?
- What proxy is used for trust?
- Does the score attach to the person or the pair?
- What threshold triggers intervention?
- Could intervention create the observed instability?
- Can both people challenge the classification?
- Are harms to care and protection measured?
- What evidence would falsify the assessment?
- Is the relationship being treated as risky because it reduces vulnerability?
- Can the score distinguish protection from threat?

A system that cannot answer these questions should not treat the edge as a risk category.

## ⚠️ What This Node Is Not Claiming

This node does not claim that:

- connection proves relationship;
- relationship proves coordination;
- trust proves command;
- care proves political purpose;
- complementarity proves conspiracy;
- protection proves danger;
- pair-level scoring capability proves deployment;
- relationship difficulty automatically proves intervention.

The node identifies:

- emergent capacity;
- pair-level scoring;
- relational protection;
- latent relationship risk;
- intervention hazards;
- governance requirements.

Specific allegations require specific evidence.

## 🧿 Compact Definition

**Relationship Risk, Not Person Risk** describes the shift from assessing individuals in isolation to assessing the capacities created between people.

A relationship may create:

- trust;
- care;
- credibility;
- access;
- protection;
- coalition potential.

The central danger is that lawful human interdependence may be classified as risk merely because it makes people harder to isolate, discredit, or harm quietly.
