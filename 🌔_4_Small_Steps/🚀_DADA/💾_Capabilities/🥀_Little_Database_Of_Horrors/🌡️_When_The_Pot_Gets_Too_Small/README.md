# 🌡️ When The Pot Gets Too Small
**First created:** 2026-07-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-25  
*How systems built for one political temperature can expand their categories, confidence claims, data appetite, and interventions when reality becomes harder to predict.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

A system is built for a particular world.

It has a stated purpose.

It collects particular kinds of information.

It covers a particular population.

It uses particular categories.

It operates according to particular thresholds.

It is given particular powers.

It sits inside particular legal, political, and institutional assumptions about what normal looks like.

Then normal changes.

There is an election.

A protest movement grows.

A leadership succession becomes uncertain.

A war begins.

Sanctions arrive.

A diplomatic relationship collapses.

An attack occurs.

A government becomes frightened of defection, mobilisation, foreign influence, political embarrassment, or simply being surprised.

The system is suddenly asked questions it was not originally required to answer.

Who matters?

Who is connected?

Who might mobilise?

Who might defect?

Who influences whom?

Which relationships could become politically consequential?

Who should be investigated?

Who should be prevented from becoming a problem before anyone can demonstrate that they are one?

The pot has become too small.

The critical question is what happens next.

---

## 🪴 The Pot Is Part Of The Model

The pot is not merely the database.

It is the boundary around what the system is supposed to know and do.

It may include:

- purpose limitation;
- population scope;
- authorised data sources;
- risk categories;
- confidence thresholds;
- retention periods;
- intervention powers;
- evidential standards;
- legal authority;
- institutional jurisdiction;
- acceptable error;
- review mechanisms;
- oversight;
- routes for correction and appeal.

A system can therefore outgrow its pot in several different ways.

More people can be brought inside it.

More data can be connected.

Old information can be retained for longer.

Relationships that were previously irrelevant can become risk variables.

Weak indicators can acquire greater operational weight.

Information collected for one purpose can become attractive for another.

Outputs designed to generate investigative leads can begin influencing consequential decisions.

Administrative risk can begin drifting towards preventive intervention.

The existence of that pathway does not establish that it has occurred in any particular system.

Nor does the existence of unused capability establish an intention to activate it.

But infrastructure built during calm periods matters because crisis expansion does not necessarily require building an entirely new machine.

Sometimes the expensive part already exists.

---

## 🪴 Establish The Baseline First

Escalation requires a baseline.

A system at rest may be used for:

- community engagement;
- routine monitoring;
- grant administration;
- welfare or safeguarding;
- passive intelligence;
- narrowly defined security assessment;
- border administration;
- fraud detection;
- criminal investigation;
- protective security;
- ordinary institutional risk management.

These activities are not interchangeable.

Neither is the information they produce.

A piece of information may be suitable as:

- an investigative lead;
- criminal intelligence;
- safeguarding information;
- national-security intelligence;
- an administrative risk indicator;
- contextual information;
- evidence capable of supporting legal action.

Those are different epistemic and legal jobs.

A central question throughout this branch is therefore:

> **What evidential weight was this information originally entitled to carry?**

Without that baseline, an investigator cannot reliably distinguish ordinary use from expansion.

See [🪴 The System At Rest](./🪴_the_system_at_rest.md) for the baseline against which escalation should be assessed.

## 🌪️ Pressure Changes What Institutions Ask For

Political crisis does not magically improve the underlying data.

It can dramatically increase the demand placed upon them.

An institution that was previously asked:

> What is happening?

may suddenly be asked:

> What will happen next?

Then:

> Who should we be worried about?

Then:

> Who knows them?

Then:

> Which of those relationships might become important?

And eventually:

> Which people would become difficult to manage if they acquired greater capacity?

This is not only a technological shift.

It is an institutional one.

Pressure may come from:

- political leadership;
- senior officials;
- public criticism;
- intelligence failures;
- compressed decision time;
- fear of missing a threat;
- inter-agency competition;
- reputational exposure;
- demands to demonstrate that expensive infrastructure is useful;
- asymmetric fear of false negatives.

The system may therefore be asked to move from description towards prediction, and from prediction towards intervention.

See [🌪️ The System Under Pressure](./🌪️_the_system_under_pressure.md) for the general mechanics of this transition.

## 🗳️ Internal Political Shock

Elections, protests, leadership transitions, coup fears, elite fractures, and succession crises can change how governments interpret relationships.

A social connection that appeared politically unimportant during stable conditions may suddenly be read in terms of:

- mobilisation;
- brokerage;
- coalition;
- leadership;
- funding;
- information dissemination;
- opposition capacity;
- institutional legitimacy;
- access to external support.

Diaspora relationships may become particularly salient where they connect domestic actors to:

- journalists;
- lawyers;
- funders;
- foreign governments;
- international institutions;
- exile movements;
- civil society;
- technical expertise.

None of this establishes that every state responds to domestic instability by repressing diaspora relationships.

The question is comparative:

> **What changed in the state's treatment of the same people, information, and relationships when the political environment changed?**

See [🗳️ Elections, Protests And Succession Crises](./🗳️_elections_protests_and_succession_crises.md).

## ⚔️ External Shock

War, sanctions, terrorism, diplomatic rupture, international litigation, and geopolitical confrontation can produce a different form of expansion.

The boundary of what counts as security-relevant may move.

Nationality, family connection, travel, remittances, political speech, humanitarian activity, institutional affiliation, or contact with people in another jurisdiction can acquire new significance.

Systems outside traditional intelligence institutions may also become consequential.

Banks, employers, universities, platforms, transport systems, border authorities, contractors, and other intermediaries may possess information or powers that suddenly matter to a security problem.

This creates an important seam with [🌱 Cuttings](../🌱_Cuttings/): capabilities may already be distributed across institutions and jurisdictions before a crisis creates demand to connect or operationalise them.

See [⚔️ War, Sanctions And External Shock](./⚔️_war_sanctions_and_external_shock.md).

## 🚓 Policing, Intelligence, Prevention, And Administration

One of the most consequential forms of expansion may occur between institutional modes of reasoning.

Criminal investigation might ask:

> What happened, who was involved, and what evidence supports further action?

Intelligence assessment may ask:

> What might be happening, what remains uncertain, and what should be investigated?

Preventive risk management may ask:

> What could happen, which conditions could enable it, and what intervention might reduce the possibility?

Administrative systems may ask something different again.

All can have legitimate functions.

Problems arise when their outputs begin carrying evidential weight they were never designed to bear.

An inference sufficient to generate an investigative lead is not automatically sufficient to establish wrongdoing.

An association worth examining is not automatically a risky association.

A risk indicator is not automatically evidence.

A prediction is not automatically a fact.

This branch therefore keeps two pathways analytically separate:

> **lead → suspicion → investigation → evidence → legal threshold → action**

and:

> **data → proxy → inference → prediction → risk classification → preventive intervention**

The danger zone appears when the second pathway begins producing consequences normally associated with the first while retaining weaker evidential safeguards.

If action must sometimes occur before certainty, uncertainty must remain visible.

A lower evidential threshold cannot be compensated for by pretending that the resulting classification has greater epistemic certainty than the underlying information supports.

## 🌍 What Do We Call It When Someone Else Does It?

There is also a comparative problem.

States may condemn surveillance, political pressure, diaspora monitoring, family leverage, or interference with lawful association when undertaken by an adversarial government.

They may describe adjacent domestic capabilities using the language of:

- safeguarding;
- intelligence-led policing;
- protective security;
- community engagement;
- counter-extremism;
- foreign-interference prevention;
- risk mitigation;
- social stability.

Different systems may genuinely differ in law, purpose, safeguards, scale, accountability, and consequences.

Those differences matter.

But geopolitical alignment should not substitute for demonstrating them.

The comparative question is therefore not:

> Are all states secretly doing the same thing?

It is:

> **Are equivalent or adjacent behaviours being subjected to equivalent scrutiny?**

Across this cluster, three questions should therefore remain available:

> **What does this state condemn when another state does it?**

> **What adjacent or equivalent powers does it reserve to itself?**

> **What would it actually prosecute, disrupt, regulate, condemn, or tolerate if a foreign state did the same thing on its territory?**

Avoiding false equivalence does not require diplomatic exceptionalism.

Different evidence may justify different conclusions.

Different friendships should not justify different evidentiary questions.

## ⚖️ Lawful, Legitimate, Tolerated, And Prosecutable

These categories must also remain separate.

A practice may be:

- lawful but ethically troubling;
- lawful for one institution but not another;
- lawful only under particular authorisation;
- unlawful but difficult to prove;
- politically condemned without constituting a criminal offence;
- disruptive enough to justify protective action without supporting prosecution;
- legitimate intelligence activity until a particular intervention occurs;
- lawful in isolation but concerning when combined with other conduct.

This creates several distinct questions.

### What may the state itself lawfully do?

This concerns the powers, purposes, safeguards, authorisations, rights, and restrictions applying to domestic institutions.

### What may a foreign state lawfully do here?

The answer need not mirror the first.

Sovereignty, diplomatic law, foreign-state activity, data access, coercion, agency, and other legal questions may change the analysis.

### What can actually be prosecuted?

Conduct can be deeply concerning without fitting neatly into a criminal offence.

Conversely, conduct may potentially satisfy an offence while remaining difficult to attribute or prove.

### What can be disrupted without prosecution?

Criminal prosecution is not the only possible state response.

Protective, diplomatic, regulatory, civil, administrative, and other lawful mechanisms may operate at different thresholds.

The existence of these different thresholds matters because crisis can produce two different forms of escalation:

> **the evidential threshold for concern changes**

or:

> **the consequences attached to the existing threshold change.**

Those are not the same development.

The second may sometimes be easier to miss.

## 📈 When The Institution Demands Certainty

The underlying information may remain probabilistic.

Political demand may not.

A system may observe that two people communicate.

From that it may infer a meaningful relationship.

From the relationship it may predict cooperation.

From possible cooperation it may estimate political or security risk.

Each transition introduces uncertainty.

Yet the final dashboard may present a clean category.

Pressure can therefore create a mismatch:

> **uncertain model + institutional demand for categorical answers**

A responsible answer may be:

> We do not know.

A dangerous institutional response is:

> Feed the system until it knows.

See [📈 Demanding More Certainty From A Bad Model](./📈_demanding_more_certainty_from_a_bad_model.md).

## 🎯 Expansion Is Not Improved Accuracy

When institutions demand more certainty, the system can expand in several directions.

It can expand:

- the population assessed;
- the behaviours treated as relevant;
- the number of relationships considered significant;
- the historical period retained;
- the datasets connected;
- the categories available;
- the definition of existing categories.

Thresholds can also fall.

A larger net will generally catch more things.

That does not establish that it has become better at distinguishing the thing it was intended to find.

The resulting errors matter because classification can create real consequences.

> **False classification can produce true harm.**

See [🎯 Category Expansion And False Positives](./🎯_category_expansion_and_false_positives.md).

## 🪤 When Contradiction Stops Correcting The Model

Security analysis has a genuine problem.

People sometimes conceal harmful activity.

Absence of readily available evidence cannot therefore always be treated as conclusive evidence of absence.

But that legitimate caution contains an epistemic trap.

A system can become difficult to correct if:

> evidence supports suspicion,

while:

> absence of evidence also supports suspicion because concealment is expected.

At the extreme:

> no evidence becomes sophisticated concealment;

> contradictory evidence becomes deception;

> changed behaviour becomes adaptation;

> failed prediction becomes evidence of an unseen network.

At that point, disconfirmation stops performing its proper function.

The problem is not recognising that concealment exists.

The problem is allowing concealment to become the universal explanation for prediction failure.

See [🪤 When Error Is Reclassified As Concealment](./🪤_when_error_is_reclassified_as_concealment.md).

## 🧪 When Intervention Changes The Evidence

There is a second failure mode.

The system may alter the environment it subsequently observes.

Surveillance can change communication.

Investigation can change behaviour.

Exclusion can change networks.

Warnings can alter relationships.

Administrative pressure can change movement.

Coercion can change political attitudes.

Institutional distrust can make people less willing to cooperate with institutions.

The resulting behaviour is real.

But it is no longer independent of the intervention.

A feedback loop can emerge:

> **classification → intervention → behavioural response → observation → stronger classification**

This creates an endogeneity problem.

The model may become increasingly capable of predicting the world produced by its own interventions while becoming less capable of describing the world that would have existed without them.

See [🧪 When The Model Creates Its Own Evidence](./🧪_when_the_model_creates_its_own_evidence.md).

## 🩸 Feed It Or Review It

Prediction failure creates a governance choice.

An institution can interpret failure as evidence that the model requires review.

It can:

- reconsider assumptions;
- restore uncertainty;
- examine provenance;
- correct records;
- separate datasets;
- narrow categories;
- raise thresholds;
- reduce intervention;
- suspend processing;
- delete information;
- stop the system.

Or it can interpret failure as evidence that the system has not yet been given enough.

It can:

- collect more;
- retain longer;
- connect additional databases;
- introduce new proxies;
- widen categories;
- lower thresholds;
- score more relationships;
- acquire another tool;
- increase intervention.

There may be powerful institutional incentives for the second response.

Sunk costs matter.

Vendors have products to sell.

Organisations have reputations to defend.

Political leaders fear missed threats.

Officials may be punished more visibly for a false negative than for a dispersed population of false positives.

Secrecy can make external correction difficult.

Nobody particularly enjoys announcing that the expensive machine has spent three years confidently measuring the wrong thing.

But:

> **The model's hunger is not a validation metric.**

More data cannot establish that the underlying conceptual model is correct.

See [🩸 Feeding The System Instead Of Stopping It](./🩸_feeding_the_system_instead_of_stopping_it.md).

## 🌀 The Escalation Loop

The pathway can be represented simply:

```text
political or institutional pressure
                ↓
     demand for greater certainty
                ↓
       data/category expansion
                ↓
       more classifications
                ↓
         more intervention
                ↓
       behaviour changes
                ↓
        new observations
                ↓
apparent confirmation or unexplained error
                ↓
      demand for still more data
                ↺
```

But the loop is not inevitable.

There is another route:

```text
unexplained error
       ↓
review assumptions
       ↓
restore uncertainty
       ↓
correct / narrow / separate
       ↓
reduce / suspend / stop
```

Escalation is a choice made through institutions, rules, incentives, and decisions.

It is not a law of nature.

## 🔭 What This Branch Does Not Establish

This branch does not establish that:

- every administrative system expands during crisis;
- every government maintains a diaspora-management database;
- every risk model becomes coercive;
- every preventive intervention is illegitimate;
- every classification error is politically motivated;
- every unusual relationship pattern reflects state intervention;
- every foreign-state security activity constitutes transnational repression;
- every domestic and foreign capability is legally or morally equivalent;
- every increase in data collection produces repression;
- any particular institution has followed the pathway described here.

The branch describes an escalation model.

A particular case still requires evidence of:

1. the underlying capability;
2. relevant access;
3. actual implementation;
4. the actors responsible;
5. the relationship between implementation and observed consequences.

The smallest supportable claim comes first.

## 🥀 Into Audrey II

*When The Pot Gets Too Small* asks what happens when institutions respond to uncertainty by expanding the system.

The next problem is deeper.

What if the failure is not caused by insufficient data?

What if the thing being modelled remains unstable because human relationships involve context, loyalty, fear, trust, conflict, humour, attraction, solidarity, grief, courage, changing circumstances, and choices that cannot be reduced to stable variables merely by observing them more closely?

At that point, giving the plant a larger pot does not solve the underlying problem.

It simply gives the plant more room to grow.

Continue to [🥀 The Audrey II Problem](../🥀_The_Audrey_II_Problem/) — *why increasing data collection cannot make human relationships reliably predictable, and why model failure may increase institutional appetite rather than reduce confidence*.
