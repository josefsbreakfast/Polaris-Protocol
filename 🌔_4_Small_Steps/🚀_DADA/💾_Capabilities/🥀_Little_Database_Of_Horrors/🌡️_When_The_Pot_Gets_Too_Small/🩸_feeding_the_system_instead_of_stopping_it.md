# 🩸 Feeding The System Instead Of Stopping It
**First created:** 2026-07-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-25  
*How institutional failure can become the justification for further collection, integration, procurement, and intervention — when the safer response may be review, narrowing, deletion, suspension, or closure.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The system has a problem.

It cannot predict people reliably enough.

Its categories have widened.

False positives have multiplied.

Contradictory evidence has become difficult to interpret.

Its own interventions have changed the environment it is trying to measure.

At this point, an institution has a choice.

It can ask:

> Is the system still fit for purpose?

Or it can ask:

> What else can we feed it?

More data.

More sources.

More relationships.

More historical records.

More contractors.

More sharing.

More surveillance.

More categories.

More intervention.

This is the final escalation mechanism in **When The Pot Gets Too Small**.

The system's failure to model reality becomes evidence that the model does not yet contain enough reality.

> **The model's hunger is not a validation metric.**

Sometimes the correct response to poor performance is better information.

Sometimes it is a better model.

Sometimes it is a narrower question.

And sometimes the correct response is to stop.

---

## 🌱 Expansion Can Look Like Repair

Institutional expansion rarely presents itself as:

> Our system is failing, so we have decided to make the failure larger.

It sounds more reasonable.

There are information gaps.

Systems are fragmented.

Agencies cannot see the whole picture.

Legacy databases do not communicate.

Important relationships are missing.

Records are stale.

The model needs richer context.

Those can all be genuine problems.

Integration can improve public administration.

Data quality matters.

Poor information sharing can cause serious failures.

But the existence of a gap does not prove that filling it will solve the underlying problem.

The key diagnostic question is:

> **Is the system failing because relevant information is missing, or because the thing it is being asked to infer is not reliably inferable from the available kind of information?**

Those are very different failures.

Only one is necessarily solved by feeding the system.

---

## 🧠 Human Unpredictability Is Not A Data Defect

People change their minds.

They surprise friends.

They leave organisations.

They form unexpected loyalties.

They refuse pressure.

They succumb to pressure.

They fall in love.

They fall out.

They become politically active.

They become exhausted.

They behave differently in crisis than they did at rest.

A sufficiently rich database may improve understanding of some of those processes.

It will not abolish contingency.

That matters because an institution can mistake irreducible uncertainty for incomplete collection.

The resulting logic is seductive:

> We failed to predict the relationship because we lacked communications data.

Then:

> We failed because we lacked location.

Then:

> We lacked family context.

Then:

> We lacked financial context.

Then:

> We lacked platform data.

Then:

> We lacked community intelligence.

At each stage, the next source promises to reveal the missing variable.

Eventually the database knows a remarkable amount about the person's life.

The person remains a person.

## 🪴 Audrey II Is A Governance Problem

The Little Shop metaphor becomes most useful here.

Audrey II does not merely consume.

Its appetite restructures the environment around it.

The institution begins organising itself around feeding the system.

New workflows appear.

New data-sharing agreements are signed.

New reporting duties are created.

New contractors are procured.

New categories are standardised.

Staff learn to produce the information the system expects.

Success becomes associated with:

- fuller coverage;
- more records;
- fewer gaps;
- more connections;
- more alerts;
- greater integration.

The question:

> Should this system know this?

is displaced by:

> Why does the system not know this yet?

That is a governance inversion.

Data minimisation begins to look like deficiency.

Institutional restraint begins to look like blindness.

Privacy becomes an obstacle to model performance.

The plant has become the customer.

---

## 💾 Data Integration Creates New Capability

Joining datasets does more than make existing information easier to search.

It can create qualitatively new knowledge.

Individually:

- travel data show movement;
- financial data show transfers;
- communications metadata show contact;
- employment data show institutional position;
- event records show attendance.

Joined together, they can reveal:

- recurring relationships;
- routines;
- dependencies;
- intermediaries;
- organisational roles;
- cross-border networks.

This is why integration requires its own justification.

> **Five lawful collections do not automatically make every inference produced by joining them lawful, necessary, accurate, or proportionate.**

Purpose limitation matters precisely because combination changes capability.

The question is not only whether the institution may possess each ingredient.

It is what the institution is permitted to cook.

---

## 🏗️ Procurement Can Lock In The Appetite

Once institutions buy infrastructure, stopping becomes harder.

Contracts create:

- sunk costs;
- trained staff;
- organisational dependencies;
- renewal cycles;
- vendor relationships;
- integration work;
- political expectations.

A contractor may reasonably propose:

- additional modules;
- richer datasets;
- new analytical features;
- expanded coverage;
- improved prediction.

Each addition can appear incremental.

The architecture becomes difficult to unwind because no single procurement decision created the whole system.

This is another route by which capability can emerge without a single explicit decision to build:

> a database for managing politically consequential relationships.

The final capability may be the accumulated result of many individually narrower decisions.

---

## 🧾 The Audit Trail Can Become An Expansion Trail

After a failure, reviews often identify missing information.

That can be correct.

But institutional learning should distinguish between:

> we had relevant information and failed to use it;

> relevant information existed elsewhere and lawful sharing would have helped;

> the information did not exist;

and:

> the outcome was not reliably predictable.

If those distinctions collapse, every review can recommend more collection.

A missed event becomes proof of a gap.

The gap becomes proof of insufficient integration.

Integration produces more signals.

More signals produce more ambiguous cases.

Ambiguous cases produce demand for better discrimination.

Better discrimination produces demand for more data.

The review mechanism has become a ratchet.

---

## 🚨 False Negatives Are Visible. False Positives Disperse.

This creates an important political asymmetry.

A missed serious threat may produce:

- deaths;
- scandal;
- inquiries;
- headlines;
- ministerial pressure;
- institutional blame.

False positives are often quieter.

Their costs may be distributed across many people as:

- delay;
- additional scrutiny;
- lost opportunities;
- damaged relationships;
- repeated referrals;
- financial friction;
- reputational harm;
- administrative exhaustion.

No single case necessarily produces the same institutional shock.

So the incentive can be:

> tolerate many false positives to avoid one visible false negative.

Sometimes that trade-off is defensible.

But it must be explicit.

Otherwise the harms of overclassification disappear from the performance measure.

> **A system cannot be evaluated only by the disasters it might have prevented.**

It must also be evaluated by the ordinary harm it actually produces.

---

## 📊 More Alerts Can Mean A Worse System

Institutional dashboards often reward countable activity.

The system processed more records.

Generated more alerts.

Mapped more networks.

Made more referrals.

Completed more interventions.

Those numbers may demonstrate workload.

They do not necessarily demonstrate accuracy or benefit.

A model that generates twice as many alerts may have:

- improved detection;
- lowered thresholds;
- duplicated signals;
- become noisier;
- shifted costs onto human reviewers.

Useful evaluation therefore needs measures such as:

- substantiation;
- false-positive burden;
- correction rates;
- time to removal;
- downstream consequences;
- discriminatory impact;
- independent corroboration;
- cases where the model appropriately recommended no action.

Sometimes success is the system deciding:

> nothing needs to happen.

A dashboard should be able to count restraint.

---

## 🧱 Integration Can Make Correction Harder

More connected systems create another problem.

Errors travel.

A wrong record in one database can become:

- an intelligence lead;
- a risk marker;
- a financial concern;
- an administrative warning;
- a relationship edge.

Correction then has to travel through the same network.

Often it does not.

The original record is corrected.

A derived score remains.

A copied entry survives.

An external partner retains the old version.

A contractor's model has already ingested it.

The institution has solved the source error without repairing the consequences.

This produces an important governance principle:

> **Do not build information-sharing routes that can propagate suspicion faster than they can propagate correction.**

A system too interconnected to correct safely may be too interconnected to operate safely.

---

## 🩸 Crisis Makes Feeding Politically Easy

The pressure conditions examined throughout this branch make expansion particularly attractive.

During:

- war;
- terrorism;
- sanctions;
- protest;
- elections;
- succession crises;
- hostile-state activity;
- diplomatic rupture;

leaders may reasonably demand rapid action.

Emergency procurement becomes easier.

Data-sharing barriers may be reconsidered.

Thresholds may fall.

Temporary powers may be introduced.

The problem is not that emergency action is always illegitimate.

It is that emergency expansion can survive the emergency.

The system acquires:

- new inputs;
- new users;
- new purposes;
- new partners.

When pressure falls, those additions may remain.

The pot never becomes smaller again.

---

## 🌍 Every Capital Has Its Own Version Of This Temptation

This is not a pathology belonging only to states Britain dislikes.

Different political systems have very different:

- legal constraints;
- oversight;
- surveillance powers;
- records of repression;
- judicial independence;
- democratic accountability.

Those differences matter.

But the institutional temptation is widely recognisable:

> a serious threat exists;

> officials fear missing it;

> available information is incomplete;

> technology promises greater legibility;

> more integration appears safer than less.

A government may condemn an adversary for building intrusive systems while describing a comparable domestic impulse through more comfortable language.

An allied state may receive softer scrutiny than an adversary.

A democratic government may possess lawful powers that a foreign authoritarian state does not.

Those are reasons for careful comparison, not reasons to abandon comparison.

The consistent questions remain:

- What is being collected?
- Under what authority?
- For what purpose?
- What inference is being made?
- What consequence follows?
- What safeguards exist?
- Who can challenge error?
- When does the system stop?

The flags change.

The governance questions travel remarkably well.

---

## 🚓 Law Enforcement Cannot Solve Every Security Problem

The earlier nodes have repeatedly encountered the seam between:

- intelligence;
- policing;
- regulation;
- administrative action;
- diplomacy;
- security policy.

A state may believe that harmful conduct is occurring without possessing evidence that can support prosecution.

That does not mean nothing can lawfully be done.

Nor does it mean every preventive intervention is justified.

The danger appears when inability to prosecute becomes a reason to build increasingly expansive administrative substitutes.

If criminal proof is unavailable, the system may instead rely on:

- risk classifications;
- access restrictions;
- regulatory pressure;
- immigration powers;
- financial scrutiny;
- safeguarding routes;
- reputational warnings.

Some may be lawful and necessary.

But the lower evidential requirements of administrative action should not become a mechanism for permanently routing around the protections attached to criminal accusation.

> **“We cannot prosecute this” and “therefore we should know more about everybody around it” are not the same proposition.**

---

## ⚖️ Lawful Is Not The End Of The Question

A system can be legally authorised and still deserve scrutiny.

The relevant questions may include:

- Is the use necessary?
- Is it proportionate?
- Is the inference accurate enough for the consequence?
- Is the purpose sufficiently specific?
- Are less intrusive alternatives available?
- Is discrimination being monitored?
- Is there meaningful oversight?
- Can the person obtain correction or remedy?
- Does the system remain within the purpose for which it was created?

Likewise, conduct may be troubling without being criminal.

And conduct by a foreign state may engage different legal rules from similar information activity by domestic authorities.

So the cluster should preserve several distinct categories:

- lawful;
- unlawful;
- legitimate;
- proportionate;
- abusive;
- prosecutable;
- actionable through other legal routes;
- politically tolerated.

Collapsing them makes both security analysis and rights analysis worse.

---

## 🛑 Stopping Is An Active Capability

Institutions often discuss capabilities in terms of what they can do.

A mature institution also needs the capability **not** to do something.

Stopping requires infrastructure.

Someone must be authorised to say:

- no;
- enough;
- delete it;
- separate those records;
- lower the classification;
- stop sharing;
- end the pilot;
- do not renew the contract;
- suspend the model;
- close the programme.

Without those powers, restraint exists only rhetorically.

A system that can expand automatically but can contract only through extraordinary intervention has expansion built into its governance.

---

## 🧹 Narrowing Is Not Failure

Stopping does not always mean destroying the entire system.

Possible responses include:

### Correct

Repair inaccurate data.

### Separate

Prevent records gathered for one purpose from silently feeding another.

### Narrow

Reduce the population, variables, graph depth, or decisions covered.

### Raise thresholds

Require stronger evidence before consequential action.

### Add corroboration

Prevent model outputs from becoming self-validating.

### Expire

Remove stale classifications and relationships.

### Delete

Remove information no longer justified.

### Suspend

Stop use while harms or performance are reviewed.

### Close

End a system whose risks cannot be made proportionate to its benefits.

The key is that all of these options must be institutionally imaginable.

If every failure leads only to expansion, review is not functioning.

---

## 🧯 Stopping Rules Should Exist Before Crisis

It is difficult to invent restraint while an institution is frightened.

Stopping rules are therefore strongest when designed in advance.

Possible triggers include:

- repeated false positives;
- inability to explain outputs;
- discriminatory impact;
- purpose creep;
- failure to propagate corrections;
- use of intimate relationship data without sufficient justification;
- coercive action based primarily on weak association;
- model performance below a defined threshold;
- political manipulation of categories;
- repeated failure of independent validation;
- evidence that intervention is creating the behaviour being scored;
- inability to identify disconfirming evidence;
- inability to provide meaningful remedy.

The point of a stopping rule is not to guarantee closure.

It is to guarantee that continuation itself requires justification.

## 🪓 Do Not Feed The Model

This branch has now followed the full escalation sequence.

The system exists at rest.

Pressure arrives.

Internal and external shocks increase demand.

Officials want stronger prediction.

Categories widen.

False positives grow.

Contradiction becomes concealment.

Intervention changes behaviour.

The model begins creating some of its own evidence.

Finally, the resulting confusion is offered as justification for further collection.

That is the moment to interrupt the loop.

The institution must be able to ask:

> What if the missing variable is not more data?

> What if the category is wrong?

> What if the model is being asked an impossible question?

> What if our intervention is contaminating the evidence?

> What if the system is producing more harm than useful knowledge?

> What if we should stop feeding it?

The correct security response is not always maximum visibility.

Sometimes security depends upon knowing which systems should not be allowed to grow.

---

## 🔎 What Would We Look For?

Signs that institutional failure is producing further feeding may include:

- repeated scope expansion after poor performance;
- new data sources added without evidence that they address the identified failure;
- more integration following ambiguous rather than clearly informative results;
- emergency sharing arrangements becoming permanent;
- procurement renewal justified primarily by previous investment;
- success measured by coverage, alerts, or referrals rather than accuracy and outcomes;
- false-positive harms absent from evaluation;
- correction mechanisms weaker than sharing mechanisms;
- derived records surviving correction of source data;
- purpose limitation repeatedly broadened;
- intimate or associational data added because other variables failed to predict behaviour;
- no documented threshold for suspension or closure;
- independent reviewers unable to determine what would cause the programme to end.

None proves malign intent.

A system can become unsafe through ordinary incentives, fragmented responsibility, fear of failure, and accumulated institutional momentum.

That is why stopping has to be designed.

---

## 🧭 The Stop Test

Before adding another source, variable, partner, or power, ask:

1. What specific failure are we trying to correct?
2. Is that failure caused by missing information?
3. What evidence shows the proposed new data would address it?
4. Could the problem instead be the category, model, or question?
5. What new capability will integration create?
6. Is that capability necessary for the stated purpose?
7. What new false positives are expected?
8. What harms can those false positives produce?
9. Are correction and deletion able to travel as far as suspicion?
10. Are post-intervention effects contaminating the evidence?
11. Are we measuring accuracy or merely activity?
12. Are emergency measures becoming permanent?
13. Is sunk cost affecting the decision?
14. Is a contractor's proposed expansion independently justified?
15. Could the same purpose be achieved with less data?
16. What data should be removed rather than added?
17. What would justify narrowing the system?
18. What would justify suspension?
19. What would justify closure?
20. Who has the authority to stop feeding it?

If nobody owns question twenty, the system has a governance defect before the next byte is collected.

## 🌡️ Closing The Pot

**When The Pot Gets Too Small** is not an argument that stressed institutions inevitably become authoritarian.

It is an escalation model.

It shows how reasonable pressures can interact:

> serious threat  
> ↓  
> demand for prediction  
> ↓  
> wider categories  
> ↓  
> more false positives  
> ↓  
> contradictory evidence  
> ↓  
> concealment logic  
> ↓  
> intervention  
> ↓  
> behavioural feedback  
> ↓  
> more confusing data  
> ↓  
> demand for more collection

No individual step requires a grand design.

That is precisely the problem.

The system can grow through locally defensible decisions until its overall capability, error profile, and social consequences look very different from the project that began.

The answer is not to pretend states do not need intelligence, policing, sanctions enforcement, safeguarding, or protection against foreign interference.

The answer is to build institutions capable of recognising when:

> **more capability is no longer producing more knowledge.**

At that point, restraint is not passivity.

It is system competence.
