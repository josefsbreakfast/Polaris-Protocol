# 📈 Demanding More Certainty From A Bad Model
**First created:** 2026-07-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-25  
*What happens when institutions respond to irreducible uncertainty by demanding stronger predictions from systems whose underlying evidence has not become stronger.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The crisis has arrived.

The system has more data.

More agencies are sharing.

More relationships are visible.

More people are asking questions.

And the answer is still:

> We do not know.

This can be intolerable to an institution that has been told to prevent the next attack, anticipate the next protest, identify the next foreign-interference route, predict the next defection, stop the next sanctions breach, or explain why the last event was missed.

So the demand changes.

Not:

> What does the evidence support?

But:

> Can you give us a clearer answer?

The model is asked to become more certain.

The evidence has not necessarily become more certain with it.

That distinction is the centre of this node.

> **A demand for certainty is not evidence that certainty is available.**

---

## 🧮 Prediction Is Not Observation With Better Graphics

Databases are very good at storing things.

Analytical systems can be very good at finding patterns.

Graph analysis can identify connections that would be difficult for a person to see manually.

Statistical models can help allocate attention.

None of that makes prediction equivalent to observation.

A system may know:

- that two people communicate;
- that somebody travelled;
- that money moved;
- that two accounts interacted;
- that several people attended the same event;
- that a person has particular affiliations;
- that a pattern resembles previous cases.

It may still not know:

- what the relationship means;
- what somebody believes;
- what they intend;
- whether they will act;
- whether another person will influence them;
- whether a relationship will strengthen or collapse;
- whether behaviour observed in one context will generalise to another.

The gap between those two lists is not simply missing data.

Some of it is uncertainty inherent to human behaviour.

---

## 🌡️ Pressure Changes The Question Before It Changes The Evidence

Under ordinary conditions, an analyst may be allowed to say:

> possible;

> low confidence;

> insufficient information;

> competing explanations remain;

> monitor and review.

Under pressure, those answers can feel institutionally unsatisfying.

A minister wants assurance.

A commander wants a threat picture.

A police leader wants deployment decisions.

A regulator wants a priority list.

A border authority wants a flag.

A safeguarding body wants to know whether intervention is required.

A contractor may therefore be asked to improve:

- confidence;
- sensitivity;
- coverage;
- prioritisation;
- early warning;
- predictive accuracy.

Some improvements may be genuine.

Better data can correct bad records.

Better methods can reduce error.

Human review can add context.

The problem begins when the requested improvement is not:

> make the model better

but effectively:

> **make the uncertainty disappear.**

---

## 🎛️ A Dashboard Can Hide The Argument

Uncertainty is awkward to display.

A dashboard prefers:

- categories;
- colours;
- rankings;
- scores;
- flags;
- thresholds.

A messy analytical proposition such as:

> There are several possible explanations for this pattern, the underlying records are incomplete, the relationship is poorly understood, and the observed association may be incidental

can become:

> **Risk: 72**

The number looks precise.

The underlying world has not necessarily become precise with it.

This is not a criticism of quantitative analysis itself.

Quantification can make assumptions explicit and comparisons more disciplined.

The problem is **false precision**.

A score can compress uncertainty so effectively that downstream users forget the uncertainty existed.

> **The interface may be cleaner than the evidence.**

---

## 📊 Confidence Is Not Probability Is Not Consequence

Several concepts are especially easy to collapse.

### Confidence

How strongly does the analyst or system support the assessment?

### Probability

How likely is the predicted event?

### Severity

How harmful would the event be if it occurred?

### Priority

How urgently should the institution examine it?

### Consequence

What action is permitted because of the output?

These are not interchangeable.

A low-probability event may deserve attention because its consequences would be catastrophic.

A high-priority case may still contain substantial uncertainty.

A low-confidence intelligence assessment may justify further investigation without justifying coercive action.

A severe possible outcome does not make the underlying prediction more likely.

Under pressure, however, institutions can slide from:

> the consequence would be severe

to:

> therefore we cannot afford to treat the prediction as uncertain.

That is a decision about risk tolerance.

It is not an improvement in evidence.

---

## 🚨 The Missed-Threat Ratchet

After a failure, institutions are often asked:

> Why did you not see this coming?

Sometimes the answer is that the warning signs existed and were mishandled.

That should be investigated.

But sometimes hindsight creates a misleading impression that the outcome was obvious beforehand.

Once an event has occurred, the path towards it becomes easier to narrate.

Signals that were previously ambiguous acquire meaning retrospectively.

The temptation is then to build a system capable of finding those signals earlier next time.

That sounds sensible.

The problem is that many people who never produce the feared outcome may display the same signals.

So the system becomes more sensitive.

It finds more cases.

The new cases create workload.

The institution then needs better prioritisation.

More variables are added.

More scoring occurs.

More relationships are mapped.

The model becomes larger because the last model failed to predict an event that may never have been reliably predictable from the available evidence.

This is the missed-threat ratchet:

> **failure → demand for prediction → wider collection → more signals → more classifications → more demand for prediction.**

---

## 🔭 Rare Events Produce Difficult Models

Many security systems are trying to predict relatively rare outcomes.

That creates a basic problem.

Even a model that appears reasonably accurate can produce many false positives when the event being predicted is uncommon.

Suppose a system examines a very large population for a rare harmful event.

If the model flags even a small percentage of innocent cases incorrectly, the number of false positives can greatly exceed the number of true cases.

The practical result is not merely statistical untidiness.

People may experience:

- investigation;
- delay;
- scrutiny;
- reputational damage;
- restricted access;
- additional monitoring;
- repeated referral.

The rarer the event, the more carefully institutions need to distinguish:

> **good at identifying some true cases**

from:

> **good enough to justify consequences for everyone it flags.**

A model can be useful for triage without being reliable enough for punishment.

---

## 🕸️ Relationships Multiply The Variables

Relationship analysis makes the certainty problem harder.

A person can have hundreds or thousands of direct and indirect connections.

A system may therefore generate enormous numbers of possible associations.

If:

> A knows B,

and:

> B knows C,

the system can represent a path from A to C.

That does not establish that A knows C.

It certainly does not establish shared intent.

As graph depth expands, the number of technically identifiable relationships can increase dramatically while the meaning of each connection becomes weaker.

Under pressure, however, the institution may want:

- more hops;
- more connectors;
- more hidden networks;
- more predicted influence;
- more indirect association.

The graph grows.

The epistemic quality of the edge does not necessarily grow with it.

> **A larger graph can produce more apparent knowledge while increasing the distance from direct observation.**

---

## 🧠 Human Meaning Is A Hard Variable

The Audrey II problem begins to appear here.

A system may be able to model:

- frequency of contact;
- duration;
- co-location;
- shared membership;
- financial exchange;
- mutual contacts;
- public interaction.

But relationships are not reducible to those measures.

Frequent communication may mean intimacy.

It may mean conflict.

Money may mean support.

It may mean debt.

Co-location may mean collaboration.

It may mean attendance at the same conference.

Silence may mean estrangement.

It may mean moving to another platform.

A person may be influential because they are trusted.

That trust may not be visible in any dataset.

Another person may appear central because everyone is arguing with them.

The graph can measure structure more easily than meaning.

If an institution demands certainty about meaning, the temptation is to substitute measurable proxies.

That is where bias can enter very quietly.

---

## 🎭 Proxies Become Attractive When The Desired Variable Is Invisible

Suppose the institution wants to know:

> Who is likely to become politically consequential?

There may be no direct field for that.

So the model uses proxies:

- number of contacts;
- public reach;
- profession;
- education;
- income;
- institutional access;
- travel;
- political participation;
- family connections;
- community position;
- previous activism.

Some of those variables may genuinely improve prediction.

They may also encode:

- class;
- ethnicity;
- religion;
- disability;
- geography;
- gender;
- migration history;
- community prejudice;
- historical policing patterns.

A model can therefore reproduce social assumptions while presenting them as technical outputs.

The important question is not merely:

> Is this variable correlated with the outcome?

It is:

> **What causal or operational meaning are we assigning to that correlation, and what consequence are we allowing it to support?**

---

## 🚓 Intelligence Can Become Too Clean On The Way Downstream

The policing and intelligence seam matters again.

An intelligence analyst may understand that a proposition is:

- tentative;
- source-dependent;
- contested;
- contextual;
- one hypothesis among several.

A downstream administrative user may receive:

> risk marker present.

The caveat has disappeared.

This can happen without misconduct.

Systems are built to simplify information for decision-makers.

But simplification becomes dangerous when it removes the information needed to understand uncertainty.

A useful design principle is therefore:

> **uncertainty must travel with the claim.**

Provenance should travel with it too.

Otherwise repeated database entries can make a weak proposition appear stronger simply because it is widely distributed.

---

## 🔁 Correlation Can Become Self-Reinforcing

Predictive systems are often trained or calibrated using historical data.

But historical data are records of institutions as well as records of populations.

If one community has historically received more police attention, the system may contain more recorded incidents from that community.

If one political group has been more heavily monitored, the database may contain more associations involving that group.

If one neighbourhood has been searched more often, more offences may have been detected there.

The model can then learn:

> more recorded activity here = more risk here.

Deployment follows.

More activity is recorded.

The original pattern becomes stronger.

The system has not necessarily discovered an underlying truth.

It may partly be learning the history of where institutions chose to look.

This is one route by which:

> **measurement becomes intervention, and intervention becomes future training data.**

---

## 📈 “Give Us More Data” Is Not Always The Correct Repair

When model performance is poor, collecting more data can be useful.

Missing records can matter.

Small samples can distort.

Better data quality can improve outcomes.

But more data do not solve every failure.

The problem may instead be:

- the target variable is badly defined;
- the category is politically unstable;
- the proxy is discriminatory;
- the relationship is being misunderstood;
- the underlying event is too rare;
- the causal mechanism is unknown;
- the data are stale;
- records have been merged incorrectly;
- behaviour has changed;
- the model is being used outside its original context;
- the question itself is not reliably predictable.

In those cases, feeding the model can enlarge the system without solving the epistemic problem.

> **More information is not the same thing as a better question.**

---

## 🪴 The Pot Metaphor Becomes Literal

The pot is the model.

Reality no longer fits comfortably inside it.

Officials have several possible responses.

They can say:

> The model has limits.

They can narrow its use.

They can reduce the consequences attached to uncertain outputs.

They can require stronger corroboration.

They can redesign the categories.

They can stop using it for the particular decision.

Or they can enlarge the pot.

Add more people.

Add more data.

Add more relationships.

Add more categories.

Add more historical records.

Add more indirect associations.

Add more predictive variables.

The model now contains more of the world.

It may still not understand the world any better.

---

## ⚖️ The Legal Question Changes With The Consequence

Uncertain prediction can be legally and ethically tolerable for some purposes and unacceptable for others.

A weak indicator might reasonably support:

> look at this manually.

It may be much harder to justify:

> restrict this person's lawful association.

The relevant safeguards should therefore become stronger as consequences become more serious.

Questions include:

- Is the model advisory or determinative?
- Is human review meaningful?
- Can the person challenge the underlying data?
- Can the institution explain the classification?
- Are protected characteristics or close proxies involved?
- Is there corroborating evidence?
- Is the intervention necessary and proportionate?
- Does the system distinguish intelligence from proof?
- Are errors measurable?
- Are false positives reviewed?
- Can a classification expire?

The system should not be allowed to convert uncertainty into authority merely because the output is computational.

---

## 🧯 What A Healthy Institution Can Say

A resilient institution needs permission to produce answers such as:

> We cannot reliably predict this.

> The model is useful for triage but not for coercive decisions.

> The false-positive rate is too high.

> The available variables do not distinguish the groups well enough.

> The data do not support this inference.

> The relationship cannot be classified confidently.

> Additional collection is unlikely to solve the underlying problem.

> This output requires independent corroboration.

> We should stop using this system for this purpose.

Those are not admissions of institutional weakness.

They are evidence that the institution can distinguish uncertainty from failure.

A system becomes dangerous when every uncertainty is interpreted as a defect that more surveillance must repair.

---

## 🔎 What Would We Look For?

Evidence that an institution is demanding more certainty from a weak model might include:

- pressure for higher predictive confidence without better evidence;
- widening data collection after a missed event;
- increased graph depth;
- more proxy variables;
- expansion from triage to consequential decision-making;
- confidence caveats disappearing downstream;
- scores replacing qualitative uncertainty;
- repeated changes to thresholds;
- false-positive rates receiving little attention;
- calls for additional surveillance after model failure;
- contractors marketing stronger prediction from the same underlying data;
- poor model performance followed by scope expansion rather than review;
- retrospective claims that previous ambiguous signals were obvious;
- inability to specify what evidence would falsify a classification.

No single sign proves institutional abuse.

Together, they can indicate that uncertainty is being managed by expanding the apparatus rather than improving the inference.

---

## 🧭 Questions For The Model

Before attaching greater consequences to a predictive output, ask:

1. What exactly is being predicted?
2. How common is the predicted event?
3. What data support the prediction?
4. Which variables are direct observations?
5. Which are proxies?
6. Which are inferred relationships?
7. What does the score actually measure?
8. What does it not measure?
9. What is the false-positive rate?
10. What is the false-negative rate?
11. How does base rate affect those figures?
12. Has the model been validated for this population and purpose?
13. Are historical institutional biases embedded in the training or reference data?
14. Does uncertainty remain visible downstream?
15. Does provenance remain visible?
16. Is the model being used for triage, investigation, restriction, or punishment?
17. What independent evidence is required before action?
18. Can the subject correct erroneous data?
19. What observation would reduce the risk classification?
20. Is collecting more data actually capable of answering the underlying question?

Number nineteen becomes crucial in the next stage.

If contradictory evidence cannot reduce suspicion, the system is no longer merely making errors.

It is beginning to protect the model from correction.

---

## 🎯 The Next Expansion

Once institutions demand stronger answers from uncertain models, one apparent solution is to change the categories.

Include more people.

Lower the threshold.

Create intermediate risk groups.

Treat weak indicators cumulatively.

Expand the definition of relevant association.

The model catches more possible cases.

It also catches more people who will never produce the predicted outcome.
