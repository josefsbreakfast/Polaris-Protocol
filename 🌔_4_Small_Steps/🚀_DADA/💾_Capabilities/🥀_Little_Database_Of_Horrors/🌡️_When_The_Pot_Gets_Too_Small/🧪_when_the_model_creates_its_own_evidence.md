# 🧪 When The Model Creates Its Own Evidence
**First created:** 2026-07-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-25  
*How surveillance, classification, restriction, and social intervention can change behaviour — then feed those changes back into the system as apparent evidence that the original classification was correct.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The model predicts a problem.

The institution acts.

The person notices.

Their behaviour changes.

The model records the change.

Then comes the dangerous part:

> The changed behaviour is treated as evidence that the model was right.

This is not merely confirmation bias.

It is a feedback problem.

The system is no longer observing an environment untouched by itself.

It has entered the environment.

It may have:

- increased surveillance;
- restricted access;
- delayed travel;
- changed visibility;
- circulated warnings;
- contacted employers or institutions;
- increased police attention;
- altered financial treatment;
- pressured family or intermediaries;
- changed which opportunities are available;
- made ordinary relationships more costly.

People respond to those conditions.

They become cautious.

Angry.

Withdrawn.

More politically active.

Less trusting.

More private.

More dependent on a smaller network.

More likely to seek lawyers, journalists, campaigners, or other people already legible to the system.

The database then receives fresh data.

The plant says:

> See?

The crucial question is:

> **How much of the observed behaviour existed independently, and how much was produced or amplified by the intervention itself?**

---

## 🔁 Observation Changes The Environment

In some systems, measurement can be treated as relatively passive.

A thermometer does not normally cause the fever it records.

Social systems are different.

Surveillance can change:

- who speaks;
- what they say;
- where they meet;
- which platforms they use;
- who is willing to associate;
- how institutions treat them;
- how much trust exists;
- what risks people are prepared to take.

Intervention changes even more.

A person who knows that ordinary contact may be interpreted as suspicious may reduce ordinary contact.

A person repeatedly denied access may seek alternative routes.

A community subjected to intrusive scrutiny may become less willing to cooperate with authorities.

A person whose reputation has been damaged may become socially isolated.

Those outcomes are data.

But they are **post-intervention data**.

That distinction must survive.

---

## 🧫 The Model Has Contaminated Its Sample

Suppose a system predicts:

> Person A is becoming socially isolated and increasingly dependent on politically active contacts.

The institution then:

- warns people about A;
- restricts A's access to ordinary institutions;
- increases scrutiny around A's other relationships;
- makes professional or social opportunities harder to maintain.

Six months later, the data show:

- fewer ordinary contacts;
- greater reliance on political networks;
- increased grievance;
- more contact with lawyers and campaigners.

The prediction appears accurate.

But the observation is contaminated.

The institution helped produce the conditions it later measured.

This does not mean the original prediction was necessarily false.

It means the later behaviour cannot be treated as clean, independent validation of it.

> **An intervention cannot be silently removed from the causal history of the evidence it creates.**

---

## 🪤 Concealment Logic Makes The Loop Stronger

The preceding node examined what happens when error is reclassified as concealment.

Combine that with intervention and the system becomes especially difficult to correct.

The person changes behaviour because they are under scrutiny.

The model says:

> evasion.

The person seeks privacy.

The model says:

> concealment.

The person becomes angry.

The model says:

> escalation.

The person seeks legal advice.

The model says:

> network expansion.

The person warns friends.

The model says:

> coordination.

The person withdraws.

The model says:

> covert activity.

At each stage, a reasonable response to intervention can be translated back into the vocabulary of the original threat model.

The model does not merely survive contact with contradictory reality.

It begins converting its own effects into confirmation.

---

## 🚓 Policing Provides A Familiar Feedback Structure

Policing makes this dynamic relatively easy to see.

More enforcement in one place can produce:

- more stops;
- more searches;
- more recorded incidents;
- more intelligence entries;
- more detected offences;
- more known associations.

Those records can then justify continued attention to the same place or network.

Some of the detected activity is real.

The feedback problem is not that the records are invented.

It is that:

> **recorded prevalence is partly a function of where institutions chose to look.**

If one population is observed more intensely, it will often generate more institutional data.

A model trained on those records may then rediscover the previous enforcement decision as a risk pattern.

The system can mistake:

> the history of surveillance

for:

> the natural distribution of threat.

---

## 📣 Protest And Political Activity Can Be Produced By Pressure

Political systems create a particularly uncomfortable version of the loop.

A person or community may initially have limited political involvement.

Then they experience:

- surveillance;
- discrimination;
- administrative restriction;
- family pressure;
- reputational harm;
- police intervention;
- perceived injustice.

They respond by:

- organising;
- protesting;
- contacting journalists;
- seeking legal support;
- joining campaigns;
- documenting state conduct.

The system then observes increased political activity.

If political activity was already part of the risk model, the intervention appears to have confirmed the classification.

But the causal sequence may be:

> **classification → intervention → grievance → mobilisation.**

The model predicted mobilisation.

The institution helped create it.

That distinction is not academic.

It determines whether the system is detecting risk or manufacturing part of the behaviour it treats as risky.

---

## 🕸️ Social Graphs Can Be Rewired By The Intervention

Relationship models are especially vulnerable because intervention can change the network itself.

Suppose ordinary contacts withdraw after receiving warnings.

The person may become more dependent on:

- a lawyer;
- an activist;
- a journalist;
- a specialist support organisation;
- a small group of trusted friends.

The graph becomes narrower and more politically or institutionally concentrated.

A later analysis may conclude:

> The subject's network is unusually concentrated around politically consequential actors.

True.

But why?

Perhaps that network existed beforehand.

Perhaps the system helped remove the alternatives.

This is the relationship equivalent of measurement feedback:

> **the intervention prunes the graph, then treats the resulting shape as a natural property of the person.**

For the Little Database of Horrors, this matters enormously.

A system attempting to manage relationships can create the very dependency structures it later cites as evidence that relationship management was necessary.

---

## 🧱 Relationship Caps Can Produce Apparent Instability

Consider a system that repeatedly introduces friction when relationships become materially consequential.

A friendship remains easy while casual.

A professional relationship remains easy while peripheral.

A community connection remains easy while symbolic.

Then the relationship approaches:

- evidence exchange;
- legal support;
- employment;
- funding;
- institutional access;
- durable collaboration;
- public corroboration.

Friction increases.

The relationship breaks.

A database looking only at outcomes may record:

> subject has unstable relationships.

That description may be technically accurate.

It may be causally backwards.

If the system repeatedly raises the cost of consequential relationships, instability is partly an output of the management layer.

The plant prunes.

Then it records poor growth.

---

## 🔇 Visibility Intervention Produces Behavioural Data Too

Not every intervention is coercive in the traditional sense.

Platform ranking, recommendation, moderation, access, and visibility can change:

- who encounters whom;
- which ideas spread;
- which events gain attendance;
- who receives responses;
- which relationships form;
- how successful organising appears.

If visibility falls, a person may:

- post more often;
- change language;
- move platforms;
- contact people directly;
- become more provocative;
- abandon public communication.

Those adaptations can themselves become behavioural signals.

Again, the important question is provenance.

Was the observed pattern:

- spontaneous;
- a response to platform design;
- a response to moderation;
- a response to perceived suppression;
- some mixture of all four?

A system that influences visibility should not pretend the resulting behaviour emerged in a neutral environment.

---

## 💸 Administrative Friction Can Create The Risk Profile

The same dynamic can occur through ordinary administration.

Repeated:

- payment delays;
- account reviews;
- travel problems;
- employment difficulties;
- funding refusals;
- institutional exclusions;

may force a person towards:

- informal finance;
- alternative employers;
- non-mainstream institutions;
- unfamiliar travel routes;
- politically engaged support networks.

Those alternatives may then look less conventional to a risk model.

The person appears to be moving away from ordinary institutional channels.

They are.

But perhaps ordinary institutional channels have become unusually difficult to use.

> **A system can push somebody towards the margins and then score marginality as risk.**

That is one of the cleanest examples of a model creating its own evidence.

---

## 🧷 Family Pressure Can Produce The Contact Pattern It Predicts

Family and intimate networks create another loop.

If relatives are pressured, they may:

- reduce contact;
- communicate indirectly;
- become frightened;
- ask others to carry messages;
- avoid discussing sensitive subjects;
- change travel plans.

Those behaviours can resemble:

- compartmentation;
- covert communication;
- network restructuring;
- deliberate distancing.

Sometimes that resemblance will reflect actual security behaviour.

Sometimes it will reflect fear.

A system needs to distinguish:

> **behaviour generated by the underlying threat**

from:

> **behaviour generated by the system or coercive environment responding to the presumed threat.**

Without that distinction, pressure becomes evidentially productive.

---

## 🧠 Trauma, Stress And Exhaustion Are Not Neutral Outputs

Persistent surveillance or intervention can alter cognition and behaviour.

People under sustained pressure may become:

- hypervigilant;
- forgetful;
- inconsistent;
- irritable;
- withdrawn;
- suspicious;
- less organised;
- more reactive.

Institutions may encounter those behaviours later.

If the original model contains variables such as:

- instability;
- hostility;
- inconsistency;
- poor cooperation;
- unusual vigilance;

the effects of institutional treatment can increase the person's apparent risk profile.

This is not a reason to infer a particular cause from distress.

It is a reason to preserve temporal order.

> What was present before intervention?

> What appeared afterwards?

> What else was happening?

Without chronology, effect can be mistaken for cause.

---

## 🧮 Post-Treatment Variables

There is a useful concept from causal inference here.

If an intervention has occurred, variables measured afterwards may have been affected by that intervention.

Treating them as though they were independent baseline predictors can distort analysis.

In plain language:

> **Do not use the consequences of your own action as though they were untouched evidence for why you acted.**

For an institutional system, that means recording:

- when classification occurred;
- when surveillance increased;
- when restrictions began;
- when warnings were issued;
- when social or administrative consequences appeared;
- when the later behaviour was observed.

Temporal ordering is not decorative metadata.

It is part of the causal argument.

---

## 🧪 Natural Experiments Become Extremely Valuable

One way to investigate feedback is comparison.

Consider similar people or relationships where:

- one received intervention and one did not;
- one institution had access to the risk marker and another did not;
- one period occurred before a policy change and another after;
- one relationship became consequential and another remained casual.

If the supposedly diagnostic behaviour appears mainly after intervention, that matters.

It does not prove causation automatically.

But it helps distinguish:

> pre-existing signal

from:

> possible system effect.

This is why the [🧪 Investigation Kit](../🧪_Investigation_Kit/) emphasises chronology, comparison, alternative explanations, and natural experiments.

The investigation should be more causally disciplined than the model it is examining.

---

## 🪞 Goodhart Arrives At The Flower Shop

There is another problem.

Once a measure becomes a target, institutions and subjects adapt around it.

If success means:

> reduce the number of visible risky contacts,

the easiest administrative outcome may be fewer visible contacts.

That does not necessarily mean risk fell.

It may mean:

- relationships moved;
- reporting declined;
- people disengaged;
- data quality worsened;
- the institution successfully isolated somebody.

Likewise, if success is measured by:

- alerts generated;
- referrals made;
- networks identified;
- interventions completed;

the system may optimise activity rather than truth.

A dashboard can improve while the underlying social environment deteriorates.

The plant looks healthy.

The shop is on fire.

---

## ⚖️ Intervention Evidence Needs A Higher Standard

Once an institution has intervened, later evidence should be interpreted with greater causal care.

Questions include:

- Could the intervention itself explain this behaviour?
- Did the behaviour predate the intervention?
- Was the person aware of the intervention?
- Did third parties change their treatment of the person?
- Did access to ordinary routes narrow?
- Were family or professional relationships affected?
- Did the system create incentives for privacy or withdrawal?
- Is later behaviour being used as independent corroboration?
- Has an external reviewer examined the causal sequence?

This does not mean post-intervention evidence is unusable.

It means it is not context-free.

> **The system has become one of the variables.**

---

## 🧯 A Healthy Institution Marks Its Own Footprints

A well-governed system should record not only what the subject did.

It should record what the institution did first.

That includes:

- surveillance changes;
- contact;
- warnings;
- restrictions;
- referrals;
- information sharing;
- platform action;
- financial intervention;
- border action;
- safeguarding intervention;
- third-party notification.

Analysts should then be able to ask:

> Could our action have contributed to this outcome?

That question is uncomfortable.

It is also basic causal hygiene.

An institution that records only target behaviour and not institutional intervention creates a dataset structurally biased towards forgetting its own role.

---

## 🔎 What Would We Look For?

Possible signs of self-generated evidence include:

- risk indicators increasing after intervention;
- social isolation following warnings or access restrictions;
- political mobilisation following perceived institutional harm;
- greater reliance on specialist or activist networks after ordinary routes narrow;
- privacy behaviour increasing after surveillance becomes apparent;
- the same intervention being omitted from later causal analysis;
- enforcement intensity correlating with later recorded prevalence;
- model outputs trained on records produced by previous model-directed interventions;
- repeated relationship breakdown at similar institutional thresholds;
- post-intervention behaviour being described as independent confirmation;
- institutional metrics rewarding alerts or interventions rather than corrected predictions;
- no mechanism for separating baseline behaviour from behaviour after classification.

None of these establishes deliberate manipulation.

Feedback loops can arise through ordinary institutional behaviour.

That is precisely why they need to be designed for.

## 🧭 Causal Hygiene Checklist

Before treating later behaviour as confirmation, ask:

1. What was the original hypothesis?
2. What evidence existed before intervention?
3. When was the person classified?
4. What did the institution do next?
5. What did other institutions or people do as a result?
6. When did the allegedly confirming behaviour begin?
7. Did it exist beforehand?
8. Could the intervention plausibly have caused or amplified it?
9. Did surveillance intensity change the amount of recorded data?
10. Did ordinary routes to support, finance, travel, or association narrow?
11. Did the social graph change after warnings or restrictions?
12. Are post-intervention variables being treated as baseline predictors?
13. Is institutional action recorded alongside subject behaviour?
14. Are downstream analysts aware of the intervention history?
15. Is later evidence genuinely independent?
16. Are there comparable cases without intervention?
17. Did the model's own deployment affect the data used to validate it?
18. Could apparent improvement simply reflect people adapting to the metric?
19. What evidence would show that the intervention worsened the underlying problem?
20. Can the institution distinguish detecting a pattern from helping create it?

The final question is the heart of the node.

## 🩸 The Appetite Problem

At this point, the system has encountered several failures.

It was uncertain.

So more certainty was demanded.

The categories widened.

False positives increased.

Contradiction was reclassified as concealment.

Intervention changed behaviour.

The changed behaviour became new evidence.

Now the system appears to possess even more reasons to continue.

More data seem necessary to distinguish:

- genuine risk from intervention effects;
- real networks from adaptive behaviour;
- concealment from privacy;
- baseline relationships from relationships formed under pressure.

The plant is hungry because the system has made the environment harder to interpret.

The institutional choice is now stark.

One response is:

> collect more.

Another is:

> **stop and ask whether the system itself has become part of the problem.**
