# 🗳️ Elections, Protests And Succession Crises
**First created:** 2026-07-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-25  
*How internal political shocks can change the security meaning assigned to ordinary association, mobilisation, brokerage, legitimacy, and relationships that create political capacity.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Some political crises are frightening to governments because an external adversary has become stronger.

Others are frightening because the government is no longer certain what its own political environment will do.

An election becomes unexpectedly competitive.

A protest movement grows faster than expected.

A disputed result produces mass mobilisation.

A governing coalition fractures.

A president, monarch, party leader, military ruler, or revolutionary figure becomes ill, dies, resigns, is removed, or loses control of succession.

Elites begin defecting.

Institutions stop behaving predictably.

People who were previously separate discover that they can act together.

Under those conditions, a system designed to observe people can acquire a new question:

> **Who has the capacity to change the political situation?**

That question is wider than:

> Who has committed an offence?

It may include:

- organisers;
- connectors;
- journalists;
- lawyers;
- funders;
- religious leaders;
- trade and professional networks;
- diaspora figures;
- students;
- technical specialists;
- cultural figures;
- former insiders;
- relatives of powerful people;
- people capable of introducing one group to another.

The political significance of a relationship can therefore change without the relationship itself changing at all.

Yesterday, A knowing B was ordinary.

Today, A knowing B appears to create a bridge.

The people have not necessarily changed.

The state's interpretation of their capacity has.

---

## 🗳️ Elections Are Information Problems

Elections create uncertainty by design.

No government can know the result in advance without ceasing to have a meaningful election.

That uncertainty can be politically healthy.

It can also be institutionally uncomfortable.

A government may reasonably monitor:

- threats to candidates;
- electoral violence;
- foreign interference;
- unlawful financing;
- cyberattack;
- intimidation;
- disinformation operations;
- threats to election infrastructure.

Those are legitimate security concerns.

But election periods can also make lawful political relationships newly interesting.

Who funds whom?

Who can mobilise voters?

Who has media access?

Who can bring communities together?

Who can authenticate a story?

Who can expose misconduct?

Who has credibility outside the governing party's usual audience?

Who can connect domestic opposition to international attention?

The analytical danger is not election security itself.

It is the possibility that:

> **political effectiveness becomes difficult to distinguish from security relevance.**

A system designed to identify harmful influence can begin identifying influence.

Those are not the same category.

---

## 📣 Protest Turns Relationships Into Capacity

A protest is not merely a collection of individuals occupying the same place.

Movements depend upon relationships.

Someone knows the venue.

Someone understands the law.

Someone has a printer.

Someone can raise money.

Someone knows journalists.

Someone can provide medical support.

Someone can translate.

Someone can reach a religious community.

Someone understands digital security.

Someone has transport.

Someone can speak publicly.

Someone knows what happened last time.

Individually, these capacities may be mundane.

Connected, they can create political power.

That is precisely why relationship analysis can become attractive during protest.

A system may move from asking:

> Who attended?

to:

> Who organised?

then:

> Who enabled organisation?

then:

> Who connects this movement to other institutions?

then:

> Which relationships could make the movement more durable?

The network becomes interesting because it produces capacity.

But political capacity is not itself evidence of unlawful conduct.

A democracy in particular has to tolerate the uncomfortable fact that lawful association is often designed to make citizens harder for government to ignore.

---

## 🕸️ The Connector Problem

Highly connected people can look unusual in graph analysis.

They may bridge communities that otherwise have few direct links.

They may appear between:

- activists and journalists;
- communities and lawyers;
- diaspora groups and parliamentarians;
- protest movements and unions;
- researchers and campaigners;
- religious institutions and civil society;
- victims and investigators;
- local groups and international organisations.

In network terms, those positions can matter enormously.

In political life, they can also be completely ordinary.

A lawyer is supposed to connect people to legal institutions.

A journalist is supposed to connect information to publics.

An organiser is supposed to connect participants.

A representative is supposed to connect communities to government.

A diaspora advocate may naturally connect several jurisdictions.

So a central distinction is:

> **network importance is not equivalent to threat importance.**

A system under political stress can lose that distinction if its task becomes identifying relationships capable of changing outcomes.

---

## 🏛️ Succession Changes The Meaning Of Loyalty

Succession crises create a particularly unstable information environment.

Formal institutions may remain intact while everyone tries to work out:

- who will inherit authority;
- which faction will prevail;
- whether the military will remain loyal;
- whether security institutions will split;
- whether business elites will move;
- whether regional leaders will defect;
- whether foreign governments will recognise a challenger;
- whether exiles will return;
- whether protests will become organised opposition.

Relationships that appeared socially or professionally ordinary can suddenly be interpreted as indicators of future alignment.

A friendship becomes a factional link.

A family relationship becomes potential leverage.

A former colleague becomes a route to defection.

A foreign contact becomes possible external sponsorship.

A meeting becomes evidence of coalition.

This is fertile ground for overclassification because the thing being predicted — future loyalty — is inherently unstable.

People themselves may not yet know what they will do.

The database certainly does not acquire certainty merely because the palace would find certainty convenient.

---

## 🧮 From Dissent To Predicted Capacity

One of the most important category shifts during internal crisis is the move from evaluating conduct to evaluating capacity.

The system may stop asking only:

> What has this person done?

and begin asking:

> What could this person enable?

That can introduce variables such as:

- audience;
- credibility;
- professional expertise;
- financial independence;
- organisational experience;
- international access;
- institutional standing;
- legal knowledge;
- technical skill;
- cross-community trust;
- persistence;
- ability to document;
- ability to corroborate;
- ability to connect otherwise separate groups.

The person classified as consequential may not be the loudest dissident.

They may simply make other people more capable.

This matters to the *Little Database of Horrors* model because relationship risk can emerge precisely where capacities combine.

One person has evidence.

Another has a route to publication.

One has testimony.

Another has legal standing.

One has money.

Another has organisation.

One has local trust.

Another has international access.

The political significance lies in the connection.

That does not make the connection illegitimate.

---

## 🎯 Category Expansion During Internal Crisis

Internal shocks can widen categories in several directions.

### From actor to associate

Monitoring moves from people already of concern to people connected with them.

### From organiser to enabler

Attention expands from visible leadership to logistical, legal, financial, technical, or communicative support.

### From unlawful conduct to political relevance

People may become interesting because they can affect outcomes rather than because they are suspected of an offence.

### From current behaviour to future possibility

Prediction begins carrying more weight than observation.

### From organisation to ecosystem

Institutions may map not only a movement but the journalists, lawyers, families, donors, religious communities, academics, businesses, and diaspora networks around it.

Each expansion may have an intelligible rationale.

Together they can transform the object of analysis.

The system is no longer simply identifying harmful conduct.

It may be mapping the social infrastructure through which lawful political power is created.

## 🚓 Protest Policing And The Evidential Seam

Policing presents a particularly important boundary.

Police may legitimately need intelligence before a major protest.

They may need to assess:

- violence risk;
- public safety;
- routes;
- counter-protests;
- threats;
- criminal plans;
- vulnerable locations;
- likely numbers.

Operational planning cannot wait for offences to occur.

But information collected for public-order preparation can sit close to information about political participation.

That creates questions about:

- necessity;
- proportionality;
- retention;
- association;
- political profiling;
- information sharing;
- the distinction between criminality and lawful protest.

The key analytical question remains:

> **What evidential weight is the information being asked to carry?**

A person being relevant to operational planning does not establish that they present a criminal threat.

A person repeatedly attending protests does not by itself establish unlawful intent.

A relationship with an organiser does not establish participation in criminal conduct.

Under stress, however, these distinctions can become administratively inconvenient.

That is precisely when they matter most.

---

## ⚖️ Prevention Is Not Prosecution

Internal crisis also highlights the difference between what a state can monitor, what it can prevent, and what it can prosecute.

A government may possess lawful preventive powers that operate before criminal liability is established.

Police may investigate suspicion that ultimately produces no charge.

Administrative bodies may make decisions using standards different from the criminal courts.

Security institutions may hold intelligence that could never simply be presented as prosecutorial evidence.

That is not automatically improper.

But a person can experience serious consequences long before any criminal threshold is crossed.

So analysis should distinguish:

> **suspected**

from:

> **investigated**

from:

> **classified as risky**

from:

> **restricted**

from:

> **charged**

from:

> **convicted**.

If those states become rhetorically or administratively interchangeable, preventive systems can create a shadow form of punishment without the epistemic protections associated with prosecution.

## 🌍 The Democratic-State Temptation

Democratic states are not exempt from the underlying incentive.

They may have stronger constraints.

They may possess independent courts, opposition parties, investigative journalism, data-protection rules, parliamentary scrutiny, civil society, electoral accountability, and meaningful routes to challenge state action.

Those differences can materially alter both likelihood and severity of abuse.

But democracies still experience:

- terrorism;
- riots;
- foreign interference;
- polarisation;
- mass protest;
- election anxiety;
- leadership instability;
- public demands that officials prevent the next failure.

The relevant comparative question is therefore not:

> Do democracies behave exactly like authoritarian states?

They do not.

It is:

> **Which temptations are structurally shared, and which institutions actually prevent those temptations becoming coercive practice?**

That makes safeguards observable rather than ceremonial.

A democratic label is not itself a control mechanism.

Courts, law, oversight, correction, political competition, transparency, professional standards, and enforceable rights are.

## 🌍 What Do We Call It Abroad?

Internal political crisis also sharpens the foreign-state mirror.

British, European, American, and other democratic governments may condemn another state for:

- monitoring opposition abroad;
- mapping dissident networks;
- treating protest as extremism;
- pressuring activists' families;
- targeting journalists or lawyers;
- restricting political association;
- using embassies or community intermediaries to identify opposition.

Those condemnations may be entirely justified.

But they create a useful domestic question:

> **What exactly distinguishes the condemned mechanism from adjacent practices at home?**

Possible answers may include:

- lawful authority;
- narrower purpose;
- independent authorisation;
- stronger evidence;
- judicial oversight;
- prohibition on political direction;
- correction and appeal;
- transparency;
- lower coercive consequence;
- genuine connection to criminal or security threats.

Good.

Then those distinctions should be demonstrated.

If the only distinction available is:

> our government is legitimate and theirs is hostile,

the analysis has not yet done enough work.

---

## 🤝 Allies Do Not Remove The Question

The same discipline should apply to allied states.

A host government may have:

- security partnerships;
- diplomatic priorities;
- intelligence relationships;
- trade interests;
- military cooperation;
- migration agreements;
- domestic political reasons to preserve a relationship.

Those interests may affect how alleged foreign interference or transnational repression is described, investigated, or challenged.

That does not prove selective enforcement in any particular case.

It creates a question worth testing:

> **Would the same conduct receive the same scrutiny if the alleged state actor were an adversary?**

Again, the answer may legitimately be yes, no, or partly.

The important thing is to establish why.

Friendship between governments is not evidence about what happened to an individual.

Nor should hostility between governments be treated as proof.

---

## 🧷 Family, Community And Proxy Pressure

Internal political crises may also increase interest in people who are not themselves politically active.

Family relationships can reveal:

- identity;
- location;
- dependency;
- travel;
- financial routes;
- routes of communication;
- social legitimacy;
- possible leverage.

Community institutions may provide context that formal databases lack.

That creates both legitimate and dangerous possibilities.

A state may need community knowledge to protect people.

A repressive system may use the same kind of knowledge to identify pressure points.

The operational difference lies in purpose, authority, consent, consequence, and conduct.

This is why the question:

> **Who may safely know whom?**

becomes especially important during political instability.

The relationship graph can be used to understand vulnerability.

It can also be used to create it.

---

## 🪞 Political Stress Can Produce Circular Evidence

Once a movement or network is classified as concerning, ordinary reactions to state attention can become easy to misread.

People may:

- move conversations elsewhere;
- reduce public communication;
- seek legal advice;
- document encounters;
- warn friends;
- avoid institutions;
- become more politically active;
- become angrier;
- form stronger support networks.

Those behaviours may be rational responses to surveillance or pressure.

But a closed model can interpret them as:

- concealment;
- coordination;
- operational security;
- radicalisation;
- network consolidation.

The intervention has changed the behaviour.

The changed behaviour is then treated as independent evidence supporting the intervention.

That feedback problem becomes more important later in [🧪 When The Model Creates Its Own Evidence](./🧪_when_the_model_creates_its_own_evidence.md).

## 🔎 What Would We Look For?

Evidence of stress-related expansion around elections, protests, or succession crises might include:

- emergency or temporary powers;
- changes in protest-policing guidance;
- widened definitions of extremism or security concern;
- expanded watchlists;
- increased monitoring of lawful political activity;
- new social-media monitoring contracts;
- wider retention of protest intelligence;
- new data-sharing arrangements;
- increased diaspora monitoring;
- political direction of risk categories;
- documented family or proxy pressure;
- expanded embassy or consular activity;
- changes in border treatment;
- new restrictions on civil society;
- increased targeting of journalists or lawyers;
- parliamentary, judicial, regulatory, or oversight findings;
- documented changes before, during, and after the crisis.

The evidence should establish the narrow proposition first.

A new monitoring contract proves a new monitoring contract.

It does not, by itself, prove a programme of political repression.

---

## 🧯 What Would We Need To Rule Out?

Alternative explanations matter.

An increase in security activity around a protest or election may reflect:

- credible violence intelligence;
- a specific criminal investigation;
- foreign-state threats;
- previous disorder;
- threats against candidates;
- terrorism concerns;
- infrastructure protection;
- ordinary event planning;
- a genuine increase in relevant offending.

A strong analysis should ask whether the observed response tracks:

> **conduct and threat**

or instead tracks:

> **political identity, association, influence, or capacity**.

Mixed explanations are possible.

A legitimate security operation can still contain overbroad practices.

An intrusive capability can still be used proportionately in a particular case.

The purpose of comparison is not to force everything into one moral category.

It is to identify what the system is actually doing.

---

## 🧭 A Comparative Checklist

For each election, protest wave, or succession crisis, ask:

1. What was the political shock?
2. What security concerns were documented?
3. What was the pre-crisis baseline?
4. Which populations became newly interesting?
5. Did lawful political activity become a risk proxy?
6. Did relationship mapping expand?
7. Were connectors, enablers, or family members newly included?
8. Did thresholds change?
9. Did consequences attached to existing classifications change?
10. Were new institutions or contractors brought in?
11. Did information sharing widen?
12. Were emergency measures time-limited?
13. Did temporary capabilities persist?
14. What safeguards operated in practice?
15. What remedies existed?
16. What conduct would the same state condemn if performed abroad?
17. What genuinely distinguishes the domestic case?
18. Would an allied and adversarial foreign state receive comparable scrutiny?
19. What alternative explanations fit the evidence?
20. What would disprove the stronger interpretation?

The last question remains essential.

Political instability is exactly when unfalsifiable analysis becomes most seductive.

## 🌡️ The Pot During An Internal Shock

The system does not need to become omnipotent to become more consequential.

It only needs to move a few boundaries.

From:

> people who have acted

to:

> people who might enable action.

From:

> criminal relationships

to:

> politically consequential relationships.

From:

> known organisers

to:

> people capable of connecting organisers to resources.

From:

> evidence of conduct

to:

> prediction of capacity.

That is the internal-shock version of the pot becoming too small.
