🏷️ approved_intermediaries_and_safe_contacts.md — Node Plan

Core purpose

Examine how institutions decide which people and organisations are authorised to:

* speak for a community;
* interpret risk;
* receive sensitive information;
* authenticate claims;
* carry referrals;
* reassure officials;
* mediate access;
* define which contacts are considered safe.

This node should not duplicate 🪞_safe_contacts_and_managed_substitutes.md.

That earlier node examined the lived effect on the person when chosen trust is displaced by formal support.

This node should examine the institutional production of legitimacy:

How does someone become an approved intermediary, and what power follows once their account becomes more portable than everyone else’s?

The node should show how approval can be useful, necessary and sometimes protective—while also creating:

* gatekeeping;
* information monopolies;
* political filtering;
* false equivalence between legibility and safety;
* dependence on a narrow corridor into institutions.

⸻

Governing thesis

An approved intermediary does more than carry information.

They may acquire authority to decide:

* who is credible;
* which concern is serious;
* who represents the community;
* which relationship is safe;
* which complaint deserves escalation;
* whose account reaches decision-makers.

Possible core line

Approval does not merely identify a trusted intermediary. It can create one.

Other useful lines:

Institutions often choose the person easiest to recognise and then mistake recognition for trust.

A safe contact may be safe for the institution because they are familiar, accountable, and easy to call. That does not establish that they are safe for the person whose life they are interpreting.

The approved route can become a locked gate while continuing to call itself access.

⸻

Opening movement — the name everyone already knows

Begin with the administrative problem.

An institution needs:

* one contact;
* one representative;
* one person to brief;
* one organisation to consult;
* one route through which concerns can travel.

The approved intermediary is attractive because they simplify complexity.

Possible opening

Every complicated community eventually produces a request for one reliable person.

Someone who knows the names. Someone who can explain the politics. Someone who answers emails. Someone who will not make the meeting harder than it already is.

Then introduce the danger:

The person who makes the institution’s work easier may begin acquiring authority over people who never chose them.

⸻

Section 1 — Why intermediaries are necessary

Establish the legitimate function.

Intermediaries may provide:

* language support;
* cultural interpretation;
* trusted introductions;
* emergency communication;
* safeguarding support;
* professional expertise;
* community access;
* conflict mediation;
* referral routes;
* institutional navigation;
* protection from hostile actors.

Without intermediaries, people may remain unable to access:

* healthcare;
* law;
* housing;
* public institutions;
* media;
* funding;
* protective services.

The node should not imply that direct contact is always possible or preferable.

Key distinction

The problem is not mediation. It is mediation without choice, transparency, plural routes, or limits.

⸻

Section 2 — What makes someone administratively approvable?

Possible markers include:

* registered organisation;
* recognised professional status;
* established funding;
* previous institutional cooperation;
* clear governance;
* security vetting;
* public reputation;
* English fluency;
* formal title;
* political moderation;
* predictable availability;
* willingness to work within institutional procedures.

These qualities may be valuable.

They may also privilege people who are:

* already close to power;
* professionally polished;
* politically reassuring;
* administratively legible;
* less likely to challenge the institution’s framing.

Possible line

The safest intermediary is often the one whose unpredictability has already been trained out of the meeting.

This needs a light touch: dry, not cartoonish.

⸻

Section 3 — Approval as a transfer of institutional power

Approval may bring:

* invitations;
* confidential briefings;
* access to officials;
* referral authority;
* funding;
* recognition;
* media visibility;
* participation in safeguarding;
* influence over appointments;
* the ability to authenticate others.

The intermediary may begin as someone who conveys community knowledge.

They may become someone whose account determines:

* whether a person is heard;
* how a concern is framed;
* whether a relationship appears legitimate;
* whether a complaint travels;
* whether officials treat someone as safe.

Core line

The intermediary’s greatest power may not be the ability to speak. It may be the ability to make someone else administratively audible.

⸻

Section 4 — Recognition is not representation

Return firmly to this distinction.

A person may be recognised because they are:

* familiar;
* funded;
* professionally credible;
* senior;
* available;
* politically convenient;
* known to officials.

None of these necessarily proves that they represent:

* the whole community;
* a minority within it;
* dissidents;
* survivors;
* women;
* queer people;
* disabled people;
* younger people;
* unaffiliated people;
* people who avoid formal organisations.

Possible line

Recognition describes the institution’s relationship with the intermediary. Representation describes the intermediary’s relationship with the people.

Those are different facts.

⸻

Section 5 — Safe for whom?

This should be one of the node’s anchor sections.

The label safe contact may mean safe:

* for liability;
* for confidentiality;
* for procedural consistency;
* for institutional reputation;
* for controlled information flow;
* for security coordination;
* for the official making the decision.

But the contact may not be safe:

* emotionally;
* politically;
* culturally;
* physically;
* for disclosure;
* for a dissident;
* for a survivor;
* for someone outside the recognised faction.

Core line

“Safe contact” is incomplete until the sentence says safe for whom, from what, and according to whose evidence.

This should link back to safeguarding without repeating that node.

⸻

Section 6 — Approved intermediary versus chosen contact

Clarify the distinction.

A chosen contact may hold:

* trust;
* history;
* emotional safety;
* practical knowledge;
* willingness to challenge institutions;
* direct knowledge of harm;
* continuity.

An approved intermediary may hold:

* institutional access;
* formal authority;
* procedural competence;
* documentation;
* recognised status.

Sometimes one person holds both.

Often they do not.

Key distinction

Chosen trust may lack institutional authority. Institutional authority may lack chosen trust.

A responsible system should not force people to choose between:

* being understood;
* and being heard.

⸻

Section 7 — Authentication power

Intermediaries may be asked to authenticate:

* a person;
* an organisation;
* a complaint;
* a political position;
* a community need;
* an introduction;
* a representative;
* a relationship.

This can be protective.

It can also create a system in which one person determines who appears real.

Risks include:

* factional exclusion;
* personal rivalry;
* reputational contamination;
* ideological gatekeeping;
* over-reliance on one source;
* circular validation.

Possible line

The intermediary may not decide whether the claim is true. They may decide whether anyone important is required to consider it.

⸻

Section 8 — Information filtering

An intermediary may decide:

* what is passed on;
* what is summarised;
* what is softened;
* what is delayed;
* what is omitted;
* what is translated into institutional language;
* which concern becomes “appropriate.”

Filtering may be necessary.

No intermediary can transmit everything.

The problem begins when the person cannot tell:

* what changed;
* what was omitted;
* who received the summary;
* whether disagreement was preserved;
* whether the intermediary’s interpretation became the official record.

Core line

Mediation becomes governance when the intermediary’s summary replaces the person’s own account.

⸻

Section 9 — Political filtering

Approved intermediaries may become unofficial boundaries around acceptable politics.

They may be more likely to pass on concerns framed as:

* welfare;
* cohesion;
* moderation;
* practical service need;
* community reassurance.

They may be less likely to pass on concerns framed as:

* institutional wrongdoing;
* state violence;
* discrimination;
* foreign-policy criticism;
* radical reform;
* internal community abuse;
* criticism of the intermediary’s own role.

Possible line

The institution may hear plenty from the community while hearing very little that threatens the terms of engagement.

This is important for preserving the difference between consultation and genuine access.

⸻

Section 10 — Funding and dependency

Intermediaries may rely on:

* grants;
* contracts;
* office space;
* institutional invitations;
* security relationships;
* professional access;
* official recognition.

This does not make them dishonest.

It may create incentives to:

* preserve access;
* avoid confrontation;
* align with institutional priorities;
* protect funding;
* discourage unauthorised actors;
* keep disputes internal;
* prioritise administrative stability.

Core line

Dependence does not prove obedience. It does shape the cost of disagreement.

The node should remain careful not to convert every funded organisation into a proxy.

⸻

Section 11 — Approved corridors

Institutions may create a narrow corridor through which:

* complaints;
* safeguarding referrals;
* consultation;
* funding;
* legal access;
* political communication;
* crisis support;

must travel.

The corridor may be staffed by approved intermediaries.

People outside it may be told to:

* contact the recognised organisation;
* speak through a liaison;
* use the formal representative;
* return to the funded pathway;
* stop contacting decision-makers directly.

Possible line

The door remains open, provided everyone agrees to enter through the same person.

This is where approval becomes gatekeeping.

⸻

Section 12 — Plural intermediaries versus monopoly

A healthy system may support:

* several representatives;
* direct contact;
* specialist routes;
* issue-specific expertise;
* dissenting organisations;
* independent advocates;
* informal networks;
* personal choice.

A risky system may rely on:

* one umbrella body;
* one community leader;
* one funded provider;
* one approved translator;
* one safeguarding contact;
* one official liaison.

Core line

A single trusted route is efficient. It is also a single point of capture, failure, distortion, and fear.

This should connect back to redundancy and community protection.

⸻

Section 13 — Intermediaries and security translation

Once foreign-interference or counter-extremism concerns enter, approved intermediaries may be asked to interpret:

* political language;
* relationships;
* religious activity;
* community disputes;
* loyalty;
* influence;
* unusual behaviour;
* safe and unsafe actors.

Their local knowledge may be useful.

It may also transform:

* gossip into intelligence;
* factional rivalry into risk;
* dissent into susceptibility;
* unfamiliarity into suspicion;
* a personal grievance into a security concern.

Possible line

Local knowledge becomes dangerous when the institution imports the conclusion but leaves the local conflict behind.

The node should require:

* source disclosure;
* corroboration;
* conflict-of-interest assessment;
* separation of opinion from evidence.

⸻

Section 14 — Intermediaries and foreign-state proximity

Some recognised community actors may have relationships with:

* embassies;
* consulates;
* ministries;
* cultural institutes;
* foreign political parties;
* state-linked religious bodies;
* international funding networks.

These connections may be:

* ordinary;
* public;
* cultural;
* diplomatic;
* beneficial;
* politically significant;
* coercive.

The node should not treat contact with an embassy as proof of control.

It should ask:

* Is the relationship disclosed?
* Does the intermediary pass information?
* Are dissidents safe using the route?
* Does the origin state shape who is recognised?
* Is the intermediary able to act independently?
* Are alternative routes available?

Core line

The body presented as a bridge to the diaspora may also be a bridge back to the state from which some members need distance.

This prepares the next two nodes.

⸻

Section 15 — When a safe contact becomes a risk to disclosure

People may avoid approved contacts because they fear:

* information reaching family;
* information reaching an embassy;
* reputational consequences;
* political retaliation;
* community gossip;
* loss of services;
* being labelled difficult;
* loss of anonymity;
* being forced into a faction.

Their avoidance may then be interpreted as:

* disengagement;
* lack of trust;
* extremism;
* isolation;
* refusal of support;
* difficulty reaching the community.

Possible line

A person may avoid the approved contact because the contact is unsafe. The institution then records their absence as evidence that they are hard to engage.

This is the node’s principal feedback loop.

⸻

Section 16 — The substitute effect

An approved intermediary may not merely mediate access.

They may replace:

* direct testimony;
* chosen advocates;
* independent organisations;
* private relationships;
* alternative representatives;
* cross-community routes.

The institution may still report that:

* consultation occurred;
* support was offered;
* the community was represented;
* safeguarding contact was available.

Core line

The presence of an intermediary can conceal the disappearance of direct access.

This should connect explicitly to 🪞 Safe Contacts And Managed Substitutes while preserving the institutional focus.

⸻

Section 17 — Reputation laundering

Institutional recognition may make an intermediary appear:

* safe;
* representative;
* neutral;
* authoritative;
* widely trusted.

The institution may cite the intermediary’s recognition as evidence of credibility.

The intermediary may cite institutional access as evidence of community legitimacy.

This can create a closed loop.

Possible line

The institution trusts the intermediary because the intermediary is recognised. The intermediary is recognised because the institution trusts them.

The loop may be harmless in some cases.

It becomes dangerous when no external test of representation or safety exists.

⸻

Section 18 — Rotation, succession and permanence

Approved intermediaries may remain in place because:

* contact lists are copied;
* officials prefer continuity;
* funding renews;
* institutional memory is weak;
* nobody wants to reopen representation disputes;
* the person becomes indispensable.

A temporary liaison can become a permanent gatekeeper.

Questions include:

* How long does approval last?
* Is it reviewed?
* Can others enter?
* Does the role survive changed community opinion?
* Are conflicts recorded?
* Can affected people object safely?

Core line

Administrative continuity can preserve authority long after its social basis has disappeared.

⸻

Section 19 — When approval becomes containment

Warning signs include:

* direct contact is routinely redirected;
* one intermediary controls several unrelated routes;
* dissenting organisations lose access;
* refusal to use the route carries penalties;
* the intermediary’s account overrides direct testimony;
* representation is assumed rather than demonstrated;
* safeguarding and security information pass through the same actor;
* foreign-state proximity is not disclosed;
* complaints about the intermediary return to the intermediary;
* there is no safe alternative;
* approval has no review or expiry.

Anchor line

Approval becomes containment when the intermediary is no longer one route into the institution but the boundary around who can enter.

⸻

Section 20 — What responsible intermediary use requires

Possible safeguards:

* multiple routes;
* direct-access options;
* issue-specific representation;
* transparent approval criteria;
* declared conflicts;
* evidence of constituency;
* time-limited recognition;
* rotation;
* confidential alternatives;
* separation of community, safeguarding and security functions;
* no monopoly over complaints;
* accurate recording of disagreement;
* protection for unaffiliated people;
* independent review;
* permission for the individual to choose their own advocate;
* prohibition on treating refusal as adverse evidence.

Core line

A useful intermediary adds a route. A dangerous intermediary becomes the route.

⸻

Section 21 — Small-step audit

A reader can assess one intermediary arrangement by asking:

* Who approved this person or organisation?
* For which function?
* What evidence supports representation?
* Who is excluded?
* Can people bypass the intermediary?
* What information do they receive?
* What information do they transmit?
* Are summaries contestable?
* Are conflicts declared?
* Do they hold safeguarding or security roles?
* Is there foreign-state proximity?
* Does refusal affect access?
* Is approval reviewed?
* What direct route remains open?

The smallest testable claims might be:

* one intermediary was treated as representative without evidence;
* one direct contact was redirected;
* one summary overrode direct testimony;
* one refusal was recorded adversely;
* one approved route carried information into a security or diplomatic channel;
* one gatekeeping role had no alternative or review.

⸻

Evidentiary guardrail

Keep this concise.

Distinguish:

* useful mediation;
* poor representation;
* bureaucratic convenience;
* gatekeeping;
* institutional capture;
* conflict of interest;
* foreign-state influence;
* deliberate proxy action.

Essential line

Institutional approval does not prove an intermediary is controlling. Community criticism does not prove they are illegitimate. The question is what authority they exercise, over whom, with what evidence and alternatives.

⸻

Compact definition

Approved Intermediaries And Safe Contacts describes the people and organisations institutions authorise to interpret communities, carry information, authenticate claims and mediate access.

The arrangement becomes risky when approval:

* substitutes familiarity for representation;
* substitutes legibility for safety;
* narrows direct access;
* filters testimony;
* monopolises referrals;
* obscures conflicts;
* imports political or foreign-state judgements;
* makes support dependent on using one approved corridor.

The central danger is not that intermediaries exist.

It is that institutional convenience may give one intermediary power over who becomes audible, credible and safe.
