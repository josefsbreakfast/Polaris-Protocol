# 📏 Evidence And Confidence Scale  
**First created:** 2026-07-23 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-23  
*A proposition-level scale for stating what is established, corroborated, inferred, plausible, contested, or unsupported without allowing one strong source to inflate an entire state study.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

State capability research often develops under conditions of partial visibility.

Relevant evidence may include:

- court findings;
- official records;
- procurement documents;
- technical forensics;
- intelligence assessments;
- government statements;
- survivor and witness testimony;
- human-rights reporting;
- investigative journalism;
- leaked documents;
- institutional behaviour;
- repeated patterns;
- reasonable inference;
- unanswered questions.

These materials do not all prove the same thing.

A court judgment may establish that conduct occurred while leaving the wider policy unclear.

A technical report may establish use of a tool while leaving the operator unidentified.

A survivor may reliably describe a threat, unexplained knowledge, or pressure on relatives while being unable to reconstruct the institutional route.

A government may confidently attribute an operation while publishing little of the underlying evidence.

A widely repeated claim may still depend on one original source.

This node provides a shared language for describing those differences.

Its central rule is:

> Confidence belongs to a precise proposition, not to a country, institution, source, or whole theory.

The purpose of confidence language is not to make serious conduct sound less serious.

It is to stop uncertainty in one part of the chain from being concealed by confidence in another.

---

## 🎯 Confidence Must Attach To The Claim  

Do not write:

> Iran is high confidence.

> Russia is well documented.

> The evidence against this state is strong.

Those statements are too broad to audit.

Write instead:

- There is **high confidence** that the capability existed during the relevant period.
- There is **moderate confidence** that the named institution had access.
- There is **strongly corroborated evidence** of overseas threats against the identified target group.
- There is **credible reporting** of embassy involvement, but no public record establishing central instruction.
- The proposed use of relationship scoring remains a **plausible capability**, not documented deployment.
- Public evidence is currently **insufficient** to attribute the individual incident.
- A causal link between the disputed classification and the later refusal has **not been established**.

A state study may contain:

- established conduct;
- strongly supported capability;
- uncertain access;
- plausible intent;
- contested attribution;
- unproven causation;

all at the same time.

That is not untidiness.

That is what the evidence says.

## 🪜 The Proposition Ladder  

Confidence should be considered separately at each stage of the analytical chain.

### 1. Capability  

Can the relevant system or institution technically perform the function?

Possible evidence includes:

- technical documentation;
- product specifications;
- source code;
- procurement records;
- official descriptions;
- verified prior use;
- expert technical analysis.

### 2. Access  

Did the relevant actor possess the required:

- data;
- permissions;
- infrastructure;
- legal authority;
- contractor relationship;
- diplomatic reach;
- practical opportunity?

Capability existing somewhere does not establish access for the actor named.

### 3. Implementation  

Was the capability incorporated into a real:

- workflow;
- product;
- policy;
- contract;
- programme;
- operational practice?

Procurement may establish acquisition.

It may not establish installation, population, activation, or use.

### 4. Documented Use  

Is there evidence that the capability was actually deployed?

Evidence may concern:

- one incident;
- several incidents;
- a target group;
- one institution;
- one period;
- a formal programme.

Documented use in one setting does not establish universal use.

### 5. Attribution  

What evidence links deployment or conduct to a specific:

- official;
- agency;
- contractor;
- proxy;
- diplomatic mission;
- governing authority;
- state?

Attribution may be direct, inferential, contested, or unresolved.

### 6. Causation  

What evidence shows that the identified mechanism produced or materially contributed to the observed outcome?

A capability may exist.

The actor may have used it.

The particular harm may still require a separate causal showing.

### 7. Pattern Or Policy  

What evidence establishes that conduct was:

- repeated;
- organised;
- authorised;
- directed;
- structural;
- current?

One documented event is not automatically a policy.

Several similar events are not automatically centrally directed.

## 📐 The Confidence Scale  

The following categories describe the strength of support for a **specific proposition**.

They should be used descriptively in prose.

Letters or numbers may be added for internal tracking, but the words should remain visible to the reader.

---

### A. Established  

Use **established** where a proposition is supported by strong, directly relevant evidence and material uncertainty is limited.

Possible foundations include:

- final or authoritative court findings;
- official admissions against interest;
- authenticated primary records;
- verified technical forensics;
- multiple genuinely independent primary sources;
- documentary records directly linking actor, conduct, and event;
- facts accepted across credible disputing parties.

An established proposition should still remain no broader than the evidence.

Examples:

- A named spyware product was found on the device through independently reviewed technical analysis.
- A court found that the identified official authorised the unlawful transfer.
- An official record confirms that the passport was cancelled on the stated date.
- The government publicly acknowledged operating the specified programme.

**Established does not necessarily mean:**

- every detail is known;
- the conduct formed national policy;
- the same mechanism was used in another case;
- causation is proven for every later harm;
- the practice remains current.

---

### B. Strongly Corroborated  

Use **strongly corroborated** where several credible and substantially independent sources support the same proposition, but the matter has not been finally adjudicated or fully disclosed.

Possible foundations include:

- multiple independent witnesses;
- reporting based on different documentary sources;
- technical evidence consistent with testimony;
- official records combined with credible investigation;
- repeated cases showing a stable operational pattern;
- findings by several expert organisations using transparent methods.

Ask:

- Are the sources genuinely independent?
- Do they rely on different records or witnesses?
- Do they agree on the core proposition rather than merely repeat the same wording?
- Are major contradictions explained?
- Is the inference narrower than the evidence?

Strong corroboration may support a pattern claim.

It should not silently become proof of formal policy or central command.

---

### C. Credibly Reported  

Use **credibly reported** where a reputable source presents a materially supported claim, but important evidence remains unavailable, untested, or dependent on limited access.

Possible foundations include:

- high-quality investigative journalism;
- established human-rights reporting;
- a detailed survivor or witness account with clear provenance;
- parliamentary or official reporting that identifies its basis;
- technically informed analysis with acknowledged limitations;
- one strong documentary source not yet independently confirmed.

Credibly reported means the claim deserves serious weight.

It does not mean every institutional or causal link has been established.

Useful wording includes:

- “Credible reporting indicates…”
- “The available reporting supports…”
- “The incident has been documented by…”
- “The wider command structure remains unclear.”
- “The evidence establishes the experience more strongly than the attribution.”

---

### D. Reasonable Inference  

Use **reasonable inference** where known facts support a conclusion, but one or more necessary links remain indirect.

A reasonable inference should have:

- a clearly stated evidentiary foundation;
- a plausible mechanism;
- a logical route from evidence to conclusion;
- preserved rival explanations;
- identifiable conditions that would weaken it.

Examples:

- repeated use of identical non-public wording across institutions may reasonably suggest a shared source;
- action shortly after a documented referral may support an inference of influence;
- access, motive, and a matching operational pattern may support an inference of involvement;
- repeated pressure on relatives following public dissent may support an inference that family links are being used coercively.

Reasonable inference is not a decorative synonym for proof.

The missing step should be named.

---

### E. Plausible Capability  

Use **plausible capability** where the mechanism is technically and operationally possible, but public evidence does not establish deployment in the case discussed.

Plausibility may be supported by:

- known technical capacity;
- documented procurement;
- relevant access;
- compatible infrastructure;
- political incentive;
- prior use in another setting;
- weak safeguards;
- a mechanism that fits the observed outcome.

A plausible capability is useful for:

- threat modelling;
- identifying records worth preserving;
- designing safeguards;
- asking better questions;
- assessing foreseeable temptation.

It should not be written as:

> This is probably what happened.

unless additional evidence supports that probability.

Useful wording includes:

- “The existing infrastructure could support…”
- “The capability creates a foreseeable risk of…”
- “Public evidence does not establish use in this instance.”
- “This remains a mechanism to test rather than an attribution.”

---

### F. Unsupported Or Speculative  

Use **unsupported**, **speculative**, or **not presently supportable** where a claim lacks a reliable evidentiary route.

Indicators include:

- no identifiable source;
- no demonstrated access;
- no defined mechanism;
- source dependence hidden by repetition;
- contradictions treated as further proof;
- a theory too broad to disprove;
- motive standing in for evidence;
- country reputation standing in for actor attribution;
- technical possibility standing in for deployment;
- one anomaly carrying the weight of an entire architecture.

Unsupported does not mean impossible.

It means the available material does not currently earn the claim.

The correct response may be to:

- narrow the proposition;
- identify the missing evidence;
- preserve the question;
- return to the smallest testable claim;
- stop.

## 🧩 Mixed Confidence Is Normal  

A single case may look like this:

| Proposition | Assessment |
|---|---|
| The state possessed commercial spyware capability | **Established** |
| The named security service had access during the relevant period | **Strongly corroborated** |
| The target’s device was infected | **Established** |
| The named service operated the infection | **Credibly reported** |
| The central government ordered the operation | **Reasonable inference** |
| The operation caused a later employment decision | **Not established** |
| A wider relationship-scoring programme existed | **Plausible capability** |

This is more useful than assigning the whole case one label.

It shows where:

- protection may already be justified;
- investigation should focus;
- public wording must remain restrained;
- further records could change confidence.

One strong finding should not tow the rest of the theory behind it like an administrative wedding train.

## 📚 Source Order And Source Function  

A preferred source order can improve discipline, but no hierarchy should be applied mechanically.

### 1. Primary Legal And Official Documents  

Examples:

- legislation;
- court judgments;
- warrants;
- official policies;
- parliamentary records;
- regulator findings;
- diplomatic notices;
- government procurement;
- public contracts;
- official admissions.

These may establish:

- legal authority;
- formal policy;
- institutional structure;
- dates;
- acknowledged conduct;
- adjudicated facts.

They may not reveal:

- covert practice;
- unlawful deviation;
- internal disagreement;
- implementation failure;
- concealed abuse.

Official does not mean complete.

---

### 2. Court Records  

Court records may provide:

- tested evidence;
- findings of fact;
- disclosed documents;
- witness examination;
- legal attribution;
- remedies.

Ask:

- Is the decision final?
- What standard of proof applied?
- Which issues were actually determined?
- Was evidence excluded or unavailable?
- Does the judgment establish conduct, attribution, causation, or only legality?
- Is the finding binding outside that case?

A court may establish one narrow fact very strongly.

Do not expand it beyond what was decided.

---

### 3. Procurement And Corporate Records  

These may establish:

- acquisition;
- vendor relationships;
- product capability;
- licence periods;
- contract value;
- intended users;
- stated purpose;
- integration plans.

Ask:

- Was the product delivered?
- Was it operational?
- Was it populated with data?
- Was the relevant module purchased?
- Did the vendor describe possibility or actual deployment?
- Was the contract renewed, suspended, or terminated?
- Did subcontractors hold part of the route?

A brochure is evidence of what the seller claims.

It is not evidence that the system worked as advertised or was used in a particular incident.

---

### 4. Technical Evidence  

Examples include:

- forensic device analysis;
- infrastructure mapping;
- malware samples;
- access logs;
- authentication records;
- network traces;
- code review;
- reproducible tests.

Assess:

- method transparency;
- reproducibility;
- chain of custody;
- analyst expertise;
- alternative technical explanations;
- infrastructure reuse;
- compromise by third parties;
- confidence in operator attribution.

Technical evidence may strongly establish mechanism.

Operator identity may remain less certain.

---

### 5. Established Human-Rights Organisations  

These may contribute:

- structured testimony;
- case comparison;
- regional expertise;
- legal analysis;
- long-term pattern recognition;
- access to affected communities.

Assess:

- methodology;
- source protection;
- independence;
- number and diversity of cases;
- whether claims are direct findings or reliance on other reporting;
- whether uncertainty is visible.

Human-rights reporting may be strongest on pattern and lived impact.

Institutional attribution may still depend on restricted evidence.

---

### 6. High-Quality Investigative Journalism  

Investigative reporting may uncover:

- leaked records;
- insider accounts;
- procurement;
- diplomatic activity;
- covert partnerships;
- operational patterns;
- suppressed institutional findings.

Assess:

- named versus anonymous sources;
- documentary support;
- independent corroboration;
- editorial standards;
- correction history;
- whether later outlets merely repeat the original investigation.

Journalism can be the first public route to primary evidence.

It should not be downgraded merely because the institution under investigation refused to publish its own paperwork. How unexpectedly convenient for the institution.

---

### 7. Survivor And Witness Testimony  

Testimony may directly establish:

- threats received;
- coercive contact;
- family pressure;
- detention;
- document denial;
- unexplained knowledge;
- surveillance indicators;
- changes in institutional treatment;
- immediate harm;
- lived consequences.

Testimony may not independently establish:

- the complete technical mechanism;
- central authorisation;
- actor identity;
- the full data route;
- wider policy.

The correct response is not to discard the testimony.

It is to separate what the witness directly knows from what requires further attribution.

> A person can be highly reliable about what happened to them and still lack access to the minutes of the meeting that caused it.

---

### 8. Commentary And Hypothesis  

Commentary may help:

- identify patterns;
- propose mechanisms;
- compare cases;
- expose contradictions;
- frame governance risks;
- suggest research questions.

It should be marked as:

- interpretation;
- analysis;
- hypothesis;
- warning;
- unresolved question.

Commentary should not quietly inherit the status of the sources it cites.

---

## 🔁 Corroboration Is Not Repetition  

A claim may appear in:

- an NGO report;
- a newspaper article;
- a parliamentary debate;
- an academic paper;
- a policy briefing;
- a later government statement.

All six may trace back to:

- one unnamed witness;
- one original article;
- one intelligence assessment;
- one press release;
- one technical report.

This is source dependence.

Ask:

- What is the earliest identifiable source?
- Did later sources conduct independent investigation?
- Are they citing one another?
- Do they possess different documents?
- Are separate witnesses involved?
- Does the language repeat unusually closely?
- Is one assertion becoming accepted fact through circulation?
- Is the same institution generating and later citing its own claim?

Ten citations may still contain one evidentiary root.

The plant does enjoy being counted by its leaves.

---

## 🏛️ Government And Intelligence Claims  

Government and intelligence assessments may be highly informed.

They may also remain:

- classified;
- politically incentivised;
- selectively disclosed;
- difficult to test;
- framed for diplomatic effect;
- based on confidence rather than proof.

Record them carefully.

Ask:

- Which institution made the assessment?
- Was evidence published?
- Was the assessment coordinated across agencies?
- What confidence language was used?
- What does that language mean in the relevant system?
- Was the claim judicially tested?
- Did independent technical evidence support it?
- Were rival explanations addressed?
- Did later evidence confirm, narrow, or contradict it?
- Does the government have a strategic interest in the attribution?
- Is the claim stronger than the public evidence, and is that gap acknowledged?

Useful wording includes:

- “The government attributed the operation to…”
- “The public evidentiary basis remains limited.”
- “Independent technical reporting is consistent with part of the assessment.”
- “The attribution has not been judicially tested.”
- “Several allied governments issued aligned assessments.”
- “The degree of underlying source independence is not publicly known.”

Official attribution is evidence.

It is not holy water.

---

## 🗣️ Testimony, Trauma, And Attribution  

Trauma may affect:

- chronology;
- recall order;
- confidence in peripheral details;
- ability to narrate continuously;
- willingness to disclose;
- interpretation under uncertainty.

It does not make the underlying experience unreal.

Assess testimony by asking:

- What did the person directly perceive?
- What records support the account?
- Which details remain consistent?
- Which parts are interpretation?
- What information could the person realistically know?
- Are apparent inconsistencies material?
- Did risk, disability, language, fear, or coercion affect reporting?
- Was the person given a safe opportunity to clarify?
- Are institutions demanding impossible certainty from someone deliberately denied access to records?

Do not require the subject of opaque state action to provide the state’s own audit trail before their account may be taken seriously.

Also do not force testimony to prove more than it can.

That distinction protects both the survivor and the claim.

---

## 🌫️ Negative Evidence And Evidentiary Silence  

The absence of public evidence may mean:

- the conduct did not occur;
- the capability was not deployed;
- records were never created;
- records were destroyed;
- records remain classified;
- the victim could not report safely;
- the host state failed to investigate;
- the relevant institution refused disclosure;
- journalists lacked access;
- evidence exists in another jurisdiction;
- researchers tested the wrong mechanism;
- the claim is false.

No single interpretation should be automatic.

### Absence That Lowers Confidence  

Absence should lower confidence where:

- the proposed mechanism should leave clear records;
- reliable logs exist and show no access;
- the actor lacked required capability;
- timing contradicts the theory;
- independent evidence supports another explanation;
- a thorough investigation found no relevant link;
- the hypothesis repeatedly fails specific tests.

### Absence That Remains Ambiguous  

Absence may remain ambiguous where:

- records are secret or inaccessible;
- logging is weak;
- proxies or contractors obscure the route;
- the victim faced serious reporting risk;
- host institutions are conflicted;
- evidence is likely held abroad;
- the mechanism may leave few public traces.

The correct label may be:

> Not publicly established.

That is different from both:

> Disproved.

and:

> Obviously secret, therefore true.

---

## 🧯 Disproof And Confidence Reduction  

Each claim should identify what would reduce confidence.

Possible confidence-reducing evidence includes:

- proof that the actor lacked access;
- evidence that the capability was unavailable at the relevant time;
- logs inconsistent with the alleged event;
- a decision made before the proposed trigger;
- technical findings pointing to another mechanism;
- a source retracting or materially revising the account;
- demonstration that apparently independent reports shared one origin;
- evidence of local or private action rather than state direction;
- records showing the programme was not operational;
- a successful natural experiment contradicting the hypothesis;
- a more complete explanation with fewer unsupported steps.

Confidence should also fall where a theory responds to every contradiction by becoming larger.

For example:

- no trace means the actor was sophisticated;
- contradictory trace means deliberate deception;
- absence of access means hidden access;
- no motive means a deeper motive;
- disconfirming evidence means compromised investigators.

A claim that converts every possible result into confirmation has left analysis and entered devotional horticulture.

---

## 📅 Confidence Changes Over Time  

Confidence is not permanent.

It should be reassessed after:

- court judgment;
- declassification;
- parliamentary inquiry;
- regulator findings;
- technical reanalysis;
- authenticated leaks;
- official admission;
- official correction;
- source retraction;
- contradictory testimony;
- discovery of source dependence;
- change of government;
- institutional reform;
- procurement termination;
- new documented incidents;
- evidence that safeguards worked;
- evidence that safeguards failed.

Each state node should record:

- when the assessment was made;
- the latest significant evidence;
- whether the claim remains current;
- what event would trigger review.

A confidence label without a date may slowly become a fossil wearing current-affairs clothing.

---

## 🧮 Confidence Is Not Probability Theatre  

Avoid false precision such as:

- 73% likely;
- 8.4 out of 10 confidence;
- almost certainly;
- definitely;
- everyone knows.

unless the underlying method genuinely supports that language.

Numbers may create the appearance of objectivity while hiding:

- subjective weighting;
- incomplete data;
- unclear denominators;
- political judgement;
- source dependence;
- institutional bias.

Where formal probabilistic language is used by an intelligence or technical body, explain:

- whose scale it is;
- what the categories mean;
- whether they refer to source reliability, analytic confidence, or event probability;
- whether the scale is public;
- whether readers should compare it across institutions.

Precision is useful.

Decorative arithmetic is not.

## 📝 Recommended Language  

### Established  

- “Court records establish that…”
- “Authenticated documents confirm…”
- “Independent technical analysis verified…”
- “The government acknowledged…”

### Strongly Corroborated  

- “Multiple independent sources strongly support…”
- “The pattern is corroborated by…”
- “Separate technical and testimonial evidence converge on…”

### Credibly Reported  

- “Credible reporting indicates…”
- “The incident has been documented by…”
- “Available reporting supports the account, although…”

### Reasonable Inference  

- “The available evidence reasonably suggests…”
- “The timing and repeated wording support an inference that…”
- “This interpretation remains indirect because…”

### Plausible Capability  

- “The existing infrastructure could support…”
- “The capability creates a foreseeable risk of…”
- “Use in this instance is not publicly established.”

### Unsupported Or Unresolved  

- “Public evidence is currently insufficient to establish…”
- “The attribution remains unresolved.”
- “The claim should not presently be stated as fact.”
- “No reliable evidentiary route has yet been identified.”

### Causation  

- “The evidence links the classification to the decision.”
- “The sequence is consistent with influence but does not establish causation.”
- “A causal contribution is plausible but unproven.”
- “The available record does not show that the mechanism produced the outcome.”

## 📊 Confidence Snapshot Template  

Each country node should include a compact section such as:

### Confidence Snapshot  

| Proposition | Confidence | Basis | Main Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relevant capability exists | Established / Strong / Moderate / Low | Technical, procurement, official, or forensic evidence | Current access, operational status, or scope |
| Named actor had access | Established / Strong / Moderate / Low | Mandate, logs, contracts, testimony, reporting | Direct records or period-specific confirmation |
| Capability was implemented | Established / Strong / Moderate / Low | Workflow, policy, integration, training, audit | Proof of activation or use |
| Transnational use occurred | Established / Strong / Moderate / Low | Cases, court findings, technical evidence, testimony | Pattern, command, or current continuity |
| State attribution | Established / Strong / Moderate / Low | Orders, infrastructure, official assessment, corroboration | Central direction, alternative actors, unpublished evidence |
| Relationship governance occurred | Established / Strong / Moderate / Low | Family pressure, association mapping, repeated thresholds | Formal scoring or coordinated intervention |
| Causal effect in the specified case | Established / Strong / Moderate / Low | Decision records, timing, reliance, correction response | Alternative causes or missing decision trail |
| Current relevance | Established / Strong / Moderate / Low | Recent evidence, retained systems, current policy | Reform effect, operational continuity |

Use prose labels rather than allowing the table to become a substitute for explanation.

## ⚖️ Rules For Assigning Confidence  

- Assign confidence to one proposition at a time.
- Name the actor as narrowly as the evidence allows.
- State the relevant period.
- Distinguish direct evidence from inference.
- Check whether apparently separate sources are independent.
- Record political and institutional incentives without using them as proof.
- Separate testimony about experience from attribution of mechanism.
- Separate procurement from implementation.
- Separate implementation from individual use.
- Separate use from central authorisation.
- Separate attribution from causation.
- Preserve evidence against the preferred explanation.
- State the main missing record.
- Lower confidence when material contradictions remain unexplained.
- Raise confidence only when new evidence supports the actual missing link.
- Do not raise confidence merely because the claim has become familiar.
- Do not lower confidence merely because the conduct is politically inconvenient.
- Date every assessment.
- Review it when material evidence changes.

## 🚫 What The Scale Is Not  

This scale is not:

- a mechanism for sanitising severe harm;
- a demand that survivors possess classified records;
- a presumption that official sources are neutral;
- a way to call every unresolved claim implausible;
- a ranking of victims;
- a score for the moral character of a state;
- a substitute for legal standards of proof;
- a substitute for technical forensic standards;
- a licence to repeat weak allegations with a small disclaimer;
- an excuse to treat secrecy as innocence;
- an excuse to treat secrecy as proof;
- probability theatre;
- a loyalty test for researchers;
- a method for forcing every case into one confidence label.

The scale should make uncertainty visible without making the subject disappear inside it.

---

## 🧿 Compact Definition  

**Evidence And Confidence Scale** is the proposition-level framework used to describe how strongly a claim is supported across the Regime and State Capability Studies branch.

It distinguishes:

- established findings;
- strongly corroborated findings;
- credible reporting;
- reasonable inference;
- plausible capability;
- unsupported or speculative claims.

It requires separate assessment of:

- capability;
- access;
- implementation;
- documented use;
- attribution;
- causation;
- pattern or policy;
- current relevance.

Its governing rule is:

> Confidence belongs to the proposition.  
> It does not spill automatically into the next claim.
