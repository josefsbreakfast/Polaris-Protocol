# 🔍 Questions To Ask Of Every State  
**First created:** 2026-07-23 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-23  
*A reusable research and drafting instrument for examining state capability, documented conduct, diaspora pressure, relationship governance, and host-state responsibility without allowing the country name to do the analytical work.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

A state study should not begin with:

> What do we already believe about this country?

It should begin with:

> What precise claim are we trying to test, against which actor, during which period, through which possible mechanism?

Country names are dangerously efficient containers.

They can make:

- one agency look like the whole state;
- one government look permanent;
- one incident look systematic;
- one capability look deployed;
- one motive look proven;
- one nationality look politically uniform;
- one diaspora look like an overseas extension of government.

This node provides the common questions to be asked of every state, governing authority, security institution, diplomatic network, proxy system, or fragmented political structure examined within `🌍_Regime_And_State_Capability_Studies`.

The questions are deliberately repetitive.

Repetition is useful here.

It prevents the method from becoming more sceptical for allies, more credulous for adversaries, or more sweeping when the country already has a frightening reputation.

The questions do not require identical answers.

They require every answer to identify:

- the actor;
- the conduct;
- the mechanism;
- the evidence;
- the uncertainty;
- the rival explanation;
- the practical significance.

> The country name is the beginning of the filing path. It is not the conclusion.

---

## 🧭 How To Use This Node  

These questions can be used:

- before research begins;
- while constructing a country-study node;
- when reviewing an existing draft;
- when comparing two states;
- when assessing a reported incident;
- when checking whether a claim has outrun its evidence;
- when identifying records worth requesting;
- when planning a safer protective response.

Not every question will be answerable in every case.

An unanswered question should be recorded as:

- unknown;
- unavailable;
- contested;
- not publicly documented;
- outside the scope of the node.

It should not be silently filled with assumption.

The questions should be answered at the smallest useful level.

Where possible, prefer:

> A named security service possessed access to a named system during a defined period.

over:

> The state could see everything.

Prefer:

> A family member reported receiving a threat after a specific public action.

over:

> The regime controls the whole diaspora.

The smaller claim is not necessarily less serious.

It is simply more difficult to evade.

## 1. 🪪 What Is The Precise Unit Of Analysis?  

Before describing capability or conduct, identify exactly who or what is being studied.

Possible units include:

- the state as a constitutional or international legal entity;
- the current government;
- a previous administration;
- a ruling party;
- a presidency or royal court;
- a ministry;
- an intelligence service;
- a military intelligence body;
- a police or internal-security service;
- a border agency;
- a diplomatic mission;
- a consular network;
- a territorial authority;
- an occupying power;
- a regional government;
- a militia;
- a party-state organisation;
- a contractor;
- a state-owned company;
- a state-aligned media network;
- a proxy or intermediary network.

Ask:

- Which actor is actually named by the evidence?
- Does the evidence concern a formal institution or an informal network?
- Is authority centralised, fragmented, contested, federal, devolved, occupied, or militia-based?
- Is the conduct linked to national policy, local initiative, factional rivalry, or an individual official?
- Is the study about current practice or a historical period?
- Has the relevant institution changed name, mandate, leadership, or legal authority?
- Does “the government” conceal disagreement between agencies?
- Does “the state” conceal a contractor, proxy, or allied actor?

A study should be no broader than its evidence permits.

## 2. 📅 What Period Does The Evidence Cover?  

State capability is not timeless.

Practices may change because of:

- elections;
- coups;
- succession;
- war;
- revolution;
- sanctions;
- peace agreements;
- emergency powers;
- legal reform;
- technological acquisition;
- institutional collapse;
- diplomatic realignment;
- leadership scandal;
- international litigation;
- changes in host-state tolerance.

Ask:

- What is the earliest relevant date?
- What is the latest reliable date?
- Does the evidence describe present practice or historical conduct?
- Was the capability available throughout the period?
- Did the governing authority remain the same?
- Did the legal framework change?
- Did an emergency measure become permanent?
- Did a reform alter practice or merely language?
- Has a court, regulator, or later investigation revised the earlier account?
- Are old examples being used to imply current continuity without new evidence?

Historical conduct remains relevant to:

- institutional habit;
- retained infrastructure;
- survivor risk;
- foreseeable temptation;
- confidence in safeguards.

It should still be labelled historical.

> The plant may have been in the shop for decades. The procurement date still matters.

## 3. 🧾 What Conduct Is Actually Documented?  

Begin with conduct, not reputation.

Ask:

- What happened?
- To whom?
- Where?
- When?
- How often?
- Through which documented or suspected route?
- What immediate harm occurred?
- What longer-term consequence followed?
- Was the conduct domestic or transnational?
- Was it attempted, completed, prevented, or alleged?
- Was it publicly acknowledged?
- Was it judicially tested?
- Is the account based on one source or several independent sources?

Possible conduct includes:

- surveillance;
- communications interception;
- spyware deployment;
- account compromise;
- online harassment;
- impersonation;
- threats;
- family coercion;
- passport restriction;
- consular pressure;
- employment pressure;
- financial restriction;
- doxxing;
- community monitoring;
- protest monitoring;
- misuse of international policing systems;
- extradition pressure;
- deportation or forced return;
- informal rendition;
- abduction;
- assault;
- poisoning;
- attempted assassination;
- assassination;
- interference with legal representation;
- interference with journalism;
- interference with political association;
- suppression of introductions or access;
- reputational warnings;
- exclusion from institutions;
- pressure through religious or community organisations.

Do not allow several distinct acts to collapse into one vague phrase such as:

> repression abroad.

Name the conduct.

### Pattern classification  

Ask whether the evidence supports:

- **One incident** — a single documented event.
- **Repeated incidents** — more than one comparable event.
- **Operational pattern** — recurrence with meaningful similarities.
- **Authorised programme** — evidence of organised implementation.
- **Formal policy** — explicit legal, political, or administrative direction.
- **Structural practice** — conduct embedded across institutions or maintained over time.

An incident does not become policy through repetition in prose.

## 4. 🎯 Who Is Targeted?  

Identify the people or relationships affected as precisely as possible.

Possible targets include:

- political dissidents;
- opposition politicians;
- journalists;
- human-rights defenders;
- lawyers;
- academics;
- students;
- artists;
- defectors;
- former officials;
- whistleblowers;
- religious minorities;
- ethnic minorities;
- national minorities;
- separatist or autonomy movements;
- labour organisers;
- environmental activists;
- feminist activists;
- LGBTQ+ people;
- anti-war networks;
- protest organisers;
- diaspora institutions;
- refugee organisations;
- relatives;
- partners;
- carers;
- funders;
- publishers;
- professional supporters;
- ordinary migrants or travellers.

Ask:

- Is targeting individual, categorical, associative, or predictive?
- Is the person targeted for conduct, identity, family connection, or inferred future behaviour?
- Is the target described through a lawful-activity category such as journalism, protest, religion, or association?
- Are relatives targeted because the principal subject is inaccessible?
- Are supporters targeted because they create legal, financial, political, or emotional capacity?
- Does the system distinguish violent conduct from lawful dissent?
- Are whole populations being treated as suspect because of the conduct of a government or organisation?
- Are diaspora members assumed to support, represent, or oppose a state based on nationality alone?

A target category should not be broader than the evidence.

A diaspora is not one political object.

## 5. ⚙️ What Capability Exists?  

Identify what the relevant actor can technically, legally, institutionally, diplomatically, financially, or coercively do.

Possible capabilities include:

- communications interception;
- device exploitation;
- spyware;
- malware;
- account access;
- metadata analysis;
- social-graph construction;
- biometric identification;
- facial recognition;
- location tracking;
- travel-history analysis;
- financial monitoring;
- identity verification;
- civil-registry access;
- border screening;
- watchlisting;
- automated risk scoring;
- platform monitoring;
- content suppression;
- influence operations;
- family mapping;
- community mapping;
- diplomatic reporting;
- consular leverage;
- visa or passport control;
- extradition requests;
- deportation pressure;
- return agreements;
- asset restriction;
- employment pressure;
- procurement of private investigators;
- use of organised crime or informal enforcers.

For each capability, ask:

- What function does it perform?
- What inputs does it require?
- What output does it produce?
- Who receives the output?
- What decision may follow?
- What observable traces might remain?
- What are its technical limits?
- What are its legal limits?
- What are its practical limits?
- What error rates or data-quality problems are known?
- Can the capability be used remotely?
- Does it depend on host-state or commercial cooperation?
- Is the capability documented, advertised, procured, inferred, or merely imaginable?

Capability answers:

> What could this system do?

It does not answer:

> Did it do this here?

## 6. 🔑 Who Has Access?  

A capability may exist without being available to the actor named in the claim.

Ask who can access:

- raw data;
- derived scores;
- contact graphs;
- travel records;
- border systems;
- financial records;
- platform dashboards;
- communications content;
- metadata;
- biometric systems;
- consular files;
- community reports;
- commercial datasets;
- contractor tools;
- allied intelligence;
- international policing systems.

Then ask:

- Is access direct or mediated?
- Is access lawful, covert, purchased, shared, stolen, or inferred?
- Does access require a warrant, ministerial approval, contract, diplomatic request, or informal relationship?
- Is access limited to one agency?
- Can regional or local bodies use it?
- Can contractors retain or reuse outputs?
- Can foreign partners query the system?
- Can an embassy request information?
- Can community intermediaries supply context?
- Can access be audited?
- Are logs retained?
- Is misuse detectable?
- Are there known workarounds around formal controls?

Capability plus no access remains theoretical for the actor under examination.

Access plus no implementation may still remain unused.

## 7. 🏢 What Administrative Infrastructure Supports The Capability?  

Capabilities become consequential through ordinary institutions.

Map the route through:

- legislation;
- executive orders;
- emergency regulations;
- ministerial powers;
- security mandates;
- databases;
- identity systems;
- border infrastructure;
- procurement;
- vendor contracts;
- cloud systems;
- telecommunications arrangements;
- policing agreements;
- financial compliance systems;
- consular procedures;
- asylum or migration systems;
- public-private partnerships;
- data-sharing agreements;
- regional organisations;
- international legal mechanisms;
- informal reporting networks.

Ask:

- Where is the capability authorised?
- Who funds it?
- Who procures it?
- Who operates it?
- Who maintains it?
- Who receives its outputs?
- Who makes the consequential decision?
- Which institution owns correction?
- Which institution owns appeal?
- Can responsibility be passed between bodies?
- Does one body collect while another decides?
- Is the infrastructure temporary, permanent, or expanding?
- Was it built for another purpose?
- Has the original purpose drifted?
- Is security language preventing ordinary scrutiny?
- Does fragmentation make the route difficult to see?

The front counter may be clean.

The teeth may still be distributed through the supply chain.

## 8. 🧠 What Political Or Institutional Incentive Exists?  

Intent and incentive help assess plausibility.

They do not prove use.

Ask what the relevant actor may seek to:

- protect;
- suppress;
- predict;
- deter;
- punish;
- redirect;
- conceal;
- monopolise;
- stabilise;
- delegitimise.

Possible incentives include:

- regime continuity;
- ruling-party dominance;
- royal or elite protection;
- territorial control;
- wartime cohesion;
- prevention of defection;
- suppression of opposition;
- control of exile politics;
- protection of international reputation;
- prevention of testimony;
- prevention of litigation;
- control of religious authority;
- control of remittances;
- maintenance of ethnic or sectarian hierarchy;
- discouragement of cross-community coalitions;
- disruption of organising;
- protection of security relationships;
- avoidance of sanctions;
- management of leadership succession.

Ask:

- Is the incentive publicly stated?
- Is it evident in law or policy?
- Is it inferred from repeated conduct?
- Is it merely geopolitically convenient to assume?
- Does the actor have something concrete to gain?
- Would the proposed operation be proportionate to that gain?
- Is there a cheaper administrative route?
- Would the operation create legal or diplomatic risk?
- Has crisis lowered the expected cost?
- Does the state distinguish criticism from disloyalty?
- Does it frame lawful association as foreign influence or security risk?

A motive may make an explanation plausible.

It cannot complete the evidentiary chain on its own.

## 9. 🕸️ How Are Relationships Used?  

This is the governing question of the Little Database of Horrors.

Ask what relationships are:

- observed;
- recorded;
- inferred;
- classified;
- scored;
- warned about;
- exploited;
- redirected;
- discouraged;
- disrupted.

Relevant relationships may include:

- family;
- intimate partners;
- friends;
- carers;
- lawyers;
- journalists;
- employers;
- funders;
- political allies;
- religious leaders;
- community organisers;
- diaspora institutions;
- professional associations;
- academic collaborators;
- technical experts;
- witnesses;
- publishers;
- host-state officials.

Ask:

- Are contact records being treated as proof of loyalty or coordination?
- Are family links being used as a proxy for political risk?
- Are relatives threatened because they are accessible?
- Are professional links interpreted as political alignment?
- Are legal or journalistic relationships treated as hostile bridges?
- Are some intermediaries treated as approved representatives?
- Are unapproved contacts excluded from meetings, lists, or referrals?
- Are relationships tolerated while private but obstructed when they become protective or consequential?
- Does pressure rise when a relationship can produce:
  - evidence;
  - publication;
  - funding;
  - legal action;
  - institutional access;
  - political coalition;
  - escape;
  - durable solidarity?
- Are substitutes offered that provide contact without material protection?
- Is social contact being mistaken for social safety?
- Are relationship effects visible even where individual targeting is difficult to prove?

The system may tolerate affection.

It may object to consequence.

## 10. 🏛️ What Role Do Embassies And Consulates Play?  

Diplomatic missions perform legitimate functions.

Their involvement should not be presumed coercive.

It should not be treated as unimaginable either.

Ask whether embassies or consulates are documented or credibly reported to have participated in:

- community outreach;
- event monitoring;
- political reporting;
- passport restriction;
- document delay;
- visa pressure;
- welfare checks;
- return encouragement;
- representative selection;
- informal warnings;
- contact with relatives;
- reporting on dissidents;
- pressure through cultural or religious institutions;
- facilitation of surveillance;
- requests to host-state authorities;
- assistance with extradition or return;
- coordination with state-aligned organisations.

Ask:

- What is the stated diplomatic purpose?
- What conduct is actually documented?
- Is the activity lawful diplomatic observation, political engagement, intelligence collection, or coercion?
- Is the mission acting under central instruction?
- Are staff linked to security institutions?
- Are community organisations required to report contacts?
- Does refusal of consular engagement create practical harm?
- Are passport or civil-document services being used as leverage?
- Has the host state restricted, expelled, prosecuted, or warned diplomatic personnel?
- What evidence would distinguish ordinary engagement from coercive monitoring?

An embassy contact is not proof of intelligence direction.

A diplomatic title is not proof of innocence.

## 11. 🎭 What Role Do Proxies And Intermediaries Play?  

Transnational pressure may travel through people and organisations outside formal state structures.

Possible intermediaries include:

- community leaders;
- religious bodies;
- cultural associations;
- student organisations;
- business networks;
- charities;
- political groups;
- state-aligned media;
- private investigators;
- contractors;
- lobbyists;
- organised crime;
- militias;
- family elders;
- employers;
- landlords;
- educational institutions;
- online influencers.

Ask:

- Is the intermediary formally directed?
- Is it funded by the state?
- Is it ideologically aligned?
- Is it acting opportunistically?
- Is it protecting its own status?
- Is it responding to informal expectation?
- Is it independently pursuing a factional dispute?
- Is it repeating information supplied by another actor?
- Does it possess information it should not plausibly have?
- Is it performing welfare, engagement, or safeguarding functions that also generate intelligence?
- Can the target refuse contact safely?
- Does the intermediary control access to institutions?
- Are warnings direct, coded, reputational, or administrative?
- Is there evidence of tasking, reporting, payment, instruction, or consequence?
- Could the same conduct arise from gossip, prejudice, internal politics, or personal conflict?

Do not turn every unpleasant community actor into an agent.

Do not make formal tasking the only recognisable form of state influence.

## 12. 🔗 What Cross-Border Cooperation Matters?  

A state may extend reach through other institutions.

Ask whether the relevant pathway involves:

- intelligence sharing;
- policing agreements;
- extradition;
- deportation;
- rendition;
- border cooperation;
- regional security organisations;
- shared watchlists;
- mutual legal assistance;
- migration partnerships;
- sanctions systems;
- airline data;
- passenger-name records;
- biometric systems;
- shared identity infrastructure;
- financial compliance;
- cloud providers;
- telecommunications firms;
- spyware vendors;
- data brokers;
- commercial platforms;
- allied diplomatic pressure.

For each route, ask:

- What agreement or practice supports cooperation?
- Is cooperation formal, informal, secret, commercial, or inferred?
- Which state initiates the request?
- Which state performs the intervention?
- Who reviews legality?
- Can the subject discover the request?
- Can inaccurate data be corrected?
- Do corrections propagate across systems?
- Are political offences excluded from cooperation?
- Are asylum status or refugee protections respected?
- Are security labels accepted without independent testing?
- Has cooperation continued despite documented abuse?
- Does a powerful ally receive access unavailable to others?
- Is one state effectively outsourcing conduct it could not lawfully perform at home?

Shared capability does not automatically prove shared intent.

The route still requires evidence.

## 13. 🏠 How Does The Host State Respond?  

Host-state response is not an afterthought.

It is part of the capability environment.

Ask whether the host state:

- receives complaints;
- records them accurately;
- investigates;
- prosecutes;
- issues public warnings;
- provides protective security;
- restricts foreign officials;
- expels diplomats;
- regulates contractors;
- reviews data sharing;
- protects asylum status;
- prevents forced return;
- offers safe reporting routes;
- informs affected communities;
- corrects harmful records;
- minimises the conduct;
- redirects the complaint;
- treats it as private community conflict;
- prioritises diplomatic or commercial relationships;
- shares data with the suspected actor;
- leaves the subject to confront the risk alone.

Ask:

- Which institution owns protection?
- Which institution owns investigation?
- Are intelligence concerns used to deny disclosure?
- Are victims treated as unreliable because the mechanism is difficult to prove?
- Are communities expected to self-police?
- Are complaints fragmented across police, security, immigration, regulators, and diplomatic bodies?
- Does each institution deny ownership because another holds part of the route?
- Are allied-state actions described more cautiously than adversary actions?
- Does strategic dependence produce selective blindness?
- Are safeguards available in practice to disabled, isolated, poor, undocumented, or traumatised people?

A host state may not have initiated the pressure.

It may still enable, ignore, or fail to interrupt it.

## 14. 🛡️ What Safeguards Exist?  

Identify both formal safeguards and their practical operation.

Possible safeguards include:

- judicial review;
- independent courts;
- parliamentary oversight;
- data-protection rights;
- freedom-of-information routes;
- inspectorates;
- ombuds institutions;
- independent regulators;
- audit requirements;
- warrant procedures;
- retention limits;
- source and provenance requirements;
- correction rights;
- appeal rights;
- asylum protections;
- diplomatic restrictions;
- whistleblower protection;
- press freedom;
- civil-society monitoring;
- technical transparency;
- human review;
- notice duties.

Ask:

- Can the subject discover that a decision was made?
- Can they identify the responsible institution?
- Can they inspect the evidence?
- Can they challenge the legal basis?
- Can they correct inaccurate data?
- Does correction travel to every recipient?
- Can they obtain an independent review?
- Are emergency or security exemptions swallowing the safeguard?
- Are proceedings accessible?
- Are costs prohibitive?
- Are deadlines realistic?
- Are secrecy and jurisdiction preventing remedy?
- Are safeguards available before harm or only afterward?
- Have courts or regulators actually constrained the capability?
- Are public findings implemented?
- Is retaliation for complaint prevented?

A safeguard that cannot be reached, understood, or enforced may be decorative.

## 15. 🧪 What Evidence Would Establish Implementation?  

A capability may exist in theory or procurement without entering an operational workflow.

Ask what evidence would show actual implementation.

Possible evidence includes:

- procurement documents;
- product specifications;
- contracts;
- licences;
- budgets;
- training materials;
- internal policy;
- operating procedures;
- data-protection assessments;
- audit records;
- job descriptions;
- technical integrations;
- system diagrams;
- user manuals;
- official testimony;
- court disclosure;
- authenticated leaks;
- repeated institutional wording;
- evidence that outputs were received and acted upon.

Ask:

- Was the capability merely purchased?
- Was it installed?
- Was it configured?
- Was data populated?
- Were users trained?
- Was it connected to another system?
- Did it produce an output?
- Was the output available to decision-makers?
- Was its use authorised?
- Was use limited to a stated purpose?
- Did the purpose expand?
- Was the system suspended, discontinued, or replaced?
- Does current documentation describe actual use or vendor possibility?

Procurement is stronger than imagination.

It is still not the same as deployment in a particular case.

## 16. 🧷 What Evidence Would Establish Attribution?  

Attribution asks who performed, directed, enabled, or knowingly supported the conduct.

Possible evidence includes:

- official instructions;
- command records;
- access logs;
- payment trails;
- contracts;
- diplomatic correspondence;
- court findings;
- technical infrastructure links;
- verified operator accounts;
- authenticated communications;
- witness testimony;
- repeated operational patterns;
- admissions;
- state-linked resources;
- coordination records.

Ask:

- Does the evidence identify an individual, agency, ministry, contractor, proxy, or state?
- Is the attribution direct or inferential?
- Does technical infrastructure prove control, access, prior use, or merely proximity?
- Could infrastructure have been compromised or reused?
- Is the actor state-directed, state-tolerated, state-linked, or independently aligned?
- Does the evidence establish central instruction?
- Could the conduct reflect local initiative?
- Is the attribution judicially tested?
- Is it an intelligence assessment without published evidence?
- Are confidence levels disclosed?
- Are competing attributions credible?
- Is geopolitical convenience substituting for evidence?
- Is “state-linked” being used so broadly that it hides uncertainty?

Attribution should stop at the narrowest actor the evidence can support.

## 17. 🔬 What Evidence Would Establish Causation?  

Causation asks whether the identified mechanism produced or materially contributed to the observed outcome.

Ask:

- Did the decision follow the relevant data access, classification, request, or warning?
- Is there a timestamped sequence?
- Is the disputed record referenced in the decision?
- Did the actor receiving the output have authority to act?
- Is there evidence that they relied on it?
- Would the outcome likely have occurred without it?
- Are there alternative causes?
- Did several mechanisms combine?
- Was the harm direct, cumulative, probabilistic, or administrative?
- Did correction alter the outcome?
- Did removing the suspected mechanism change the pattern?
- Are comparable cases treated similarly?
- Is the observed outcome too broad to attribute to one intervention?

The existence of a mechanism and a matching harm may establish plausibility.

It does not automatically establish causation.

The system may alter probabilities rather than issue one visible command.

That still requires evidence.

## 18. 🔎 What Rival Explanations Remain?  

Rival explanations are not a dismissal ritual.

They are part of finding the real mechanism.

Possible alternatives include:

- ordinary bugs;
- administrative error;
- stale data;
- mistaken identity;
- commercial tracking;
- platform recommendation;
- employer caution;
- bank de-risking;
- resource scarcity;
- prejudice;
- gossip;
- family conflict;
- factional politics;
- interpersonal retaliation;
- criminal conduct;
- independent proxy action;
- poor institutional communication;
- unrelated policy change;
- coincidence;
- trauma-related interpretation;
- several causes operating at once.

Ask:

- Which alternative explains the same observations?
- Which requires fewer assumptions?
- Which has better access to the relevant data?
- Which better fits timing?
- Which explains repeated wording?
- Which explains cross-institutional effects?
- Which explains only part of the pattern?
- Could one ordinary failure and one malicious act coexist?
- Could commercial and state systems interact without central coordination?
- Could a community actor be using state rhetoric for local purposes?
- Could the institution be over-complying rather than following direct instruction?

A complicated environment may contain several causes.

The model should not demand one master explanation.

## 19. 🧯 What Would We Expect To See If The Claim Were Wrong?  

Every meaningful hypothesis needs disproof conditions.

Ask what evidence would count against the proposed mechanism.

Examples include:

- the actor lacked access to the required data;
- the capability did not exist during the relevant period;
- the decision predated the alleged trigger;
- logs show no relevant access;
- another institution independently produced the outcome;
- the technical artefact is inconsistent with the proposed tool;
- the suspected system was not operational;
- the actor named had no authority over the decision;
- the same outcome occurred across cases lacking the proposed connection;
- a correction changed nothing because another cause controlled the outcome;
- an alleged proxy acted against state preference;
- the repeated language came from a public template rather than shared intelligence;
- testimony was based on one recycled source;
- later evidence contradicts the original attribution.

Ask:

- What result would make us lower confidence?
- What evidence would cause us to abandon the hypothesis?
- Are we defining the theory so broadly that nothing can disprove it?
- Are contradictory findings being absorbed as further proof of sophistication?
- Does every failure of prediction become a reason to feed the model more data?

A model that cannot lose is not an evidentiary model.

It is a captivity device.

## 20. 📏 What Confidence Is Justified?  

Confidence should attach to each proposition separately.

Record confidence for:

- **Capability**
- **Access**
- **Intent**
- **Implementation**
- **Documented use**
- **Attribution**
- **Causation**
- **Pattern or policy**
- **Current relevance**

Ask:

- What is established?
- What is strongly corroborated?
- What is credibly reported?
- What is a reasonable inference?
- What is only a plausible capability?
- What remains unsupported or speculative?
- Are several sources genuinely independent?
- Does the claim depend on secret evidence?
- Has the finding been judicially tested?
- Is the source describing direct experience or wider attribution?
- Has confidence changed over time?
- What evidence gap matters most?

Do not write:

> This is a high-confidence country.

Write:

> There is high confidence that the capability exists, moderate confidence that the named institution had access during the relevant period, and insufficient public evidence to attribute this incident.

Precision about uncertainty strengthens the claim.

## 21. 🩺 What Does The Study Mean For The Reader?  

A state study should not end by making the threat feel total.

Ask what practical value follows.

Possible outputs include:

- one record worth preserving;
- one institution worth identifying;
- one data-sharing route worth questioning;
- one consular dependency worth planning around;
- one trusted contact requiring an alternative route;
- one correction that must be propagated;
- one host-state duty that should be invoked;
- one warning that should be verified directly;
- one unsafe intermediary that should not receive further data;
- one specialist route for legal or technical support.

Ask:

- What is the smallest testable claim?
- What can be protected without destroying function?
- What should not be confronted impulsively?
- What evidence can be preserved safely?
- Which relationship is essential?
- Which route can be diversified?
- What can remain unresolved?
- When should investigation stop for the day?
- Does the proposed defensive step increase isolation?
- Does it remove care, income, accessibility, or ordinary life?

The purpose of capability analysis is to increase choice.

It should not require the reader to become a permanent intelligence service devoted to their own captivity.

## 📋 Reusable State-Study Checklist  

### Scope  

- [ ] The exact actor or institution is named.
- [ ] The relevant territory or jurisdiction is identified.
- [ ] The period covered is stated.
- [ ] Historical and current findings are separated.
- [ ] Government, state, population, and diaspora are not collapsed.

### Conduct  

- [ ] The documented conduct is described precisely.
- [ ] Incident, pattern, programme, policy, and structural practice are distinguished.
- [ ] Targets are identified without overgeneralisation.
- [ ] Domestic and transnational conduct are separated.

### Capability And Access  

- [ ] Relevant capabilities are named.
- [ ] Required inputs are identified.
- [ ] Access is attributed separately from capability.
- [ ] Technical, legal, and practical limits are stated.
- [ ] Observable traces are identified.

### Infrastructure  

- [ ] Relevant laws, systems, contracts, and institutions are mapped.
- [ ] The decision point is identified.
- [ ] Fragmented responsibility is examined.
- [ ] Capability drift and emergency logic are considered.

### Intent And Use  

- [ ] Political incentive is described without being treated as proof.
- [ ] Implementation evidence is separated from possession.
- [ ] Documented use is separated from general capability.
- [ ] Attribution is no broader than the evidence.
- [ ] Causation is addressed separately.

### Relationships  

- [ ] Family, intimate, legal, professional, religious, financial, and political relationships are considered.
- [ ] Observation is distinguished from classification or intervention.
- [ ] Consequential relationships and protected bridges are identified.
- [ ] Diaspora communities are not treated as state extensions.

### Cross-Border Route  

- [ ] Embassy and consular roles are examined.
- [ ] Proxies and intermediaries are examined.
- [ ] State direction, alignment, and independent action are distinguished.
- [ ] Contractors, vendors, allies, and regional systems are considered.
- [ ] Host-state protection, facilitation, and neglect are examined.

### Evidence  

- [ ] Primary and secondary sources are distinguished.
- [ ] Source independence is checked.
- [ ] Confidence attaches to propositions.
- [ ] Evidence gaps are explicit.
- [ ] Rival explanations are preserved.
- [ ] Disproof conditions are stated.

### Reader Safety  

- [ ] The node does not encourage confrontation.
- [ ] The node does not encourage amateur counter-intelligence.
- [ ] The node does not require constant monitoring.
- [ ] Practical implications preserve care, access, evidence, and relationships.
- [ ] The study ends with one or more proportionate questions or protective steps.

## 🚫 What These Questions Must Not Become  

This instrument is not:

- a loyalty questionnaire;
- a ranking system for nationalities;
- a template for community suspicion;
- a demand to prove innocence;
- a mechanism for treating family or association as guilt;
- an excuse to inflate every capability into an operation;
- a way to dismiss testimony because attribution remains uncertain;
- a substitute for jurisdiction-specific legal advice;
- a reason to confront suspected actors;
- a demand for continuous investigation;
- a test the reader must complete before being allowed to protect themselves.

The same framework used to criticise relationship scoring must not quietly reproduce it.

The purpose is to inspect institutional power.

It is not to build a better plant.

## 🔭 Compact Definition  

**Questions To Ask Of Every State** is the standard research and drafting instrument for the Regime and State Capability Studies branch.

It asks:

- who acted;
- during which period;
- what conduct is documented;
- what capability exists;
- who has access;
- what infrastructure supports use;
- what relationships are affected;
- what cross-border route matters;
- how the host state responds;
- what safeguards function;
- what evidence establishes implementation, attribution, and causation;
- what rival explanations and disproof conditions remain;
- what confidence each proposition has earned.

The governing discipline is simple:

> Ask the same questions of every state.  
> Do not force the evidence to give the same answer.
