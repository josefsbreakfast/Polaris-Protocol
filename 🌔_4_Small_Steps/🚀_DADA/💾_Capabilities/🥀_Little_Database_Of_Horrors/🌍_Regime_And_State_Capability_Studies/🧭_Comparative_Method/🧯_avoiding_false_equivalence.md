# 🧯 Avoiding False Equivalence  
**First created:** 2026-07-23 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-23  
*A comparative safeguard for identifying recurring mechanisms without pretending that all states, systems, harms, safeguards, or evidentiary records are the same.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

Comparative analysis can fail in two opposite directions.

The first failure is flattening:

> Every state uses surveillance, therefore every state is basically the same.

The second failure is exceptionalism:

> This is a democracy, an ally, or a familiar institution, therefore comparable mechanisms cannot matter here.

Both failures protect power.

Flattening erases differences in:

- scale;
- violence;
- legality;
- political structure;
- institutional control;
- available remedies;
- target breadth;
- evidentiary strength.

Exceptionalism turns elections, alliances, courts, regulators, or friendly rhetoric into presumed innocence.

A serious comparative method must resist both.

The purpose of comparison is not to force moral or operational symmetry.

It is to ask consistent questions while allowing the evidence to produce different answers.

> Equal scrutiny does not require equal conclusions.

---

## 🌍 What False Equivalence Is  

False equivalence occurs when two cases are treated as meaningfully alike despite material differences in:

- the proposition being examined;
- the time period;
- the scale of conduct;
- the severity of harm;
- the strength of evidence;
- the degree of state direction;
- the availability of safeguards;
- the ability to challenge the action;
- the political environment;
- the likelihood of correction.

It may appear as:

- comparing documented assassination with hypothetical monitoring;
- comparing one rogue official with formal policy;
- comparing domestic data collection with overseas abduction;
- comparing a purchased tool with verified deployment;
- comparing an appealable decision with disappearance;
- comparing an isolated incident with a sustained transnational campaign;
- comparing government rhetoric with operational orders;
- comparing democratic oversight on paper with authoritarian impunity in practice without checking whether either actually functions.

The presence of one shared feature does not make two systems equivalent.

Two states may both possess spyware.

That fact alone says nothing about whether they:

- deploy it;
- against whom;
- under what authority;
- at what scale;
- with what consequences;
- subject to which remedies.

---

## 🧭 What Comparison Is For  

Useful comparison can reveal:

- recurring tools;
- common vendors;
- transferable repression models;
- shared administrative pathways;
- cross-border cooperation;
- differences in restraint;
- differences in consequence;
- institutional learning;
- selective enforcement;
- host-state complicity;
- safeguards that reduce harm;
- safeguards that exist mainly as decoration.

The question is not:

> Which state is worst?

It is:

> Which mechanism exists here, how is it used, what surrounds it, and what happens to the person affected?

Comparison should sharpen distinctions.

It should not produce a single undifferentiated category called:

> alarming government behaviour.

---

## 🔍 Compare The Same Proposition  

Before comparing two states, identify the exact proposition.

For example:

- possession of spyware;
- documented overseas deployment;
- family coercion;
- use of embassies for monitoring;
- misuse of extradition;
- relationship mapping;
- suppression of lawful association;
- host-state facilitation;
- absence of effective remedy.

Do not compare:

- one state’s documented use;
- with another state’s technical capability;

and then describe both as equivalent repression.

Do not compare:

- one state’s official policy;
- with another state’s alleged local practice;

without stating the difference.

Do not compare:

- one state’s current conduct;
- with another state’s historical record;

without marking the period.

The comparison must be proposition-matched.

If the proposition changes, the comparison changes.

---

## 📅 Compare The Same Period  

States change.

Relevant shifts may include:

- elections;
- coups;
- revolutions;
- war;
- emergency government;
- succession;
- reform;
- democratic backsliding;
- institutional collapse;
- new legislation;
- technological acquisition;
- sanctions;
- diplomatic realignment.

A country may have:

- one historical period of extensive repression;
- a later reform period;
- retained technical capability;
- uncertain current deployment.

Another may have:

- relatively limited historical use;
- rapid recent expansion;
- weaker safeguards than before.

Comparisons should state:

- the period examined;
- whether the evidence is current;
- whether institutions changed;
- whether conduct persisted;
- whether reform was implemented;
- whether old infrastructure remains.

A timeless national character is not an analytical category.

---

## ⚙️ Similar Tool, Different Political Environment  

The same technology can produce very different outcomes.

A shared system may be used for:

- fraud prevention;
- serious-crime investigation;
- border administration;
- protest monitoring;
- ethnic classification;
- political screening;
- exile targeting;
- relationship disruption;
- forced return.

The technology does not determine the political use by itself.

Relevant surrounding factors include:

- law;
- mandate;
- oversight;
- target definition;
- data quality;
- human review;
- appeal;
- secrecy;
- enforcement culture;
- emergency logic;
- political rhetoric;
- practical impunity.

Two institutions may use similar tools.

One may:

- provide notice;
- preserve correction rights;
- limit retention;
- permit judicial challenge.

Another may:

- conceal use;
- deny access;
- punish complaint;
- use family members as leverage;
- produce detention or disappearance.

Shared infrastructure does not erase political context.

Political context also does not make the infrastructure irrelevant.

---

## 🎯 Similar Outcome, Different Mechanism  

The same adverse outcome may arise through different routes.

A person may lose:

- employment;
- banking;
- visibility;
- institutional access;
- travel;
- community support;
- legal opportunity.

Possible causes include:

- direct state instruction;
- sanctions compliance;
- border policy;
- platform moderation;
- employer fear;
- commercial risk scoring;
- community gatekeeping;
- bank de-risking;
- administrative error;
- prejudice;
- an independent proxy;
- resource scarcity;
- several mechanisms operating together.

Similar harm does not prove identical architecture.

The correct comparison asks:

- What was the decision point?
- Which actor had authority?
- What information was used?
- Was there direct instruction?
- Was there over-compliance?
- Was the route commercial, diplomatic, legal, or informal?
- Was the outcome intended or incidental?
- Could it be challenged?

The person may experience the same practical exclusion.

The responsibility chain may differ substantially.

---

## 🧱 Scale Must Remain Visible  

Comparison should preserve scale.

Ask:

- How many people were affected?
- Over what period?
- Across how many institutions?
- Was conduct geographically limited?
- Was it systematic?
- Was it repeated?
- Was it centrally directed?
- Was it an emergency measure or routine practice?
- Did it target one individual, one organisation, one minority, or a broad population?

A single unlawful database query is not equivalent to a national system of political classification.

A national system of political classification is not equivalent to a campaign of disappearance and assassination.

All may be serious.

They are not the same kind of serious.

Scale changes:

- institutional meaning;
- foreseeability;
- responsibility;
- protective response;
- legal analysis.

---

## 🩸 Severity Must Remain Visible  

Relevant harms may include:

- inconvenience;
- delay;
- reputational damage;
- exclusion;
- financial restriction;
- passport denial;
- family pressure;
- detention;
- torture;
- disappearance;
- forced return;
- assault;
- assassination.

Comparison should not flatten these into one category of:

> interference.

Administrative friction may be the beginning of a coercive pathway.

It may also remain administrative friction.

Lethal repression requires language that preserves its gravity.

Likewise, the existence of more severe conduct elsewhere should not make lower-level coercion acceptable.

The comparison should avoid both:

- exaggerating lesser conduct into equivalence;
- minimising lesser conduct because something worse exists.

---

## 🏛️ Political Structure Matters  

Relevant distinctions may include:

- constitutional democracy;
- electoral authoritarianism;
- monarchy;
- military rule;
- one-party state;
- occupation;
- fragmented sovereignty;
- federal system;
- militia governance;
- transitional authority;
- collapsed or contested state.

Political structure affects:

- who controls the capability;
- how policy is authorised;
- whether institutions contest one another;
- whether courts can intervene;
- whether the press can investigate;
- whether officials can be removed;
- whether targets can organise lawfully;
- whether abuse is hidden, tolerated, or openly celebrated.

Regime labels should not substitute for evidence.

But political structure should not be treated as irrelevant context either.

The same mechanism may become more dangerous where:

- authority is concentrated;
- opposition is criminalised;
- detention is arbitrary;
- courts are captured;
- family punishment is normalised;
- host states defer.

---

## ⚖️ Lawfulness Is Not The Same As Legitimacy  

Conduct may be:

- lawful under domestic law;
- unlawful under domestic law;
- lawful but disproportionate;
- formally authorised but discriminatory;
- contrary to international obligations;
- beyond effective review;
- covertly outside the stated legal framework.

Do not treat legal authorisation as proof of legitimacy.

Do not treat illegality as proof of systematic state policy without further evidence.

Ask:

- What law authorises the conduct?
- Is the law accessible?
- Is it specific?
- Is it foreseeable?
- Is it reviewable?
- Is it compatible with rights obligations?
- Is the stated legal basis used consistently?
- Can the subject challenge it?
- Are emergency powers normalised?
- Are protected activities being reframed as risk?

A lawful database can still support abusive decisions.

An unlawful act may still be isolated rather than structural.

The legal status is one dimension, not the whole conclusion.

---

## 🛡️ Safeguards Must Be Tested, Not Admired  

Democratic and hybrid systems may possess:

- courts;
- regulators;
- ombuds institutions;
- parliamentary committees;
- data-protection rights;
- freedom-of-information laws;
- investigative journalism;
- electoral accountability;
- civil society.

These matter.

They may reduce harm.

They may also fail because of:

- secrecy;
- delay;
- cost;
- standing rules;
- national-security exemptions;
- jurisdictional fragmentation;
- inaccessible procedures;
- institutional deference;
- retaliation;
- weak enforcement.

Do not write:

> The state has courts, therefore misuse is unlikely.

Ask:

- Can the person reach the court?
- Can they discover the decision?
- Can they inspect the evidence?
- Can the court compel disclosure?
- Can the remedy arrive before irreversible harm?
- Are security bodies practically constrained?
- Are rulings implemented?
- Do corrections propagate?
- Are disabled, isolated, poor, migrant, or traumatised people able to use the route?

A safeguard should be credited for what it does.

Not for the dignity of its letterhead.

---

## 🚪 Remedy Changes The Comparison  

Two identical intrusions may have different consequences where one system provides:

- notice;
- correction;
- review;
- compensation;
- deletion;
- judicial restraint;
- institutional accountability.

and another provides:

- secrecy;
- denial;
- retaliation;
- detention;
- no appeal;
- no correction;
- no safe reporting route.

The initial capability may be similar.

The total authoritarian effect may not be.

Comparison should therefore ask:

- Can the person know?
- Can the person challenge?
- Can the person correct?
- Can the person remain safe while challenging?
- Can the institution be compelled to stop?
- Can the harm be repaired?
- Does remedy depend on publicity, wealth, citizenship, or political status?

A system that makes challenge impossible should not be treated as equivalent to one where meaningful correction occurs.

A system with formal remedy that never functions should not receive full democratic credit.

---

## 🏛️ Democratic-State Temptations  

Democratic states may possess extensive capability to:

- integrate data;
- map associations;
- screen borders;
- classify risk;
- conduct covert disruption;
- monitor protest;
- share intelligence;
- restrict accounts;
- pressure platforms;
- use emergency powers;
- rely on private contractors;
- outsource judgement.

Elections do not make those mechanisms harmless.

Democratic-state risks may include:

- exceptional powers becoming routine;
- national-security secrecy;
- weak appeal;
- political profiling through proxies;
- administrative punishment without trial;
- over-compliance by institutions;
- data errors spreading across systems;
- border powers applied with limited review;
- friendly foreign states receiving privileged access;
- covert action escaping ordinary democratic scrutiny.

The correct response is not:

> Democracy is fake, therefore all systems are identical.

It is:

> Democratic institutions possess serious coercive capability and must be assessed by whether safeguards, contestation, and correction work in practice.

Democratic structure may reduce risk.

It does not abolish temptation.

---

## 🤝 Allied-State Exceptionalism  

Strategic allies often receive softer analytical language.

Adversaries may be described as:

- directing;
- targeting;
- coercing;
- interfering.

Allies may be described as:

- engaging;
- coordinating;
- managing;
- protecting;
- sharing concerns.

The underlying conduct may be similar.

Ask whether language changes because of:

- intelligence relationships;
- trade;
- energy;
- defence cooperation;
- migration agreements;
- diplomatic convenience;
- ideological familiarity;
- domestic political sensitivity.

Warning signs include:

- accepting allied security claims without evidence;
- demanding impossible proof for allied misconduct;
- treating adversary motive as self-proving;
- overlooking host-state complicity;
- using “community engagement” where “political monitoring” would be used for an adversary;
- describing coercion as informal misunderstanding;
- treating strategic importance as evidence of restraint.

Equal scrutiny means applying the same evidentiary questions.

It does not mean pre-deciding that the findings must be equal.

---

## 🎭 Adversary Inflation  

The opposite distortion also matters.

A state with a documented history of repression may be blamed too quickly for:

- unrelated cyber activity;
- ordinary platform failure;
- independent criminal conduct;
- factional disputes;
- commercial surveillance;
- administrative error;
- actions by loosely aligned actors.

A frightening reputation can make an allegation feel self-authenticating.

Ask:

- Is the relevant actor identified?
- Did it possess access?
- Was the capability operational?
- Does the technical evidence fit?
- Is there a direct route?
- Is the attribution being driven by geopolitical expectation?
- Are rival explanations being considered?
- Is one government assessment being repeated as independent consensus?
- Is historical conduct substituting for current evidence?

A state may be capable, hostile, and previously responsible.

The present claim still needs to earn its name.

---

## 🕸️ State, Population, And Diaspora Must Remain Separate  

Never collapse:

- government into population;
- ruling party into nationality;
- security service into ethnicity;
- state policy into religion;
- diplomatic mission into diaspora;
- diaspora identity into political loyalty.

This matters especially where communities abroad are:

- targets of surveillance;
- divided in political opinion;
- dependent on consular services;
- vulnerable to family pressure;
- wrongly treated by host states as extensions of a foreign government.

A study of Israeli state capability must not treat Jews abroad as Israeli state infrastructure.

A study of Iranian state capability must not treat Iranian diaspora communities as regime proxies.

A study of Chinese state capability must not turn Chinese ethnicity into a risk marker.

A study of Russian state operations must not treat Russian-speaking people as state-linked by default.

The comparative method fails if it reproduces the association logic it is criticising.

---

## 🧩 Centralised And Fragmented Systems  

A centralised system may provide:

- clearer command;
- more consistent targeting;
- wider data integration;
- stronger attribution;
- larger-scale implementation.

A fragmented system may involve:

- competing agencies;
- militias;
- parties;
- contractors;
- regional authorities;
- foreign sponsors;
- informal networks;
- contradictory interventions.

Fragmentation may reduce central control.

It may also:

- make abuse harder to challenge;
- obscure the decision-maker;
- allow actors to deny ownership;
- produce cumulative harm;
- create multiple unsafe routes.

Do not assume fragmentation means weakness.

Do not assume coherence means one central database.

The architecture may be coherent enough to harm and fragmented enough to defeat appeal.

---

## 🌐 Domestic And Transnational Conduct Are Not Identical  

A state may conduct severe domestic repression but possess limited overseas reach.

Another may use relatively narrow domestic controls while conducting sophisticated transnational monitoring.

Ask separately:

- What happens within the territory?
- What happens abroad?
- Which capabilities travel?
- Which depend on host-state cooperation?
- Which use family members at home?
- Which use embassies, proxies, platforms, or commercial systems?
- Which require physical reach?
- Which rely on return pressure?

Domestic conduct may support an assessment of intent or institutional habit.

It does not automatically prove overseas deployment.

Transnational conduct may depend more on:

- alliances;
- migration systems;
- diplomatic tolerance;
- contractors;
- weak host-state protection.

---

## 🔗 Shared Vendor Does Not Mean Shared Regime  

Two states may purchase from the same:

- spyware company;
- border contractor;
- biometric provider;
- cloud platform;
- data broker;
- analytics firm.

This may establish:

- shared technical capability;
- vendor diffusion;
- procurement routes;
- common design assumptions;
- transferable risk.

It does not establish:

- identical purpose;
- identical target groups;
- identical thresholds;
- shared operational control;
- equivalent political consequence.

The vendor may also:

- configure systems differently;
- provide different modules;
- retain access;
- support one customer more directly;
- encourage capability drift;
- facilitate cross-customer learning.

Comparison should examine both:

- what is shared;
- what differs after implementation.

A cutting from the same plant may grow differently in another pot.

It is still worth checking who sold the cuttings.

---

## 📊 Evidence Strength Must Remain Visible  

Do not compare:

- an established court finding;
- with a plausible hypothesis;

as though they are two equal cases.

Do not use identical certainty language where:

- one case has authenticated records;
- another has one anonymous allegation;
- one has technical forensics;
- another has geopolitical inference;
- one has repeated independent testimony;
- another has circular reporting.

The comparative table should preserve:

- source quality;
- source independence;
- confidence;
- gaps;
- dispute;
- what is established;
- what remains plausible.

Equal scrutiny requires the same evidence rules.

It does not require pretending that all evidence is equally strong.

## 📐 Dimensions For Responsible Comparison  

Country and regional studies should compare across multiple dimensions.

### Political Structure  

- concentration of authority;
- institutional contestation;
- opposition rights;
- leadership accountability.

### Legal Environment  

- clarity of authority;
- judicial independence;
- emergency powers;
- rights protection;
- practical enforceability.

### Surveillance Capacity  

- technical sophistication;
- data centralisation;
- platform access;
- biometric and border infrastructure;
- commercial partnerships.

### Targeting  

- target categories;
- breadth;
- identity-based classification;
- lawful activity treated as risk;
- family or associative targeting.

### Severity  

- administrative friction;
- financial harm;
- travel restriction;
- detention;
- torture;
- forced return;
- lethal violence.

### Scale  

- isolated;
- repeated;
- programmatic;
- national;
- transnational;
- structural.

### State Direction  

- individual misconduct;
- local initiative;
- proxy action;
- tolerated practice;
- central instruction;
- formal policy.

### Transparency  

- notice;
- published law;
- public reporting;
- auditability;
- access to records.

### Remedy  

- correction;
- appeal;
- court review;
- independent investigation;
- compensation;
- protection from retaliation.

### Host-State Environment  

- protection;
- investigation;
- prosecution;
- diplomatic restraint;
- selective blindness;
- data sharing;
- return facilitation.

### Evidence  

- established;
- corroborated;
- credibly reported;
- inferred;
- plausible;
- unsupported.

No one dimension should be allowed to stand in for all the others.

## 🧪 A Practical Comparison Test  

Before placing two cases side by side, ask:

- Are we comparing the same proposition?
- Are the periods comparable?
- Is the evidence similarly strong?
- Is the scale similar?
- Is the severity similar?
- Is state direction established in both?
- Are we comparing capability with capability, or capability with conduct?
- Are the target groups comparable?
- Are remedies available in both?
- Are formal safeguards effective?
- Is one case domestic and the other transnational?
- Is one centralised and the other fragmented?
- Are geopolitical preferences shaping the language?
- Are populations or diasporas being collapsed into governments?
- Would we use the same wording if the ally and adversary labels were reversed?

If several answers are no, the comparison may still be useful.

It must be framed as a difference, not an equivalence.

---

## 📝 Disciplined Comparative Language  

Useful wording includes:

- “Both states possess the capability, but documented use differs.”
- “The mechanism is similar; the scale and consequences are not.”
- “The cases share a vendor but not a demonstrated operational purpose.”
- “One case concerns formal policy; the other concerns alleged local practice.”
- “Both systems create weak appeal routes, although only one has documented lethal enforcement.”
- “The host-state response differs materially.”
- “The evidence is substantially stronger in the first case.”
- “The comparison identifies a transferable pathway rather than equivalent conduct.”
- “Formal safeguards exist in both jurisdictions but function differently.”
- “The same administrative category produces different risks because the surrounding political systems differ.”
- “No equivalence is claimed between the severity of the cases.”

Avoid:

- “Everyone does it.”
- “They are all the same.”
- “This is no different from…”
- “At least it is not as bad as…”
- “Because the state is democratic, this cannot be authoritarian.”
- “Because the state is authoritarian, it must be responsible.”
- “The shared technology proves shared intent.”
- “The outcome is similar, therefore the mechanism is the same.”

## 🧯 Common False-Equivalence Failures  

### Shared Tool Fallacy  

Same vendor or technology becomes proof of equivalent political use.

### Shared Outcome Fallacy  

Similar harm becomes proof of identical mechanism.

### Regime Label Fallacy  

“Democracy” or “authoritarian state” substitutes for evidence.

### Ally Discount  

Conduct by an ally receives euphemism or reduced scrutiny.

### Adversary Inflation  

A hostile state’s reputation substitutes for attribution.

### Severity Collapse  

Administrative exclusion and lethal repression are grouped without distinction.

### Scale Collapse  

One incident and national policy are treated alike.

### Safeguard Formalism  

The existence of courts or regulators is treated as proof of effective remedy.

### Historical Collapse  

Past and present practice are merged.

### Population Collapse  

Government, nationality, ethnicity, religion, and diaspora are treated as interchangeable.

### Capability-Conduct Collapse  

Possession in one case is compared with deployment in another.

### Evidence Collapse  

A credible hypothesis is presented alongside an established finding without visible confidence differences.

These failures may make a comparative section look neat.

They also make it useless.

---

## 🌱 Asymmetry Is A Valid Result  

A disciplined comparison may conclude that:

- one state conducts extensive documented lethal transnational repression;
- another primarily uses family and consular pressure;
- another possesses advanced capability but has limited public evidence of overseas deployment;
- another relies heavily on private contractors;
- another depends on allied-state cooperation;
- another produces fragmented proxy harm without clear central direction;
- another has meaningful safeguards that interrupt abuse;
- another has formal safeguards that repeatedly fail;
- another remains too poorly documented for a confident conclusion.

These are not failures of consistency.

They are the findings.

The method is equal.

The conclusions are allowed to be uneven.

---

## 🩺 Why This Matters For DADA  

False equivalence can harm the reader in several ways.

It may:

- make every threat appear total;
- encourage the strongest imaginable threat model;
- erase practical distinctions;
- direct the person toward the wrong protective measure;
- obscure the responsible institution;
- make democratic remedies seem pointless;
- make authoritarian risk seem ordinary;
- encourage suspicion of whole communities;
- collapse evidence into geopolitical identity.

A useful comparison should help the reader decide:

- which actor matters;
- which route is plausible;
- which safeguard may work;
- which host-state duty is relevant;
- which relationship needs protection;
- which claim can be tested;
- what remains unknown.

The purpose is not to prove that every pot contains Audrey II.

It is to recognise the teeth without accusing the geraniums.

## 📋 Comparative Review Checklist  

### Proposition  

- [ ] The same proposition is being compared.
- [ ] Capability is not being compared with documented conduct.
- [ ] Incident, pattern, programme, and policy are distinguished.

### Time  

- [ ] The periods are stated.
- [ ] Historical and current conduct are separated.
- [ ] Institutional changes are acknowledged.

### Scale And Severity  

- [ ] Scale is visible.
- [ ] Severity is visible.
- [ ] Administrative, coercive, and lethal harms are not collapsed.

### Political And Legal Context  

- [ ] Political structure is described.
- [ ] Legal authority is examined.
- [ ] Formal law is not treated as automatic legitimacy.
- [ ] Regime labels do not substitute for evidence.

### Safeguards  

- [ ] Courts, regulators, press, and oversight are assessed in practice.
- [ ] Remedy, accessibility, delay, and enforcement are considered.
- [ ] Safeguards are neither presumed effective nor dismissed automatically.

### Attribution  

- [ ] State direction is assessed separately.
- [ ] Proxy, contractor, local, and central action are distinguished.
- [ ] Fragmented responsibility is visible.

### Evidence  

- [ ] Confidence levels are comparable.
- [ ] Source independence is checked.
- [ ] Evidence gaps are visible.
- [ ] One established case is not balanced against one speculation as though equivalent.

### Identity  

- [ ] State, government, population, religion, ethnicity, and diaspora remain separate.
- [ ] Diaspora communities are not treated as extensions of the state.

### Geopolitics  

- [ ] Allied conduct is not softened.
- [ ] Adversary conduct is not inflated.
- [ ] Language would survive reversal of the ally/adversary labels.

### Conclusion  

- [ ] Shared mechanisms are identified.
- [ ] material differences remain explicit.
- [ ] No artificial symmetry is imposed.
- [ ] The practical DADA relevance is stated.

## 🚫 What This Node Is Not  

This node is not:

- a demand to avoid comparison;
- a defence of allied states;
- a claim that democratic safeguards always work;
- a claim that regime type is irrelevant;
- a way to minimise administrative coercion;
- a way to normalise lethal repression;
- a requirement to produce moral neutrality;
- a licence for geopolitical both-sidesism;
- a claim that every use of the same technology is equivalent;
- a reason to ignore shared vendors or transferable methods;
- a presumption that lack of evidence means innocence;
- a presumption that authoritarian reputation proves responsibility.

The goal is not false balance.

The goal is accurate difference.

---

## 🧿 Compact Definition  

**Avoiding False Equivalence** is the comparative safeguard that allows recurring capabilities, tools, and pathways to be examined across states without erasing differences in:

- conduct;
- scale;
- severity;
- political structure;
- state direction;
- law;
- safeguards;
- remedy;
- evidentiary strength.

It requires equal scrutiny.

It permits asymmetrical findings.

> Equal scrutiny does not require equal conclusions.
