🧭 Drafting Considerations For Diaspora-Facing Country Capability Studies

A flexible drafting aid for regional case studies within 🥀 Little Database Of Horrors and 🚀 Defence Against Digital Authoritarianism.

⸻

🛰️ Purpose

Diaspora-facing country studies examine how political, legal, administrative, technical, commercial and relational systems may continue to affect people after they leave a country, move between jurisdictions, or rebuild life elsewhere.

They are not intended to provide:

* a complete national history;
* a generic human-rights profile;
* a ranking of repressive states;
* an intelligence assessment;
* a prediction that every diaspora member will be targeted;
* a declaration that every identified capability forms part of one integrated programme.

Their purpose is to help a reader understand:

* why the present political environment exists;
* which actors and institutions matter;
* how domestic control operates;
* which parts of the system can travel across borders;
* how family, documentation, identity and relationships may remain exposed;
* what conduct is documented;
* what remains inference;
* what further evidence would strengthen, narrow or disprove the analysis.

The node should make the relevant theatre legible to a reader who may have little prior knowledge of the country without flattening the country into a regime description.

⸻

🧭 A Shared Spine, Not A Fixed Template

Country nodes should be recognisably related, but they do not need identical headings, proportions or narrative order.

The strongest organising structure may depend upon the case.

A node may be organised principally around:

* a recent constitutional rupture;
* a succession or leadership transition;
* a long-standing court or security system;
* citizenship and statelessness;
* a protest cycle;
* one well-documented exile pathway;
* a cluster of diaspora cases;
* regional rendition or policing;
* a new law or technical infrastructure;
* the political use of family relationships;
* an unusually important archive of humour, slogans or public culture.

Equal questions do not require equal structures or equal conclusions.

A rigid template may obscure the mechanism that is most important in the country being studied. A completely unstructured node may make comparison and evidentiary review difficult.

The aim is therefore:

shared investigative questions, locally appropriate narrative form.

⸻

👥 Begin With The Reader

Establish who may need the node.

Depending on the country, this may include:

* citizens living abroad;
* former or denaturalised citizens;
* refugees and asylum seekers;
* stateless people;
* mixed-nationality families;
* students;
* journalists;
* academics;
* activists;
* former officials or elected representatives;
* artists and writers;
* labour organisers;
* migrant workers;
* relatives, partners and carers;
* nationality, immigration and asylum lawyers;
* host-state officials;
* people dependent upon passports, property, inheritance or consular services.

Do not assume that the diaspora is:

* politically unified;
* formally organised;
* opposed to the government;
* supportive of the government;
* legally homogeneous;
* equally exposed.

Where the term “diaspora” conceals major differences in citizenship, class, ethnicity, religion, migration route or legal status, identify those differences early.

⸻

🌍 Give The Reader Enough Country Context

A Western or general international audience may not know:

* what kind of political system exists;
* whether elected institutions hold meaningful power;
* how succession works;
* which institutions can challenge the executive;
* whether political parties or independent associations are permitted;
* how citizenship is distributed;
* which historical events continue to shape political language;
* why the country possesses a particular international reputation;
* why foreign partners consider it strategically valuable.

Provide enough context to answer:

Why does this machinery exist in this form, and why might outsiders fail to recognise it?

This does not require a comprehensive national history.

Select the political and historical context that explains the current capability environment.

Useful context may include:

* colonial or imperial arrangements;
* dynastic development;
* civil conflict;
* protest movements;
* constitutional bargains;
* coups or attempted coups;
* emergency rule;
* sectarian or regional inequality;
* labour and migration systems;
* oil, port, military or logistical importance;
* foreign-security partnerships;
* major political settlements;
* previous reform or reconciliation efforts;
* the road not taken.

Where a country is widely described through favourable language—such as stability, moderation, tolerance, reform, democracy or strategic reliability—test what that reputation explains and what it conceals.

Positive findings should remain attached to the specific:

* right;
* population;
* institution;
* period;
* remedy.

A positive record in one domain is not credit that can be spent against abuse in another.

⸻

🧭 Country-Facing, Actor-Specific

Use the country name as a reader-facing entrance.

Inside the node, separate relevant actors.

These may include:

* the ruler or presidency;
* royal courts or presidential offices;
* ruling-family branches;
* ministers and cabinets;
* intelligence and internal-security bodies;
* police;
* prosecutors;
* courts;
* border and nationality authorities;
* telecom regulators;
* civil-identity agencies;
* universities;
* licensed media bodies;
* sovereign funds;
* state-linked businesses;
* local contractors;
* foreign vendors;
* embassies and consulates;
* regional policing bodies;
* militias or informal authorities;
* allied governments;
* strategic and military partners.

Do not collapse:

* the country into the government;
* the government into the ruler;
* the ruler into every operational decision;
* the population into the state;
* religion into the state;
* diaspora institutions into covert state activity;
* a ruling family into a single internally coherent actor;
* commercial proximity into operational responsibility.

Actor separation is not a method for dissolving responsibility.

It is a method for locating it.

⸻

👑 Where Power Is Concentrated, Map The Surrounding System

In monarchies and other highly centralised systems, formal authority may sit at the apex while operational reality is shaped by:

* household officials;
* senior relatives;
* private secretaries;
* security briefers;
* military advisers;
* clerics;
* business intermediaries;
* investment managers;
* foreign partners;
* consultants;
* vendors;
* officials who control access and information.

Consider:

* Who reaches the ruler?
* Who controls the diary?
* Who supplies threat assessments?
* Who translates reform into danger?
* Who excludes non-coercive alternatives?
* Who drafts the relevant law?
* Who procures and maintains the capability?
* Who reports that silence means success?
* Who benefits from expanded discretion, budget or status?

Do not presume that every repression programme was personally designed by the person at the apex.

Do not use institutional complexity to absolve the apex of responsibility for the system operating in its name.

Humanising a ruler is not absolution. It is mechanism-finding.

Where relevant, include an institutional-continuity test:

* Which laws survive the ruler?
* Which archives survive?
* Which advisers remain?
* Which security commanders transfer to the successor?
* Which vendors retain their contracts?
* Which databases remain unreviewed?
* Can affected people access, challenge or correct their records?

⸻

🚨 Ask Who Made Repression Appear Necessary

Do not analyse coercion only as an expression of cruelty or ideological hostility.

Investigate how it was presented internally as:

* responsibility;
* stability;
* national protection;
* public order;
* anti-corruption;
* administrative correction;
* counterterrorism;
* protection from foreign agendas;
* defence of social values;
* defence of the ruler or state’s prestige;
* prevention of chaos or sedition.

Ask:

* Who defined the event as a security problem?
* Which evidence was supplied?
* Which explanations were excluded?
* Which peaceful options were dismissed?
* Who benefited from escalation?
* Which agency gained authority?
* Which contractor gained work?
* Which political rival was weakened?
* Who later interpreted public quiet as approval?

This is especially useful where official language converts:

* reform into disorder;
* criticism into incitement;
* journalism into treason;
* political disagreement into phone misuse;
* public speech into misinformation;
* citizenship review into neutral administration.

⸻

🗣️ Preserve The Local Political Language

A country may look politically quiet in English while containing an extensive political conversation in:

* Arabic or another local language;
* dialect;
* slogans;
* jokes;
* songs;
* poetry;
* chants;
* memes;
* stickers;
* voice notes;
* retweets;
* dream narratives;
* religious phrases;
* apparently loyal appeals;
* satire;
* place names;
* ordinary complaints.

These materials help a distant reader understand:

* how people describe injustice;
* what they consider politically possible;
* how loyalty and criticism coexist;
* how risk alters grammar and tone;
* which official terms are being resisted or repurposed;
* how politics moves when formal organising is restricted.

Where possible, preserve:

* original wording;
* transliteration where useful;
* literal translation;
* political or cultural explanation;
* date and setting;
* circulation route;
* response or reprisal;
* uncertainty about provenance.

Do not:

* sanitise the public into policy language;
* invent missing wording;
* treat indirect speech as politically empty;
* assume loyal language is either wholly sincere or wholly false;
* remove humour because it appears unserious.

The relevant question is not merely what was said.

It is:

What forms of saying remained possible under the conditions people inhabited?

⸻

🎭 Reconstruct The Political Theatre

Direct diaspora testimony is valuable but will not always be available.

A node can still help a Western audience understand likely diaspora effects by reconstructing the political theatre from which people migrated.

This may include:

* offences they learned to avoid;
* public punishments they witnessed;
* what happened to writers, retweeters, relatives or publishers;
* what kinds of jokes remained safe enough to circulate;
* which institutions required permission;
* whether public loyalty provided protective cover;
* whether speech differed between languages;
* how passports and return were understood;
* how family members could become administratively reachable;
* what rumours or official warnings shaped conduct;
* which memories are carried by particular towns, protests or generations.

This enables carefully framed conclusions about anticipatory behaviour without claiming direct surveillance in every case.

For example, the node may identify why a person abroad might:

* use a pseudonym;
* avoid photographs;
* separate family and political chats;
* publish only in English;
* avoid official community spaces;
* wait for secure immigration status;
* delete material before travel;
* choose not to research their country publicly;
* avoid returning for funerals or family care.

Such adaptations do not, by themselves, prove an active overseas operation.

They may show that:

migration changes territorial law faster than it changes learned political boundaries.

⸻

🧳 Treat Migration As A Change In Exposure, Not Automatic Exit

Identify what changes when a person leaves and what may remain.

Migration may provide:

* territorial safety;
* wider speech rights;
* independent courts;
* asylum protection;
* access to journalists, lawyers and organisations;
* distance from immediate domestic arrest.

It may not end:

* nationality jurisdiction;
* passport dependence;
* consular requirements;
* family exposure;
* property and inheritance;
* future travel needs;
* return calculations;
* old phone numbers;
* persistent accounts;
* cloud records;
* regional transit risk;
* foreign legal requests;
* platform visibility;
* community pressure.

Avoid both extremes:

* migration does not mean the system disappears;
* origin-state connection does not mean the person is permanently controlled.

Diaspora freedom is unevenly distributed across:

* territory;
* law;
* mobility;
* documentation;
* identity;
* family;
* technology;
* social relationships.

⸻

🕸️ Identify The Relationship Pathways

Little Database of Horrors studies are particularly concerned with how control moves through relationships.

Consider whether the documented or plausible pathways involve:

* spouses;
* children;
* parents;
* siblings;
* extended family;
* employers;
* colleagues;
* lawyers;
* journalists;
* publishers;
* sources;
* funders;
* academics;
* student associations;
* religious institutions;
* community organisations;
* political collaborators.

Relevant mechanisms may include:

* detention of relatives;
* travel restrictions;
* passport confiscation;
* derivative nationality loss;
* property or asset pressure;
* questioning;
* device searches;
* workplace consequences;
* reputational warnings;
* demands that relatives persuade the person abroad to stop;
* separation from family;
* control of family reunion;
* uncertainty about who reports what;
* pressure upon publishers, lawyers or intermediaries.

Do not assume one universal relationship mechanism.

For one country, the family may remain the principal reachable border.

For another, nationality law may allow legal consequences to cascade through the family tree.

For another, the main pathway may involve regional rendition, embassy monitoring, financial pressure or platform attack.

The node should identify the smallest defensible relationship claim.

⸻

🪪 Examine Belonging As Infrastructure

Where relevant, examine:

* citizenship;
* denaturalisation;
* derivative nationality;
* statelessness;
* passport cancellation;
* Civil ID;
* residency;
* birth and marriage registration;
* property;
* inheritance;
* welfare;
* public employment;
* banking access;
* digital identity;
* electronic signatures;
* document wallets;
* government-service platforms.

Distinguish carefully between:

* formal legal power;
* documented implementation;
* threatened use;
* administrative discretion;
* actual propagation across systems;
* fear of possible consequences.

Do not infer automatic integration merely because several systems exist.

Ask:

* Which systems communicate?
* Which changes propagate automatically?
* Which require manual decisions?
* What happens during appeal?
* Are historic records preserved?
* Who can reverse an error?
* Can dependants retain access?
* Is there an auditable correction route?

A useful administrative service may still increase coercive capability when legal status is politically insecure.

Convenience and risk can be true simultaneously.

⸻

📱 Follow Speech Through The Network

Do not examine only the original author.

Track whether enforcement or pressure reaches:

* retweeters;
* editors;
* publishers;
* hosts;
* event organisers;
* translators;
* family-group participants;
* people preserving screenshots;
* journalists quoting the speech;
* diaspora organisations amplifying the case.

The state or governing authority may show interest not only in expression but in:

* circulation;
* amplification;
* preservation;
* association;
* institutional consequence.

A node should distinguish between:

* an organised opposition network;
* an informal circulation field;
* people linked only because the state prosecuted them similarly;
* relationships established directly;
* relationships merely inferred from overlapping reporting.

Repression may connect people who were not previously organised by:

* driving them into the same exile cities;
* placing them in the same rights reports;
* making them rely upon the same lawyers;
* causing them to preserve or amplify one another’s cases.

⸻

🛂 Include Return, Transit And Third-Country Risk

Diaspora safety should not be measured only by what the origin state can do in Britain, Europe, North America or another host country.

A person may become exposed through:

* return;
* airport transit;
* a regional holiday;
* family travel;
* deportation;
* extradition;
* rendition;
* international or regional police systems;
* a friendly third country;
* invalid or contested travel documents.

Ask:

* Which body issued the notice?
* Which body operationalised it?
* What legal review existed?
* Was the political nature of the case recognised?
* Did refugee status protect the person’s territory but not their movement?
* Was the offence translated into an ordinary-crime category?
* Could the person challenge the request?
* Which country completed the coercive pathway?

Avoid using “Interpol” as a generic term for every international or regional warrant mechanism.

Administrative ambiguity may itself be part of the danger.

⸻

⚖️ Examine Host-State Crossover

Origin-state pressure may enter host-country institutions through apparently ordinary processes:

* police cooperation;
* mutual legal assistance;
* immigration;
* extradition;
* deportation;
* university safeguarding or discipline;
* banking compliance;
* employment vetting;
* platform disclosure;
* civil litigation;
* diplomatic contact.

Ask whether the receiving institution can recognise when terms such as:

* public order;
* misinformation;
* insulting the ruler;
* phone misuse;
* national unity;
* state prestige;
* foreign agendas;

describe activity protected in the host country.

A political dispute does not become apolitical merely because it is translated into a database field or legal request.

⸻

🏢 Treat Embassies And Community Institutions Carefully

Embassies, consulates, bilateral societies and student organisations may provide genuine:

* documentation;
* welfare;
* cultural connection;
* education;
* networking;
* emergency support;
* practical assistance.

They may also be unavoidable state-controlled gateways or institutionally close to government.

Do not describe ordinary consular activity as surveillance without evidence.

Do not ignore:

* compulsory attendance;
* identity verification;
* address disclosure;
* scholarship dependence;
* difficulty appealing delays;
* questioning about political activity;
* uncertainty about information-sharing;
* the absence of clearly independent community spaces.

Institutional proximity is not proof of covert activity.

It may still shape how safe disagreement feels.

⸻

💻 Separate Technical And Administrative Capabilities

Do not allow “digital authoritarianism” to become a single vague category.

Separate where possible:

Communications and device access

* spyware;
* phishing;
* interception;
* forensic extraction;
* device seizure;
* access-code powers;
* telecom retention.

Platform and public-sphere control

* blocking;
* content removal;
* coordinated reporting;
* influence activity;
* bot or troll networks;
* search contamination;
* platform-government cooperation.

Identity and administration

* Civil ID;
* biometrics;
* government-service accounts;
* nationality records;
* border systems;
* document wallets;
* banking and KYC.

Social and relationship information

* contact lists;
* family records;
* event attendance;
* association membership;
* community intermediaries;
* social-graph analysis.

Physical and legal enforcement

* arrest;
* detention;
* prosecution;
* torture;
* forced return;
* family punishment;
* nationality loss;
* border restriction.

These mechanisms may converge.

Procurement of one does not prove deployment of another.

Technical possibility does not establish access, implementation, attribution or causation.

⸻

🏭 Follow Vendors, Infrastructure And Money

Where evidence permits, map:

* telecom ownership;
* cloud providers;
* biometric suppliers;
* identity platforms;
* border systems;
* cybersecurity contractors;
* monitoring tools;
* local resellers;
* systems integrators;
* consultants;
* maintenance providers;
* sovereign funds;
* royal or state-linked commercial interests;
* defence and port agreements;
* foreign training.

Do not stop at a familiar product or company name.

Ask:

* Who specified the requirement?
* Who purchased it?
* Who integrated it?
* Who maintains it?
* Who holds administrator access?
* Who provides updates?
* Who receives recurring revenue?
* Which agency operates it?
* Which institution benefits politically or commercially?
* What evidence links it to the conduct being discussed?

A vendor’s product may establish capability.

It does not establish use against a particular person without further evidence.

⸻

🤝 Map What Travels Regionally

Country nodes should identify relevant cross-border systems without surrendering country-specific accountability.

These may include:

* regional policing;
* intelligence cooperation;
* extradition and return;
* shared vendors;
* shared telecom or cloud infrastructure;
* investment;
* counterterrorism agreements;
* defence partnerships;
* data-sharing arrangements;
* diplomatic normalisation;
* maintenance and training;
* public-relations and reputation systems.

Where a pathway belongs principally to several countries or institutions, route the wider mechanism to a regional cooperation node.

Country nodes should still state the specific role played by each actor.

Distributed responsibility must not become distributed disappearance.

⸻

🪖 Account For Strategic Utility And Foreign Understatement

Western and regional governments may value a country for:

* military basing;
* maritime geography;
* oil and gas;
* ports;
* counterterrorism;
* mediation;
* intelligence;
* investment;
* migration control;
* diplomatic access.

This does not prove concealment or complicity.

It may create incentives for:

* polite language;
* narrow criticism;
* slow investigation;
* deference to official explanations;
* asylum disbelief;
* continued procurement;
* presenting coercion as stability.

Ask:

* What did partners know?
* What equipment, training or data were supplied?
* Were safeguards tested?
* Did strategic access affect the response?
* Is the exile’s account competing with a much larger state friendship narrative?

The country’s usefulness may help explain why documented political harm is repeatedly treated as surprising or exceptional.

⸻

🦠 Use Emergencies As Capability Stress Tests

Where relevant, examine periods of:

* pandemic;
* war;
* protest;
* succession;
* terrorism;
* economic crisis;
* diplomatic confrontation;
* leadership scandal.

These conditions may reveal:

* who already holds access;
* which legal thresholds can be lowered;
* which databases can be joined;
* how quickly categories widen;
* what temporary procurement occurs;
* which powers remain afterward.

Do not assume that every emergency measure was illegitimate.

Ask what infrastructure, legal authority and institutional habit survived the emergency.

⸻

🗄️ Preserve The Public Archive

Country nodes should record the risk that platform decay will erase political life unevenly.

Potentially endangered materials include:

* protest videos;
* parliamentary broadcasts;
* memes;
* stickers;
* slogans;
* online satire;
* newspaper cartoons;
* citizen footage;
* deleted interviews;
* X threads;
* WhatsApp material;
* removed articles;
* official custody images;
* replies and quote-posts.

Where possible preserve:

* original language;
* date;
* platform;
* URL or archive route;
* author;
* surrounding context;
* responses;
* deletion history;
* degree of authentication.

Do not expose private relatives or unlawfully obtained intimate material merely for archival completeness.

The aim is to prevent an archive in which state documents remain intact while ordinary political culture disappears.

⸻

🧪 Maintain An Explicit Evidence Ladder

Separate:

* documented capability;
* documented use;
* credible testimony;
* reasonable inference;
* foreseeable risk;
* unresolved hypothesis;
* unsupported allegation.

For important propositions, record:

* the exact claim;
* relevant date and jurisdiction;
* source type;
* what the source proves;
* what it does not prove;
* confidence;
* dispute;
* alternative explanations;
* evidence needed to strengthen or disprove it.

The smallest supportable claim should come first.

Examples:

* One documented case of pressure upon relatives does not establish a universal family-pressure policy.
* Participation in a regional policing body does not establish abuse of that body.
* A reporting platform does not establish hidden political scoring.
* Digital identity integration does not establish automatic political exclusion across all services.
* Public fear does not establish an active surveillance operation.
* Absence of spectacular overseas violence does not establish absence of diaspora constraint.
* A politically useful allegation does not establish that the underlying vendor or foreign-state claim is true.

The metaphor may be memorable.

The claim still has to earn its name.

⸻

🧯 Include Rival Explanations And Disproof Conditions

Relevant alternatives may include:

* ordinary criminal investigation;
* genuine fraud prevention;
* administrative error;
* lawful consular processing;
* platform failure;
* interpersonal conflict;
* community gossip;
* employer action;
* non-political moderation;
* coincidence;
* fragmented rather than centrally directed action.

Claims of coordinated or state-directed repression become stronger where there is:

* repeated reference to protected political conduct;
* private information unavailable publicly;
* temporal proximity to speech or organising;
* coordinated activity across institutions;
* embassy or official involvement;
* threats involving relatives, return or passports;
* political offences translated into ordinary crime;
* corroboration from independent sources;
* a documented actor, route or contractor.

They become weaker where:

* the alleged actor lacked plausible access;
* the conduct began before the political activity;
* transparent and reviewable non-political grounds exist;
* records show a different origin;
* the claim depends entirely upon ambiguous technical anomalies;
* the proposed coordination explains less than ordinary institutional fragmentation.

The analysis may conclude that several actors contributed without one controlling centre.

⸻

📏 Let Confidence Attach To Propositions

Avoid declaring one confidence level for an entire country node.

A node may contain:

* high-confidence legal capability;
* high-confidence domestic enforcement;
* medium-confidence anticipatory diaspora effects;
* one strongly documented family case;
* low-confidence vendor attribution;
* unresolved claims about overseas surveillance.

State clearly what is:

* strongly supported;
* plausible but not established generally;
* unresolved;
* contradicted;
* dependent upon one source;
* dependent upon translation;
* dependent upon current legal developments.

Evidence should determine the conclusion.

The desire for a complete comparative table should not inflate weak material.

⸻

🧷 Include A Compact Finding

A compact finding should state the smallest useful country-specific conclusion.

It may explain:

* the dominant diaspora pathway;
* the strongest documented case;
* how domestic and cross-border systems connect;
* what remains unproven.

It should not merely repeat that the country is authoritarian.

A useful compact finding often follows this form:

A person connected to [country] may experience [documented or strongly supported pathway], particularly through [specific legal, family, mobility, administrative or regional mechanism]. Public evidence does not currently establish [stronger claim], but the documented capability means [practical diaspora consequence].

Where appropriate, include a human-centred line that shows what the mechanism did to an actual life without turning the person into a case-file ornament.

⸻

🛠️ End With Practical Questions, Not Instructions To Become A Spy

Questions for diaspora readers may include:

* Which documents remain dependent upon the origin state?
* Who inside the country may be exposed through my speech or contacts?
* Do I need to return?
* Which transit jurisdictions matter?
* What happens when my passport expires?
* Which accounts remain tied to an old phone number?
* Can event photographs be published safely?
* What evidence should be preserved?
* Does my university or employer understand transnational repression?
* Does my lawyer understand the political context of the offence?
* Have threats referred to private information?
* Is the adverse action documented, plausibly linked, socially generated or currently unattributable?

Do not encourage readers to:

* confront embassies;
* bait suspected agents;
* accuse relatives or community members;
* unlawfully access accounts;
* sever every relationship;
* engage in amateur counter-intelligence;
* treat ordinary anomalies as proof of state action.

Prioritise:

* evidence preservation;
* careful attribution;
* independent legal and professional advice;
* diversified document and recovery routes;
* protection of important relationships;
* host-state reporting where appropriate;
* maintenance of ordinary life.

The node should increase agency without making permanent vigilance the price of safety.

✅ A Node Is Probably Working When

The reader can answer:

* What kind of political system is this?
* Why has it developed these particular control mechanisms?
* Which actors hold relevant powers?
* What conduct is actually documented?
* How is political speech translated or disguised administratively?
* What changes when a person leaves?
* Which family, identity, mobility or relationship dependencies remain?
* What could travel through a regional or host-state system?
* What remains unproven?
* What evidence would change the conclusion?
* What practical questions should an affected person ask?

The reader should leave with greater understanding, not a vague conviction that everything is connected.

⸻

🚫 Warning Signs In Drafting

Reconsider the draft where it:

* reads like a generic human-rights profile;
* treats the ruler as the sole actor;
* treats the population as the regime;
* assumes one regional model applies everywhere;
* gives the same weight to every country;
* substitutes reputation for evidence;
* substitutes procurement for use;
* substitutes motive for attribution;
* treats indirect speech as political emptiness;
* assumes migration ends exposure;
* assumes diaspora fear proves surveillance;
* turns one case into a universal policy;
* treats official community infrastructure as covert activity without evidence;
* omits stateless or legally insecure groups;
* preserves government documents but not public culture;
* gives the reader no practical route except fear;
* makes the metaphor more certain than the evidence.

⸻

🔭 Working Principle

A diaspora-facing country node should localise the political world from which exposure emerges, identify the mechanisms capable of continuing across borders, preserve the language and culture through which people experienced them, and distinguish carefully between documented conduct, available capability and unresolved risk.

It should be specific enough to help someone recognise their situation.

It should be disciplined enough not to manufacture one for them.
