# 🕌 Middle East And North Africa  
**First created:** 2026-07-23 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-23  
*A diaspora-facing capability map for understanding how states, governing authorities, security institutions, vendors, diplomatic networks, and regional alliances may affect people connected to the Middle East and North Africa while they are living abroad, in transit, or rebuilding life elsewhere.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

This branch is organised country by country because diaspora risk is experienced through specific:

- governments;
- security services;
- embassies;
- passports;
- borders;
- families;
- records;
- vendors;
- political histories;
- administrative dependencies.

The primary reader is not a person seeking one abstract account of “digital authoritarianism in MENA.”

It is someone asking:

> I am from this country, connected to people there, dealing with its institutions, or working alongside people affected by it. What capabilities, pressures, dependencies, and relationship risks may matter to me while I am living abroad?

That question cannot be answered responsibly through one generic regional description.

A person may face:

- passport dependence;
- consular leverage;
- relatives remaining in-country;
- political monitoring;
- spyware or phishing;
- impersonation;
- smear campaigns;
- family pressure;
- border risk;
- forced-return pressure;
- professional or institutional exclusion;
- host-state underreaction.

The relevant route will vary by country, government, institution, period, legal system, diplomatic relationship, and individual circumstance.

This branch therefore uses a simple design rule:

> **Country-facing for accessibility; actor-specific inside the analysis.**

The country name provides the reader with a recognisable door.

It does not imply one coherent actor behind everything that happens inside or beyond that state.

## 🧭 The Governing Question  

The branch asks:

> How can state, commercial, diplomatic, military, intelligence, administrative, and proxy systems affect people connected to a country while they are living abroad?

Each country node should examine:

- capability;
- access;
- documented use;
- relationship pressure;
- consular and administrative dependency;
- cross-border reach;
- host-state response;
- practical protection.

This is not a ranking of “bad states.”

It is not a claim that every state behaves equally.

It is not a claim that people from a country are politically aligned with its government.

It is not a request that diaspora readers become permanent intelligence analysts devoted to their own captivity.

The purpose is to make the machinery more legible so the reader has more choice.

## 🌍 Why Use “MENA” At All?  

“Middle East and North Africa” is a flawed and reductive regional category.

The region includes:

- monarchies;
- republics;
- electoral systems;
- military governments;
- occupations;
- fragmented authorities;
- militias;
- civil-war systems;
- external sponsors;
- contested sovereignties;
- radically unequal levels of wealth, infrastructure, and digital access.

The states and governing structures in this folder do not share one:

- political system;
- security architecture;
- technology base;
- target population;
- legal framework;
- relationship with diaspora communities;
- relationship with Western, Chinese, Russian, or regional powers.

The regional label is retained as a practical heuristic because:

- security technologies travel across the region;
- intelligence relationships cross borders;
- vendors and contractors serve several states;
- narratives and influence operations circulate;
- families and diasporas span jurisdictions;
- regional rivalries shape targeting;
- normalisation agreements reshape procurement and cooperation;
- the same platform or telecom infrastructure may serve several political systems.

The region is therefore treated here as:

> not one system, but a field through which tools, money, expertise, narratives, security partnerships, and coercive practices travel.

## 👑 Monarchies Are Court Systems  

Concentrated formal authority must not be mistaken for solitary operational control.

A monarch may sit at the apex while the reality reaching the throne is shaped by:

- royal household officials;
- senior relatives;
- private secretaries;
- tutors and long-standing mentors;
- security chiefs;
- military advisers;
- trusted clerics;
- business families;
- investment managers;
- diplomatic intermediaries;
- foreign partners;
- consultants, contractors, and vendors.

These people may influence:

- who receives access;
- which complaints appear credible;
- what counts as danger;
- which evidence is withheld;
- which alternatives are described as weak or impossible;
- which institutions become indispensable;
- who benefits when coercive capability expands.

A ruler can be:

- an architect of repression;
- an approver of repression;
- a beneficiary of repression;
- partly managed by the system around the throne;
- several of these at once.

Humanising the ruler is not absolution.

It is mechanism-finding.

The analytical question is not only:

> Why did the ruler choose repression?

It is also:

> Who convinced the ruler that every other option was reckless, disloyal, weak, or impossible?

### Who Controls Access?

Ask who controls:

- the diary;
- private meetings;
- petitions;
- informal correspondence;
- intelligence briefings;
- contact with affected families;
- the route through which bad news reaches the throne.

### Who Controls The Threat Picture?

Ask who:

- defines protest as sedition;
- translates reform into instability;
- describes criticism as foreign influence;
- classifies humour as disrespect;
- converts social pressure into a security problem;
- benefits from threat inflation.

### Who Controls Implementation?

Map who drafts, authorises, interprets, procures, and enforces:

- laws;
- regulations;
- surveillance;
- arrest;
- prosecution;
- detention;
- censorship;
- passport action;
- border restriction.

### Who Controls The Money And The Story?

Trace:

- procurement;
- royal-linked companies;
- sovereign funds;
- telecom ownership;
- resellers and integrators;
- commissions and maintenance contracts;
- public-relations firms;
- think tanks;
- foreign briefings;
- media ownership;
- the people who translate coercion into “stability.”

> **Do not only profile the person on the throne. Profile the people who have been explaining the world to them since childhood.**

> **Absolute monarchy places one person above the chart. Court politics determines who draws the chart placed in front of them.**

### After The Ruler

Removing one ruler may leave intact:

- intelligence archives;
- security commanders;
- prosecutors;
- telecom infrastructure;
- surveillance contracts;
- citizenship powers;
- public-order offences;
- foreign agreements;
- commercial beneficiaries;
- advisers able to transfer themselves to a successor.

Country nodes should therefore ask:

- Which powers survive succession?
- Who controls or destroys the archives?
- Which agencies remain effectively unsupervised?
- Which contracts continue automatically?
- Which advisers move into the next court?
- Can victims access, correct, or challenge their records?
- Are watchlists, nationality decisions, and secret evidence reviewed?

> **Regime change is not system change when the advisers, databases, contracts, security institutions, and beneficiaries remain in place.**

## 🧳 Migration Is Not Automatic Exit  

Moving to Britain, France, Canada, Australia, the United States, another European state, another Gulf state, or anywhere else may change territorial law without ending the person’s connection to the system they left.

Migration can bring real safety and real legal freedom.

It may not dissolve:

- origin-state jurisdictional claims;
- citizenship dependence;
- passport renewal;
- consular contact;
- property and inheritance;
- family exposure;
- military or administrative obligations;
- digital continuity;
- old phone numbers and account recovery routes;
- platform history;
- border and transit risk;
- international policing pathways;
- the future need to return.

A person can therefore be:

- safe from immediate domestic arrest;
- legally free to speak in the host country;
- unsafe if they return;
- constrained by relatives who remain reachable;
- dependent on the origin state for documents;
- visible through accounts created before migration.

Diaspora freedom is not binary.

It is distributed unevenly across:

- territory;
- law;
- identity;
- relationships;
- technology;
- mobility.

> **The person may have left the jurisdiction without fully leaving the system.**

### Host-State Legality Is Not The Whole Risk Assessment

A statement may be lawful in London, Paris, Montréal, Sydney, New York, or Brussels while remaining punishable, reportable, or consequential elsewhere.

Ask separately:

- Is the conduct lawful here?
- Is it criminalised there?
- Can relatives be reached?
- Does the person need to return?
- Could a foreign request enter host-state systems?
- Could a third country detain the person in transit?
- Could consular or nationality powers be used?
- Will the host institution recognise the allegation as political?

> **Host-state legality does not automatically remove origin-state consequences.**

### Migration Changes The Law Faster Than The Nervous System

A person raised around offences involving:

- insulting a ruler;
- public order;
- sedition;
- misleading information;
- state prestige;
- misuse of social media;

may continue to:

- post pseudonymously;
- avoid demonstrations;
- step out of photographs;
- separate political and family groups;
- delete jokes;
- speak more freely in one language than another;
- treat community spaces as potentially observable.

This is not evidence that every fear reflects an active operation.

It is evidence that coercive systems can continue shaping behaviour after physical relocation.

> **Migration changes the applicable law faster than it changes the nervous system.**

## 🪪 Citizenship Under Strain  

The century’s citizenship problem is sharply contradictory.

States are extending coercive reach across borders while weakening citizenship as a durable guarantee of belonging.

Citizenship may increasingly be treated as:

- conditional membership;
- an administrative privilege;
- a reward for political conformity;
- a security classification;
- something revocable at the edge;
- a mechanism for exporting unwanted people;
- a status distributed unequally within the same polity.

Track:

- denaturalisation;
- nationality revocation;
- passport cancellation;
- discriminatory descent rules;
- statelessness risk;
- political-loyalty conditions;
- administrative limbo;
- consequences for children and spouses.

The dangerous combination is:

1. the origin state continues to claim authority over conduct abroad; and
2. citizenship becomes less reliable as protection against state exclusion.

> **States are extending their reach across borders while weakening the security of belonging within them.**

A country node must distinguish:

- legal capability;
- threatened use;
- documented use;
- actual consequences;
- anticipatory fear.

Capability matters without automatically proving implementation.

## 🎭 The Deception Order  

Marc Owen Jones’s work on digital authoritarianism in the Middle East provides an important regional lens: the **deception order**.

This includes:

- bots;
- trolls;
- sockpuppets;
- fake journalists;
- impersonation;
- contradictory smear campaigns;
- coordinated amplification;
- fake grassroots support;
- hostile labelling;
- platform manipulation;
- commercial PR;
- reputation laundering;
- transnational influence operations.

Disinformation does not need to persuade everyone of one coherent lie.

It may be enough to:

- contaminate search results;
- overwhelm verification;
- manufacture apparent consensus;
- create fake representatives;
- discredit a journalist;
- trigger mass reporting;
- intimidate collaborators;
- make the target socially expensive to defend.

Contradictory smear campaigns can still work.

The objective may not be belief.

It may be identity noise.

> **Disinformation does not only govern what people believe. It governs who may safely be believed, funded, published, defended, or known.**

## 🧩 No Single Discipline Sees The Whole System  

A technical researcher may identify malware but not the diplomatic bargain around its export.

A political scientist may understand the alliance structure but not verify a device compromise.

A human-rights organisation may document lived harm without access to platform datasets.

## 🗃️ Platforms, Culture, And The Public Archive  

Political history is not preserved only in manifestos, court records, speeches, and formal organisations.

It also lives in:

- jokes;
- memes;
- dialect;
- hashtags;
- replies;
- quote-posts;
- stickers;
- voice notes;
- citizen video;
- ordinary photographs;
- local forums;
- private-group screenshots;
- strange phrases everyone suddenly understands.

People are rarely trying to create a future archive.

They are:

- talking to friends;
- complaining about work;
- making each other laugh;
- flirting;
- grieving;
- passing along something that feels true;
- recording an event before professional media arrive.

That ordinary social behaviour may later reveal:

- what everybody already knew;
- which official was considered corrupt or ridiculous;
- how slogans travelled;
- when fear became mockery;
- how state language was repurposed;
- which towns, classes, genders, or generations used different political registers.

Platform deletion, broken search, closed APIs, lost media, account suspension, and inaccessible private archives can destroy:

- chronology;
- provenance;
- local vocabulary;
- reply context;
- relationship pathways;
- evidence of reception;
- evidence of deletion;
- cultural texture.

A screenshot may preserve the words while losing:

- timestamp;
- thread position;
- author metadata;
- replies;
- edits;
- engagement history;
- embedded links;
- proof that the item existed at the claimed address.

Each country node should therefore preserve local-language public culture where possible, including humour, profanity, bad grammar, satire, songs, poetry, prison writing, slogans, graffiti, and memes.

Do not sanitise people into policy language.

Do not invent missing obscenity or missing context.

> **Platform destruction creates an archive in which governments remain documented and the governed slowly disappear.**

> **The populace likes to vibe. The vibe is often where the evidence lives.**

## 🪴 The “One Little Database” Problem  

The system may begin with a promise to keep one sensible list.

Then the list needs:

- identifiers;
- contact details;
- family links;
- associates;
- context;
- sentiment;
- scoring;
- prediction;
- intervention data;
- outcome data;
- model retraining.

Each limitation becomes the justification for the next capability.

The system may then create a feedback loop:

- intervention creates isolation;
- isolation becomes data;
- new contact becomes suspicious;
- resistance becomes confirmation;
- harm becomes a training signal.

The database stops merely recording the social world.

It begins manufacturing the conditions it claims to measure.

> **The model creates the damage, consumes the damage as data, and calls improved prediction evidence that it was right.**

## 🦠 Emergencies As Governance Stress Tests  

Emergency conditions reveal which institutions already hold the keys.

Country nodes should examine:

- emergency laws;
- health apps;
- contact tracing;
- location monitoring;
- quarantine enforcement;
- biometric or identity linkage;
- misinformation and rumour offences;
- communications restrictions;
- migrant-worker treatment;

When a blocked communications service is selectively enabled for approved institutions, the exception may show who held the gate all along.

## 🌡️ Political Stability And Fracture  

Systems designed to prevent mobilisation may suppress the warning signs that would otherwise reveal political pressure.

Coerced silence may be misread as consent.

Dashboards may measure performance under surveillance rather than political stability.

Repression may weaken:

- lawful opposition;
- independent unions;
- trusted journalism;
- civic organisations;
- credible polling;
- public argument;
- peaceful leadership development;
- mechanisms for concession.

When pressure finally breaks through:

- mobilisation may be sudden;
- information may be unreliable;
- trusted intermediaries may be absent;
- states may overreact;
- allies may scramble to protect shared systems and secrets.

> **A surveillance state may become very good at preventing society from expressing pressure while becoming catastrophically bad at knowing how much pressure exists.**

This is a governance warning.

It is not a prediction that revolution is inevitable.

## 🌐 Foreign-Policy Complication  

Digital authoritarian systems complicate foreign policy because several states may simultaneously be:

- suppliers;
- buyers;
- allies;
- intelligence partners;
- host states;
- regulators;
- investigators;
- beneficiaries.

A target may:

- live in one country;
- be attacked by a vendor from another;
- on behalf of a third;
- using infrastructure in a fourth.

Diplomatic incentives may discourage investigation.

Exposure may reveal:

- intelligence arrangements;
- export decisions;
- target lists;
- platform cooperation;
- covert diplomacy;
- allied complicity.

Each actor may say:

- we only issued the licence;
- we only sold the product;
- we only supplied the infrastructure;
- we did not select the target;
- another jurisdiction must investigate;
- the evidence is classified;
- bilateral relations prevent further comment.

Distributed responsibility creates distributed deniability.

> **The teeth may be distributed through the alliance structure.**

## 🧯 Comparison Is Not Rebuttal  

Each country node examines the conduct and capability associated with the named state or governing structure.

Evidence that another state:

- does the same;
- does worse;
- supplied the technology;
- is an ally;
- is an adversary;

does not answer the conduct examined here.

Another state’s conduct may justify:

- a cross-reference;
- a comparative note;
- a regional analysis;
- a separate investigation.

It does not remove the present actor from scrutiny.

> **“But Country X—” is a routing instruction, not a defence.**

The appropriate response is:

> Yes. Open the relevant node.

## ⚖️ No False Parity  

Country-by-country structure does not imply equal conduct.

Nodes may differ greatly in:

- length;
- evidence;
- scale;
- severity;
- capability;
- overseas reach;
- current relevance.

Some states may be:

- major producers;
- major purchasers;
- regional exporters;
- fragmented users;
- highly documented;
- poorly documented;
- heavily dependent on external systems.

Equal questions do not require equal conclusions.

Do not inflate weak evidence merely to make every flag appear equally represented.

## 🧿 State, Population, Religion, And Diaspora Are Separate  

No country node should collapse:

- government into people;
- security service into nationality;
- state policy into religion;
- embassy into diaspora;
- ethnicity into political loyalty.

A study of Israeli state capability must not treat Jews abroad as Israeli state infrastructure.

A study of Iranian state capability must not treat Iranian diaspora communities as regime proxies.

A study of Turkish state capability must not treat Turkish or Kurdish people as automatic state extensions.

A study of Palestinian governance must not treat Palestinians as one governing authority.

A study of Gulf states must not treat all Arab diaspora communities as politically interchangeable.

The branch must not reproduce the relationship-risk logic it critiques.

## 🧱 Handling Fragmented Governance  

Country nodes remain the reader-facing entrance.

The analysis inside them must separate actors.

For Syria, Iraq, Lebanon, Yemen, and Palestinian governance, identify:

- territorial control;
- state and non-state actors;
- foreign sponsors;
- telecom control;
- border control;
- diaspora-facing institutions;
- external vendors;
- security coordination;
- documentary dependencies.

State weakness does not mean absence of coercive capability.

Fragmentation may make:

- attribution harder;
- records thinner;
- responsibility more distributed;
- remedy more difficult;
- foreign-state influence more important.

The architecture may be coherent enough to harm and fragmented enough to defeat appeal.

## 📏 Evidence Rules  

Every node should follow the comparative method.

Separate:

- capability;
- access;
- intent;
- implementation;
- documented use;
- attribution;
- causation.

Confidence attaches to individual propositions.

Country reputation is not evidence.

Procurement is not deployment.

Motive is not use.

State-linked is not automatically state-directed.

Historical conduct is not automatically current.

Lack of public evidence is neither innocence nor proof of hidden guilt.

Name:

- source dependence;
- translation dependence;
- evidentiary silence;
- disagreement;
- uncertainty;
- disproof conditions.

## 🩺 Reader-Safety Rules  

Do not encourage confrontation with:

- embassies;
- suspected agents;
- relatives;
- community organisations;
- vendors;
- security bodies.

Do not require the reader to sever every relationship.

Do not imply that perfect anonymity is possible.

Do not turn ordinary diaspora life into permanent counter-intelligence work.

Prioritise:

- preserving evidence;
- diversifying dependencies;
- protecting key relationships;
- identifying host-state duties;
- safer professional advice;
- maintaining ordinary function.

The branch should increase choice.

It should not reproduce captivity.
