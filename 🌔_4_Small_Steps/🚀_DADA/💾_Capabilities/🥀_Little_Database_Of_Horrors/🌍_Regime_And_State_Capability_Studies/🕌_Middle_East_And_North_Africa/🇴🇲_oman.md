🇴🇲 Oman — consolidated node bullets

Orientation

* Oman is a hereditary absolute monarchy in which the Sultan sits at the apex of legislative, executive, security and judicial authority.
* Formal concentration of authority does not mean the ruler personally designs or supervises every coercive act.
* The node should map:
    * the Sultan;
    * the Royal Court;
    * palace advisers;
    * the Internal Security Service;
    * Royal Oman Police;
    * prosecutors and judges;
    * the Ministry of Information;
    * the Telecommunications Regulatory Authority;
    * telecom operators;
    * state investment bodies;
    * royal-linked businesses;
    * foreign security partners;
    * vendors and consultants.
* The governing question is not only:
    What did the Sultan order?
* It is:
    Who around the throne defined the danger, filtered the evidence, excluded alternatives, benefited from escalation and made repression appear necessary?

The throne is not the whole system

* Monarchs are managed from childhood through:
    * tutors;
    * household officials;
    * senior relatives;
    * military mentors;
    * private secretaries;
    * security advisers;
    * trusted clerics;
    * business proxies;
    * foreign partners.
* These actors help determine:
    * who reaches the ruler;
    * which complaints appear credible;
    * which threats appear existential;
    * which policy options are presented;
    * which failures are concealed;
    * which vendors become “indispensable.”
* Absolute legal authority may therefore operate through a reality curated by people whose status depends upon continued access.
* This does not absolve the monarch.
* It identifies the decision environment.

Absolute monarchy places one person above the chart. Court politics determines who draws the chart placed in front of him.

Who made repression seem like the only option?

Journalists should ask:

* Who first labelled a protest, tweet or strike a security threat?
* What Arabic terminology was used internally?
    * فتنة — discord, sedition, civil strife;
    * إخلال بالنظام العام — disturbance of public order;
    * التحريض — incitement;
    * الإساءة إلى هيبة الدولة — affront to state prestige;
    * معلومات مضللة — misleading information.
* Who supplied the evidence?
* Which peaceful explanations were omitted?
* Who advised that concessions would show weakness?
* Who predicted chaos, foreign interference or dynastic collapse?
* Did security bodies benefit through:
    * larger budgets;
    * new legal powers;
    * expanded data access;
    * procurement authority;
    * greater proximity to the palace?
* Who reported afterward that repression had “worked”?
* Was frightened silence misreported upward as restored consent?

Repression is often presented to rulers not as cruelty, but as the last remaining form of responsibility. Journalism must identify who closed off the other options.

After-the-ruler test

* Removing one monarch does not remove:
    * security files;
    * secret evidence;
    * prosecutors;
    * telecom interception systems;
    * censorship regulations;
    * citizenship powers;
    * vendor contracts;
    * royal-business networks;
    * foreign military agreements;
    * advisers able to transfer loyalty to a successor.
* A transition may leave the machinery untouched while changing its public face.
* Investigate who would control:
    * archives;
    * watchlists;
    * biometric records;
    * detention records;
    * procurement files;
    * evidence destruction;
    * succession briefings.

Regime change is not system change when the advisers, databases, contracts, security institutions and beneficiaries remain in place.

Political and constitutional structure

* Political parties are prohibited.
* Representative institutions possess limited independent power.
* Criticism of the Sultan has long been criminalised.
* The ruler’s legal inviolability places political criticism beside protected categories involving religion, dignity and public order.
* The law does not literally make the Sultan divine.
* It politically sacralises him by treating scrutiny of a human ruler as an offence against an inviolable order.

God may judge the Sultan eventually. The Ministry of Information would prefer nobody submit interim observations.

Abrahamic accountability

* The node should reject the claim that religious legitimacy automatically requires political silence.
* Islamic traditions distinguish:
    * obedience to God;
    * obedience to the Messenger;
    * conditional obedience to human authority.
* Relevant ethical language includes:
    * كلمة حق عند سلطان جائر
    * “a truthful or just word before an oppressive ruler.”
* The analytical point is not theological adjudication.
* It is that the state’s demand for extraordinary reverence is not the only recognisably Islamic account of political duty.

The Prophetic tradition honours truthful speech before unjust power. The state criminalises it, then borrows religious language to demand obedience.

Formal protections and qualified positives

* Oman’s Basic Law contains formal anti-discrimination language covering categories such as:
    * الجنس — gender;
    * الأصل — origin;
    * اللون — colour;
    * اللغة — language;
    * الدين — religion;
    * المذهب — sect;
    * الموطن — domicile;
    * المركز الاجتماعي — social status.
* Oman has often been described as comparatively tolerant of registered non-Muslim worship.
* Non-Muslim communities may practise through approved institutions while facing controls over:
    * registration;
    * publication;
    * proselytising;
    * approved places of worship.
* Formal prohibition of private religious gatherings was reportedly not always actively enforced.
* That is better than aggressive enforcement, but remains discretionary tolerance rather than a right secured against government action.

A positive finding must remain attached to the right, population, institution and period it actually describes. It cannot launder unrelated repression.

Speech and censorship baseline

* Self-censorship among journalists, writers and academics has been repeatedly reported.
* Restrictive concepts have included:
    * النظام العام — public order;
    * الآداب العامة — public morals;
    * التضليل — misleading the public;
    * هيبة الدولة — prestige of the state;
    * إهانة السلطان — insulting the Sultan;
    * إساءة استخدام وسائل التواصل الاجتماعي — misuse of social media.
* Even disclosure of censorship mechanisms has reportedly attracted punishment.
* A web publisher was punished after revealing that a supposedly live television programme had been pre-recorded to remove criticism.
* That is an excellent compact example:

Merely revealing how criticism had been removed became punishable criticism.

2024 Media Law

* The 2024 Media Law contains 60 articles and substantially reinforces state control over publishing and broadcasting.
* Reported powers include:
    * broad Ministry of Information regulation;
    * licensing;
    * publication prohibitions;
    * control over reporting on investigations and trials;
    * financial guarantees;
    * licence suspension;
    * licence revocation;
    * prison sentences;
    * very large fines.
* The structure appears to:
    * declare expression rights;
    * attach vague exceptions;
    * assign interpretation to executive institutions;
    * make publishers carry the risk of guessing wrongly.
* Restrictions on ongoing investigations may suppress:
    * corruption reporting;
    * scrutiny of security conduct;
    * political trials;
    * public examination of judicial fairness.
* Media can be destroyed without a permanent ban through:
    * temporary suspension;
    * financial depletion;
    * proceedings lasting years;
    * advertiser loss;
    * staff dispersal;
    * licence uncertainty.

Historical media control

* Print media have been subject to pre-publication control.
* Writers have reportedly been barred from speaking publicly.
* Books and literary works have been excluded from the Muscat International Book Fair.
* Private newspapers and broadcasters have tended to maintain a pro-government line.
* State outlets consistently present government-aligned coverage.
* Censorship therefore reaches:
    * news;
    * history;
    * literature;
    * academic discussion;
    * public memory.

Academic freedom

* University staff have reportedly faced restrictions on discussing local politics.
* Possible consequences include dismissal.
* Research questions:
    * Are controls statutory, contractual or informal?
    * Who approves politically sensitive research?
    * Are students monitored?
    * Can foreign scholars publish what local academics cannot?
    * Do partnerships with foreign universities create protective space or reinforce caution?

Association and assembly

* Associations require permission.
* Approval may take years or be refused.
* Unapproved foreign funding can create criminal exposure.
* Public meetings and cultural events require state approval.
* Political gatherings and parties are prohibited or heavily constrained.
* This creates a society that may look socially active while remaining unable to build durable independent political institutions.

2011 protest cycle

* Demonstrations demanded combinations of:
    * employment;
    * political reform;
    * an end to corruption;
    * justice;
    * dignity;
    * wider participation.
* Protest speech frequently distinguished the Sultan from corrupt officials around him.
* Documented slogans included:
    * لا للفساد
        * “No to corruption.”
    * يداً بيد مع السلطان ضد الفساد
        * “Hand in hand with the Sultan against corruption.”
    * الحرية للجميع
        * “Freedom for all.”
    * العدالة للجميع
        * “Justice for all.”
    * كلنا شركاء في الوطن
        * “We are all partners in the nation.”
    * الشعب يحب السلطان
        * “The people love the Sultan.”
    * الشعب يريد الإصلاح
        * “The people want reform.”
    * لا للظلم لا للفساد
        * “No to injustice, no to corruption.”
    * جامعة واحدة لا تكفي
        * “One university is not enough.”  
* These cannot be flattened into either:
    * loyalist theatre;
    * revolutionary rejection.
* Loyalty could be sincere, protective, strategically useful or all three.

Counter-mobilisation language

* Pro-government speech used terms such as:
    * لا للتخريب نعم للحوار
        * “No to sabotage; yes to dialogue.”
    * لا شقاق ولا فتنة
        * “No division and no fitna.”
* فتنة is politically important because it may evoke:
    * sedition;
    * social rupture;
    * civil conflict;
    * religiously weighted disorder.
* This shows how the same event can be translated:
    * protesters say إصلاح — reform;
    * security ecosystems report فتنة — dangerous discord.

The protester says reform. The surrounding system reports fitna.

Sohar and political place-making

* Sohar’s Globe Roundabout became a physical public sphere.
* It was also associated with دوار الإصلاح:
    * “Reform Roundabout.”
* A place can become:
    * an assembly point;
    * a political memory;
    * a geographic slogan;
    * a physical hashtag.
* Destruction, redesign or renaming of such places should be examined as possible memory management.

2012 repression

* Writers and bloggers were arrested after criticising failures to deliver promised reforms.
* People protesting those arrests were themselves detained.
* Facebook posts and tweets formed part of prosecutions for insulting the Sultan.
* Twenty-nine activists had convictions upheld on charges including:
    * insulting the Sultan;
    * unlawful assembly.
* The period establishes an operational chain:
    public disappointment → online criticism → solidarity protest → arrest → prosecution → imprisonment.

2021 protest language

Documented language includes:

* اطلع اطلع يا مظلوم.. طالب حقك مهما يكون
    * Literal:
        * “Come out, come out, you who have been wronged; demand your right, whatever happens.”

The political donkey

* Omani political messages have reportedly been written on donkeys used as mobile billboards.
* The method:
    * separates author from carrier;
    * makes the message mobile;
    * gives the carrier no criminal intent;

Compact definition

Oman is a strategically valuable absolute monarchy whose reputation for moderation and quiet diplomacy obscures a long-standing system of political censorship, restricted organisation, security-led detention and punishment of online speech. Its newer media, cybercrime, citizenship and digital-reporting frameworks increase the potential for expression, identity, devices and relationships to be governed together, including beyond Oman’s physical borders. The Sultan sits at the apex, but the durable object of investigation is the court, security, commercial, technical and foreign-partner ecosystem that makes repression reproducible.

And the human counterweight:

When the state controls the newspaper, broadcast, university and meeting, politics migrates into the chant, retweet, dream, lament, sticker, hashtag and donkey.
