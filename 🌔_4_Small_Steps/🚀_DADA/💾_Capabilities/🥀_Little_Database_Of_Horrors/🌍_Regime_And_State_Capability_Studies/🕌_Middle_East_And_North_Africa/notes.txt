Proposed country-node structure

1. Orientation

* One-paragraph summary of why the country matters for diaspora-facing capability analysis.
* Identify whether the main concern is:
    * consular leverage;
    * surveillance;
    * digital repression;
    * family pressure;
    * transnational targeting;
    * vendor/export role;
    * fragmented governance;
    * host-state cooperation.
* State the period covered.
* Include a plain warning that the node concerns institutions and governing actors, not the population or diaspora.

2. Who This Node Is For

* Citizens abroad.
* Dual nationals.
* Former citizens.
* Refugees and asylum seekers.
* Stateless people.
* Journalists, academics, activists, lawyers, artists, labour organisers.
* Relatives, partners, carers, funders, publishers, and institutional supporters.
* People with documentary, family, property, inheritance, military-service, or travel dependencies.

This should be short but explicit.

3. Country-Facing, Actor-Specific

* Identify the actual actors:
    * government;
    * presidency or royal court;
    * ruling party;
    * military;
    * intelligence services;
    * police;
    * border agencies;
    * diplomatic missions;
    * contractors;
    * militias;
    * proxies;
    * occupying powers;
    * external sponsors.
* Explain fragmentation or overlap.
* State clearly where “the state” would be too broad.

4. Political And Institutional Context

Only the context needed to understand capability:

* present governing structure;
* recent major transition;
* emergency or security framework;
* relevant territorial divisions;
* legal position of security bodies;
* relationship between civilian and military authority;
* major changes in the period examined.

Not a general history lesson.

5. Domestic Capability Baseline

What is documented inside the country:

* censorship;
* network filtering;
* shutdowns;
* platform monitoring;
* spyware;
* device seizure;
* biometric systems;
* social-graph analysis;
* border databases;
* online-speech prosecution;
* detention;
* family pressure;
* physical coercion.

This section establishes institutional habit and available infrastructure without automatically proving overseas use.

6. Documented Transnational Conduct

What has occurred abroad or across borders:

* spyware;
* phishing;
* hacking;
* impersonation;
* threats;
* smear campaigns;
* surveillance of protests;
* embassy monitoring;
* family coercion;
* passport pressure;
* extradition or deportation;
* forced return;
* abduction;
* assault;
* assassination;
* misuse of Interpol or legal cooperation.

Separate:

* established incidents;
* repeated pattern;
* alleged conduct;

* employment;

* public political activity.

This should answer:

What keeps the relationship with the state alive after migration?

* Prosecute?

* Share data?
* Facilitate return?
* Minimise the complaint?
* Treat it as community conflict?
* Prioritise strategic relations?
* Provide a safe reporting route?
* Correct harmful records?

This is essential. The foreign state’s capability only explains part of the risk.

16. Capability, Intent, And Documented Use

A compact analytical ladder:

Stage	Finding
Capability	What can be done?
Access	Who can reach the relevant data or system?
Intent	What objective is evidenced?
Implementation	What entered a real workflow?
Documented use	What deployment is established?
Attribution	Which actor is linked?
Causation	What effect is proven?

No narrative smearing between rows.

17. Evidence And Confidence Snapshot

Separate confidence for:

* domestic capability;
* transnational use;
* embassy involvement;
* family pressure;
* spyware or cyber attribution;
* relationship governance;
* state direction;
* causal effect;
* current relevance.

Include:

* basis;
* main gap;
* last reviewed.

18. Rival Explanations And Disproof Conditions

* Commercial surveillance.
* Platform failure.
* Local factionalism.
* Personal retaliation.
* Community conflict.
* Administrative error.
* Criminal activity.
* Contractor overreach.
* Allied-state action.
* Several causes operating together.

State what evidence would lower confidence.

This keeps the node from becoming a country-shaped explanation for everything unpleasant.

19. Practical Questions For Diaspora Readers

A short, non-alarmist checklist:

* Which documents still depend on the state?
* Could travel create compulsory contact?
* Are relatives exposed?
* Which accounts contain sensitive contact graphs?
* Which relationships are essential?
* Which host-state institution owns protection?
* What evidence can be preserved safely?
* Which dependency can be diversified?
* What should not be confronted directly?
* What can remain unresolved?

20. Specialist Knowledge And Evidence Base

* Key languages.
* Important country specialists.
* Digital-rights organisations.
* Human-rights organisations.
* Technical laboratories.
* Legal sources.
* Local and diaspora journalism.
* Fact-checking or platform research.
* Main evidentiary gaps.
* Whether the public record is dominated by:
    * state;
    * opposition;
    * exile;
    * international;
    * classified;
    * English-language sources.

21. Comparison Is Not Rebuttal

Standard callout in every node:

Comparison is not rebuttal.
Evidence that another state uses similar or more severe methods does not answer the conduct examined here. It may justify a cross-reference or separate node. It does not remove the present actor from scrutiny.

Internal version:

“But Country X—” is a routing instruction, not a defence.

22. What This Node Does Not Establish

A tailored restraint section:

* no collective suspicion of the population;
* no claim that every diaspora member is targeted;
* no claim that capability proves individual use;
* no claim that historical conduct is automatically current;
* no claim that every community organisation is an intermediary;
* no claim that every cyber incident is state-directed;
* no advice to confront suspected actors.

23. Compact Country Definition

A short closing synthesis:

* actor;
* main diaspora pathways;
* strongest documented capabilities;
* principal uncertainty;
* main regional connection;
* practical significance.

24. Standard Polaris Close

* ## 🌌 Constellations
* ## ✨ Stardust
* ## 🏮 Footer
* Country-specific cross-references.
* Comparative method links.
* Regional README.
* Regional cooperation node.
* Relevant LDoH mechanism nodes.
* Sovereignty line and update date.

Flexible weighting

The headings stay broadly consistent, but not every node gets equal length.

For example:

* Oman: more restrained, with major attention to evidence gaps and administrative or speech-control pathways.
* Morocco: stronger spyware, diaspora, European cooperation, and prosecution sections.
* Lebanon: fragmented actors, party structures, telecoms, foreign sponsors, and weak remedy.
* Saudi Arabia: very large transnational repression, spyware, family pressure, influence, vendor, and diplomatic sections.
* Israel: very large domestic/occupation, production, licensing, export, industrial dependency, and diaspora-distinction sections.
* Yemen: fragmentation, armed actors, external control, telecoms, humanitarian dependence, and thin attribution.
* Palestinian governance: separate authorities, occupation, security coordination, border and telecom dependence, donor architecture, and diaspora/refugee structures.

The common spine gives the reader consistency. The variable weight prevents fake symmetry.

Opening purpose

* Introduce the folder as a diaspora-facing capability guide, not a general regional history.
* Explain why it is organised country by country:
    * people encounter specific states;
    * passports, borders, relatives, embassies, courts, security services, and records are country-specific;
    * readers should not have to reconstruct their own risk pathway from abstract regional themes.
* State the governing question:
    * How can state, commercial, diplomatic, and proxy systems affect people connected to this country while they are living abroad?
* Make clear that the node is about:
    * capability;
    * documented use;
    * relationship pressure;
    * cross-border reach;
    * host-state response;
    * practical protection.
* Not a ranking of “bad states.”
* Not a claim that all people from a country are politically aligned with its government.

    * monarchies;
    * republics;
    * electoral systems;
    * occupations;
    * fragmented governments;
    * militias;
    * civil-war systems;
    * external sponsors;
    * contested sovereignties.

    * security technologies travel regionally;
    * intelligence relationships cross borders;
    * vendors and contractors serve multiple states;
    * narratives and influence operations circulate;
    * families and diasporas span several jurisdictions;

Strong lines worth preserving

Country-facing for accessibility; actor-specific inside the analysis.

The target may be an individual, but the operational object is often the relationship field around them.

Disinformation may not need to establish one coherent lie. It may only need to make the target expensive to verify.

Democracy is not an answer to a supply-chain question.

Normalisation does not merely open embassies. It joins procurement systems, investors, vendors, security institutions, and technical infrastructure.

Diaspora repression follows people across borders. Procurement carries the border machinery into everybody else’s institutions.

A surveillance state may become very good at preventing society from expressing pressure while becoming catastrophically bad at knowing how much pressure exists.

The teeth may be distributed through the alliance structure.

“But Country X—” is a routing instruction, not a defence.

Equal questions do not require equal conclusions.

Yes. Following the money turns the capability map into an incentive map.

Suddenly the question is no longer only:

Which government wants this system?

It becomes:

Who gets paid when the government keeps wanting it?

And then the cast expands rapidly:

* spyware vendors;
* defence primes;
* cloud providers;
* telecoms;
* biometric firms;
* analytics companies;
* data brokers;
* consultancies;
* PR firms;
* law firms;
* compliance contractors;
* investors;
* sovereign wealth funds;
* export-credit agencies;
* insurers;
* training providers;
* resellers;
* subcontractors;
* former officials;
* universities and research partnerships.

At which point some actors are present because they believe in “regional stability,” some because they want diplomatic leverage, and some because the contract has eight zeroes and a maintenance tail.

That absolutely needs to enter the README under a section like:

💸 Follow The Money

* Identify who funds, builds, licenses, sells, finances, insures, integrates, updates, and maintains the capability.
* Trace:
    * parent companies;
    * subsidiaries;
    * investors;
    * sovereign wealth involvement;
    * joint ventures;
    * local resellers;
    * lobbying;
    * government grants;
    * export support;
    * revolving-door appointments;
    * university and research partnerships.
* Distinguish:
    * purchaser;
    * operator;
    * beneficiary;
    * financier;
    * regulator;
    * diplomatic sponsor.
* Ask who profits from:
    * expansion;
    * threat inflation;
    * recurring licences;
    * data retention;
    * interoperability;
    * crisis procurement;
    * weak oversight;
    * failed reform.
* Note that commercial dependency may keep a capability alive after its original political justification has weakened.
* Identify where accountability is diluted across corporate layers and jurisdictions.

The central point is:

Repressive capability is not sustained by ideology alone. It is sustained by invoices, renewals, investment returns, procurement careers, and institutions that become dependent on the system continuing to look necessary.

And that is when you get the “bro, why are you even here?” actors:

* the PR company helping an authoritarian client clean up the backlash from surveillance;
* the consultancy writing the human-rights framework for the system it helped procure;
* the investor calling the product “trust infrastructure”;
* the university providing legitimacy and technical talent;
* the allied government promoting export controls while supporting the wider security relationship;
* the platform selling monitoring tools while issuing annual democracy reports;
* the contractor paid first to build the risk system and then to assess whether it works.

It becomes a self-licking procurement ecosystem.

And following the money also helps with attribution because cash often leaves clearer traces than covert intent:

* contracts;
* beneficial ownership;
* acquisition history;
* lobbying disclosures;
* trade missions;
* export licences;
* investor presentations;
* procurement notices;
* sanctions records;
* partnership announcements;
* training agreements;
* conference sponsorship;
* revolving-door employment.

So yes, add this as a major README principle:

Follow the money before accepting the mission statement.

Because the mission statement says “security.”

The cap table may say “regional authoritarianism as recurring revenue.”

The logic is:

* Oman through Qatar: comparatively contained starting points, though not “safe” or concern-free.
* Tunisia through Iraq: meaningful repression or institutional fragility, but generally less developed evidence of globally projected diaspora capability than the later group.
* Jordan and Morocco: move upward because documented spyware use and civil-society targeting make them more serious than their moderate regional image suggests. Citizen Lab has documented Pegasus targeting in Jordan and linked Morocco to abusive spyware use.  
* Bahrain through Türkiye: established online repression, surveillance, prosecution, and—in Egypt and Türkiye especially—aggressive pursuit of critics abroad.  
* UAE, Saudi Arabia, and Iran: substantial state capability, documented transnational reach, severe consequences, and strong regional or international projection. Saudi Arabia remains among the world’s lowest-scoring internet environments, while the UAE has been implicated in regional cooperation facilitating forced return and disappearance.  
* Israel: late not because it can be cleanly ranked “worse” than the previous three, but because its node combines domestic state use, occupation infrastructure, technology production, export licensing, regional diplomacy, and the crucial separation of Israel from Jewish diaspora identity.
* Syria, Yemen, and Palestinian fragmented governance: last because “the state” ceases to be a usable simple unit. They require actor-by-actor treatment of territorial control, armed groups, foreign sponsors, occupation, telecommunications, borders, and documentary dependencies.
* Regional cooperation last: once every country node exists, we can trace the actual vendor, intelligence, deportation, investment, and normalisation links without turning the regional node into speculative spaghetti. Regional cooperation is demonstrably relevant to transnational repression, including through bodies such as the Arab Interior Ministers Council.  

I would place a note in the README explaining that drafting order is not a permanent risk ranking. Conditions change, and concern depends on who the reader is. An Iranian dissident, Bahraini activist, Palestinian organiser, Israeli anti-government journalist, or Moroccan critic will not experience the same hierarchy.

But as a workload strategy: yes. Begin with Oman, learn and stabilise the country-node template, and gradually approach the absolute industrial wheelie bin at the far end.
