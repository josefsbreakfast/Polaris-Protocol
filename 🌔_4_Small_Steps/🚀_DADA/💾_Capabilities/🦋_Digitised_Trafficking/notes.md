🦋_Digitised_Trafficking/ belongs inside 🚀 Defence Against Digital Authoritarianism because the useful work is not only naming the abuse. It is teaching people how to recognise and interrupt the machinery:

* identity and vulnerability mapping
* compromised or deceptive accounts
* coercive contact and relationship engineering
* image, voice, and identity replication
* reputational and economic pressure
* surveillance across devices and platforms
* isolation from trusted people
* evidence fragmentation and institutional buck-passing
* practical steps for preserving agency, records, consent, and routes out

So the historical material explains what kind of system this can become, while DADA supplies the practical defensive method.

The folder description could eventually be something like:

Practical ways to recognise and resist trafficking systems that use data, platforms, synthetic media, surveillance, coercive relationships, and distributed institutional power.

And the key DADA distinction should remain:

This section does not require a person to prove the whole system before taking sensible protective steps against the parts they can observe.

That is very much the existing DADA model: reduce confusion, separate observable mechanics from uncertain attribution, preserve evidence, widen support, and recover choices one layer at a time.

In a digital age, the nearest analogue would not necessarily be a row of guarded buildings. It would be a distributed system for making women sexually accessible to men while preserving institutional deniability.

The architecture might look like this:

Recruitment becomes targeting. Vulnerable women are identified through platform data, leaked records, dating apps, financial distress, immigration status, health information, activism, sexual history, or intimate communications. Some are lured through work, romance, protection, visibility, money, or promises of access.

Confinement becomes informational and economic. A woman may be physically free to leave her home while being unable to escape the system around her: blackmail, surveillance, account interference, reputational threats, manipulated search results, employment pressure, benefit disruption, doxxing, family targeting, or the threat that intimate material will be released.

The brothel becomes a platform stack. Access is mediated through private groups, paid channels, encrypted chats, dating systems, livestreams, synthetic pornography, compromised devices, data brokers, and intermediaries who can each pretend they only supplied one harmless technical component.

Repeated rape can become repeated digital sexual violation. This includes coerced sexual performance, remote sexual control, non-consensual recording, livestreamed abuse, deepfake pornography, voice cloning, sexualised impersonation, image-based abuse, and the circulation of private material to an audience the victim cannot identify or stop.

The queue of soldiers becomes scalable demand. Thousands or millions of men can consume, comment on, train models upon, modify, trade, or commission sexualised representations of the same woman. The victim does not need to be physically present for her identity and body to be repeatedly exploited.

Military “morale” becomes engagement, cohesion, or operational need. The justification is updated: keeping personnel motivated, building trust, securing access, preventing misconduct elsewhere, managing lonely men, supporting intelligence collection, testing systems, maintaining community morale, or providing “harmless fantasy.” The woman’s consent is treated as an administrative inconvenience.

The overseer becomes an algorithm plus human handlers. Recommendation systems identify what attracts attention. Operators test pressure points. Online crowds perform harassment. Employers, police, intelligence personnel, contractors, platform moderators, or journalists may each hold a fragment of the picture while no one accepts responsibility for the whole.

Punishment becomes visibility control. Compliance may produce temporary calm, reach, money, affection, restored accounts, or reduced harassment. Refusal may produce isolation, algorithmic burial, sexual smears, hostile content floods, exposure of private information, fabricated evidence, or threats against relationships and livelihood.

Medical inspection becomes data extraction. Menstrual data, fertility information, medication history, trauma responses, sexual preferences, biometrics, facial expressions, sleep patterns, and physiological reactions can be collected and used to optimise coercion or sexual content.

The paper register becomes the database. The system records who viewed, requested, paid, shared, trained, flagged, contacted, or exploited her—but those records may be classified, commercially protected, fragmented among contractors, or described as metadata rather than evidence of abuse.

The euphemism survives. It would not be called sexual slavery. It would be called safeguarding, companionship, content moderation, influence work, behavioural research, relationship facilitation, asset handling, user personalisation, adult entertainment, welfare monitoring, or national security.

The essential continuity is this:

Men with institutional power decide that a woman’s sexual personhood is infrastructure for male needs, then divide the process among enough departments, contractors, platforms, and euphemisms that nobody has to admit what the system does.

There are also important differences. Digital abuse is not automatically equivalent to the physical captivity, repeated rape, torture, disease, forced pregnancy, and death suffered by historical “comfort women.” The comparison becomes serious when digital systems are used to organise, compel, conceal, distribute, or profit from sexual access without meaningful consent—especially where leaving triggers punishment and institutions knowingly maintain the arrangement.

The modern question is therefore not merely, “Was she physically locked in a room?” It is:

Could she meaningfully refuse, leave, identify the beneficiaries, recover her private life, and obtain protection without being punished by the system?

When the answer is no, “online” is not the opposite of captivity. It is the delivery mechanism.
