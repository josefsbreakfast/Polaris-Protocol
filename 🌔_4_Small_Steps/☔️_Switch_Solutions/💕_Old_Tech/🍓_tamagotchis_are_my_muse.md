# 🍓 Tamagotchis Are My Muse
**First created:** 2026-09-28 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-28  
*Maybe the future of personal technology needs fewer universal interfaces and more kawaii little guys.*

---

> Silicon Valley:
> 
> What if we created an intimate, persistent, personalised computational companion that you carry around all day, form an attachment to, regularly interact with, and which develops an ongoing relationship with you?  
>
> Bandai, 1996:  
>
> 🥚 beep beep **bitch feed me**

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

This is not an argument that old technology was better because it was old.

It is not an argument that smartphones are bad because they are powerful.

It is not even, strictly speaking, an argument that everybody needs a Tamagotchi.

Although.

🥚

The useful thing about Tamagotchis, Digimon, Digivices, pedometer pets and the strange little dedicated electronic objects of the 1990s and 2000s is that they make a design assumption visible:

**a personal computer does not have to be a general-purpose computer.**

It can be a thing.

It can have a bounded purpose. A recognisable shape. Three buttons. A ridiculous little creature living inside it. It can ask something of its owner without needing to know everything about its owner.

And perhaps, after more than a decade of the smartphone absorbing cameras, maps, diaries, music players, games, social lives, payments, work, identity, location, advertising and increasingly AI assistance into the same anxious rectangle, there is something worth recovering from that.

Not necessarily the technology.

The **idea of the object**.

---

## 🍓 The Tamagotchi Is The Muse

The Tamagotchi is interesting precisely because it is not trying to become anything else.

It does not want to replace your camera.

It does not want to organise your work.

It does not want to infer what you might buy next.

It does not need to become the universal interface between you and the rest of the computational world.

It has a little creature in it.

The creature requires care.

Sometimes the creature shits.

This is, in its own way, an extraordinarily coherent product proposition.

The limitation is not merely an unfortunate consequence of primitive hardware. The limitation helps produce the relationship. The object is comprehensible. Its demands are bounded. Its rules can be learned. Its personality emerges inside those constraints.

The Tamagotchi can become intensely **personal** without becoming intensely **personalised**.

That distinction matters.

A scratched plastic egg can be personal because I carried it around. Because I remember where I bought it. Because I raised a particular creature. Because I named the little bastard. Because it beeped during maths. Because it belongs to a period of my life.

None of that requires a company to construct an increasingly detailed computational model of me.

**Personal is something a human can make.**

**Personalised is something a system can do to the human.**

Those categories can overlap.

They do not have to.

## 👾 Digimon Was Boss, Actually

There is also a danger in retrospectively treating dedicated devices as charming little technological fossils which people tolerated before proper computers arrived.

No.

Digimon was **boss**.

By the 2000s, children were already surrounded by increasingly capable electronic devices. We still wanted the Digivice.

Because the Digivice was not merely a computer with fewer features.

It was a **prop from the fictional universe**.

The form of the object was part of the experience. Carrying it made the imaginative world slightly more materially available. It had identity. It had rules. It had collectability. It had tactile controls. It looked cool clipped to your clothes.

And, critically:

**it had a little guy in it.**

Then somebody took the Tamagotchi proposition and asked the obvious next question:

> What if the little guy could fight another little guy?

Technological progress.

The lesson here is not that consumers secretly want worse computers.

It is that **computational capability and desirability are not the same axis**.

Sometimes a thing becomes more desirable because it refuses capabilities that would make it more general.

## 🌙 Then Sailor Moon Happened

There is another branch of the same childhood technological imagination.

Tamagotchi:

> Care for the little creature.

Digimon:

> Care for the little creature and make him fight.

Sailor Moon:

> The little creature is now your adviser. **You are the weapons platform.**

This is important.

Children's media was full of specific magical objects: brooches, transformation pens, communicators, wands, compacts, Digivices, Pokédexes.

Their desirability came partly from their specificity.

The object did not contain every possible function.

It did **one impossible thing extremely well**.

There is a reason a magical compact that calls down the power of the Moon has more imaginative force than:

> Please authenticate to access personalised lunar services.

Some of this is obviously fiction.

But fiction is allowed to teach product design.

The object should make somebody want to pick it up.

## 📱 The Extremely Anxious Rectangle

The smartphone achieved something extraordinary.

It collapsed an ecology of separate devices into one portable general-purpose computer:

- camera;
- music player;
- map;
- diary;
- alarm clock;
- games console;
- calculator;
- notebook;
- radio;
- pedometer;
- communicator;
- browser;
- payment device;
- television;
- work terminal.

That consolidation was enormously useful.

It also changed the economics and information environment surrounding many ordinary activities.

A walk can now involve an account, a location service, a behavioural profile, notifications, cloud infrastructure, advertising systems and multiple layers of data processing before we have even got to the bit where the human being is **walking**.

The question is therefore not:

**How do we undo the smartphone?**

It is:

**Now that the universal rectangle already exists, which functions might become nicer if they became things again?**

## 🕵️ Sometimes I Don't Want The Object To Know Me

Personalisation was sold for years as an uncomplicated improvement.

The machine knows more about you.

Therefore the machine can serve you better.

Sometimes this is genuinely useful.

But knowing somebody better computationally can require observation, retention, inference, profiling and increasingly sophisticated models of what that person is likely to want or do.

There is a point at which convenience can become tiring.

Sometimes I do not want the object to anticipate me.

Sometimes I want it to **do its little job**.

This may be one reason bounded technology feels newly attractive in an environment saturated with recommendation systems, behavioural analytics and AI assistants.

A Tamagotchi does not need to infer my purchasing intent.

It needs to know:

**did you feed the egg?**

This is not an argument against all personalisation.

It is an argument for remembering that **non-personalisation is also a design choice**.

Privacy can be a feature.

Legibility can be limited.

A product can leave parts of its owner gloriously irrelevant to its operation.

## 🗺️ Pokémon GO And The Fracture In The Magic

Pokémon GO demonstrated how extraordinarily powerful the opposite combination could be.

The original experience was magical because it placed a fictional layer over ordinary geography.

There are Pokémon in the world now.

Go outside.

Find them.

A park becomes somewhere to explore. A statue becomes relevant. A familiar street acquires another informational layer. Walking itself becomes play.

That remains a brilliant interaction model.

But the wider Niantic ecosystem also demonstrated how valuable the informational by-products of location-based play can become. Player-contributed locations, scans and other spatial information have supported increasingly sophisticated geospatial infrastructure.

The point should not be flattened into the claim that every Pokémon GO player action was secretly "training AI". Different features, datasets and consent arrangements matter.

The more useful design lesson is this:

**an interaction can be delightful to the human and simultaneously produce valuable machine-readable knowledge about the physical world.**

Once the second layer becomes visible, the first can feel different.

The childlike proposition:

> 🌳 Go outside and find a Pokémon.

can coexist with an industrial proposition:

> This population is generating structured information about physical space.

That does not erase the game.

It does complicate the magic.

And it gives us a useful question for whatever comes next:

**Can we make technology that produces magic for its user without requiring the user's life to become valuable informational exhaust?**

## 🌸 Flower Go Walk Says Yes, Obviously

Takashi Murakami's **Flower Go Walk** is useful because it proves that the Tamagotchi lineage does not have to remain retro.

The object deliberately looks backwards while doing something perfectly contemporary.

You carry a little flower.

You walk.

The device counts the steps.

The flower progresses, changes and travels through its little world.

That is enough to create a relationship between:

**physical movement → tiny computational object → delightful consequence.**

No universal interface is required.

The user still gets:

- movement;
- interaction;
- progression;
- collection;
- attachment;
- play;
- a reason to carry the object;
- a little guy.

This is not technologically maximal.

That is part of why it works.

**Woman walked to Tesco with little flower** can simply be a successful interaction.

It does not inherently need to become the first stage of an industrial geospatial-data pipeline.

## 👓 Mark, I See What You Are Seeing

There is something genuinely interesting in the current push towards AI wearables and devices beyond the smartphone.

The diagnosis contains a useful insight:

**people may not want every computational interaction to require pulling out the phone and staring into the rectangle.**

Correct.

But there is a danger of recognising the desire for another object while preserving all the assumptions of the universal platform.

Camera.

Assistant.

Context.

Accounts.

Ecosystem.

Personalisation.

Increasingly seamless mediation.

More capability.

More integration.

More expensive hardware.

At which point:

**Mark, I see how you are seeing that we want the handheld device.**

But perhaps we do not want to spend hundreds of pounds on a **non-fun Tamagotchi**.

The market signal might not be:

> Please move the universal computing environment closer to my body.

It might be:

> Give me a delightful little object with a comprehensible purpose that belongs to me and does not require my entire digital identity to justify its existence.

This is a materially different design brief.

Meta's current AI-glasses work is technically ambitious: cameras, microphones, audio, displays, gesture input, motion sensing, location-aware experiences and AI integration can all be combined into increasingly capable wearable interfaces.

Fine.

But capability is not the only thing people buy.

**Where is my creature, Mark?**

## 🥚 The Little Guy Principle

Perhaps there is a usable design heuristic here.

### The Little Guy Principle

Before adding another layer of general-purpose computation, ask:

1. **What is this object actually for?**
2. **Can the user understand what it knows about them?**
3. **Can it work without knowing anything else?**
4. **Does it have an identity separate from the platform behind it?**
5. **Would limiting its capabilities make it more comprehensible, trustworthy or fun?**
6. **Can the interaction create meaning without extracting additional information?**
7. **Does it need an account?**
8. **Does it need the cloud?**
9. **Does it need to personalise?**
10. **Could there simply be a little guy?**

This is not appropriate for every device.

That is rather the point.

Not every device needs to be everything.

## 💕 Old Tech Does Not Mean Going Backwards

This is why **Old Tech** belongs inside Switch Solutions.

The objective is not historical reenactment.

It is to look at technological branches which were abandoned, consolidated or made economically unfashionable and ask what design properties disappeared with them.

Useful properties might include:

- bounded functionality;
- physical controls;
- local processing;
- offline capability;
- low power requirements;
- user ownership;
- comprehensible data flows;
- repairability;
- collectability;
- durability;
- strong object identity;
- playfulness;
- privacy by irrelevance;
- the ability to function without an account;
- the ability to be personal without being personalised.

Some old devices will fail this test spectacularly.

Some new devices will pass it.

That is why **old** describes the source of inspiration, not the age requirement for the solution.

Flower Go Walk belongs here just as comfortably as a Tamagotchi.

The question is not:

> Was this made in 1998?

The question is:

> **What did this design tradition know that we forgot?**

## ☔️ Why This Is A Switch Solution

Switch Solutions should not require everybody to withdraw from modern technology and live in a Faraday cage with a Game Boy.

The useful intervention is smaller.

Where there is a viable alternative, users can move some demand away from products which maximise integration, extraction or platform dependence and towards products which compete on different properties.

That creates another signal.

Not:

**make the assistant know me better.**

But perhaps:

**make the object need to know me less.**

Not:

**put every service into one interface.**

But:

**give this function an object worth owning.**

Not:

**maximise engagement.**

But:

**make the interaction delightful enough that I come back voluntarily.**

Not:

**build another platform.**

But:

**give us the little guys.**

---

## 🌱 The Small Step

Nobody needs to throw their smartphone into the North Sea.

The small step is simply to stop treating the current arrangement as technologically inevitable.

When replacing, buying or designing something, it is permissible to ask:

> Would I actually prefer this function as its own thing?

Maybe the answer is no.

The phone is extremely useful.

But sometimes the answer might be:

**yes, actually.**

A camera.

An MP3 player.

An e-reader.

A handheld game.

A pedometer pet.

A weird little electronic flower.

A device whose entire computational model of its owner consists of:

> she walked 4,000 steps today :)

There are worse technological futures.

---

## 🧭 Design Questions To Carry Forward

The Tamagotchi is the muse because it opens better questions.

Not:

> How much intelligence can we put into the object?

But:

> **How much intelligence does this object actually need?**

Not:

> How personalised can the experience become?

But:

> **How personal can the experience become without personalisation?**

Not:

> How many functions can we consolidate?

But:

> **Which functions become better when they are allowed to have their own form?**

Not:

> How seamlessly can the platform disappear into everyday life?

But:

> **Should the platform be here at all?**

And, naturally:

> **Would this be improved by a little guy?**

Sometimes the answer will be no.

But Silicon Valley should perhaps be required to show its working.
