## 1. 🙄 The Man Has Entered the Seminar

The initial question is interesting. Could AI accelerate a longstanding scientific problem? Yes, potentially. Could it produce a breakthrough no human has yet produced? Also potentially. Could anyone responsibly promise a complete theory of quantum gravity within a year on the strength of a few exciting mathematical results? That depends on what the results actually demonstrate and what the promised endpoint is. Those are the questions to discuss.

Instead, we get the Jews.

The formulation matters. Historically, the “Jewish Question” was a European political and social formulation that long predates Nazism; Nazi Germany's “Final Solution” transformed that language into the administrative vocabulary of genocide. Invoking a “Jewish problem” is not a neutral way to describe human beings. Nor, by itself, does a short provocative post establish an intention to commit genocide. The responsible response is to interrogate what was said, why that formulation was chosen, and how it relates to the subject under discussion.

**Did you mean to reference a genocidal historical tradition? What did you mean? And what has that got to do with quantum gravity?**

I do not need to win a computer-science competition to ask those questions. I need only have read the sentence.

The physicists were trying to unify fundamental forces. Curtis was trying to become one.

## 2. 🧿 Expertise Is Not a Universal Access Pass

Yarvin's documented career includes software engineering, the Urbit project and political writing under the name Mencius Moldbug. His political proposals and influence deserve to be discussed on their own terms. But engineering expertise is not identical to expertise in theoretical physics, neuroscience, political legitimacy, Jewish history, reproductive autonomy or institutional accountability.

That is not a special limitation peculiar to Yarvin. It applies to me. It applies to the researcher who asked the original question. It applies to everyone. The purpose of disciplines is not to prohibit intellectual curiosity across boundaries. It is to remind us that different problems require different methods and evidence.

A person may be very good at designing a computational architecture and rather poor at explaining the social consequences of deploying it. Another person may be brilliant at political history and misunderstand machine learning. Neither becomes worthless. Both become more useful when someone asks a question they cannot immediately answer.

Why, then, does the mere performance of expertise so often carry political authority? In certain networks, technical fluency is taken as evidence of superior judgement about human societies. That is a category error. A working programme does not prove that its author has discovered the correct constitution for everyone else.

**SIR. SHOW YOUR CREDENTIALS.** Not as a demand for permission to speak, but as a request to identify which claims your credentials actually support.

## 3. 🧮 An Extraordinary Calculator Is Still Not Magic

I keep calling AI a spectacular calculator, and I mean that affectionately. A calculator that can help write code, explore proofs, propose hypotheses, explain difficult material, recognise patterns and coordinate tools is an extraordinary achievement. But the analogy needs taking apart, because otherwise I risk doing precisely what I'm accusing the gentlemen of doing: relying on a phrase that sounds explanatory without showing the mechanism.

### 🪙 The coin machine, the pinball table and the actual machinery

Imagine the arcade coin machine. You insert a coin; it encounters obstacles; the path it takes depends on the arrangement of the machine. Now imagine the arrangement has been altered through exposure to enormous numbers of examples, and the machine can use tools and repeat its work. That's the intuition I want from the analogy: **structure affects the pathway, and previous training affects the structure**. The pinball comparison conveys sensitivity to inputs. Neither model literally describes the computations inside a transformer. The ball is not secretly doing matrix multiplication, babes.

Here's a simplified journey through a contemporary language-model interaction, without pretending every model or AI system has the same architecture:

1. **Input and tokens.** The text is broken into units called tokens. Tokens are not necessarily whole words; they can be pieces of words, punctuation or other encoded units.
2. **Representations.** Tokens are mapped to numerical representations. Rather than looking up one fixed definition of each word, the model processes relationships in context.
3. **Learned parameters.** Training has adjusted large numbers of numerical parameters. They influence how information is transformed through the network. This is the bit the weighted-pinball analogy is trying to gesture towards.
4. **Attention and transformations.** In transformer-based models, attention helps different parts of the input influence one another. Layers of computation construct representations useful for predicting subsequent output.
5. **Training versus inference.** Training changes the model's parameters using optimisation and feedback. Inference uses those learned parameters to process a new request. A normal conversation does not automatically retrain the underlying model's weights.
6. **Generation.** A language model produces output incrementally. The procedure is not equivalent to consulting a complete encyclopaedia and copying a stored answer, nor does fluent wording guarantee a correct one.
7. **Tools and agents.** Some deployed systems can call a calculator, search material, execute code, inspect results, revise a plan or act over multiple steps. That extra machinery matters: a tool-using system is not just a single pass through a text model.

The Enigma machine belongs in the analogy cabinet too: it reminds us that computation can transform information in ways humans cannot conveniently reproduce by hand. But an electromechanical cipher machine and a trained neural network are profoundly different architectures. Analogies are for opening the door, not for locking us inside the wrong room.

And yes, the *Dungeons & Dragons* beholder and displacer beast are still invited. They are excellent reminders that **what a system appears to be doing and how it actually produces the effect are separate questions**. We can enjoy the Monster Manual without citing it as a neuroscience paper.

### 🧠 Four words that are not interchangeable

**Computation** concerns operations on representations. **Intelligence** is a contested umbrella term for capacities such as learning, reasoning, adaptation and solving problems. **Cognition** concerns processes including perception, memory, reasoning and planning; whether and in what sense machines possess cognition is debated. **Consciousness** refers, centrally, to subjective experience. Producing a convincing answer does not establish subjective experience; lacking human-like subjective experience would not necessarily prevent useful discoveries.

That last qualification is important. I am not claiming AI can only mimic existing material. Systems can generate genuinely novel and useful results, and may outperform humans on particular tasks. I am saying that **successful performance does not, by itself, tell us which mechanisms produced it**. If we want to compare machine intelligence with human intelligence, we must do more than point at an impressive output and shout, BEHOLD, A BRAIN.

## 4. 📰 The Mathematics Controversy: The Machine, the Mathematicians and the Marketing Department

This is not a hypothetical dispute. **There was a documented, public controversy in September and October 2026**, and it is part of the context in which the quantum-gravity speculation appeared. We can now name the announcements, distinguish the disagreements and inspect how correction happened. What we *cannot* do is establish that the first poster was responding to a particular headline without their saying so.

### 🗓️ What actually happened?

**8 September — the Navier–Stokes announcement.** OpenAI [announced a purported solution to the Navier–Stokes existence-and-smoothness Millennium Prize Problem](https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/). Navier–Stokes equations model fluid motion: air, water and other flowing substances. The long-standing question concerns whether initially smooth three-dimensional solutions can develop a singularity, a mathematical breakdown, in finite time. OpenAI described an internal model coordinating roughly **10,000 concurrent agents**, with the relevant effort taking approximately **88 hours**, followed by another **17 hours** for formalisation and checking using GPT-6 Astra. It published a mathematical write-up and a proof formalised in **Lean**, software that can mechanically check whether formal proof steps follow from their stated premises.

The company's precise claim was not that an AI had solved *every* fluid-dynamics problem or produced a new universal physical theory. It reported a particular singularity construction under the conditions of the official problem, including a smooth external force. OpenAI said this established alternatives **C and D** in the Clay formulation and explicitly said it **would not claim the Millennium Prize**. Those qualifications belong beside the headline, not buried underneath it.

**8–10 September — the priority and data-use dispute.** Mathematicians **Tristan Buckmaster** and **Levent Alpöge** had independently been working on a related Euler-equation problem, using AI tools. Buckmaster raised concerns about whether material entered into OpenAI's Codex could have influenced OpenAI's internal system, while explicitly not alleging that he knew such use had occurred. OpenAI denied accessing their unpublished work and, in a **10 September update**, said its investigation had found the relevant prompts could not have influenced the result, including through training. OpenAI also distinguished the *forced Euler* result of the other researchers from its own *unforced Euler* result. These are **competing statements about provenance and priority**, not a licence to announce either theft or complete exoneration by editorial fiat. [OpenAI's statement](https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/) and [*Nature*'s 17 September report](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02910-w) document the dispute.

**21 September — institutional response.** OpenAI [announced an independent Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence](https://openai.com/index/advisory-group-on-mathematics-and-ai/). Its proposed remit included evaluating the significance of emerging results, communicating them responsibly, and considering scholarly standards and access. OpenAI acknowledged mathematicians' concerns about the consequences of using open research problems as model benchmarks. The advisory group's independence and its ability to criticise OpenAI matter; so does the limit OpenAI stated: it would **not** decide the pace of OpenAI's internal mathematics progress.

**6 October — a mass release.** OpenAI [released a large collection of AI-generated mathematics manuscripts](https://openai.com/index/sharing-ai-progress-in-mathematics/). Its developer-community description counted **722 manuscripts grouped into 372 related result families**, arising from an evaluation involving approximately **4,000 problems**. Some came with machine-checkable Lean formalisation; **not all did**. Ten abridged reasoning summaries were also released. These numbers refer to different units: manuscripts are not identical to distinct solved problems, and a result family is not a guarantee of a fully verified breakthrough. [OpenAI's release details](https://community.openai.com/t/first-look-at-mathematics-manuscripts-from-an-internal-frontier-model-at-openai/1403886).

**7–8 October — the first public corrections.** OpenAI **withdrew three manuscripts** after a sign error invalidated an argument and affected dependent constructions. It also reported revisions to **14 other manuscripts**, including proof repairs, corrected statements and clearer assumptions. According to [*Retraction Watch*](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/10/08/openai-withdraws-preprints-722-manuscripts-unsolved-math-problems/), OpenAI said it found the errors in an audit, welcomed outside scrutiny and would correct or withdraw results where necessary. Its spokesperson said roughly half the released results lacked full confirmation at publication. This does not mean half were wrong; it means **verification status and mathematical correctness must not be conflated**.

**7–9 October — the scholarly argument broadens.** [*Nature* reported](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03196-8) sharply different reactions: excitement at the mathematical capability, concerns about researchers being scooped, and criticism that the release shifted a substantial verification and interpretation burden onto mathematicians. Oxford mathematician **Ursula Martin** objected to the burden of sorting through a mass of unfinished exposition. Other researchers argued that making results public was valuable and that the field should adapt. The [*Guardian*'s account](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/oct/07/openai-mathematical-findings-concerns) recorded concerns about proprietary models and whether the human researchers using them could fully understand or take responsibility for their outputs.

### 🔬 What were people actually disagreeing about?

Not one thing. **At least five different things.**

1. **Correctness:** Does the proof follow? The withdrawn papers show why this cannot be assumed from a confident announcement.
2. **Scope:** Does the result solve the stated original problem, a variant, or a narrower case? Formal conditions matter. An impressive answer to the wrong question remains the wrong answer.
3. **Provenance and credit:** Who supplied the problem formulation, intermediate ideas, prompts and human mathematical work? The Buckmaster–Alpöge dispute made this concrete.
4. **Verification and communication:** A Lean-checked proof can offer unusually strong assurance *about the formalised statement*, but readers must still inspect whether that statement captures the intended question and whether its mathematical exposition is intelligible. Not every manuscript was formalised.
5. **Research ecology:** What happens when a company releases hundreds of results at once? Who has the time and resources to check them? Who gets to choose the questions? Can researchers continue building careers and collaborative communities if the pace of release changes dramatically?

Those are not interchangeable objections. Someone may regard the Navier–Stokes proof as a major mathematical achievement **and** criticise the way the results were announced, attributed or released. Conversely, criticism of publication practices does not demonstrate that the underlying mathematics is false.

The October reaction included both mathematicians who were enthusiastic about the possibilities and mathematicians who feared that discovery would become detached from human comprehension, mentorship and collaborative scholarship. [*Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03196-8) and [*Retraction Watch*](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/10/08/openai-withdraws-preprints-722-manuscripts-unsolved-math-problems/) document that disagreement. The split is not conveniently reducible to *humans versus machines*. It is also about **how the institution of mathematics should change when a new tool changes the speed of production**.

### 🧿 This is what corrective feedback actually looks like

The three withdrawals are not proof that AI mathematics is a sham. Nor should we pretend they are irrelevant because the other hundreds of manuscripts remain available. They are an observable instance of **an output encountering scrutiny, an error being identified, and the public record being corrected**.

And the independent advisory group is a governance response to the same underlying issue: an extremely capable technical system is producing results faster than existing institutions can comfortably interpret, credit and absorb them. Whether that response proves sufficient is a further question, not an established outcome.

There is also a wonderfully uncomfortable lesson for the AI writing this very node. Earlier in our conversation I gave precise dates and figures without actually verifying them, then overcorrected by hiding the entire controversy behind a research placeholder. **Both moves were failures of intellectual method.** The correct procedure is to check the claims, show the documents, separate the competing accounts, and preserve the uncertainty where it genuinely remains. The author challenged me; the node improved. The stove, once again, provided peer review.

### 🌌 What this does *not* establish about quantum gravity

Even a formally checked proof of a major open mathematical problem is not, by itself, an experimentally confirmed physical theory. A mathematical theorem is assessed against specified assumptions and proof rules. A theory of quantum gravity must also explain physical observations and, ideally, make discriminating predictions that can be tested. AI could make a spectacular contribution to either task. But **mathematical acceleration does not erase the distinction between mathematical validity and empirical adequacy**.

So the reasonable response to “unified field theory / quantum gravity within the next twelve months?” is neither automatic ridicule nor automatic awe. It is: **What precisely would count as solving it? Which part can this system do? How will we check?**

And then, into that entirely legitimate scientific conversation, comes Curtis Yarvin with the Jews.

## 5. 🌌 Quantum Gravity Is Not a Benchmark Score

The original question asks whether AI might deliver unified field theory or quantum gravity within twelve months. These are related ambitions, not necessarily the same problem. General relativity describes gravitation and spacetime at large scales; quantum theory describes physical phenomena through a different mathematical framework. Finding a successful quantum account of gravity remains a major open challenge. But the field does not lack candidate approaches, and the difficulty is not simply that humans have failed to do enough arithmetic quickly enough.

Let's distinguish the things a triumphant announcement might mean:

**A new mathematical result.** Perhaps an identity, proof, approximation or computational technique relevant to an existing research programme. Valuable, but not a complete physical theory.

**A consistent candidate framework.** Perhaps a formulation that avoids a contradiction under specified assumptions. Also valuable. Mathematical consistency alone does not tell us which possible description corresponds to our universe.

**A unifying explanation.** A framework might recover established results in appropriate limits and explain phenomena previously treated separately. That is a more ambitious theoretical achievement.

**A distinctive prediction.** A theory gains empirical traction when it yields consequences that could, at least in principle, discriminate it from alternatives. In quantum gravity, obtaining feasible tests is itself a formidable difficulty.

**Observational confirmation.** Evidence would then need to be gathered, analysed and challenged. Theoretical elegance is not a substitute for measurement.

This is not a neat conveyor belt on which every discovery moves through five stations in exactly that order. Research is messier. But these are different kinds of evidence, and the differences matter.

### 🔭 What could AI actually accelerate?

It might explore enormous families of equations or models, search for hidden relationships, test limiting cases, generate candidate conjectures, verify calculations, find counterexamples, analyse data, design simulations or suggest experimentally discriminating predictions. It could help researchers eliminate a bad line of inquiry in an afternoon that might otherwise take months. It could contribute a genuinely unexpected idea. Increasingly capable systems may perform some of this work with limited human direction.

But scientific communities do not only produce candidate answers. They decide which questions are meaningful, whether the assumptions match the physical problem, how evidence should be interpreted, and whether an apparent result survives challenge. AI can assist in those activities too. The claim is not that human consciousness must perform every step. **The claim is that scientific validity cannot be inferred from computational impressiveness alone.**

There is a further distinction between *solving a mathematical description* and *learning which description nature follows*. A model could produce a stunningly elegant formulation that remains empirically underdetermined. The challenge is not solved merely because the output looks like a solution.

So, when someone says **twelve months**, I would like to know: twelve months until a candidate theory? A proof of a mathematical result? A falsifiable prediction? A decisive observation? What baseline evidence makes that timeline credible? What would count as failure?

Those are not hostile questions. They are the beginning of doing science.

## 6. 🧠 Artificial Neurons Are Not Biological Neurons

Here is a place where the language of computer science can mislead even technically sophisticated people. We call units in artificial networks *neurons*, and the name suggests a direct analogy with living nervous systems. There is a historical relationship, and abstract similarities matter. But an artificial neuron is generally a mathematical operation combining numerical inputs, learned parameters and a transformation. A biological neuron is a living cell.

A simplified biological account begins with **dendrites**, which receive signals; the **cell body**, where many influences are integrated; the **axon**, along which action potentials can travel; and **synapses**, where signalling influences other cells. Neural circuits involve excitatory and inhibitory effects, changing synaptic strengths, neuromodulators, metabolism, glial support and ongoing interaction with the body. Even that sketch leaves out an enormous amount of biology.

An artificial unit does not have a digestive system, a childhood, a spinal cord or a hand that has just encountered the oven. Artificial networks can nevertheless implement powerful forms of learning and information processing. The mistake is not borrowing biological inspiration. **The mistake is forgetting which properties were abstracted away.**

### 🫀 Intelligence happens somewhere

Human perception, action, memory, language and emotion develop in an organism that must move, regulate itself, respond to bodily states and interact with other organisms. A child learns about gravity not merely from a textbook but from falling, reaching, balancing, watching other people and adjusting expectations. Human understanding is shaped by development, culture, relationships and a body that has consequences when its predictions are wrong.

That is the relevance of Embodied Information Ecology. Information is not experienced as an isolated mathematical string. The conditions of reception and action matter. A warning, a social slight, a scientific hypothesis and a burn are not processed in identical ways simply because all can be described as information.

Some AI systems have sensors, robots, memory, simulation environments or opportunities for interactive learning. So we should not claim all AI is forever disembodied. We should ask **what embodiment, experience and interaction a particular system actually has**, rather than assuming it possesses the human version because its answers sound familiar.

### 🧩 Consciousness is another question again

A system may perform well on reasoning tasks without establishing that it has subjective experience. There is no universally accepted test for machine consciousness, and scientific theories disagree about what would be required. Likewise, proving that a system differs from a human brain does not prove that it cannot make a discovery. Human-style consciousness is not a demonstrated prerequisite for mathematical innovation.

This is where the comparison becomes genuinely interesting instead of merely flattering to whichever species or machine is being discussed. **Different cognitive architectures may solve overlapping problems by different means.** Understanding those differences can help us decide where collaboration is useful, where error is likely and which claims require caution.

### 🧬 The edge cases are not a master race

Theoretical physics has benefited from unusual perspectives, intense specialisation, collaboration and disagreement. Cognitive diversity can make a research community more productive. But we cannot conclude from celebrated individual scientists that quantum theorists as a group are predominantly neurodivergent, nor should we confuse neurodivergence with a ranking of human worth. Some discoveries are flashes of insight; others depend on teams, instruments, painstaking checking and decades of accumulated work. AI may help with all of these without reproducing a particular human cognitive profile.

The question isn't whether a machine can be Leonardo da Vinci. It is which capabilities it actually has, what it lacks, and how we know.


### 🔁 Two kinds of learning, two kinds of feedback

Here is the comparison I wish more discussions of AI would actually make. **Human learning and machine learning can produce overlapping abilities without being the same process.** The resemblance in the output is not a licence to assume resemblance in the mechanism.

| Question | Developing human | Contemporary AI system |
|---|---|---|
| Where does the structure come from? | Biology, development, bodily growth and experience shape a living nervous system. | Engineers select architectures, training procedures and interfaces; optimisation adjusts parameters. |
| What supplies feedback? | Sensation, movement, other people, rewards, setbacks, memory and the consequences of action. | Training examples, loss functions, human preferences, reward signals, evaluations and, for some systems, observations from tools or environments. |
| What changes? | Neural circuits, learned habits, expectations, skills and relationships can change across a lifetime. | Model weights change during training or further training; deployed systems may also adapt through context, external memory or tool-mediated loops. |
| Does every encounter rewrite the system? | No. Human memory and learning are selective, context-dependent and not guaranteed. | No. A conversation with a deployed model does **not** ordinarily update its underlying weights. |
| What are the stakes of getting it wrong? | Embodied, emotional and social consequences, sometimes immediate. | Consequences depend on deployment: an incorrect answer may be harmless, or it may affect a person or institution acting on it. |
| Who establishes the objective? | Human motivation is multiple, embodied, socially shaped and not reducible to one external score. | Developers and organisations specify training objectives and deployment conditions, though behaviour can diverge from what they intended. |

These are **functional comparisons**, not claims that a reward model is a parent, that a spinal reflex is gradient descent, or that biological neurons are miniature transformers. A human learns about a hot object through physiology, experience and interpretation; a language model may learn to describe burns from training data without ever being burned. A robot might acquire some physical feedback, but that still would not give it a human childhood. Conversely, AI may explore mathematical possibilities at a speed no unaided person can match. Different histories of learning produce different strengths, blind spots and risks.

This is also why **guardrails are not an embarrassing admission that intelligence has failed**. Both human and artificial systems benefit from ways to notice error, although the mechanisms, ethical obligations and consequences are different. The comparison leads directly to the later argument about reflexes, social correction and institutional oversight. If you want to compare brains with machines, babes, you have to compare how they learn — not merely marvel that both can answer a question.

## 7. 🕸️ Who Told the Machine What the Problem Was?

This is the cybernetic centre of the argument. A machine can perform its assigned optimisation process beautifully while the assigned objective is disastrous. A technically correct calculation can answer a morally grotesque question. **Technical correctness is not moral correctness, and neither is democratic legitimacy.**

Consider a simplified deployment sequence:

1. People choose training sources, tasks, architecture and optimisation objectives. These choices do not perfectly determine behaviour, but they shape it.
2. During training, feedback changes model parameters. Later evaluations measure selected kinds of success and failure. Anything not measured well can be overlooked.
3. A product team wraps the model in an interface, tools, permissions, defaults and incentives. That changes what users can ask and what the system can do.
4. Users encounter outputs and may trust, challenge, copy or act on them. A fluent answer can be persuasive even when wrong.
5. Institutions may embed outputs into workflows: hiring, public services, research, intelligence analysis or decisions about who receives attention.
6. Those decisions alter opportunities, records and behaviour in the world. The resulting information may later enter datasets, evaluation practices or new deployments.
7. The next generation of the system inherits part of that changed environment. The loop continues.

Not every deployment uses online learning, and an individual output does not magically update the model's weights. **The feedback may operate through people, institutions, datasets and later retraining**, not through an instant internal memory of every interaction.

### 🏛️ A worked example: the wrong objective, efficiently achieved

Imagine an institution asking an AI system to identify which reports are most *credible*. It trains and evaluates the system primarily on historical reports that officials previously accepted. Suppose those officials had systematically discounted testimony from a particular group. The model could learn to reproduce that institutional pattern. It might score brilliantly against the historical benchmark while continuing the original exclusion. Nobody needs to write a line of code saying *disbelieve these people*. The choice of proxy for credibility can do the damage.

This is a hypothetical mechanism, not a claim about any named deployment. It illustrates why we must ask who defined success, whose experience was represented, and whether affected people can challenge the output.

Now return to the original post. Why should Jewish people appear as a *problem* for a computational system to *solve*? The historical formulation is not a harmless neutral variable. It invites scrutiny of the human political assumptions entering the question **before** anybody starts optimising an answer.

Catastrophic harms could result from deliberate misuse, negligent deployment, misaligned objectives or unforeseen interactions. We should not pretend the only danger is a programmer explicitly ordering a machine to commit an atrocity. Nor does a provocative tweet establish that its author has a concrete plan to do so. The right response is to ask what the claim means and how technical, institutional and ethical safeguards would work.

The designer is inside the information ecology. The policymaker is too. So is the person who decides a confident man must be right because he sounds like an engineer.

## 8. 🏛️ The Dark Enlightenment Has Forgotten to Bring Its Methodology

THE DARK ENLIGHTENMENT.

Is it the Dark Ages? Is it the Enlightenment? Who can tell? Mr Pickup Artist has entered the quantum-gravity discussion and apparently we're doing feudalism again.

The Enlightenment was not a single school, and its thinkers were not uniformly empirical, democratic or egalitarian. Scientific inquiry has a longer, globally entangled history. Medieval scholars made substantial contributions to philosophy, medicine, astronomy and mathematics. **THE MIDDLE AGES HAVE NOT CONSENTED TO THIS COMPARISON.**

Nevertheless, invoking the Enlightenment invites questions about reason, criticism, evidence and the contestability of authority. Yarvin's neoreactionary proposals, including concentrated executive power and analogies to corporate governance, can be analysed as political proposals. They do not become scientifically demonstrated merely because their advocates use technical language.

What would count as evidence that a proposed political arrangement was failing? How would the people governed by it challenge decisions? How would independent information circulate? ## 11. 🦚 The Peacock Has Been Asked for Its Aerodynamic Specifications

### 🏛️ How the peacock gets into the policy meeting

Here is the step between online status games and actual institutional risk. The authority is now circulating independently of the original proof of competence.

Imagine a defence briefing. An adviser says an AI system can identify an emerging threat with extraordinary accuracy. So the conversation moves to procurement before anyone asks: *Accurate against which test data? How often is it wrong? Who was excluded from the evaluation? What happens when the environment changes? Who can override it?*

No one in that room has to out-program the adviser. They have to understand the claim well enough to make a responsible decision. **Prestige is a reason to listen, not a substitute for verification.** The same is true when a software engineer proposes a political constitution, or when a political commentator borrows the authority of physics. Each claim needs evidence appropriate to its subject.

This is a hypothetical pathway, not evidence that Yarvin's post influenced a defence or foreign-policy decision. But it explains why the ordinary question — *explain it to me like I'm five* — is an institutional safeguard rather than a social insult. If the claim cannot survive an explanation of its assumptions, it should not be promoted into policy by the sheer force of the speaker's CV.

## 16. 🧠 Gravity Is Peer Review

Here is the thing about guardrails: human beings encounter them long before they can pronounce the word *regulation*. The world gives us information about the consequences of our actions. Gravity does not negotiate. Hot surfaces are famously unenthusiastic about our theories of heat transfer.

**The stove is providing peer review.**

Technically, of course, a withdrawal reflex is not a scholarly review process. When you touch something painfully hot, sensory signals can activate spinal circuits that withdraw the limb rapidly, while other pathways convey information to the brain. The reflex and conscious pain perception are related but not identical. Learning to avoid that situation later involves additional processes: memory, prediction, attention, context and adaptation. A reflex is not itself a complete theory of learning.

That's useful, because it shows **multiple kinds of feedback** operating together rather than one magic correction switch.

**Physiological feedback** helps an organism regulate movement and respond to danger. **Learning feedback** helps it update predictions through experience, practice and observation. **Social feedback** tells us when our expectations collide with another person's boundaries, knowledge or interpretation. These mechanisms overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

A child learns about distance by reaching and missing, about balance by adjusting movement, and about other people by discovering that their own intentions do not determine everyone else's response. Adults continue learning in this way. Expertise doesn't end the need for correction; it can make particular corrections more consequential.

### 🪭 Enter Mr Darcy, again

Elizabeth Bennet does not perform a neurological intervention. She provides social information Darcy has reason to take seriously. His growth depends on what he does with it. The crucial event is not simply being told he is wrong. It is recognising the mismatch between his self-understanding and another person's experience, then revising his behaviour. Elizabeth also learns. **The loop works because both can change.**

Not every painful experience teaches the right lesson. Humiliation can produce defensiveness; punishment can produce concealment; coercive constraints can suppress useful information. We are not arguing for maximum suffering as an educational philosophy. We are arguing for **proportionate, interpretable, accountable feedback** that makes errors visible and improvement possible.

This is where AI and human institutions meet by analogy, not identity. Model evaluation, external auditing and deployment safeguards are different from sensory reflexes or social correction. But all address a common design question: **How does a consequential system discover when its predictions or actions are wrong, and what can it do about it?**

If someone tells you their intelligence makes them too sophisticated for guardrails, they have confused the possession of a capability with proof of its reliability. A system that cannot incorporate correction is not thereby more advanced. It may simply be harder to govern.

## 17. ⚙️ You Are Not Too Intelligent for Guardrails

AI safety debates involve testing, evaluation, monitoring, constraints, human oversight, security and accountability. Those mechanisms are not proof that the technology is worthless. They are ways of making powerful systems more reliable.

Human institutions need feedback too. Scientific communities use criticism and replication. Courts use review and appeal. Democratic institutions use scrutiny and mechanisms for removing decision-makers. Professional teams use incident reporting, audits and peer challenge. These mechanisms are not identical, and humans have rights and moral standing that must not be collapsed into software-governance metaphors.

The transferable principle is simple: **a powerful system needs ways to detect error and respond to it.**

The more complex the system, the less sensible it is to assume that one clever individual can anticipate every consequence. Complexity can make regulation difficult; it does not make the need for accountability disappear. Poorly designed rules can be counterproductive, so they too must be tested and revised. That is the point of feedback.

When somebody says their field is too complicated for outsiders to question, the answer is not necessarily to demand that they disclose every proprietary detail or prove every theorem on a napkin. It is to ask them to identify the claim, the evidence, the uncertainty and the decision that depends upon it.

**“It's complicated” may be true. “It's complicated, therefore shut up” is not a methodology.**

## 18. 🛡️ Explain It to Me Like I'm Five

I would particularly like the men around influential technologists—in politics, defence, foreign policy, security and government—to discover a very simple phrase: **Explain it to me like I'm five.**

Not because an expert can compress a difficult mathematical proof into nursery-school language without losing anything. Sometimes the technical detail is essential. The request is for the *causal structure*: what is being claimed, how it is supposed to work, which assumptions carry the argument, what evidence exists and what could go wrong.

Picture a hypothetical meeting. A technical adviser announces that a new AI capability will transform intelligence analysis. The minister cannot inspect the model's weights or reproduce the evaluation pipeline. Fine. The minister can still ask: What task was it tested on? Against what baseline? Who chose the test cases? How often does it fail? Are the failures concentrated in particular circumstances? Does a human review consequential decisions? Can the system's outputs be traced and challenged? What happens when the environment changes? What is the fallback when it breaks?

Those questions do not make the minister a machine-learning researcher. They make the minister someone doing their job. The adviser may have excellent answers. **Then the conversation has improved.** If the answers are missing, the institution has discovered an uncertainty *before* building a policy around it.

This matters in defence and geopolitics because consequential institutions can be impressed by technical fluency they are not equipped to evaluate directly. That is not evidence that the original Yarvin exchange exposed a particular vulnerability to China, Russia or Iran. It is a general institutional risk: unexamined claims can become assumptions in decisions with real consequences.

The everyday version is even simpler. When somebody makes a grand assertion, ask:

> What do you mean?  
> How would that work?  
> Which part is established, and which part is speculation?  
> What evidence would change your mind?  
> What does that have to do with the question we were discussing?  
> Did you intend to invoke the historical language of genocide?

You don't have to shout. You don't have to humiliate anybody. And women should not have to take personal risks to provide the feedback that other members of a powerful man's social network decline to offer.

Sometimes another bloke needs to say: **“Mate. What the fuck are you talking about?”**

Then wait for the answer.

This is not anti-intellectualism. **It is how an intellectual claim becomes accountable to somebody other than the person who made it.**

### 🛡️ Defence Is Not Exempt from Peer Review Either

There is a difference between **asking an impressive man to explain himself** and **having an institution capable of doing something with the answer**. I would like the latter, please. Preferably before somebody buys a system, adopts a doctrine or decides that a software engineer has also somehow become an authority on civilisation.

Defence, intelligence and foreign-policy institutions do not exist merely to admire clever people. They are supposed to make consequential judgements under uncertainty. That means a claim must be able to travel through a chain of scrutiny: **claim → clarification → evidence → independent challenge → recorded decision → review → correction**. Who owns the decision? What assumptions are being accepted? What is the evidence threshold? Who can raise a concern without career damage? What happens when an adviser is wrong? What happens when somebody is right but inconvenient?

An expert can establish that a tool performs well on a particular benchmark. That does not, by itself, establish whether using it in a military or intelligence setting is lawful, proportionate, robust under adversarial conditions, appropriate to the mission or accountable to democratic authority. Those are additional questions, not footnotes. Nor does a uniform make a person exempt from peer review: military expertise, civilian oversight, legal scrutiny and the knowledge of people affected by a decision must all have routes into the process.

There is also an extremely basic security point that I fear some gentlemen are treating as an optional module. **THE INTERNET IS NOT A PRIVATE MEMBERS' CLUB.**

When influential people post publicly, people in **Iran, Russia and China** can read it too. So can allied analysts, journalists, researchers, ordinary citizens and anybody else with an internet connection. Public arguments, status rituals, endorsements and failures to challenge extraordinary statements can offer outsiders *clues* about whose judgement is celebrated, which ideas circulate and whose contributions appear to be dismissed. Open-source observers routinely study publicly available information. They do not need a secret microphone to notice who is being treated as the cleverest boy in the room.

This does **not** mean a single ridiculous post gives a foreign intelligence service an accurate organisational chart, proves who holds real power, or establishes what any state has actually inferred. Online visibility is selective; performance and institutional influence are not identical. But public behaviour can still generate reputational signals and reveal vulnerabilities in a culture of judgement. If a purported meritocracy repeatedly rewards confident spectacle while demanding endless proof from women and other less-favoured contributors, it risks advertising a very peculiar account of *merit*. It also risks failing to use the expertise it already has.

**You do all realise Iran can see this? Russia can see it. China can see it.** They can see who gets applauded for saying something extraordinary and who gets asked to produce three dissertations before being allowed into the conversation. They cannot see the whole institution from a timeline, but they can see what the timeline rewards. And they can ask what that suggests about the people claiming to run a meritocracy.

So, gentlemen: **if you are all going to remain at the tippity top of the hierarchy, could you at least be somewhat intellectual about it?**

That means more than performing cleverness in public. It means asking for definitions, admitting uncertainty, challenging your peers, recording dissent and being willing to revise decisions when the evidence changes. It means understanding that a hostile environment for criticism is itself an information-security problem: the warnings that never get spoken cannot reach the people making decisions. When women or junior colleagues bear disproportionate costs for objecting, senior men have an especially obvious responsibility not to outsource all the difficult conversations to them.

Sometimes the first intervention really is: **“Mate, what the fuck are you talking about?”** But professional accountability cannot end at the punchline. If the answer does not withstand scrutiny, the institution needs the capacity—and the courage—to act on that information.

This is a general argument about public conduct and institutional design, **not** an allegation that the Yarvin post exposed classified information, created a specific operational vulnerability or was acted upon by any foreign government. The standard is simpler and more demanding: **people entrusted with power should demonstrate that their judgement can survive questions.**
