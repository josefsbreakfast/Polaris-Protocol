# 🐕 Mad Dogs and Englishmen, But Make It SaaS
**First created:** 2026-10-27 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-27  
*The Americans have an ambitious plan to clone military working dogs. The Englishwoman would like to know whether anyone has read the examination question.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation — The Americans Have Invented Dogs-as-a-Service

In October 2026, a Texas defence-biotechnology venture called **Warhound** emerged from stealth with a reported **$200 million seed round** and **$1.2 billion valuation**. Conceived by biotechnology entrepreneur **Ben Lamm** and defence-technology entrepreneur **Palmer Luckey**, and led by chief executive **David Spirk**, it proposes to strengthen American domestic provision of military working dogs through cloning, genetic technologies, training infrastructure and equipment supplied by **Anduril**. Warhound's announcement describes dogs serving the military, law enforcement and emergency responders. [1–4]

The strategic argument is understandable. Warhound says that more than 80% of American military working dogs are sourced from Europe, and that fewer than half of acquired animals ultimately qualify for service. These figures are central to the company's case; they should be independently interrogated, including the denominators, definitions, time periods and causes of attrition, rather than treated as self-proving. [1–3]

Lamm has also discussed a prospective **“Canine-as-a-Service”** model: providing a continuing supply of trained animals rather than simply selling individual dogs. At launch, the company had not disclosed a settled price or signed customer contracts. That is a proposal, not evidence of an already functioning subscription programme. [2]

And I have questions about the dogs.

I should make my starting position clear. **I already have strong objections to the excessive commodification of animals.** That is not merely a complaint about inadequate food, poor veterinary care or unnecessary suffering, although those matter enormously. It is a concern about treating living beings as resources whose reproduction, biology, relationships and entire lives can be organised primarily around someone else's institutional requirements. A well-cared-for commodity may still be a commodity.

I also have a great deal more to say about PTSD, veterans, working dogs and the human–canine relationship. Much of my wider ethical outlook is already visible elsewhere in Polaris. I'm parking that discussion here, deliberately. The purpose of this node is not to produce my complete philosophy of animals. It is to ask the American cousins to explain **their** values, their assumptions and their limits.

Two examinations follow: **what responsibilities arise when dogs become an ongoing commercial military service?** And **why is cloning, potentially combined with genome editing, the appropriate response to the stated supply problem?** There is also a final question that runs through both: **what would the people building this capability refuse to do?**

The men are entirely welcome to have good answers. I would quite like to hear them.

> **🎭 On the title:** Noël Coward's 1931 *Mad Dogs and Englishmen* contains colonial-era stereotypes worth acknowledging. The allusion here is to the song's joke about unreasonable conduct undertaken with complete confidence in one's own good sense. I am prepared to be either the mad dog or the Englishman. The American military's separate use of *Mad Dog* as a nickname adds an incidental joke, not a claim about where any particular nickname originated.

## 🦴 Part I — What Are We Doing With the Dogs?

### 🐕 The Dog Is Not a Service

Dogs already do an extraordinary amount for people. They detect explosives, locate missing people, assist disabled people, accompany the lonely, help people through illness and form relationships that often matter far more than any formal task. Sometimes their greatest contribution is simply being there. Sometimes a dog is very good at being a dog and very bad at whatever job a human originally imagined for it. This is not, in my view, a defect in the dog.

I understand that humans have bred, bought, sold and employed dogs for centuries. Warhound has not invented animal commodification. But **cloning dogs, potentially altering their genomes and proposing their provision as a recurring service** brings that commodification into particularly sharp focus.

A dog *performing* a service is not necessarily the same thing as a dog being *conceived of* as a service. And an animal's worth does not vanish when it stops meeting a procurement specification.

So what is actually being supplied? An individual animal? Training and continuing veterinary support? A contractual guarantee of replacements? A number of deployable canine teams? Does ownership pass to the customer? Who makes decisions when the interests of the provider, purchaser, handler and dog conflict? Is the business model flexible enough to recognise that dogs are not interchangeable simply because their genomes are similar?

These are not questions that can be answered by saying the animals will be treated nicely. They concern the underlying terms on which the animals exist in the system at all.

### 🛑 Before We Invent More Uses for Dogs

We might also examine how humans already use dogs before celebrating our capacity to produce new kinds of them.

Dogs are vulnerable to exploitation precisely because they depend on people, respond to conditioning and cannot meaningfully consent to human purposes. The sexual abuse of animals, including cases in which dogs have been trained or coerced into abusive acts, deserves unequivocal condemnation. Where animals are used to inflict sexual violence against humans, both human and animal victims matter. There is no need for graphic detail to establish that point. Nor is there any suggestion here that Warhound is connected to such conduct: **the question is what our existing treatment of dogs tells us about the limits we ought to set before expanding human control over them.**

There is a related inconsistency worth investigating in the public regulation of dogs. In Britain, breed-specific restrictions and associated housing, insurance and enforcement arrangements can impose particularly difficult burdens on owners with fewer resources. Whether and to what extent enforcement is class-disproportionate needs evidence, not assumption. But the contrast in *language* is striking. A dog associated with an ordinary civilian may become a public-safety problem. A dog deployed by a powerful institution may be described in terms of capability, deterrence, security and mission success.

These are different contexts; a dangerous civilian dog and a military working dog cannot simply be equated. Nevertheless, **why is the potential for harm sometimes discussed as a problem with the dog or its owner, and elsewhere as an operational question about what an institution can accomplish with the dog?**

Military dogs can be deployed lawfully and humanely. They have also been implicated in documented abuses in detention and conflict settings. Particular cases, chains of command, allied knowledge and governmental responses must be sourced individually; silence cannot simply be treated as proof of endorsement. But where abusive deployment is established, the questions extend beyond the individual handler. What did the institution authorise? What safeguards failed? What did partners know? Were there investigations, restrictions, diplomatic representations or consequences?

I'd rather like our American cousins to join in stating unequivocally that **dogs must not be used for sexual abuse, unlawful violence, or the coercive degradation of people**, and that institutional status does not excuse cruelty to the animal either. I would like the same consistency from Britain and our allies. That is not an anti-American proposition. It is a request for shared standards.

Before designing more sophisticated working dogs, can we establish what humans are **not** entitled to make dogs do?

### 🫀 The Relationship Cannot Be Cloned

Working dogs do not arrive as completed military capabilities. They learn. They develop preferences. They build confidence, experience fear, acquire habits and form relationships with handlers. The quality of those relationships may be operationally important, but their value is not exhausted by operational performance.

Who pairs a dog with a handler? Who decides whether the partnership works? How much continuity is expected? Can a handler contest a reassignment that would disrupt an established bond? What happens when the handler is injured, leaves the service or develops PTSD? What happens when the dog is injured, develops behavioural difficulties or retires first? What role do veterinary behaviourists and experienced handlers play in these decisions?

The prospect of cloning makes the distinction especially important. **A clone shares substantially the same nuclear genetic material as its donor; it does not reproduce the donor's life, training, attachments or personality.** Identical genetic starting points do not make relationships interchangeable.

There is much more to say about veterans and dogs, including dogs who support people living with PTSD. I am deliberately not turning this into that essay. I will say this: a relationship can be profoundly valuable to a human without making the other living participant merely a therapeutic or operational instrument.

### 📜 Please Put It in the Contract

I am particularly interested in the contractual arrangements, because predictable problems should not be left to a vague promise that everybody will do the right thing when the time comes.

| Foreseeable question | What needs to be established up front |
|---|---|
| **Ownership** | Who legally owns each dog at every stage of its life? |
| **Handler relationships** | What standing do handlers have in decisions about separation and reassignment? |
| **Veterinary decisions** | Who can authorise treatment, rehabilitation, retirement and humane end-of-life care? |
| **Disability or unsuitable temperament** | Who takes responsibility when a dog cannot fulfil its intended role? |
| **Retirement and adoption** | Are former handlers prioritised or meaningfully consulted? |
| **Contract expiry** | Do care obligations survive the end of the service agreement? |
| **Company insolvency** | Is care funded and guaranteed if the supplier disappears? |
| **Abusive deployment** | What uses are prohibited, what monitoring exists, and what triggers refusal or termination? |
| **Disputes** | Who has independent authority when veterinary advice conflicts with commercial or operational demands? |
| **Enforcement** | Are the commitments enforceable, auditable and funded? |

Warhound has publicly discussed animal-welfare partnerships, including work with **American Humane** on training and reunification of retired dogs with handlers. That is relevant and potentially constructive. It does not, by itself, tell us the precise contractual rights, lifetime funding arrangements or independent enforcement mechanisms. [2–3]

The company should receive credit for safeguards it can substantiate, not for safeguards we have merely imagined on its behalf.

**A dog's relationship with its handler may outlast a procurement contract. Its need for care certainly can.** The paperwork needs to understand that before the first delivery.

## 🧬 Part II — Why Are We Cloning the Dogs?

### 🔬 What Problem Are We Actually Solving?

Warhound's stated problem is American dependence on imported military working dogs, coupled with low qualification rates and health or performance concerns. That is a coherent problem statement at a high level. It is not yet a demonstration that cloning is the optimal remedy.

Is the bottleneck the number of breeding animals? The number of healthy puppies? The suitability of available dogs? The length or quality of training? Veterinary attrition? The shortage of experienced handlers? Procurement practices? Price competition? Reliance on a limited number of overseas breeders? Different causes require different remedies.

This is where I feel the need to invoke the exam technique we all learned at school. **Before spending two hours writing an elaborate essay, read the question and make a plan.** A technically impressive answer is not especially useful if it answers a different question.

We have apparently found $200 million and an extremely enthusiastic set of men. Splendid. Could we establish what proportion of the problem each proposed technology actually addresses?

### ♻️ Have We Considered the Alternatives?

The familiar **3Rs** of humane animal research are **Replacement, Reduction and Refinement**. They were formulated by Russell and Burch in 1959. They are not a magic regulatory formula that automatically governs every breeding or procurement decision, but they offer a useful discipline for evaluating research involving animals: could the work be replaced, could fewer animals be used, and could suffering be reduced?

Before adopting cloning as a central supply strategy, what has been established about alternatives?

- **Existing populations:** Are there already suitable domestic dogs who could be identified through better assessment? Not every rescue dog is suited to military work; the point is to test suitability, not force unsuitable animals into dangerous roles.
- **Conventional breeding:** Could domestic breeding capacity expand with appropriate genetic screening, welfare standards and investment?
- **Veterinary care:** Could prevention, earlier diagnosis, rehabilitation and more rigorous health selection reduce attrition?
- **Training:** Is the constraint partly in trainers, facilities, methods, timelines or handler availability?
- **Procurement:** Are acquisition rules or fragmented demand making the supply problem worse?
- **Diversification:** Could a more resilient supply chain be built without making every solution depend on cloning?

Perhaps Warhound has considered all of this. If so, the comparison would be useful to publish. The relevant measure is not merely how many puppies a technology can produce, but **how many healthy, suitable dogs it produces over time, at what welfare cost, and compared with which alternatives**.

### 🐑 Dolly Wasn't That Long Ago

Dolly the sheep was born in 1996 and announced in 1997. Mammalian cloning has advanced since then; it would be scientifically sloppy to transfer early sheep-cloning risks directly to contemporary canine techniques. It would be equally sloppy to treat successful cloning as proof that the full reproductive process is harmless.

Cloning, selective breeding and genome editing are distinct interventions. **Cloning** reproduces substantially the donor's nuclear genome. **Selective breeding** chooses parents with desired traits. **Genome editing** deliberately changes DNA sequences. A company can use more than one method, but the risks and evidence requirements must be assessed separately.

Warhound's public accounts indicate biobanking of promising working-dog lines and work towards cloning and editing. The first proposed animals and dates remain future-facing, and reporting differs on precise milestones. At the time of this node, there is no basis for writing as though a mature Warhound canine deployment programme already exists. [1–4]

What are the current canine cloning success rates under the particular protocols proposed? How many oocytes, embryos, surrogates and pregnancies are needed per surviving healthy puppy? What are the risks to surrogate mothers? What happens to animals involved in unsuccessful attempts? What long-term studies support health claims, and who will follow animals after military service?

**A healthy puppy is an outcome worth welcoming. It is not a complete accounting of every animal involved in producing that puppy.**

### 🧬 What Are You Selecting For?

There is an important difference between preventing a painful inherited disease and deliberately producing an animal whose characteristics better serve a military task. The two goals can overlap. They can also conflict.

Warhound has discussed improving health, longevity and performance, including possible sensory and physical traits. Public reporting also indicates that the company has not settled every proposed target or demonstrated that all its ambitions are scientifically actionable. [2–4]

So which variants and pathways are being targeted? What evidence connects them to the intended outcome? Are the traits strongly polygenic? What environmental and developmental factors matter? What are the possible pleiotropic effects—where a genetic change influences more than one characteristic? What independent assessment exists of unintended consequences?

If greater sensory acuity is the goal, could it also increase distress or overload in some environments? If greater compliance is the goal, could that make an animal more vulnerable to coercion? These are **questions about possible trade-offs**, not assertions that Warhound is editing for those traits or producing those harms.

And who decides what counts as an improvement? A dog might become more operationally useful without becoming healthier or happier. **Human utility and animal welfare are related considerations, not synonyms.**

### 🩺 What Happens When Something Goes Wrong?

A serious welfare framework has to count more than successful military dogs.

It must include surrogate mothers, reproductive animals, unsuccessful pregnancies, puppies with congenital abnormalities, healthy dogs unsuited to military work, dogs who develop delayed conditions, and retired dogs whose care no longer produces a commercial return.

For each group, what happens in practice?

If a dog is born with a disability, who funds treatment and rehabilitation? If a healthy dog fails behavioural assessment, is there a suitable rehoming route? If a cloned or edited dog develops a disease years later, who investigates whether it is related to the intervention? Are negative findings reported? What predefined adverse-event thresholds would stop a particular technique? Who can order that stop independently of management?

What about the animals whose contribution to the programme was reproductive rather than operational? Their welfare cannot disappear from the denominator because they never wore a vest.

Nor can the company assume that a government buyer will shoulder every obligation. Responsibility should be allocated, funded and enforceable, including after contract expiry or corporate failure.

We should also distinguish the partners. Public reporting describes **Colossal-linked biotechnology and cloning capabilities** and **Anduril-provided equipment and systems**. That does not establish that Anduril controls Warhound's genetic decisions. Each participant's actual authority and liabilities need to be identified rather than blurred together. [1–4]

The central test is simple: **does the ethical accounting include the animals who do not become the success story?**

## 🛑 Part III — Where Are Your Red Lines, Gentlemen?

### 🇺🇸 Dear American Cousins, What Would You Refuse to Do?

Here is the bit that interests me most. I am not asking American companies to inherit every British cultural attitude towards dogs. Britain has plenty of animal-welfare failings of its own, and our military and commercial histories hardly put us above examination.

I am asking the people building these capabilities to **state their own boundaries**.

Technology companies often speak fluently about innovation, disruption and refusing to accept existing limits. Sometimes that rhetoric slides into the suggestion that having *no red lines* is evidence of bravery or vision. I find that a rather immature way to talk about power over living beings.

Most people have boundaries. Most institutions do too, whether or not they articulate them well. Claiming to have none might mean that the hard cases have not been considered, that the limits are being kept private, or simply that the rhetoric has outrun the judgment. We cannot infer which explanation applies to Warhound from a slogan. We can, however, ask the company to tell us.

Would Warhound refuse to develop a modification that improved mission performance but foreseeably diminished the dog's quality of life? Would it stop a promising programme after credible evidence of serious harm? Would it decline to supply an organisation credibly implicated in abusive deployment? Would it refuse a contract without funded retirement care? Would it protect a handler–dog relationship even when separating them would be commercially easier?

And would the same standards apply to law-enforcement customers and military allies? What monitoring, independent review, customer restrictions and termination rights make the answers meaningful?

These are **hypothetical tests of principle**, not allegations of Warhound misconduct. The company may have thoughtful answers. It may have safeguards that have not yet been made public. I would like to know.

There is also a distinction between a statement of values and a genuine red line. *We care about welfare* is a value. *We will not proceed with a specified intervention when an independent review finds an unacceptable risk, even if it costs us the contract* is the beginning of a boundary. It identifies the point at which the institution is prepared to give something up.

**Innovation is a capability. Restraint is a capability too.** Neither is impressive without judgment.

### 🐝 Start Acting Your 250 Years of Age, Please

The United States reaches 250 years since its Declaration of Independence in 2026. Happy birthday, cousins. Please start acting your age.

I do not mean that you should stop inventing things. Nor do I mean that I must approve every American military procurement decision. I mean that an adult institution ought to be capable of explaining the difference between **what it can do, what it intends to do, and what it would refuse to do**.

Sometimes somebody doesn't need to innovate something that's shit. Sometimes the more interesting achievement is recognising, before spending a fortune, that an ingenious answer may not address the question—or that the answer creates responsibilities nobody has adequately planned to meet.

Warhound might yet show that its proposed methods substantially improve canine health, reduce failed training, strengthen domestic supply and protect animals throughout their lives. That would be evidence worth examining. It would not erase every objection I have to animal commodification. But it would be a serious answer to some serious questions.

What I do not want is a billion-dollar atmosphere of *but innovation!* in place of an explanation of why this is necessary, how the dogs will be treated, who can intervene when things go wrong, and where the people involved would draw the line.

The men have arrived with an ambitious answer. The Englishwoman has looked at the examination paper.

**Gentlemen, before you write for two hours, can we establish that you've understood the question?**

And, while we're here: **what would you never do to the dogs, however much somebody offered to pay you?**
