# 🗂️ Documentation, Complaints & Legal Pressure

**First created:** 2026-08-20 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-20  
*Practical documentation rules for corrections, complaints, takedown requests and legal pressure around Palestine factchecking.*

---

## 🚨 Read This First — This Is Not Legal Advice

**This node is not legal advice.**

The author is not acting as your lawyer.

The Polaris Protocol is not a substitute for advice from a qualified solicitor, barrister, attorney or other appropriately regulated legal professional in the jurisdiction that applies to you.

Defamation, privacy, data protection, harassment, contempt, copyright, confidentiality, national-security law, platform liability, employment obligations, contractual duties and publication risk vary significantly between jurisdictions and between factual situations.

If you are publishing material that could create legal exposure, **get proper legal advice in advance**.

Do not wait until:

- a solicitor's letter arrives;
- a platform threatens removal;
- an employer becomes involved;
- a source withdraws;
- a court deadline is running;
- or a complainant demands an immediate answer.

Where possible, obtain advice **before publication**, particularly where a node concerns:

- serious allegations about identifiable people or organisations;
- allegations of criminality, dishonesty, corruption or professional misconduct;
- disputed factual claims likely to cause reputational harm;
- confidential or leaked material;
- private personal information;
- children or vulnerable people;
- active legal proceedings;
- copyrighted material reproduced beyond quotation or fair dealing/fair use;
- sanctions, terrorism, national-security or export-control issues;
- or information obtained under legal, employment or contractual restrictions.

> **Document carefully. Publish carefully. Lawyer yourself appropriately.**

This node describes archival and factchecking practice.

It does not tell you what your legal rights or liabilities are.

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Factchecking creates records.

Those records may later be challenged.

Sometimes because the archive is wrong.

Sometimes because the evidence is incomplete.

Sometimes because a source has changed.

Sometimes because a legal or factual distinction has been missed.

Sometimes because a person or institution strongly dislikes accurate reporting.

Sometimes because several of those things are true at once.

The purpose of this node is not to teach people how to “fight lawyers”.

It is to make sure the archive remains reconstructable when a claim is challenged.

That means preserving:

- what was published;
- when it was published;
- what evidence supported it;
- what changed;
- what was complained about;
- what response was received;
- and what action was taken.

A factcheck that cannot reconstruct its own history is much harder to defend, correct or learn from.

---

## 🧭 Principle One — Preserve The Original

If a substantive complaint arrives, do not immediately overwrite the challenged material and destroy the previous version.

Preserve:

- the published text;
- publication date and time;
- relevant commit or version history;
- screenshots where useful;
- linked sources;
- archived copies of sources where lawful and appropriate;
- and the exact wording challenged.

If the claim later proves wrong, the original version is still part of the evidential history.

If the complaint later proves unfounded, the original version may also matter.

The archive should be able to answer:

> **What exactly did we say at the time?**

---

## 📮 Principle Two — Preserve The Complaint

Keep the complaint itself.

Do not reduce:

> **a six-page legal letter alleging five different errors**

to:

> **they complained about the article.**

Preserve where appropriate:

- sender;
- date;
- delivery method;
- full text;
- attachments;
- deadlines;
- specific statements identified;
- remedies requested;
- legal provisions cited;
- factual evidence supplied;
- demands for correction, deletion, apology or undertakings;
- and any threat of proceedings or platform escalation.

If the complaint arrives verbally, create a contemporaneous note.

Distinguish:

**what the complainant actually said**

from:

**what you think they meant.**

---

## 🔬 Principle Three — Identify The Exact Proposition Under Challenge

A broad complaint may contain several different disputes.

For each challenged statement, ask:

1. What exact proposition did the archive publish?
2. Is it factual, inferential, legal, interpretive or ethical?
3. What evidence supported it at publication?
4. What new evidence has been supplied?
5. Does the new evidence contradict the proposition, narrow it, contextualise it or leave it unchanged?
6. Is the complaint about factual accuracy, wording, implication, fairness, privacy, copyright, legal exposure or reputational effect?

Do not respond to a specific factual complaint with a general political argument.

Do not respond to a legal concern by simply restating that the underlying cause is morally important.

The narrower the issue, the easier it is to assess.

---

## ⚖️ Principle Four — Separate Factual Correction From Legal Position

These are different questions:

> **Is the statement accurate?**

> **Was it lawful to publish?**

> **Was it fair to publish?**

> **Was it responsibly sourced?**

> **Does it create legal risk?**

A statement may be accurate and still raise legal questions.

A statement may create little legal exposure and still be factually wrong.

A lawyer may advise on risk without deciding historical truth.

A factchecker may establish evidence without being qualified to advise on litigation.

Keep those functions separate.

---

## 🧯 Principle Five — Correct Real Errors Properly

If the archive is wrong, correct it.

Do not defend an error merely because the complainant is unpleasant, politically opposed or institutionally powerful.

A proper correction should normally record:

- what the previous text said;
- what was wrong;
- what the corrected text says;
- what evidence prompted the correction;
- and the date of correction.

Where an error propagated into linked nodes, update those too.

Do not quietly replace:

> **X**

with:

> **Y**

and leave readers unable to understand why the archive changed.

A correction should increase trust.

---

## 🪞 Principle Six — Do Not Confuse A Complaint With Proof Of Error

The fact that a person or organisation has complained does not establish that the archive is wrong.

Likewise:

- a lawyer's letter is not a judicial finding;
- a takedown request is not proof of illegality;
- a platform removal is not proof that the underlying claim was false;
- a threatened lawsuit is not proof that a claimant would win;
- and institutional discomfort is not an evidential category.

Take complaints seriously.

Do not automatically treat them as authoritative.

---

## 🗂️ Principle Seven — Build A Complaint File

For substantial disputes, create a clear internal record.

A useful structure may include:

```text
complaint/
├── 01_published_version/
├── 02_sources_at_publication/
├── 03_incoming_complaint/
├── 04_new_evidence/
├── 05_internal_review/
├── 06_legal_advice/
├── 07_response/
└── 08_final_outcome/
```

The exact structure can vary.

The important thing is preserving chronology and provenance.

Do not mix privileged legal advice into public folders unless a lawyer has specifically advised that doing so is appropriate.

---

## 🔐 Principle Eight — Treat Legal Advice As Sensitive

Legal professional privilege and confidentiality rules vary by jurisdiction.

Do not assume that forwarding legal advice widely, quoting it publicly or storing it casually has no consequences.

Where legal advice is obtained:

- keep it appropriately restricted;
- label it clearly;
- do not summarise it into public-facing text without advice;
- avoid unnecessary circulation;
- and follow the lawyer's instructions on storage and disclosure.

This archive can preserve the fact that legal advice was sought without necessarily preserving the advice itself in the public repository.

---

## ⏳ Principle Nine — Deadlines Are Real

Legal and platform processes can contain short deadlines.

Record them immediately.

Examples may include:

- response deadlines in correspondence;
- court or tribunal deadlines;
- platform appeal windows;
- limitation issues;
- injunction-related urgency;
- deadlines in information or regulatory processes;
- and contractual notice periods.

Do not rely on memory.

Use a calendar or case log.

If you are uncertain whether a deadline matters legally, that is a reason to ask a lawyer rather than guess.

---

## 📸 Principle Ten — Preserve Ephemeral Evidence Early

Online evidence changes.

Pages are edited.

Posts disappear.

Accounts are deleted.

Search results change.

Videos are removed.

Platform metadata can become inaccessible.

Where material may matter later, preserve it lawfully and proportionately.

Depending on context, this may include:

- screenshots;
- PDFs;
- web archives;
- downloaded public documents;
- timestamps;
- source URLs;
- publication metadata;
- hashes;
- contemporaneous notes;
- or repository commits.

Preserving public evidence is not the same thing as publishing private information.

Do not turn evidence preservation into unnecessary redistribution of sensitive material.

---

## 🧵 Principle Eleven — Record Wording, Not Just Meaning

Complaints often turn on exact wording.

Preserve:

- headlines;
- captions;
- section headings;
- quotations;
- qualifiers;
- hyperlinks;
- nearby context;
- and publication dates.

There is a material difference between:

> **X committed a crime**

and:

> **X has been accused of a crime**

and:

> **prosecutors allege X committed a crime**

and:

> **a court found X guilty of a crime.**

Version history matters because one changed verb can change the legal and factual character of a statement.

---

## 🚨 Principle Twelve — Escalate Serious Allegations Before Publication

Where publication concerns an identifiable person or organisation and the allegation is serious, consider legal review before publication.

This is especially important for claims involving:

- criminal conduct;
- fraud;
- corruption;
- deliberate misinformation;
- covert influence;
- professional misconduct;
- harassment;
- discrimination;
- financial impropriety;
- conspiracy or coordination;
- or knowing participation in unlawful conduct.

Evidence of **interest** is not evidence of **corruption**.

Evidence of **alignment** is not evidence of **coordination**.

Evidence of **coordination** is not necessarily evidence of **illegality**.

Do not let rhetorically satisfying words outrun the evidential record.

---

## 📣 Principle Thirteen — Give Relevant Responses Fair Treatment

Where a person or institution has provided a materially relevant denial, explanation or correction, record it fairly.

That does not mean giving every denial equal evidential weight.

It means avoiding the impression that no response exists when one does.

Where practical:

- preserve the response;
- summarise it accurately;
- identify evidence supporting or contradicting it;
- and avoid selective quotation that changes its meaning.

A right of reply may also be legally significant depending on jurisdiction and context.

Ask a lawyer where necessary.

---

## 🧭 Principle Fourteen — Distinguish Removal, Correction And Qualification

These are different actions.

### Correction

The previous factual proposition was wrong or materially inaccurate.

### Qualification

The proposition remains broadly supportable but requires narrower wording, additional context or uncertainty.

### Removal

The material is taken down or omitted.

Removal does not necessarily mean the material was false.

It may occur because of:

- privacy;
- copyright;
- safety;
- legal risk;
- evidential insufficiency;
- source protection;
- active proceedings;
- or platform rules.

Where legally safe, preserve enough internal documentation to show **why** a change occurred.

---

## 🕸️ Principle Fifteen — Platform Action Is A Separate Evidential Event

A platform may:

- remove content;
- reduce visibility;
- disable monetisation;
- add warnings;
- suspend an account;
- reject an appeal;
- or restore content.

Record the platform action as its own event.

Do not automatically infer motive.

Do not describe a moderation decision as government censorship without evidence of government involvement.

Likewise, do not assume a platform decision is politically neutral simply because the platform describes it as policy enforcement.

Record:

- the notice;
- policy cited;
- content affected;
- date;
- appeal;
- outcome;
- and any evidence concerning external involvement.

Then make causal claims only as strong as that evidence permits.

---

## 🧿 Principle Sixteen — Legal Pressure Is Not All The Same

The phrase **legal pressure** can cover very different things.

It may mean:

- a legitimate request to correct a defamatory falsehood;
- a privacy complaint;
- a copyright demand;
- a cease-and-desist letter;
- a regulatory complaint;
- a contractual dispute;
- threatened litigation;
- an injunction application;
- police contact;
- a subpoena or disclosure order;
- or strategic litigation intended to create cost and deterrence.

Do not classify the nature of the pressure before examining the documentation.

If a legal tactic appears abusive or strategic, that assessment itself requires evidence and may require legal advice.

---

## 🛡️ Principle Seventeen — Do Not Publish Your Defence Strategy Into The Dispute

There is a difference between transparency and self-sabotage.

Publicly documenting:

- sources;
- corrections;
- methodology;
- and factual reasoning

can be valuable.

Publishing:

- privileged advice;
- internal legal assessments;
- confidential source identities;
- litigation strategy;
- speculative accusations about a complainant;
- or unnecessary private correspondence

may be unwise or harmful.

A public archive does not need to contain every internal document.

Preservation and publication are separate decisions.

---

## 🫥 Principle Eighteen — Protect Sources And Vulnerable People

A complaint may create pressure to disclose:

- source identities;
- private communications;
- unpublished evidence;
- or personal information.

Do not make disclosure decisions casually.

Source protection, confidentiality, privacy and legal obligations can be complex.

Get legal advice where needed.

For vulnerable people, consider whether preserving or republishing evidence could itself create harm.

The evidential value of a document does not automatically justify public exposure.

---

## 🧮 Principle Nineteen — Keep A Decision Log

For significant disputes, record:

- issue;
- evidence reviewed;
- who reviewed it;
- decision;
- rationale;
- date;
- and follow-up required.

This is especially useful where several people edit the archive or where a dispute lasts for months.

A decision log reduces the risk of later reconstructing history from memory.

---

## 🔄 Principle Twenty — Update Downstream Material

If a corrected claim appears in:

- another node;
- a README;
- a timeline;
- a graph;
- a social post;
- a presentation;
- a source summary;
- or an AI prompt pack,

correct the downstream use as well.

An error is not fixed merely because the first occurrence has been repaired.

Track propagation.

---

## 🧪 Principle Twenty-One — Test The Complaint Against The Evidence

For substantive complaints, ask:

> **What evidence would show the complainant is right?**

Then:

> **What evidence would show the archive is right?**

Then:

> **What evidence would show both sides have overstated their case?**

This prevents the review process from becoming either defensive or submissive.

The complaint is a new evidential input.

Treat it as one.

---

## ⚖️ Principle Twenty-Two — Jurisdiction Matters

A publication accessible worldwide can intersect with more than one legal system.

The applicable law may depend on:

- where the author is based;
- where publication occurs;
- where harm is alleged;
- where a claimant is based;
- where servers or platforms operate;
- contractual governing-law clauses;
- and the type of claim involved.

Do not assume that something is lawful because it would be lawful somewhere else.

Do not assume that a foreign threat automatically applies either.

This is exactly the sort of question to take to a qualified lawyer.

---

## 📚 Principle Twenty-Three — Wikipedia, AI And Secondary Summaries Are Not Legal Advice

They may be useful for orientation.

They are not substitutes for:

- current legislation;
- judgments;
- procedural rules;
- regulator guidance;
- contractual documents;
- or qualified legal advice.

Legal summaries age.

Cases are appealed.

Statutes are amended.

Guidance changes.

Jurisdiction matters.

Always check the date and the underlying authority.

---

## 🪫 Principle Twenty-Four — Do Not Let Urgency Destroy The Record

Pressure creates a temptation to act quickly and document later.

Try to reverse that instinct.

Before making a major change:

- preserve the current state;
- record why the change is being considered;
- preserve the complaint;
- preserve the sources;
- and note the time.

Then act.

A thirty-second preservation step can matter enormously later.

---

## 🌾 Practical Complaint Workflow

When a significant complaint arrives:

1. **Preserve the challenged publication.**
2. **Preserve the complaint and attachments.**
3. **Record deadlines immediately.**
4. **Identify the exact propositions challenged.**
5. **Classify each issue: factual, legal, privacy, copyright, procedural, reputational or other.**
6. **Retrieve the evidence relied on at publication.**
7. **Review any new evidence supplied.**
8. **Do not destroy or overwrite the evidential history.**
9. **Escalate serious legal issues to a qualified lawyer.**
10. **Correct genuine errors promptly and transparently.**
11. **Do not concede unsupported claims merely because pressure is forceful.**
12. **Preserve the response and final outcome.**
13. **Update downstream nodes where necessary.**
14. **Record what was learned for future publication practice.**

---

## 🧯 Before Publishing High-Risk Material

For material likely to attract serious challenge, consider a pre-publication file containing:

- the exact proposed wording;
- the proposition being asserted;
- primary sources;
- corroborating sources;
- relevant contrary evidence;
- notes on source reliability;
- relevant responses or denials;
- a chronology;
- legal questions identified;
- and, where appropriate, legal review.

The purpose is not to make publishing impossible.

It is to avoid discovering after publication that the archive cannot explain where an allegation came from.

---

## 🧿 What This Node Does Not Promise

Good documentation does not make litigation impossible.

Careful sourcing does not prevent complaints.

Legal advice cannot guarantee that nobody will sue.

Platform compliance does not guarantee visibility.

And preserving evidence does not mean every dispute will be resolved fairly.

The purpose is narrower:

> **make the archive able to reconstruct what happened, correct what was wrong, evidence what was right, and obtain proper legal help when legal questions arise.**

That is enough.
