# ⚖️ Factchecking Principles

**First created:** 2026-08-20 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-20  
*Working rules for checking claims about Palestine without flattening evidence, law, uncertainty, language or ethics into one undifferentiated verdict.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Factchecking is not the production of little green ticks and red crosses.

Sometimes a proposition really is straightforwardly true or false.

Often it is not.

A claim may be:

- accurate but missing decisive context;
- based on a real event but attached to the wrong date;
- numerically correct but using a misleading denominator;
- a reasonable estimate presented as an exact count;
- a genuine quotation stripped of its surrounding meaning;
- an allegation reported as a finding;
- a legal argument reported as settled law;
- an ethical judgement disguised as an empirical proposition;
- technically true while encouraging an inference the evidence does not support;
- or impossible to establish from the evidence currently available.

The purpose of this archive is not to force every question into a binary answer.

It is to identify **what kind of claim is being made, what evidence would be capable of establishing it, what the available evidence actually supports, and what remains uncertain**.

---

## ⚖️ Principle One — Identify The Proposition Before Checking It

Before asking whether something is true, establish exactly what is being claimed.

Compare:

> **A hospital was struck.**

> **Israel struck a hospital.**

> **Israel deliberately struck a hospital.**

> **Israel deliberately struck a hospital in order to kill civilians.**

These are not four phrasings of the same proposition.

They require different evidence.

The first concerns an event.

The second concerns attribution.

The third concerns intent.

The fourth concerns both intent and purpose.

Evidence capable of establishing one proposition may be insufficient to establish the next.

A useful factcheck therefore begins by writing the proposition as narrowly and plainly as possible.

If several propositions have been bundled together, separate them.

---

## 🧬 Principle Two — Classify The Claim

Different propositions require different methods.

At minimum, distinguish between:

### Established fact

A proposition supported strongly enough by the available evidence that the archive can state it as fact, while remaining open to later correction.

### Attributed claim

A proposition that can reliably be established as something a person or institution **said**, without treating the underlying assertion as independently proven.

> **The IDF said X.**

may be established even where:

> **X happened.**

remains unresolved.

### Allegation

A claim asserting wrongdoing, responsibility or conduct that has not yet been established to the evidential standard required for the context.

Allegations should remain allegations unless the evidence supports stronger language.

### Estimate

A quantity derived through methodology rather than direct exhaustive counting.

An estimate is not inferior merely because it is an estimate.

But it should not quietly become an exact number.

### Inference

A conclusion reasonably drawn from evidence that does not itself directly record the proposition.

Inference is legitimate.

Hidden inference is not.

### Interpretation

An account of what facts mean, how they relate, or how they should be understood.

Historical interpretation, political interpretation and textual interpretation may be well supported without becoming simple empirical facts.

### Legal argument

A contention about how law applies.

Lawyers, governments, academics, NGOs and litigants can make strong legal arguments.

That does not make those arguments judicial findings.

### Legal finding

A determination actually made by a competent court, tribunal or other legal body within the scope and procedural stage of the relevant proceeding.

The exact finding matters.

So does what the body did **not** decide.

### Ethical judgement

A normative conclusion about what is right, wrong, justified, proportionate, permissible, obligatory or intolerable.

Evidence informs ethical judgement.

It does not eliminate the normative step.

---

## 🔬 Principle Three — Ask What The Evidence Can Establish

A source does not become evidence for every proposition mentioned inside it.

A government press release can establish what the government announced.

It may or may not establish whether the government's account of an event is correct.

A photograph may establish that something was visible at a particular place and time.

It may not establish who caused it.

A witness may establish what they saw or experienced.

They may not know events occurring elsewhere.

Satellite imagery can show physical change.

It may not establish motive.

A court document can establish what was alleged, argued or decided at a particular procedural stage.

Those are three different things.

The question is always:

> **Evidence of what?**

---

## 📚 Principle Four — Provenance Before Prestige

Do not ask only:

> **Is this a reputable source?**

Ask:

> **Where did this information originate?**

Prestigious publications can repeat agency copy.

Multiple newspapers can repeat the same government statement.

Ten websites can reproduce one wire report.

A hundred social-media accounts can share one photograph.

That is not ten, one hundred or one thousand independent confirmations.

Trace information backwards where possible:

**summary → article → cited report → dataset → collection method**

or:

**post → video → original upload → location/date verification**

or:

**news report → government announcement → decision → underlying document**

The closer the archive can get to the evidential origin, the easier it becomes to understand what the evidence actually supports.

---

## 🧮 Principle Five — Count Independent Evidence, Not URLs

Source diversity is valuable.

Source multiplication is not the same thing.

If seven articles rely on the same FARA filing, they may provide seven editorial interpretations of one primary source.

They do not become seven independent confirmations that the filing says what it says.

Likewise:

- repeated government statements may share one intelligence assessment;
- multiple NGOs may use the same underlying casualty dataset;
- different news outlets may rely on the same eyewitness;
- several viral posts may derive from the same video;
- AI answers may reproduce the same highly indexed webpages.

Record common ancestry where it can be established.

The information ecosystem has family trees.

---

## 🧭 Principle Six — Source ≠ Endorsement

Using a source does not require accepting its politics, institutional worldview or complete account.

Israeli government sources belong in an archive about Israeli government decisions.

Palestinian sources belong in an archive about Palestinian institutions and Palestinian experience.

Military statements may be necessary to establish military claims.

Advocacy organisations may possess important evidence.

Partisan researchers can produce accurate work.

Organisations with material interests can publish true information.

Critics of those organisations can publish false information.

The source's incentives and limitations matter.

They do not substitute for examining the evidence.

The archive follows **provenance rather than teams**.

---

## 🪞 Principle Seven — Disclose Relevant Interests Without Turning Them Into Verdicts

Financial, political, institutional, ideological or strategic interests can matter when evaluating information.

Document them where relevant.

Do not perform the following shortcut:

> **Actor has an interest → actor is lying.**

That conclusion does not follow.

Nor should the opposite shortcut be used:

> **No lie has been proven → the interest is irrelevant.**

An interest can affect:

- what questions are commissioned;
- which facts receive emphasis;
- which experts are promoted;
- what terminology is preferred;
- what research is funded;
- what communications are optimised;
- what legal risks institutions avoid;
- and what information becomes easy to encounter.

Treat interests as context and possible explanatory variables.

Require additional evidence before attributing motive or causation.

---

## 🕸️ Principle Eight — Do Not Assume Coordination Where Incentives Are Enough

Similar outputs do not necessarily imply central direction.

Governments, banks, universities, corporations, media organisations, platforms, lobbyists and advocacy groups can behave in mutually reinforcing ways because their incentives overlap.

That is different from proving coordination.

Use language carefully:

**aligned** is not necessarily **coordinated**.

**coordinated** is not necessarily **directed**.

**directed** is not necessarily **covert**.

If coordination is alleged, look for evidence of coordination.

Do not use structural analysis as decorative conspiracy theory.

---

## 🧿 Principle Nine — Do Not Confuse Absence Of Evidence With Evidence Of Absence

Sometimes an expected record is missing because the event did not happen.

Sometimes because records were never created.

Sometimes because they were destroyed.

Sometimes because access is impossible.

Sometimes because evidence remains classified, sealed, inaccessible or undiscovered.

Sometimes because wartime conditions make ordinary documentation impossible.

Therefore:

> **No evidence currently available to this archive**

is not automatically equivalent to:

> **There is no evidence**

and neither automatically means:

> **It did not happen.**

Conversely, the possibility that evidence might exist somewhere cannot be used to declare an allegation true.

Unknown remains a legitimate category.

---

## ⏳ Principle Ten — Time Is Part Of The Claim

Palestine factchecking is unusually vulnerable to stale information.

Casualty totals change.

Hostage numbers change.

Territorial control changes.

Aid access changes.

Court proceedings advance.

Investigations publish new findings.

Officials revise statements.

Identifications are completed.

Early reporting is corrected.

Every rapidly changing claim should therefore carry enough temporal information to establish **when it was true, estimated, reported or checked**.

Prefer:

> **As of 20 August 2026...**

over:

> **Currently...**

where the date materially affects the proposition.

Do not silently update old quotations or statistics in ways that make historical reporting appear to have known things it could not yet have known.

---

## 📊 Principle Eleven — Numbers Need Methods

A statistic without its methodology can be profoundly misleading while remaining numerically accurate.

For important figures, ask:

- Who collected the data?
- What is being counted?
- Over what dates?
- In what geographic area?
- What is the denominator?
- What definitions are being used?
- Are duplicate records possible?
- How are missing records handled?
- Is the number reported, verified, identified, modelled or estimated?
- Has the methodology changed?
- Has the figure been revised?
- What uncertainty exists?

Preserve the verbs.

People were **reported** killed.

Bodies were **identified**.

Researchers **estimated** excess mortality.

Investigators **verified** incidents.

A database **recorded** cases.

A survey **sampled** respondents.

Those are different operations.

---

## ⚖️ Principle Twelve — Legal Language Requires Procedural Precision

Law is particularly vulnerable to headline compression.

Distinguish:

- allegation;
- application;
- investigation;
- charge;
- arrest warrant;
- jurisdictional decision;
- provisional measure;
- admissibility decision;
- finding;
- judgment;
- advisory opinion;
- appeal;
- and enforcement.

Do not say a court **ruled X** when it ruled on whether a case could proceed.

Do not say a court **cleared X** merely because it has not made a final adverse finding.

Do not turn a prosecutor's allegation into a judicial conclusion.

Do not turn a provisional measure into a final merits judgment.

Where practical, quote or closely paraphrase the operative legal language and link to the underlying decision.

---

## 🧵 Principle Thirteen — Define Contested Terms Before Using Them As Conclusions

Words such as:

**occupation, apartheid, terrorism, resistance, Zionism, anti-Zionism, antisemitism, genocide, ethnic cleansing, settler, indigenous, colonialism, blockade, siege, proportionality, human shield, civilian, combatant**

can carry legal, historical, political and ordinary-language meanings that do not perfectly overlap.

Do not pretend contested terminology can always be solved by a dictionary.

Instead ask:

- Which meaning is being used?
- Is this a legal classification?
- A historical framework?
- A political identity?
- An activist slogan?
- An ordinary-language description?
- Who is applying the term?
- According to what definition?

Sometimes the disagreement is factual.

Sometimes it is definitional.

Sometimes both.

---

## 🌍 Principle Fourteen — Translate Carefully

Translation is evidence handling.

Where wording matters:

- preserve the original where possible;
- identify the language;
- distinguish literal translation from idiomatic translation;
- note meaningful ambiguity;
- avoid quietly translating a disputed political term into the interpretation preferred by one side;
- and use qualified language when the archive cannot independently verify the translation.

A translation can be grammatically plausible and politically misleading.

Context matters.

So does register.

---

## 🖼️ Principle Fifteen — Authentic Media Can Carry False Claims

A photograph does not have to be fabricated to mislead.

A real image can be:

- old;
- from another location;
- cropped;
- reversed;
- stripped of sequence;
- paired with an inaccurate caption;
- attributed to the wrong actor;
- or presented as evidence for something outside the frame.

The same applies to video and audio.

Authentication and interpretation are separate tasks.

Where visual evidence is important, establish as much as possible about:

**origin, date, location, sequence, editing, uploader, surrounding footage and the proposition the media is being used to prove.**

---

## 🤖 Principle Sixteen — AI Is A Research Tool, Not A Primary Source

Generative AI can help:

- identify questions;
- locate terminology;
- compare claims;
- discover possible sources;
- summarise large bodies of material;
- identify contradictions;
- and suggest evidential routes worth following.

It can also:

- hallucinate;
- flatten disagreement;
- lose provenance;
- merge separate events;
- repeat highly indexed misinformation;
- reproduce outdated information;
- overstate legal conclusions;
- and present uncertain synthesis in fluent, authoritative prose.

Therefore:

> **“ChatGPT said it” is not a citation.**

AI-generated claims should be followed to inspectable sources.

This rule applies to favourable answers as much as unfavourable ones.

---

## 📣 Principle Seventeen — Headlines Are Evidence Of Headlines

A headline can accurately establish:

> **This publication presented the story this way.**

It does not necessarily establish every proposition implied by that presentation.

Read beyond headlines.

Compare headline, article body and underlying documentation.

Pay particular attention when causal verbs escalate:

**aims to influence → influences → changes → controls**

or when procedural verbs escalate:

**alleges → investigates → charges → finds**

or when uncertainty disappears:

**estimated → counted**

**reported → confirmed**

**possible → established**

Factchecking includes checking what happened to the proposition while it travelled.

---

## 🧠 Principle Eighteen — Separate Fact From Ethical Judgement

Some of the most important questions about Palestine are not resolvable by checking another document.

Questions about:

- justice;
- collective responsibility;
- resistance;
- punishment;
- proportionality in ordinary moral reasoning;
- obligations to strangers;
- political legitimacy;
- reconciliation;
- forgiveness;
- complicity;
- and what people owe one another

contain normative premises.

Facts matter enormously to those discussions.

But a factchecker should not smuggle an ethical conclusion into the factual layer and then announce that the morality has been “verified.”

`🧿_Ethics/` exists because ethical reasoning deserves to be visible rather than disguised as data.

---

## 🧯 Principle Nineteen — Corrections Are Part Of The Evidence

Getting something wrong is not cured by deleting the evidence that it was once wrong.

When a substantive error is identified:

1. preserve the previous version where appropriate;
2. record what was wrong;
3. identify the evidence that changed the assessment;
4. correct the current text;
5. date the correction;
6. update downstream nodes where the error propagated.

A correction is evidence that the archive can change its mind.

That is a feature.

Not an embarrassment to conceal.

---

## 🗂️ Principle Twenty — Preserve Challenges And Complaints

A challenge may reveal:

- an error;
- missing evidence;
- an alternative interpretation;
- a legal risk;
- a factual dispute;
- an attempt to suppress accurate reporting;
- or some mixture of these.

Do not decide which it is merely from the identity of the complainant.

Preserve enough documentation to reconstruct:

- what was challenged;
- by whom;
- on what basis;
- what evidence was supplied;
- what response was made;
- and what changed, if anything.

The dedicated documentation and complaints node governs the detailed procedure.

---

## 🚨 Principle Twenty-One — Serious Allegations Need Serious Attribution

The greater the potential harm of a factual allegation, the greater the need for precision.

For allegations concerning identifiable people or organisations:

- distinguish allegation from finding;
- identify the source of the allegation;
- seek primary documentation where possible;
- record denials or materially relevant responses;
- avoid implying criminality from association alone;
- avoid presenting inference as knowledge of motive;
- and do not add rhetorical certainty where the evidence does not supply it.

Precision is not timidity.

It is what allows serious claims to survive scrutiny.

---

## 🪫 Principle Twenty-Two — Uncertainty Must Survive Editing

Uncertainty is often lost downstream.

A researcher writes:

> **may**

A report writes:

> **likely**

A newspaper writes:

> **appears**

A social-media post writes:

> **did**

An AI summary writes:

> **was**

That drift matters.

When summarising, preserve material qualifiers.

Do not make prose cleaner by making evidence stronger.

---

## 🧭 Principle Twenty-Three — Use The Narrowest Defensible Conclusion

If the evidence establishes:

> **The contract sought to influence GPT-facing web results**

do not upgrade it to:

> **The contract changed ChatGPT.**

If the evidence establishes:

> **The organisation reported 100 incidents**

do not automatically write:

> **100 incidents occurred.**

If the evidence establishes:

> **A court found jurisdiction**

do not write:

> **The court found the underlying allegation proven.**

A narrower accurate conclusion is stronger than a dramatic conclusion the evidence cannot carry.

---

## 🔄 Principle Twenty-Four — Recheck Claims That Can Change

Some nodes will age.

Build that expectation into them.

Claims concerning:

- current casualty figures;
- hostages and detainees;
- aid access;
- government officeholders;
- military control;
- sanctions;
- legal proceedings;
- public-diplomacy spending;
- platform policy;
- and active investigations

should carry dates and, where useful, explicit recheck notes.

Historical stability should be earned rather than assumed.

---

## 🧿 Principle Twenty-Five — Apply The Same Standard When The Answer Hurts

This is the hardest rule.

A claim does not deserve weaker scrutiny because it supports Palestinian rights.

A claim does not deserve weaker scrutiny because it supports Israeli security.

A source does not become reliable because its conclusion is morally satisfying.

An error does not become harmless because the person making it is on the “right side.”

And correcting misinformation favourable to your own position is not betrayal.

It is evidence that the method exists independently of the desired answer.

> **The standard should remain recognisable when the conclusion is inconvenient.**

Otherwise this is not factchecking.

It is advocacy wearing a lab coat.

## 🧮 Working Claim Status

Where a short status is useful, this archive may use the following language.

### Established

The available evidence strongly supports the proposition.

### Strongly supported

Multiple substantial evidential routes support the proposition, while some limitation prevents stronger wording.

### Supported

There is meaningful evidence for the proposition, but relevant uncertainty or limitation remains.

### Contested

Substantial competing claims or interpretations remain and cannot honestly be collapsed into one settled account.

### Unresolved

The available evidence is insufficient to determine the proposition.

### Unsupported

The evidence supplied for the proposition does not establish it.

### Contradicted

Reliable evidence materially conflicts with the proposition.

### Misleading

Some component may be accurate, but framing, omission, denominator, chronology, wording or implication produces a materially distorted understanding.

### Outdated / Superseded

The proposition may once have accurately reflected the available information but no longer represents the best current evidence.

These labels are tools.

They are not substitutes for showing the evidence underneath them.

## 🔍 Minimum Factcheck Workflow

For substantial claims, use this sequence:

1. **State the proposition.**
2. **Classify the claim.**
3. **Timestamp it where necessary.**
4. **Locate the nearest available primary evidence.**
5. **Identify independent corroboration.**
6. **Trace common source ancestry.**
7. **Record relevant interests and limitations.**
8. **Check terminology, translation, statistics or legal procedure as applicable.**
9. **Separate established evidence from inference.**
10. **Look deliberately for evidence that could falsify the preferred conclusion.**
11. **State what remains unknown.**
12. **Use the narrowest defensible finding.**
13. **Preserve sources.**
14. **Record later corrections or revisions.**

The purpose is not bureaucracy.

The purpose is to make the reasoning reconstructable by someone who was not inside the author's head.

---

## 🧪 A Useful Adversarial Check

Before publishing a contentious factcheck, ask:

> **Would I accept this evidential standard if the conclusion pointed in the opposite political direction?**

If the answer is no, something needs another pass.

Also ask:

> **What evidence would change my mind?**

If the honest answer is **nothing**, the exercise is no longer functioning as factchecking.

---

## 🌾 What A Good Factcheck Should Leave Behind

A reader should be able to tell:

- what was claimed;
- what kind of claim it was;
- when it was checked;
- which evidence was used;
- where that evidence came from;
- what interests or limitations materially matter;
- what contrary evidence exists;
- what the archive concluded;
- how confident that conclusion is;
- and what remains unknown.

The reader does not have to agree with the author's politics.

They should be able to inspect the evidential chain anyway.

That is the standard.
