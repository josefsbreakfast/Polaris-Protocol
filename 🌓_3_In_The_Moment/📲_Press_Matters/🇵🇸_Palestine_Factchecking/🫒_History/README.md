## 🛰️ Orientation

This folder is the historical branch of **🇵🇸 Palestine Factchecking**.

It exists because many apparently current questions about Israel and Palestine are actually historical questions in disguise.

A claim about:

- borders;
- statehood;
- indigeneity;
- migration;
- displacement;
- land ownership;
- nationalism;
- refugees;
- partition;
- war;
- occupation;
- settlements;
- diplomacy;
- or political identity

often depends on an earlier claim about **what happened, when it happened, who was present, what institutions existed, what people understood themselves to be doing, and what the surviving evidence can establish**.

History here should therefore be treated as evidence work rather than as a collection of origin stories.

## 🫒 What This Folder Is For

This branch should eventually help answer questions such as:

- What happened?
- When?
- What happened immediately before and after?
- Which actors existed at that point in time?
- Which borders or administrative units actually existed?
- What did contemporary documents call them?
- What populations lived where?
- What do demographic records establish?
- What did political movements say they wanted at the time?
- What was proposed, accepted, rejected or never implemented?
- What did people flee from?
- What expulsions or forced removals are documented?
- What land was privately owned, publicly administered or otherwise classified?
- Which claims come from contemporary evidence?
- Which are later interpretations?
- Which historical claims remain genuinely disputed?
- Which once-disputed claims are now well established by archival evidence?
- How has later political language altered the way earlier events are described?

The exact node list should be built after a dedicated historical-source review.

## ⏳ Chronology Before Narrative

Historical arguments often become misleading because events are arranged around a preferred conclusion rather than reconstructed chronologically.

This folder should privilege:

**date → event → contemporary evidence → immediate consequences → later interpretation**

before attempting large narrative synthesis.

Chronology does not make interpretation disappear.

It makes it harder to smuggle later knowledge backwards into earlier events.

---

## 🧭 Historical Actors Must Stay In Their Own Time

Do not casually project present-day institutions, borders, identities or political positions backwards.

Ask:

- Did this state exist yet?
- Did this organisation exist yet?
- Was this territory governed under the same legal structure?
- Did the term have the same meaning?
- Was this political identity used in the same way?
- Was the document written before or after the event it describes?
- Is a later memoir being treated as contemporaneous evidence?

Historical precision includes resisting anachronism.

- geographic datasets.

Different evidence classes answer different questions.

## 🧿 Historiography Is Not A Defect

Historians disagree.

Sometimes because evidence is incomplete.

Sometimes because they interpret the same evidence differently.

Sometimes because new archives become available.

Sometimes because earlier national narratives excluded evidence that later scholarship incorporated.

Sometimes because political commitments genuinely affect historical interpretation.

This folder should not hide those disagreements.

It should distinguish:

> **The evidence establishes X.**

from:

> **Historian A interprets X as Y.**

from:

> **Historians disagree about whether X is better explained by Y or Z.**

A historiographical dispute should not be flattened into “nobody knows”.

Nor should one scholarly interpretation quietly become an uncontested fact.

## 🧵 Terminology Changes Across Time

Historical terminology requires dates.

Words such as:

- Palestine;
- Palestinian;
- Israel;
- Israeli;
- Zionist;
- Jewish;
- Arab;
- settler;
- colony;
- mandate;
- partition;
- refugee;
- indigenous;
- occupation;
- nationalism;
- and state

can refer to different political, geographic, legal or social realities in different periods.

A quotation should be read according to what its language meant in its own context before being recruited into a modern argument.

## 📊 Numbers Need Historical Methods Too

Historical statistics can be especially fragile.

Potential issues include:

- changing borders;
- incomplete censuses;
- incompatible administrative categories;
- population estimates rather than counts;
- land categories being mistaken for ownership categories;
- missing records;
- wartime displacement;
- retrospective reconstruction;
- and later political reuse of old statistics.

When a number becomes politically important, trace it.

Ask:

> **Where did this number first come from?**

Then:

> **What exactly was being counted?**

---

## 🗺️ Maps Are Arguments As Well As Evidence

Maps can clarify history.

They can also compress complicated legal and demographic realities into persuasive blocks of colour.

For important historical maps, ask:

- Who made the map?
- When?
- For what purpose?
- What do the colours represent?
- Are they population, ownership, sovereignty, administration, proposed borders or military control?
- What has been omitted?
- Are later borders projected backwards?
- Is a schematic political map being presented as cadastral evidence?

A map can be accurate and still be used to support a proposition it was never designed to establish.

## 🚧 Common Historical Failure Modes

Watch for:

- beginning the chronology at the moment most favourable to the argument;
- treating modern borders as timeless;
- treating demographic presence as identical to sovereignty;
- treating sovereignty as identical to private land ownership;
- treating private land ownership as identical to demographic presence;
- quoting political leaders as if one statement represented an entire population;
- turning proposals into implemented law;
- treating later memoirs as contemporaneous records;
- collapsing multiple wars into one event;
- presenting disputed motives as established facts;
- assuming absence from one archive means an event did not occur;
- treating one map as self-explanatory;
- using present-day terminology without checking historical meaning;
- and citing a modern infographic where the underlying historical source is available.
