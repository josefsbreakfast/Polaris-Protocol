# ❓ Why Are Israel And Palestine Fighting?
**First created:** 2026-08-20 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-20  
*A first-route answer to the deceptively simple question: why does the Israel–Palestine conflict keep producing violence, and which historical disputes are actually still unresolved?*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

> **Why are Israel and Palestine fighting?**

The shortest useful answer is:

**because two national movements developed claims to self-determination in the same land, and the political order produced through British rule, partition, the 1948 war, Palestinian dispossession, the 1967 occupation and repeated failed or incomplete peace processes never produced an agreed settlement of sovereignty, borders, security, refugees, Jerusalem or Palestinian statehood.**

That is the skeleton.

It is not the whole body.

And the conflict is **not** adequately explained as:

> Jews and Muslims have always hated each other.

Nor is it simply:

> an ancient religious war.

Religion matters, especially around Jerusalem and particular political movements.

But the recognisably modern Israel–Palestine conflict is principally a modern political conflict involving:

- nationalism;
- land;
- migration;
- empire;
- state formation;
- displacement;
- war;
- occupation;
- security;
- refugees;
- settlements;
- political violence;
- and competing claims to self-determination.

If somebody gives you 3,000 years of biblical history and then jumps directly to Gaza, several rather important administrative centuries have fallen out of the PowerPoint.

---

## 🧭 First: “Israel And Palestine” Is Useful Shorthand, But Imprecise

There are not simply two unitary actors called **Israel** and **Palestine** repeatedly deciding to fight one another.

Relevant actors have included, at different times:

- the Ottoman Empire;
- the British government;
- the Zionist movement;
- Palestinian Arab political organisations;
- Jewish paramilitary organisations;
- the State of Israel;
- neighbouring Arab states;
- the Palestine Liberation Organization (**PLO**);
- Fatah;
- the Palestinian Authority (**PA**);
- Hamas;
- Palestinian Islamic Jihad;
- Hezbollah;
- foreign governments;
- settlers;
- civilians;
- and numerous political, military and religious factions within both Israeli and Palestinian society.

So the question:

> **Why are Israel and Palestine fighting?**

is best treated as an entry point into several related conflicts rather than as proof that everyone on either side wants the same thing.

## 🏺 The Land Was Not Empty — And Colonisation Was Not A Secret Word

Before the creation of Israel in 1948, Palestine was not an uninhabited blank awaiting a political project.

For centuries it formed part of the Ottoman Empire. Its population included predominantly Arabic-speaking Muslim and Christian communities alongside longstanding Jewish communities and others.

During the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, modern **Zionism** developed substantially in Europe as a Jewish national movement in a context of severe and recurring antisemitism. Zionists differed among themselves about ideology, religion, territory, socialism, nationalism and the eventual political form of Jewish national life.

But we should not clean up the historical vocabulary to make the argument more comfortable in the present.

**Organised Zionist settlement in Palestine was deliberately described by Zionists themselves as colonisation.** Institutions, organisations and political writing of the period used the language of colonies, colonisation and settlement openly. In that period, *colonisation* did not automatically function as the moral condemnation it often does after twentieth-century decolonisation. It could be used positively by the people organising migration, land acquisition, agricultural settlement and institution-building.

That matters for two reasons.

First, calling the early project **colonising** is not simply a hostile twenty-first-century label imposed retrospectively on people who would never have recognised the description. The historical vocabulary itself contains it.

Second, the word does not by itself settle every later moral or political argument. Zionism was also a Jewish national movement shaped by persecution, pogroms, exclusion and eventually the catastrophe of the Holocaust, and Jewish attachment to the land long predates modern Zionism. Those facts matter. They do not make the Palestinian population already living in the country disappear.

The collision was therefore not simply two abstract national ideas discovering the same map at the same moment. It involved an organised movement seeking substantial Jewish migration, settlement and national institution-building in a country whose existing Arab population possessed political interests and increasingly articulated its own national claim to self-determination.

> **This is one of the places where the conflict exposes a problem in our present vocabulary. We are trying to understand a nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century political project through a twenty-first-century world that has undergone decolonisation. The moral meaning of the word changed. The historical record did not.**

That is precisely why the archive should neither euphemise the historical language nor treat one historically accurate word as though it automatically decides every contemporary question.

Neither population disappears because the other has a historical attachment to the land. Neither persecution nor indigeneity functions as a blank cheque over another population's political rights.

## 🇬🇧 Britain Helped Build The Problem, Then Left It Unresolved

Britain did not merely inherit an unfortunate disagreement and then fail to referee it successfully.

British imperial policy became part of the political architecture of the conflict.

In 1917 — before Britain possessed sovereign title to Palestine — the British government issued the **Balfour Declaration**, stating that it viewed with favour the establishment in Palestine of a “national home for the Jewish people” and would use its “best endeavours” to facilitate it. The declaration referred to the Arab majority not as a political people with an equivalent national claim, but as the existing “non-Jewish communities”, whose **civil and religious rights** were not to be prejudiced. It did not give them an equivalent guarantee of political or national rights.

The subsequent League of Nations Mandate did not leave that promise sitting on a shelf. It incorporated the Balfour policy into the machinery of government. The Mandatory administration was charged with creating political, administrative and economic conditions for the Jewish national home; it formally recognised a Jewish agency to cooperate with the administration; and Article 6 instructed the administration to facilitate Jewish immigration and encourage close Jewish settlement on the land, subject to its stated safeguards.

That is more than Britain “allowing” Zionism to happen.

It is an imperial government giving a colonising national project institutional and administrative pathways inside a country whose existing majority had not consented to that political programme and whose own demand for national self-government was not equivalently realised.

This does not mean British policy was static, that Zionist organisations simply obeyed Britain, or that Britain and the Zionist movement always wanted the same thing. Their relationship became deeply conflictual, particularly as Britain later restricted immigration and Zionist armed organisations fought British rule. Palestinian Arabs also resisted British rule directly, most dramatically in the Arab Revolt of 1936–39, which Britain suppressed.

But later conflict between Britain and Zionist organisations does not erase the earlier imperial structure.

During the Mandate period:

- Jewish immigration increased substantially;
- Zionist institutions developed considerable organisational capacity;
- land acquisition and settlement expanded;
- Palestinian Arabs demanded independence and representative government;
- Palestinian resistance increasingly targeted British rule as well as Zionist settlement;
- Britain repeatedly attempted to manage a contradiction its own policy had helped institutionalise;
- and political violence escalated among Palestinian Arabs, Jewish organisations and British authorities.

Nazi persecution in Europe during the 1930s made Jewish refuge catastrophically urgent. Then came the Holocaust.

The murder of six million Jews transformed the humanitarian and political context in which demands for Jewish refuge and statehood were heard.

But the catastrophe inflicted on European Jews did not transfer responsibility for solving European antisemitism onto Palestinians, make the Palestinian population cease to exist, or extinguish Palestinian political rights.

By 1947 Britain was not simply handing the UN a mysterious ancient quarrel. It was withdrawing from a territory after three decades in which British imperial government had helped structure the institutions, migration regime and political contradiction through which the conflict had developed.

That does not make Britain the sole cause of everything that followed.

It does make Britain an actor in the history rather than scenery behind it.

## 🗺️ 1947: Partition

In November 1947, the UN General Assembly adopted **Resolution 181**, proposing the partition of Mandatory Palestine into:

- a Jewish state;
- an Arab state;
- and a special international regime for Jerusalem.

Jewish political leadership accepted the partition plan.

Palestinian Arab leadership and Arab states rejected it.

Their objections included the allocation of territory and the principle that the country should be partitioned to create a Jewish state despite Palestinian Arabs forming the majority population of Mandatory Palestine.

Violence between Palestinian Arabs and Jews escalated before the British Mandate had even ended. citeturn0search0turn0search5

This matters because the war did **not** begin as a clean international war between an already-existing Israel and an already-existing Palestine.

The political order itself was collapsing.

---

## 🫒 1948: Israel, War And The Nakba

Israel declared independence in May 1948 as British rule ended.

Neighbouring Arab armies subsequently entered the war.

Israel survived and expanded beyond the territory allocated to the proposed Jewish state in the UN partition plan.

But the war produced another foundational fact:

**a very large part of the Palestinian population was displaced.**

At least around 700,000 Palestinians fled or were expelled during the conflict.

Palestinians call this the **Nakba** — *catastrophe*. citeturn0search0turn0search5

The proposed Palestinian Arab state did not emerge.

After the war:

- Israel controlled most of former Mandatory Palestine;
- Jordan controlled the West Bank and East Jerusalem;
- Egypt controlled Gaza;
- and a large Palestinian refugee population existed across Gaza, the West Bank and neighbouring states.

So 1948 produced radically different national memories.

For many Israeli Jews:

> survival, independence and statehood after centuries of persecution and the Holocaust.

For Palestinians:

> dispossession, destroyed or depopulated communities, exile and the failure of Palestinian statehood.

Those memories are not interchangeable.

They are both structurally important to understanding why later political arguments become existential so quickly.

---

## 🗝️ The Refugee Question Never Went Away

The descendants and families of Palestinians displaced in and around 1948 became one of the conflict's central unresolved political questions.

Arguments concern:

- who qualifies as a Palestinian refugee;
- whether and how refugees can return;
- property;
- compensation;
- citizenship;
- demographic consequences;
- responsibility for displacement;
- and the relationship between return and Israeli Jewish self-determination.

This is why “just let everyone go home” and “the refugee issue was seventy-eight years ago” both skip the actual dispute.

There are unresolved claims attached to real people, real property, political sovereignty and competing visions of what each state would be.

## ⚔️ 1967 Changed The Conflict Again

In June 1967, Israel fought Egypt, Jordan and Syria in the **Six-Day War**.

Israel captured:

- the West Bank;
- East Jerusalem;
- Gaza;
- the Sinai Peninsula;
- and the Golan Heights.

The Sinai was later returned to Egypt.

The West Bank, East Jerusalem and Gaza became the core territories of the subsequent Israeli–Palestinian territorial dispute. citeturn0search2turn0search5

The Palestinian population of the West Bank and Gaza was now living under Israeli military occupation.

That fact profoundly reshaped Palestinian politics and the conflict itself.

---

## 🏘️ Occupation And Settlements

After 1967, Israeli settlements were established and expanded in occupied territory.

This added another layer to the conflict:

> **If the West Bank is supposed to form a major part of a future Palestinian state, what happens when Israeli civilian communities, infrastructure and systems of control become increasingly embedded across that territory?**

In its 2024 advisory opinion, the International Court of Justice reaffirmed that Israeli settlements in the West Bank and East Jerusalem are being maintained in violation of international law and concluded that Israel's continued presence in the Occupied Palestinian Territory is unlawful. The opinion also treated the Occupied Palestinian Territory as comprising the West Bank, East Jerusalem and Gaza as a single territorial unit, while addressing the particular complexities of effective control over Gaza after Israel's 2005 disengagement. citeturn1search0

That legal analysis belongs properly in the Law branch.

For this introductory node, the historical point is simpler:

**occupation, settlement and control over territory became enduring rather than temporary features of the conflict.**

## 🪧 Palestinian National Politics Changed Too

Palestinian political organisation developed substantially after 1948.

The **PLO** was founded in 1964 and became the dominant international representative of the Palestinian national movement.

Palestinian organisations used:

- diplomacy;
- armed struggle;
- guerrilla warfare;
- international advocacy;
- protest;
- and, at times, attacks deliberately targeting civilians.

The **First Intifada**, beginning in 1987, was a mass Palestinian uprising against Israeli occupation.

Hamas also emerged during this period.

It combined:

- Palestinian nationalism;
- Islamist politics;
- social and charitable organisation;
- and armed activity.

Palestinian politics has never been one organisation with one ideology.

## 🕊️ Oslo Nearly Changed The Architecture — But Did Not Finish It

The 1990s **Oslo process** created the Palestinian Authority and transferred limited powers of self-government to Palestinian institutions in parts of Gaza and the West Bank.

But Oslo did not settle the hardest questions.

Those included:

- permanent borders;
- Jerusalem;
- Israeli settlements;
- Palestinian refugees;
- security;
- sovereignty;
- and final Palestinian statehood.

The interim arrangement became very long-lived.

The peace process deteriorated amid:

- continued settlement expansion;
- Palestinian attacks;
- Israeli military responses;
- political opposition on both sides;
- the assassination of Israeli Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin by a Jewish extremist;
- failed negotiations;
- and the Second Intifada.

The temporary architecture did not mature into the promised permanent settlement. citeturn0search1turn1search0

That failure is one of the most important pieces of the present conflict.

---

## 🍉 Gaza Became Its Own Political And Military Theatre

Israel removed its permanent settlements and ground forces from Gaza in 2005.

Hamas won Palestinian legislative elections in 2006.

After violent conflict between Hamas and Fatah, Hamas took control of Gaza in 2007, while the Palestinian Authority remained dominant in parts of the West Bank.

Israel and Egypt imposed or maintained extensive restrictions around Gaza, with Israel exercising substantial control over access, airspace, maritime access and movement of goods and people.

Israel and Hamas subsequently fought repeated rounds of conflict.

Hamas and other Palestinian armed groups fired rockets and carried out attacks against Israel.

Israel conducted major military operations and airstrikes in Gaza.

Palestinian civilians lived inside an increasingly severe and recurring cycle of blockade, military escalation and humanitarian vulnerability.

Israeli civilians, particularly communities near Gaza, lived with rocket fire and the threat of cross-border attack.

None of that made the underlying political questions disappear.

It made them more militarised.

---

## 🩸 October 7 Did Not Begin The Conflict

On 7 October 2023, Hamas and other Palestinian armed groups launched a major attack from Gaza into Israel.

They killed Israeli civilians and security personnel and took hostages into Gaza.

Israel responded with war in Gaza.

October 7 therefore matters enormously.

But:

> **October 7 is an explanation for the immediate war that followed. It is not the starting date of the Israel–Palestine conflict.**

Likewise, explaining the history before October 7 does not excuse atrocities committed on October 7.

Chronology is not absolution.

This distinction will recur throughout the pack.

## 🧩 So What Are They Actually Fighting About?

Once the history is stripped down, several unresolved questions keep reappearing.

### 🗺️ Territory and borders

Where should Israel end?

Where should a Palestinian state begin?

What happens to settlements?

What is the status of Gaza?

What territory is actually capable of forming a viable Palestinian state?

### 🏛️ Palestinian self-determination

Will Palestinians have a sovereign state?

If so:

- where;
- with what borders;
- with what control over security, movement, resources and airspace;
- and under what political institutions?

### 🛡️ Israeli security

How can Israelis be protected from:

- armed attacks;
- rockets;
- hostage-taking;
- massacres;
- and hostile armed organisations?

And which security measures are legitimate rather than mechanisms of indefinite domination?

### 🏘️ Occupation and settlements

What happens to Israeli control of the West Bank and East Jerusalem?

What happens to Israeli settlements and settlers?

### 🗝️ Refugees

What happens to Palestinians displaced in 1948 and their descendants?

Return?

Compensation?

Resettlement?

Some combination?

### 🕌 Jerusalem

Who exercises sovereignty?

How are holy sites governed?

How are Israeli and Palestinian claims accommodated?

### ⚔️ Political violence

Can armed struggle achieve Palestinian liberation?

Can overwhelming Israeli military power produce durable security?

What happens when each side interprets the other's violence as proof that compromise is suicidal?

### 🏛️ Political representation

Who legitimately represents Palestinians?

What Israeli government could make and sustain a final agreement?

What happens when political factions benefit from preventing compromise?

These are not decorative “final-status issues”.

They are the conflict.

## ♻️ The Security–Domination Loop

One of the recurring structures can be expressed crudely as:

```text
Israeli fear of attack
        ↓
greater security control / military force
        ↓
Palestinian restriction, dispossession and violence
        ↓
anger / resistance / armed recruitment
        ↓
attacks on Israelis
        ↓
greater Israeli fear
        ↺
```

That diagram is deliberately incomplete.

It does not assign equal power, equal legal status, equal casualties or equal responsibility.

It shows a **feedback loop**.

Understanding the loop is useful precisely because breaking it requires more than identifying which population experienced the previous turn.

## 🪞 The Two Stories Are Not Mirror Images

A common teaching device says:

> Israelis want security; Palestinians want freedom.

There is truth in that compression.

There is also danger in it.

Palestinians also want security.

Israelis also understand themselves as seeking national self-determination and freedom.

Meanwhile, the two populations do **not** possess symmetrical:

- state power;
- military capability;
- territorial control;
- freedom of movement;
- international recognition;
- or exposure to occupation.

Equal human worth does not require pretending the political structure is symmetrical.

And recognising structural asymmetry does not make attacks on Israeli civilians morally or legally irrelevant.

The archive needs to be able to hold both thoughts at once.

It is allowed.

## 🧵 It Is Not Simply A Religious Conflict

Judaism, Islam and Christianity all have profound connections to the land.

Jerusalem contains sites of extraordinary religious significance.

Religious movements also play major roles in contemporary politics.

But reducing the conflict to religion obscures:

- nationalism;
- colonial and imperial history;
- borders;
- statehood;
- refugee claims;
- military occupation;
- land ownership;
- settlements;
- security;
- and international power.

Secular Jews and Palestinians have been central participants in the conflict.

So have religious ones.

Religion is part of the system.

It is not the master key that explains everything.

## 🌍 Nor Is It Only A Local Conflict

External powers have repeatedly shaped the conditions under which Israelis and Palestinians act.

These include:

- Britain;
- the United States;
- neighbouring Arab states;
- Iran;
- European states;
- the Soviet Union and later Russia;
- Gulf states;
- international institutions;
- arms suppliers;
- donors;
- and diplomatic mediators.

Foreign involvement has included:

- empire;
- military aid;
- weapons;
- diplomacy;
- mediation;
- intelligence;
- humanitarian funding;
- sanctions;
- political recognition;
- and international legal action.

So “why are they fighting?” cannot ultimately be answered as though two populations were locked alone in a room.

## 🧿 The Most Important Thing Not To Do

Do not search for **the one event that proves who started it**.

There are meaningful starting points for particular questions:

- late Ottoman political change;
- the Balfour Declaration;
- the British Mandate;
- the 1947 partition plan;
- the 1948 war and Nakba;
- the 1967 war and occupation;
- the First Intifada;
- Oslo;
- the Second Intifada;
- the Gaza blockade;
- Hamas's takeover of Gaza;
- October 7;
- the subsequent Gaza war.

Which starting point is appropriate depends on the claim.

Choosing a date is itself framing.

The answer is not to begin at the dawn of civilisation every time.

It is to state **why this chronology begins where it does**.

## 🧠 The Polaris Answer

So:

> **Why are Israel and Palestine fighting?**

Because a century-plus political struggle over **self-determination, territory and political control** has never been resolved.

The creation of Israel secured Jewish statehood but was accompanied by mass Palestinian displacement and did not produce the Palestinian state envisaged by partition.

The 1967 war placed the West Bank, East Jerusalem and Gaza under Israeli occupation; decades of occupation, settlement, resistance and violence followed.

Peace processes created partial Palestinian self-government but left the central disputes unresolved.

Israeli civilians have faced wars, terrorism, rocket attacks, hostage-taking and the October 7 massacre.

Palestinians have faced dispossession, occupation, settlement expansion, restrictions on movement, blockade, repeated military operations and the continuing absence of full sovereign statehood.

Political actors on both sides have used violence.

But the parties do not occupy identical positions of power or territorial control.

And none of this is adequately described as an eternal ethnic or religious feud.

**It is a modern political conflict with a history.**

That history explains the conflict.

It does not predetermine what anybody is entitled to do next.

## 📚 Starting Sources

These are **starting routes**, not a closed canon.

- **League of Nations / Mandate for Palestine** — primary text for the British Mandatory obligations, including the establishment of the Jewish national home, recognition of the Jewish Agency and facilitation of Jewish immigration and close settlement on the land.
- **Balfour Declaration (1917)** — primary British statement promising to use British “best endeavours” to facilitate a Jewish national home in Palestine while describing the Arab majority as the existing “non-Jewish communities”.
- **UN, *The Origins and Evolution of the Palestine Problem, Part I: 1917–1947*** — useful institutional history of Balfour, the Mandate, Palestinian self-determination and Zionist colonisation; it should be read as a UN-produced historical account rather than as a neutral substitute for the underlying primary record.
- **British Palestine Royal Commission / Peel Commission (1937)** — particularly useful because the British inquiry itself identified Palestinian desire for national independence and fear of the Jewish National Home as the underlying causes of the revolt against Mandatory rule.
- **United Nations — History of the Question of Palestine** — useful institutional chronology covering the Mandate, partition, 1948, displacement, 1967 and subsequent UN involvement. citeturn0search5
- **Council on Foreign Relations Education — Israeli-Palestinian Conflict Timeline** — accessible chronological overview from partition through the contemporary conflict. citeturn0search0
- **Council on Foreign Relations — What Is U.S. Policy on the Israeli-Palestinian Conflict?** — useful compact background on the conflict's territorial and political origins, with the caveat that it is written through a US foreign-policy lens. citeturn0search3
- **International Court of Justice — 2024 Advisory Opinion summary** — primary institutional source for the Court's findings concerning occupation, settlements, annexation and Palestinian self-determination. citeturn1search0

As this cluster develops, the broad survey sources should give way to dedicated primary sources, historical scholarship and specialist nodes for each disputed proposition.
