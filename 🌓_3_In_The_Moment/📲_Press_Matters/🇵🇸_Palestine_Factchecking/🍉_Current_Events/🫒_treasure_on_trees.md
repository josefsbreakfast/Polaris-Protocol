# 🫒 Treasure on Trees
**First created:** 2026-10-22 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-22  
*Palestinian olive trees, the people who care for them, and the rather extraordinary amount of politics required to explain why somebody should be allowed to pick their own fruit.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation — What Is an Olive Tree Worth?

An olive tree is a living thing. It is also agricultural infrastructure, food production, family labour, a source of income, a piece of landscape, a repository of knowledge and, sometimes, a very old acquaintance.

These are not mutually exclusive descriptions. They are also not interchangeable.

If a Palestinian farmer cannot reach an orchard, the loss is not adequately described by counting the trees that have physically been destroyed. A tree can remain standing while its owner loses the ability to prune it, inspect it, protect it from disease, harvest its fruit or sell the resulting oil. An orchard can remain visible on a satellite image while ceasing to function as part of the family's livelihood.

Nor is the problem resolved by declaring the tree a symbol. Palestinian olive trees *are* important symbols, in literature, politics, memory and art. But a Palestinian family should not have to demonstrate that an orchard possesses some exceptional spiritual significance before its members can safely go to work.

The question is almost offensively ordinary: **can people reach their own trees, gather the harvest and use what they produce to live?**

That is where this node begins. Not with the international volunteer, the visiting politician, the rabbi, the journalist or the British woman writing about it. With the farmer.

The wider questions matter because they affect the answer: biology, economics, land tenure, international law, historical memory, Israeli settlement policy, restrictions on movement, violence, and the ways institutions describe the consequences of their decisions. We will also eventually arrive at Tolkien, because apparently the trees have been waiting for us there too.

But the farmer comes first.

---

## ✨ Key Features

- **The living asset:** why mature olive trees cannot simply be replaced by an equivalent number of saplings.
- **The household economy:** olives as food, cash income, employment, trade and access to other necessities.
- **The land:** ownership, cultivation, access, displacement and the difference between territorial lines and property rights.
- **The harvest under occupation:** distinct West Bank and Gaza mechanisms of agricultural harm.
- **The legal question:** civilian protection, property, occupation, settlement and the limits of international institutions.
- **The cultural question:** Palestinian knowledge and traditions on their own terms, without requiring folklore to establish rights.
- **The information question:** what disappears when official records count damaged trees but not interrupted lives, work and transmission.
- **The opportunity cost:** the articles, stories and conversations that cannot take centre stage while people are forced to defend the minimum conditions for ordinary life.

# PART I — 🫒 THE TREASURE

## 🫒 1. A Harvest Is Not an International Incident by Nature

An olive harvest ordinarily involves a series of rather unglamorous operations. People decide when the fruit is ready. They organise labour and equipment. They spread sheets or nets, pick or comb fruit from branches, sort and collect it, move it to a press or prepare it for curing, and work out what is for household consumption and what can be sold.

There are arguments about timing, quality, transport, weather and who has brought the wrong equipment. These are not unique to Palestine. Agricultural families everywhere have opinions about how other members of the family are doing the job.

A functioning harvest requires more than a tree. It requires the farmer to be able to get there. It requires a reasonable expectation of physical safety. It requires a route for the crop to reach processing facilities and markets. It requires the family to believe that returning tomorrow will be possible.

When international observers accompany Palestinian farmers during the olive harvest, the existence of the observers is not the principal agricultural fact. The principal fact is that **ordinary agricultural work has become sufficiently insecure that accompaniment is thought necessary**.

The observers may document attacks, discourage interference, provide practical assistance or attract publicity. Their presence can matter. Their experience is not, however, the story that ought to displace the people whose work they have come to support.

There is an understandable tendency for international coverage to become more animated when a recognisable outsider is detained or injured. The resulting attention can reveal a much larger problem. It can also reproduce a familiar hierarchy: a Palestinian family's long experience of danger becomes newsworthy when somebody from outside the community briefly encounters it.

We should be capable of holding both facts at once.

**Research note:** Before publication, anchor this opening in one consented, attributed Palestinian farmer account with date, location, cultivation history, harvest circumstances and source. Do not invent a composite family and present it as testimony.

## 🌳 2. The Trees Themselves — You Cannot Replace a Century with a Sapling

The cultivated olive, *Olea europaea*, belongs to a long history of Mediterranean agriculture. It is a perennial woody plant, capable under suitable conditions of surviving and producing for a very long time. That does not mean every old olive tree has a scientifically established age, nor that every tree produces a dependable annual yield.

Cultivation involves choices and skills: which variety to grow, how to propagate it, whether and how to graft, how to prune, how to manage soil and water, how to identify disease, when to pick, and what sort of oil or preserved fruit the harvest is intended to become. Palestinian cultivation includes locally important varieties and naming traditions; varietal claims need to be checked against agricultural and genetic research rather than copied from tourist descriptions.

Olives can tolerate relatively dry conditions, but **drought tolerance is not immunity to environmental damage**. Establishment, productivity, soil condition, extreme weather and access to water all matter. Nor does an orchard look after itself merely because its trees have survived for decades.

There is a useful distinction between four different losses:

1. **The organism:** a tree is cut, burnt, uprooted or killed.
2. **The productive asset:** the farmer loses the fruit and future yields while a replacement develops.
3. **The agricultural system:** terraces, soil, irrigation, access paths, tools, pressing facilities and accumulated management knowledge are damaged or interrupted.
4. **The particular relationship:** a family loses *that* tree, in *that* place, cared for by particular people over time.

The first three are at least partly measurable. The fourth is not therefore imaginary.

If somebody destroys a mature productive tree and offers to plant a sapling, the arithmetic is not one tree minus one tree plus one tree equals zero. The new tree has to survive, establish, grow and eventually become productive. Meanwhile, the lost crop does not wait politely for the accounts department.

This matters even when a tree is not destroyed. If a family is prevented from accessing an orchard over successive seasons, the consequences may compound. Maintenance is deferred. Harvests are missed. Soil and infrastructure deteriorate. Income is lost. Knowledge that would ordinarily be passed between generations through shared work has fewer opportunities to travel.

An orchard is a system with memory.

## 🧺 3. The Harvest Is a Social Institution

There is a temptation, especially in writing about Palestine, to make every ordinary act carry the entire weight of national resistance.

The temptation is understandable. It can also become exhausting for the people concerned.

Harvesting olives may express attachment to land, family continuity or *sumūd* — steadfastness. It may also be a job somebody needs to finish before the weather turns, because the fruit is ready and the press is expecting it.

Those are not competing explanations.

Harvest work can bring generations together. Practical knowledge travels through observation and repetition: how to approach a branch without damaging it; which trees are ready; how to recognise a problem; where the old paths run; what a good yield looks like in this particular field. The knowledge may be partly spoken, partly demonstrated and partly acquired through years of making mistakes under the supervision of relatives with extremely strong opinions.

The social institution is not just a festival or a photograph of people smiling among the branches. It is an arrangement of labour, time, responsibilities, shared resources, equipment and expectations. Some people enjoy it more than others. Some households depend on it more heavily. Not every Palestinian community farms olives, and not every Palestinian farmer has the same relationship to a particular crop.

The point is to take the work seriously enough that we do not romanticise it.

An orchard can be simultaneously a workplace, a family inheritance and a landscape of affection. None of those descriptions cancels the others.

## 💰 4. The Economics of an Olive — Unfortunately You Cannot Eat Olives All Year

Olives can be eaten. Olive oil can be consumed, stored, traded and used in food preparation. Olive cultivation can also support employment, processing, transport, packaging and sales. Historically and today, olive products have supported a wider range of uses, including soap production.

But a household is not an olive tree. People need housing, medicine, clothing, school supplies, transport, other foods and the money to obtain things they do not grow themselves.

**The crop is a way of participating in an economy.**

The basic chain is straightforward:

**Land and trees → access and maintenance → harvest → processing → consumption or sale → household resources.**

Every arrow in that chain is a possible point of failure.

A good crop is not necessarily a good income if the farmer cannot transport it. An operating press is of limited help if access roads are closed. An export market is not much use if goods cannot move reliably. A family may have trees and still lack the cash to buy food because the productive relationship between the orchard and the wider economy has been severed.

### 📊 Counting carefully

Agricultural reporting uses several measures that should not be treated as synonyms:

- **Tonnes of olives harvested:** the weight of the fruit.
- **Tonnes of olive oil produced:** a processed output, affected by extraction rates and other factors.
- **Farm-gate value:** what producers receive for a product at the point of sale.
- **Gross output:** the value of production, not necessarily net income.
- **Gross value added:** output less intermediate consumption, not household take-home pay.
- **Export value:** the value of products sold abroad, not total sector income.
- **Economic loss:** a calculation requiring explicit assumptions about what would otherwise have happened.

The Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics has published olive-pressing surveys and agricultural statistics. Those should provide the quantitative spine of this section, with each number labelled by year, geographical coverage and definition. A 2014 figure must not be presented as a current 2026 measurement.

A lost harvest is not merely lost fruit. It can mean less purchasing power for a household, less work for processors and transporters, less money circulating locally and greater dependence on external assistance.

And yes, olives do lots of jobs. **They still cannot be expected to do every job in the household.**

# PART II — 🌍 THE LAND

## 🌿 5. The Tree as Symbol, Asset and Living Relationship

Palestinian olive trees appear in poetry, visual art, political imagery, family accounts and writing about place. That symbolism did not arise from a public-relations exercise. It draws on long agricultural histories and the very ordinary experience of living with cultivated trees.

A symbol can communicate something real. It can also obscure it.

When the olive tree becomes a universal emblem of peace, it is possible to forget that somebody has to prune it, pick its fruit, carry the sacks and pay the pressing bill. When it becomes an emblem of resistance, it is possible to forget that the people doing those things may also want a normal working day.

Palestinian writers including Mourid Barghouti and Raja Shehadeh offer ways of reading landscape through memory, movement, dispossession and lived experience. Anthropological work on olive production and Palestinian everyday life can help keep the analysis grounded in actual practice. Specific passages require edition-level verification before quotation.

### 🌙 Sacred trees, and what we do not get to assume

Palestinian traditions also include historically documented relationships with sacred trees, shrines, saints, healing practices and local landscapes. Tawfiq Canaan's early twentieth-century ethnographic work is an important entry point, alongside contemporary Palestinian scholarship and Arabic-language research.

But **a sacred tree is not automatically an olive tree**. Nor is every Palestinian relationship with an olive tree religious. Quranic references to the olive and to light are significant within Islamic traditions; their existence does not prove that every cultivator holds a particular mystical belief. Palestinian Muslim, Christian, Jewish, secular and local traditions must not be collapsed into a single imaginary folklore.

There is a genuinely exciting separate investigation here: Palestinian sacred landscapes, olive cultivation, oral history, Sufi interpretation, and the ways relationships with trees are transmitted across generations.

We are not going to make Palestinian farmers wait for us to complete a comparative religion seminar before agreeing that they should be safe at work.

## 🪓 6. What Does Destroying an Orchard Actually Mean?

A photograph of a cut tree can be powerful evidence. It is not the whole accounting system.

Agricultural harm can involve uprooting, burning, cutting branches, poisoning or otherwise damaging trees, destruction of terraces or irrigation, theft of fruit, damage to equipment, obstruction of access, or threats that prevent people from entering their land. These actions differ in severity, attribution, reversibility and legal consequences.

A careful incident record should distinguish **what happened to the tree** from **what happened to the farmer**.

For example, a tree may remain alive while the family loses an entire season's crop because access was denied. Conversely, a destroyed tree may represent a loss extending over many future seasons. Repeated interference may produce cumulative effects that a single incident count cannot capture.

This is also why statistics require care. An estimate of trees damaged over a long period cannot be added uncritically to a newer figure that may overlap with it. A report of attacks on farmers is not the same metric as a count of trees uprooted. An orchard's loss of productive capacity cannot always be inferred from the number of trunks visible afterwards.

For the West Bank, reporting should distinguish violence by settlers, conduct by Israeli state personnel, formal administrative restrictions and other documented causes rather than assigning all events to one actor without evidence.

For Gaza, wartime agricultural destruction must be assessed through a separate evidentiary framework, including the effects of military operations, displacement, access restrictions, infrastructure damage and conditions affecting restoration.

**The ecological system does not care which administrative category was used to record its interruption. The evidence file still must.**

## 🏡 7. Whose Land Is It?

The history of landholding in Palestine cannot be compressed into the proposition that whoever currently controls a field must therefore own it.

Nor is a modern land dispute resolved by pointing to a single map without asking what the map actually represents.

Relevant histories include Ottoman land categories and registration practices; British Mandate administration; the displacement and property consequences of the 1948 war; subsequent Israeli land and absentee-property legislation; the 1967 occupation; settlement expansion; and contemporary systems governing land registration, access and use.

At each stage, the questions are distinct:

- Who holds legal title, and under which legal system?
- Who has historically cultivated the land?
- Who can physically enter it?
- Who administers the territory?
- Who can transfer or expropriate property, and on what lawful basis?
- What remedies are available when those powers are abused?

**Ownership, possession, cultivation, access and sovereignty are different concepts.**

A farmer may retain a property claim while being prevented from cultivating. A family may have long-standing use rights that are not adequately represented by a simplistic title-search narrative. Territorial administration does not confer an unlimited right to dispose of privately held land.

The precise answers depend on the location, the historical records and the applicable law. This is why serious reporting needs actual parcel-level evidence where available, rather than declaring an entire dispute settled by a coloured line on a map.

## 🌲 8. Planting Trees Over History — Canada Park

Canada Park provides a case study in the relationship between landscape, memory and displacement.

The Palestinian villages of **Imwas, Yalu and Bayt Nuba** were destroyed in the context of the **1967 war**. Their histories are relevant to subsequent land use and the development of the park associated with the Jewish National Fund and Canadian fundraising.

The date matters. These villages should not be casually folded into the history of villages depopulated in 1948. The histories intersect, but they are not interchangeable.

A park can be a real place of recreation and ecological management while also occupying a landscape with an earlier human history. Trees planted later do not make that history disappear, even when visitors have not been told it.

The investigation should use historical maps, village records, testimonies from former residents and their descendants, land documentation and records of the park's development. It should not assert that every modern path or planted tree occupies a specific former family parcel without evidence.

The central question is what happens when a landscape becomes legible primarily through its newest use, while the people who remember its previous use are displaced from it.

## ⚖️ 9. The Green Line Is Not a Conveyancing Solicitor

The Green Line emerged from the **1949 armistice arrangements**. It is central to discussions of the territories occupied in 1967 and the territorial framework of diplomatic proposals. It is not a universal deed of ownership for every plot on either side.

The 1967 occupation engages international humanitarian law and questions about the administration of occupied territory, settlements, property and the rights of the protected population. The International Court of Justice's **19 July 2024 advisory opinion** addressed the legal consequences arising from Israel's policies and practices in the Occupied Palestinian Territory, including East Jerusalem.

That opinion is an important legal source. It is not a substitute for investigating the title history of a particular orchard, and an advisory opinion should be described as such rather than as a judgment in a private property case.

The principle is simple enough to fit on a sign:

**A territorial line is not a conveyancing solicitor.**

Where organisations make statements about land sales or property exhibitions, reporting should identify exactly what was claimed, what evidence supports it and whether the issue concerns territorial status, ownership, transaction legality or all three. The categories are not decorative.

# PART III — 🚜 THE HARVEST UNDER OCCUPATION

## 🚰 10. The Jordan Valley — Access Is an Agricultural Input

The Jordan Valley offers a way to examine the relationship between land, water, movement and livelihood. The Ein al-Hilweh area is a useful case study, subject to precise sourcing of its local history and contemporary circumstances.

Agriculture depends on access to more than soil. Water systems, grazing or cultivation routes, equipment, roads, electricity, markets and predictable working conditions all contribute to whether a household can continue to farm.

Restrictions on any one of those components may have effects elsewhere. A family that cannot reliably reach a field may invest less in maintenance. Reduced production may undermine its ability to pay for transport or equipment. Economic pressure may make continued residence harder.

These mechanisms should be investigated through Palestinian testimony, land and planning records, reporting on settlement and outpost activity, and the documented decisions of relevant authorities.

Do not turn one Jordan Valley community into a proxy for all Palestinians. Its value as a case study lies in the detail.

## 🫒 11. The West Bank Harvest, 2023–2026

The olive harvest has become an important recurring point of international attention to violence and restrictions affecting Palestinian agricultural communities in the occupied West Bank.

Reporting by UN OCHA and human-rights organisations documents harvest-related incidents and constraints on access. The specific scale, locations, perpetrators, responses and outcomes must be tied to dated sources rather than repeated as a single undifferentiated claim.

A useful chronology separates:

- **2023:** incidents and restrictions during the harvest season, in the wider context of escalating violence.
- **2024:** the next harvest, including access conditions and documented incidents.
- **2025:** changes in restrictions, attacks and responses, assessed against the earlier baseline.
- **2026:** contemporary accounts and the extent to which conditions have changed or persisted.

### 🧾 What an incident entry must establish

Each case needs a date, village or location, the affected Palestinian people, the activity underway, a description of the interference or harm, attributed witness accounts, independently verified facts and any known official response.

Cases proposed for detailed examination include reporting from **Turmus Ayya**, **Abu Falah**, and the **October 2026 incident near Nahalin involving Brad Lander, rabbis and harvest volunteers**. The precise claims, participants, political titles, locations and outcomes require checking against original reporting before being asserted as settled facts here.

That is not a reason to leave Palestinian testimony out. It is a reason to attribute it accurately.

An international visitor's experience may reveal what is happening in an orchard. It does not establish that the problem began when the visitor arrived.

### ✡️ Why rabbis and Jewish volunteers matter — and what they do not replace

Jewish participation in harvest accompaniment is worth examining in its own right. It complicates accounts that treat Jewish identity as synonymous with Israeli state policy or with support for settlement activity. Different Jewish participants may be motivated by religious ethics, political commitments, human-rights concerns, personal relationships or combinations of these.

Their presence does not entitle them to become the central characters in a story about Palestinian land and labour.

The most useful question for any accompanying organisation is not whether it produced a moving photograph. It is whether its actions improved the farmer's practical ability to work safely, document interference or obtain a remedy.

### 📌 Reporting caution

The exact 2026 OCHA incident totals and individual contemporary cases remain **verification tasks**, not figures to improvise. A publication-ready revision should add a dated incident table with links to primary reporting and avoid conflating attacks on people with attacks on trees.

## 🏚️ 12. Gaza — When Food Production Is Destroyed

Gaza's agricultural circumstances require their own section. They should not be treated as merely an intensified version of a West Bank olive-harvest access dispute.

Agricultural production depends on cultivable land, water, soil quality, equipment, seeds or planting material, labour, electricity, roads, processing capacity, storage and markets. During war, damage to those components can make the return to production difficult even after a particular episode of fighting ends.

An orchard may be physically damaged. It may also become inaccessible because of displacement, insecurity, restrictions or contamination. Farmers may be unable to maintain trees, transport crops or obtain the materials needed to resume production. Other agricultural sectors may be affected differently; an olive-focused article should not erase Gaza's wider food-production system.

Assessments by FAO and other UN agencies, together with Palestinian agricultural sources and organisations such as Gisha, should be used to distinguish observed damage from estimated loss and projected recovery needs.

A further distinction is essential:

**Delivering food is not the same thing as restoring the ability to produce food.**

Emergency food assistance can be necessary for survival. It does not automatically replace land, irrigation, equipment, markets, employment or the practical autonomy of growing and selling a crop.

The right to survive an emergency should not quietly become an expectation that a population will remain permanently dependent on emergency provision.

# PART IV — ⚖️ WHO GETS TO DECIDE?

## ✡️ 13. Not Even the Fallen Olives

Jewish legal literature has a great deal to say about property, loss, recovery, damage and the obligations people owe one another. Some of it is surprisingly specific about fruit.

**Bava Metzia 21b** includes discussion relevant to fallen olives and carobs within the wider law of lost property and the owner's abandonment of hope of recovery, or *ye'ush*. Its precise application depends on the legal reasoning and circumstances; it should not be flattened into a universal rule that every olive on the ground always belongs to the same person.

Other relevant concepts include the prohibition on theft, obligations concerning lost property and *bal tashchit*, the prohibition against wasteful destruction, with its biblical roots in the treatment of fruit trees during siege.

These are different bodies of law. They do not automatically decide modern property disputes or the obligations of an occupying power.

Nevertheless, the contrast is striking. A tradition can reason in extraordinary detail about when a person has relinquished a claim to fallen fruit. Contemporary political argument can somehow struggle to keep its attention on whether a farmer should be allowed to reach a productive orchard in the first place.

Jewish ethics is not a single political programme. Jews disagree, sometimes profoundly, about the implications of their traditions for Israel and Palestine. Nor should Palestinian rights depend on Jewish religious approval.

The point is that the moral and legal questions are not beyond human comprehension.

**We have, as a species, already managed to develop opinions about individual fallen olives.**

## ♀️ 14. Coercive Control Is Not a Peace Agreement

There is a reason the language of coercion is useful when examining systems that restrict people's practical options.

A person can be told that they are free to make a choice while being denied the material conditions that would make alternatives possible. Control over movement, money, work, housing or access to necessities changes what choices mean in practice.

This does not make military occupation legally identical to domestic coercive control. Different legal regimes, actors and evidentiary standards apply. The comparison is analytical, not a claim that a domestic criminal statute can simply be pasted onto an international conflict.

The mechanism worth examining is **dependency manufactured through constrained alternatives**.

If a family cannot reliably farm its land, cannot safely travel to work, cannot maintain productive assets and cannot secure effective protection, then the resulting economic decisions should not be interpreted as though the family were choosing freely from an ordinary range of options.

A subsequent offer of assistance, relocation or conditional access does not erase the conditions that made the offer necessary.

This is where safeguarding and political analysis intersect: it is not enough to ask whether someone technically agreed. We must also ask what meaningful alternatives were available and who controlled them.

## 🗳️ 15. One State, Two States — And the People Who Actually Live There

Constitutional proposals are often discussed as though selecting a label will solve the administrative problem of human rights.

It will not.

Whatever political settlement is proposed, the people living within it need workable answers about citizenship, voting, equality before the law, freedom of movement, land rights, water, security, return or restitution claims, independent courts and remedies for abuse.

A Palestinian farmer should not have to wait for a perfect final-status formula before enjoying basic civilian protections. Nor does recognising Palestinian rights require pretending that Jewish Israeli civilians have no legitimate security interests.

The difficult work is to specify institutions, rights, enforcement and accountability, rather than use either one-state or two-state language as a substitute for them.

When contemporary political proposals are introduced, they should be dated, attributed and assessed against those practical questions. A party conference motion or advocacy statement is evidence of a position, not proof that the proposed system would work as described.

**The orchard is a useful test:** under the proposed arrangement, who can reach it, who can protect it, who can challenge an unlawful interference and what happens when an official ignores the rules?

## 🇬🇧 16. Bro. We've Had to Read the Missing Chapters Too

Britain has a particular historical responsibility to understand its role in Palestine. British imperial administration and the Mandate are not peripheral background details in the history of modern territorial and political arrangements.

But acknowledging that history does not give a British writer ownership of Palestinian experience. Nor does being Jewish make somebody an authorised spokesperson for Israeli state policy, Palestinian society or all other Jews.

There are multiple Jewish historical and political traditions, including traditions of anti-Zionism and dissent. There are also Palestinian historians, writers, archivists and communities who have documented their own histories despite displacement and loss.

The work is not to assemble a flattering British account in which we finally discover that we have read the missing chapters and are therefore qualified to grade everybody else.

The work is to stop treating the missing chapters as optional reading.

The history of Britain's own imperial systems should encourage methodological humility: administrative categories can become very effective at describing land, populations and economic output while obscuring who is being made to bear the cost.

## ⚖️ 17. International Law Is Not an Optional Editorial Style

International law cannot guarantee that a farmer will be safe tomorrow. That limitation is serious. It does not mean the law is a decorative preference.

The law of occupation, international humanitarian law, human-rights obligations and the protection of civilian property establish frameworks for evaluating conduct. Different instruments address different questions; they should not be merged into a single vague invocation of 'the international community'.

The ICJ's July 2024 advisory opinion concerns the legal consequences of Israel's policies and practices in the Occupied Palestinian Territory. The separate proceedings brought by South Africa under the Genocide Convention involve a different case and different procedural decisions. Provisional measures, advisory opinions, allegations and final merits judgments must be identified accurately.

Likewise, it matters whether a report establishes that an attack occurred, identifies a suspected perpetrator, records an official allegation or documents a judicial finding. Journalism and legal reasoning both depend on preserving those distinctions.

The question is not whether law has magically prevented all harm. It plainly has not. The question is what duties exist, who must act on them, how violations are documented and what remedies are realistically available.

### 🏛️ Governance is not a substitute for rights

External plans for administering Gaza or reconstructing its institutions raise questions about legitimacy, representation, accountability, security, resources and the participation of Palestinians in decisions affecting their lives. A governance proposal is not validated merely by sounding administratively comprehensive.

Where historical planning documents resemble later proposals, resemblance alone is not evidence of common authorship or a direct causal chain.

For the orchard, the test remains concrete: does the proposed system protect civilians, restore productive capacity, recognise legitimate rights and provide enforceable remedies?

Jewish ethical traditions contain a useful reminder, commonly associated with **Pirkei Avot 2:16**, that a person is not required to finish the work but is not free to abandon it. The wording and translation should be checked before formal quotation.

An institution's inability to solve everything does not excuse its refusal to do what is within its responsibilities.

# PART V — 🪿 THE SYSTEM

## 🪿 18. What the Orchard Records — Embodied Information Ecology

The orchard is an unusually clear example of why information cannot be separated from the conditions under which it is produced.

A farmer's harvest figures are not simply a measurement of the farmer's effort or the soil's productivity. They may also reflect access restrictions, violence, weather, maintenance, transport, processing capacity, prices and the household's available labour.

An outside observer who sees declining production and concludes that the farmer is no longer interested in cultivation may have mistaken the effects of a constraint for a personal preference.

That is an information failure with policy consequences.

### ♻️ Loop A — Continuity

**Access → cultivation → harvest → income and practical knowledge → maintenance → future productive capacity.**

### 🪓 Loop B — Disruption

**Threat or restriction → missed work → reduced harvest and maintenance → economic vulnerability → reduced capacity to sustain cultivation.**

### 👁️ Loop C — Misinterpretation

**Constrained behaviour → incomplete observation → false inference about capacity or willingness → decisions that reinforce the constraint.**

The loops are analytical models, not claims that every orchard or household follows the same trajectory. Their value is in identifying what an incident report might otherwise omit.

An attack on a farmer is not only an event in a violence database. It can alter agricultural production, future income, intergenerational teaching, movement patterns and the credibility of institutions meant to protect people.

### 🥔 Comparative mechanisms, not interchangeable histories

Polaris's work on *An Gorta Mór* and on the British opium empire offers comparative questions about agricultural governance, extraction, administrative categories and the difference between nominal market activity and genuine freedom.

Those histories are not equivalent to the occupation of Palestine. Their usefulness lies in asking carefully defined questions about mechanisms, not declaring every colonial history to be the same event in different clothing.

### 📰 Questions for journalists and policymakers

1. Who actually cultivates the orchard, and what do they say they need?
2. Can the farmer safely reach the land throughout the year, not merely on the day a reporter visits?
3. Is a reported loss a destroyed tree, a missed harvest, stolen fruit, lost access or several of these?
4. How are lost future yields and maintenance costs estimated?
5. Are processing facilities, transport routes and markets functioning?
6. What did the responsible authorities know, and what action did they take?
7. Are there independent investigations, remedies and publicly available outcomes?
8. How are women, children, older farmers and people with disabilities affected differently?
9. Are displaced farmers' property and cultivation claims being preserved?
10. Are restoration programmes designed with the farmers who possess the relevant local knowledge?
11. Does reporting centre the Palestinian people affected, or primarily the visiting outsiders?
12. What would count as evidence that conditions had actually improved?

### 🫒 Return to the harvest

A family wants to gather olives.

The trees require care. The fruit has a season. The work takes time, equipment and people. The household needs the food and income. The farmer knows the orchard in ways no visiting delegation can acquire in an afternoon.

None of this should be difficult to accommodate within an ordinary account of civilian life.

Yet the ability to do it can depend on land administration, security practices, the actions of armed people, political decisions and whether legal protections are enforced.

We can describe all of those systems in great detail.

We should not lose sight of what they are interrupting.

## 🌳 Afterword — On the Things We Haven't Even Got To

There is another article I would quite like to have written.

It would involve Palestinian olive trees, sacred landscapes, the imagery of oil and light, Sufism, oral history, and the peculiar relationships human beings develop with trees over very long periods of time.

It would probably also involve Tolkien, because I have been reading *The Fellowship of the Ring*, and because British and Irish cultural memory is full of relationships with landscapes that we sometimes inherit without quite knowing what we have inherited.

Your business with a horse chestnut tree and your business with a hawthorn tree are two very different types of business.

I think that is interesting. I think the fact that we can recognise these distinctions without necessarily subscribing to an organised theology is interesting. I think the ways people learn what may be said about such things, and to whom, are interesting. I think it is interesting that a supposedly respectable piece of writing about international law can make me hesitate before mentioning inherited folk beliefs at all.

And I am a British Jewish woman, not a Palestinian farmer. I am writing from a comparatively protected position, including advantages afforded to me within racial hierarchies that are profoundly unequal. My hesitation about discussing folklore is not equivalent to somebody else's risk of violence, dispossession or hunger. It would be ridiculous to pretend otherwise.

But it does make me think about what domination takes from the world beyond its most immediate victims.

There are stories people do not get to tell. Questions they do not get to pursue. Work that does not get written. Cultural encounters that become difficult because the first thing anybody has to explain is why the people concerned should be allowed to remain alive and carry on with their ordinary lives.

I could have been writing about Palestinian olive trees and Sufism. About Tolkien's Ents and the fact that they take an age to hold a committee meeting, right up until somebody starts burning their friends. About the difference between treating a tree as timber and recognising that a living landscape has relationships and histories of its own.

There is a conversation to be had there, and it is not a frivolous one. It is part of what people do when they are allowed to encounter one another as complicated human beings rather than primarily through evidence of their suffering.

Instead, here I am explaining why farmers should be able to get to their orchards, harvest their crops, protect their children and earn enough money to buy the things that cannot be grown on an olive tree.

You cannot just eat olives all year. Even if they are extremely versatile.

And I am aware that, from where I sit, the cost is mostly intellectual time and the loss of a rather enjoyable research tangent. For the people living with the violence, the costs are of an entirely different order. That asymmetry is the point, not an inconvenience to be edited out.

Still, there is a wider opportunity cost to making basic survival permanently negotiable. Everyone loses possible knowledge, art, conversation, friendship and cultural exchange. The people most directly harmed lose far more, and they should not have to demonstrate what the rest of us might have learned from them in order to deserve protection.

**A tree does not need to be sacred for its destruction to matter. A farmer does not need to be a symbol for their life to matter.**

We literally do not have to organise the world this way.

We could have been talking about the trees.
