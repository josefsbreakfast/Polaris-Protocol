# 🗳️ The History Of Banning “Arab” Votes
**First created:** 2026-10-01 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-01  
*You do not have to confiscate somebody’s ballot paper to make their vote count for less.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

On **23 September 2026**, less than five weeks before Israel’s 27 October general election, the Central Elections Committee voted to disqualify **every Arab-party list contesting the election**.

Ra’am — the United Arab List, led by Mansour Abbas — was disqualified.

The Joint List — led by Yousef Jabareen and bringing together Hadash/Jabha, Balad/Tajammu’ and Ta’al — was disqualified.

MK Ofer Cassif of Hadash and former MK Sami Abu Shehadeh, chairman of Balad, were separately disqualified as candidates.

As this node is first being written on **1 October 2026**, those decisions are before a nine-justice panel of Israel’s Supreme Court.

So we do not yet know what the final ballot will look like.

We do know something else.

**This did not come out of nowhere.**

Israel has a history stretching back to the 1960s of attempts to determine not merely whether Palestinian citizens can formally put a ballot into a box, but **which Palestinian political organisations they are permitted to vote for, which Palestinian political propositions may legitimately be represented, and under what conditions Palestinian citizens may exercise political power through Israeli elections.**

That distinction matters.

> **Having a vote is not identical to having equal political power.**

You can interfere with the second without formally abolishing the first.

And, as several centuries of imperial, colonial, segregationist and racialised government have already demonstrated, states have developed a rather extensive toolkit for doing exactly that.

---

## 🪪 First: what does “Arab” mean here?

Straight out of the gate:

**“Arab” is a heavily racialised political category in this history.**

When Israeli political institutions, parties, electoral statistics and public discourse talk about **“Arab voters”, “the Arab vote” and “Arab parties”**, we should not automatically understand *Arab* as though it means what people elsewhere — including people living throughout Arab countries — might mean when describing Arab ethnicity, culture, nationality or identity.

The category is doing political work.

Much of what is being contested through the language of **“Arab political participation” is Palestinian political participation by Palestinian citizens of Israel**:

- whether Palestinians vote;
- whom Palestinians can vote for;
- whether Palestinian-led parties can appear on the ballot;
- which Palestinian political positions may legitimately be represented;
- whether Palestinian representatives may participate in governing coalitions;
- and ultimately how much Palestinian political power can enter the Israeli state through electoral politics.

That does **not** mean everybody categorised as “Arab” by the Israeli state or electoral system understands themselves identically.

Bedouin citizens complicate it.

Druze citizens complicate it.

Christian Palestinians complicate it.

Different relationships to Palestinian identity, Arab identity and Israeli citizenship complicate it.

Different voting patterns complicate it.

Good.

**People are complicated.**

The racialised administrative category does not become a natural human category merely because a state has become accustomed to counting people with it.

Throughout this node, therefore, **“Arab” describes a category encountered in Israeli electoral institutions, politics, statistics and sources. It is not being adopted uncritically as Polaris’s ontology.**

Where the population can be identified more precisely, this node will be more precise.

> **“Arab” is often the category in which the political struggle is described. Palestinian political power is much closer to what the struggle is actually about.**

---

## 🗳️ Voting is not one switch

There is an important distinction to establish before going any further.

Palestinian citizens of Israel have historically possessed the formal right to vote in Knesset elections.

That fact matters.

It also does not finish the analysis.

An electoral system can interfere with political participation at several different points:

**the voter → the polling station → the ballot → the candidate → the party → parliamentary representation → coalition participation → political power**

Preventing somebody entering a polling station is one mechanism.

Removing the party they intended to vote for is another.

Disqualifying its candidate is another.

Placing surveillance equipment specifically in polling stations used by one racialised population is another.

Defining some political propositions associated with that population as legally incompatible with standing for election is another.

Treating representatives elected by that population as inherently illegitimate coalition partners is another.

These mechanisms are **not legally or practically identical**.

We should not pretend that they are.

But neither should we examine every mechanism in isolation and thereby lose sight of the system produced when they accumulate.

---

## 🕰️ 1965 — before there was even a law

The first major electoral-list disqualification came in **1965**.

The Central Elections Committee disqualified the **Socialists’ List**, whose candidates included members of Al-Ard, an Arab nationalist movement that had previously been outlawed.

There was a fairly remarkable problem:

**Israeli legislation did not expressly give the CEC the authority to disqualify the list on those grounds.**

The case reached the Supreme Court in *Yardor v Central Elections Committee*.

The Court upheld the exclusion.

The Israel Democracy Institute describes *Yardor* as a dramatic decision that permitted a serious infringement of a fundamental political right despite the absence of explicit statutory authority.

That makes 1965 important for more than antiquarian reasons.

The institutional willingness to restrict Palestinian political representation **predates the statutory machinery subsequently used to do it.**

The machinery followed.

---

## ⚖️ 1984–1985 — building the machinery

In 1984 the Central Elections Committee disqualified two very different lists:

- Meir Kahane’s far-right **Kach**;
- the Arab-Jewish **Progressive List for Peace**.

The Supreme Court reversed both exclusions.

The Knesset subsequently amended **Basic Law: The Knesset**, creating Section 7A and expressly authorising electoral disqualification.

Section 7A provides grounds on which a list or candidate may be excluded, including negating Israel’s existence as a **Jewish and democratic state**, incitement to racism, and support for armed struggle against Israel by a hostile state or terrorist organisation.

Those provisions have also been used against Jewish far-right candidates and parties. Kach and Kahane Chai were successfully excluded in later elections.

That belongs in this history too.

But something else subsequently becomes extremely visible:

**Palestinian parties and politicians keep returning to the CEC’s dock.**

---

## 🔁 The revolving door

The post-Section-7A history is striking partly because of its repetition.

According to the Israel Democracy Institute’s compilation of CEC exclusions and Supreme Court decisions, the CEC disqualified:

- **2003 — Balad**;
- **2003 — Azmi Bishara**;
- **2003 — Ahmad Tibi**;
- **2009 — Balad**;
- **2009 — Ra’am-Ta’al**;
- **2013 — Haneen Zoabi**;
- **2015 — Haneen Zoabi**;
- **April 2019 — Ra’am-Balad**;
- **April 2019 — Ofer Cassif**;
- **2020 — Hiba Yazbek**;
- **2022 — Balad**.

The Supreme Court overturned those exclusions.

This distinction is essential.

It would be historically wrong to write:

> Israel continuously prevented all of these politicians and parties from standing.

It did not.

For decades, the Supreme Court repeatedly prevented CEC majorities from doing exactly that.

But that produces its own institutional history:

> **CEC disqualifies → litigation → Supreme Court reviews → disqualification overturned → another election → another attempted disqualification.**

The existence of the judicial brake does not make the repeated attempts disappear.

It documents them.

And it tells us something important about institutions: **a system is not necessarily one mind.** Political actors, electoral bodies, courts, civil-society organisations and voters can push in different directions at the same time.

The Supreme Court’s repeated interventions are therefore not inconvenient evidence to hide because they make the story less tidy.

They are part of the machinery.

---

## 📹 2019 — hello, cameras

And sometimes the intervention happens somewhere else in the electoral system.

During the April 2019 election, Likud supplied cameras to observers deployed in polling stations in Arab communities. Contemporary reporting and subsequent CEC consideration documented the controversy over party-operated cameras specifically in Arab polling places.

Likud said the operation was intended to prevent electoral fraud.

Palestinian political representatives and civil-rights organisations argued that targeted cameras in Arab polling stations amounted to intimidation and ethnic profiling and could suppress turnout.

Before the September rerun election, CEC chairman Justice Hanan Melcer restricted political parties’ ability to operate cameras inside polling stations except in limited circumstances. Netanyahu’s government then backed legislation to permit cameras shortly before the election; the measure failed to obtain the necessary majority.

Again:

**different mechanism.**

Nobody had formally removed the franchise from Palestinian citizens.

But the political history of their franchise cannot sensibly be written while pretending the conditions under which people are asked to exercise it do not matter.

## 🚨 2026 — everybody

And then we arrive back where we started.

On **23 September 2026**, the Central Elections Committee disqualified:

**Ra’am.**

**The Joint List.**

**Ofer Cassif.**

**Sami Abu Shehadeh.**

The Joint List encompasses Hadash/Jabha, Balad/Tajammu’ and Ta’al.

Consequently, the CEC did not merely exclude one particularly controversial Palestinian politician or one particular Palestinian nationalist list.

**It voted to remove every Arab-party list contesting the forthcoming Knesset election.**

Adalah, which represents the affected lists and candidates, appealed the decisions to the Supreme Court and argues that the exclusions lack the evidential basis required by the Court’s own precedents and are discriminatory.

A nine-justice panel is hearing the cases on **1 October 2026**.

So this section presently requires a large:

> **⚠️ JUDICIAL REVIEW IN PROGRESS**

Until the Court rules, the CEC’s decision to disqualify must be distinguished carefully from the final composition of the ballot.

But whatever the Supreme Court decides, **the CEC vote itself belongs to the history.**

## 🧠 The ballot is secret. The boundary around it isn’t.

There is another reason not to measure electoral suppression solely by counting ballots physically prevented from being cast.

**Political control is communicative.**

When a state, governing party or electoral institution repeatedly attempts to prohibit political organisations associated with a marginalised population, the intervention does not communicate only with the people whose names appear on the legal petition.

It communicates with the electorate.

To the targeted population, it can say:

> **You may possess a vote, but there are limits to what we will permit that vote to mean.**

Your preferred representative may be challenged.

Your political organisation may be removed.

Your polling station may receive special surveillance.

Your participation may be represented as threatening.

Your representatives may be described as unacceptable participants in government.

And if a court eventually intervenes and restores the candidate or party to the ballot?

**The communication has still occurred.**

That does not allow us simply to assume that somebody subsequently changed their vote. An actual behavioural effect requires evidence.

But behaviour is not the only possible effect.

Repeated exclusion attempts can impose uncertainty, consume organisational resources, stigmatise political participation, shape expectations about which political identities institutions consider legitimate, and communicate the possibility of further intervention.

And the communication travels further than the targeted group.

---

## 📡 Everybody else can hear you too

The majority population is watching the same process.

If political institutions repeatedly debate whether a minority’s parties should be permitted to stand at all, that helps construct a boundary around what counts as **normal politics**.

That boundary can matter even to somebody whose ballot paper remains completely unchanged.

It communicates distinctions such as:

**these people are legitimate political participants;**

**these people are questionable political participants;**

**these coalitions are ordinary democratic bargaining;**

**these coalitions are beyond the pale;**

**these political demands may be negotiated;**

**these political demands threaten the constitutional order.**

This is one reason electoral exclusion can function as a **psychological technology of political control** as well as a legal one.

The vote remains secret.

**The political environment in which somebody decides how to use it is not.**

---

## 🔄 The legitimacy loop

The mechanism can become recursive.

A marginalised political organisation is described as illegitimate.

↓

Its participation in government becomes politically costly.

↓

Mainstream politicians distance themselves from cooperation with it.

↓

Voters learn that supporting it may produce little access to governing power.

↓

Its exclusion from government begins to appear normal.

↓

That apparent normality can then be cited as evidence that it was never a legitimate governing partner in the first place.

This does not require anybody to alter a ballot under direct instruction.

It changes the **information environment surrounding the ballot**.

And that is precisely why historical systems of racialised political control have so often concerned themselves with much more than the literal franchise.

They regulate legitimacy.

They regulate political association.

They regulate candidacy.

They regulate representation.

They regulate the perceived boundaries of possible government.

**The ballot box sits at the end of an information system.**

---

## 📺 Information outside the electoral system matters too

The information environment does not stop at the border of the electoral authority.

News organisations, foreign governments, political parties, diplomatic institutions and international organisations all help determine which events become salient and which political actors are presented as ordinary sources of authority.

The author’s observation while this node was being written is that **the BBC has, during this same period, platformed Israel’s political leadership speaking authoritatively on another matter without the electoral exclusion forming part of the context presented in that item.**

That is recorded here deliberately as an **author observation and editorial judgement**, not as a claim that this node needs to expand by identifying or reconstructing the specific broadcast.

The wider analytical point does not depend upon that single example.

Editorial omission is information too.

People do not experience each news item in a sealed container. If an audience repeatedly receives **what a political leader thinks** without receiving materially important information about **what political institutions associated with that governing environment are presently doing**, that can affect how authority, legitimacy and normality are perceived.

Sometimes people receive both pieces of information.

Sometimes, crucially:

**people receive one without receiving the other.**

## 🧱 Why the apartheid comparison belongs here

This requires precision because **apartheid is not simply another word for racism**.

In international law, apartheid concerns a system of domination by one racial group over another, systematic oppression and specified inhumane acts undertaken within that system.

Major human-rights organisations including **Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International and the Israeli organisation B’Tselem have concluded, through somewhat different legal and territorial analyses, that Israeli rule over Palestinians constitutes apartheid.** Israel rejects that characterisation.

Human Rights Watch’s analysis is particularly useful here because it expressly addresses the objection that Palestinian citizens of Israel can vote. HRW distinguishes the greater political rights possessed by Palestinian citizens inside the Green Line from the much more severe repression it identifies in the occupied Palestinian territory, while analysing both within an overarching policy it says privileges Jewish Israelis and limits Palestinian political power.

B’Tselem likewise argues that looking only at the vote of Palestinian citizens obscures the differentiated political statuses imposed on Palestinians across the territory under Israeli control.

This node does not need to pretend that an electoral-list disqualification, by itself, establishes the international crime of apartheid.

Nor should it make the lazy claim that:

> Israel = apartheid South Africa.

Different states.

Different histories.

Different laws.

Different racial classifications.

Different electoral machinery.

But **controlling the political representation available to a subordinated racialised population is entirely recognisable within the historical repertoire of apartheid and other racialised systems of government.**

That is why voting matters so much to this analysis.

Apartheid does not require every member of the subordinated population to be treated identically.

It can depend upon the opposite:

**classification.**

Different legal statuses.

Different geographical units.

Different political rights.

Different permitted representatives.

Different degrees of incorporation into the dominant political system.

The fact that Palestinian citizens of Israel possess a franchise which Palestinians elsewhere under Israeli authority do not is therefore not a reason to stop looking.

It is one of the things we need to look **at**.

## 🏺 Which fucking century does this belong in?

There is a danger in discussing Israeli restrictions on Palestinian political participation as though Israel has invented an unprecedented political problem requiring an unprecedented conceptual vocabulary.

It has not.

The mechanisms are different. The histories are different. The populations are different. The legal systems are different.

**But racialised states and empires have been fucking around with the relationship between population, citizenship, franchise, representation and political power for a very long time.**

We know what this family of political technologies looks like because, unfortunately, quite a lot of us have used them.

### 🇬🇧 The British Empire

Britain did not export universal equal democracy around its empire.

Colonial political systems repeatedly distinguished between categories of subject and citizen, attached political participation to property, wealth, education, sex and status, and created representative institutions in which colonised populations possessed radically different access to political power.

Sometimes race appeared explicitly.

Sometimes another qualification performed much of the racial sorting.

Sometimes limited representation was permitted while decisive imperial power remained elsewhere.

The lesson is not:

> **Britain did exactly what Israel is doing.**

It did not.

It is:

> **Britain has extensive historical experience of constructing political systems in which populations living under the same ultimate sovereign power possessed radically unequal routes to political representation.**

We should recognise the technology.

We helped develop quite a lot of it.

### 🇫🇷 The French Empire

France supplies another warning against taking declarations of political equality at face value.

The French Republic proclaimed universal principles while governing colonial populations through distinctions between **citizens and subjects** and, in parts of its empire, exceptional legal regimes including the *indigénat*.

Colonial Algeria made the contradiction particularly conspicuous: territory treated as integral to France contained a large colonised population whose access to equal citizenship and political power was profoundly restricted.

Again:

**formal universalism does not automatically produce equal political membership.**

A state can describe its constitutional principles beautifully while constructing administrative categories that determine who receives their full benefit.

### 🇺🇸 Jim Crow — when the Constitution already says you cannot do it

The United States supplies perhaps the clearest warning against looking only for a statute saying:

> **BLACK PEOPLE CANNOT VOTE.**

The Fifteenth Amendment, ratified in **1870**, prohibited denial or abridgement of the vote on account of race, colour or previous condition of servitude.

Wonderful.

Problem solved.

Except obviously not.

Southern states subsequently developed machinery including literacy tests, poll taxes, grandfather clauses, discriminatory registration practices and intimidation. The US National Archives describes these devices as methods used to disenfranchise Black Americans despite the constitutional guarantee.

The wording of an electoral rule and the population it actually removes from political power are therefore **different empirical questions**.

This is why this node is not going to test the history by asking only:

> **Did Israel formally abolish Palestinian citizens’ franchise?**

That is much too stupid a question for the twenty-first century.

We have centuries of historical evidence explaining why.

### 🇿🇼 Rhodesia — qualifications can have a race

Rhodesia is useful precisely because white-minority political control did not depend solely upon a simple sentence declaring that Africans could never vote.

Franchise arrangements used qualifications involving income, property and education in a society in which colonial racial inequality profoundly shaped who could satisfy them. The 1961 constitutional settlement divided political participation between A and B rolls with different qualifications and political weight; subsequent constitutional changes entrenched white-minority control further.

The mechanism matters:

> **A formally race-neutral qualification operating upon a deliberately racialised distribution of wealth, education, property and institutional power does not magically produce a race-neutral political system.**

Follow the mechanism through to the population.

Then count who possesses power.

### 🇿🇦 South Africa — separate political containers

Apartheid South Africa demonstrates another mechanism:

**you do not necessarily remove everybody from politics in exactly the same way.**

Cape Coloured voters were removed from the common electoral roll during the 1950s. Separate representative arrangements followed and were subsequently abolished.

For Black South Africans, the Bantustan project attempted to locate political citizenship in nominal ethnic “homelands” rather than in South Africa’s common national political system.

The **1983 tricameral constitution** then created separate parliamentary chambers for white, Coloured and Indian South Africans while Black South Africans remained excluded from national parliamentary representation.

This is why apartheid should not be reduced to:

> **some people weren’t allowed to vote.**

Its political technology was more sophisticated.

It involved **classification, territorialisation, differentiated citizenship and controlled forms of representation** that helped determine which populations could exercise political power over the state itself.

### 🇩🇪 Germany — citizenship itself can be the target

Germany supplies the most extreme warning in this constellation and therefore requires particular care rather than casual analogy.

Under the Nazi regime, the **Nuremberg Laws of 1935** stripped Jews of Reich citizenship and political rights before the regime proceeded towards vastly greater persecution and ultimately genocide.

That history should not be flattened into a comparison with contemporary Israeli electoral law.

It demonstrates something narrower and historically important:

> **Political exclusion can operate upstream from the ballot.**

If the state determines who belongs to the political community, citizenship itself becomes a mechanism through which political power can be racially distributed.

That is one reason citizenship law belongs in any serious examination of racialised democracy.

## 🧰 It is a toolkit, not a photocopier

None of these histories is Israel.

Israel is not Jim Crow Mississippi.

It is not Rhodesia.

It is not apartheid South Africa.

It is not French Algeria.

It is certainly not Nazi Germany.

Historical comparison does not require us to squash different societies into one giant cardboard cut-out labelled **RACISM**.

Quite the opposite.

Comparison lets us identify mechanisms:

**classification**

→ **citizenship**

→ **franchise**

→ **electoral qualification**

→ **candidate eligibility**

→ **party eligibility**

→ **territorial representation**

→ **polling conditions**

→ **parliamentary representation**

→ **access to government**

→ **effective political power**

Different systems intervene at different points.

Different systems intervene with different severity.

Different systems provide different avenues of resistance and judicial review.

Different populations retain different political rights.

But by 2026, humanity does not get to pretend that we lack comparative material for understanding what happens when racialised political categories begin determining **whose collective political power may enter the state**.

We have the files.

Britain has files.

France has files.

Germany has files.

America has an absolutely fucking enormous filing cabinet.

Rhodesia left files.

Apartheid South Africa left files.

And international human-rights law did not materialise from nowhere.

Much of it exists because the nineteenth and twentieth centuries provided an exceptionally thorough demonstration of what happens when states organise rights according to race, ethnicity, descent and national origin.

## ⚖️ Political life and physical life are not separate systems

There is a particularly important reason to place the history of Palestinian political exclusion inside the wider legal environment of **2026**.

Because while Israel’s electoral institutions are determining whether Palestinian political organisations may appear on the ballot, Israel has also dramatically expanded the circumstances in which the state may **execute Palestinians**.

These are different laws.

They concern different institutions.

They must not be collapsed into one coordinated mechanism without evidence.

But people experience states as systems.

And the population exposed to one deserves to be compared with the population targeted by the other.

### 🪢 30 March 2026 — the death penalty

On **30 March 2026**, the Knesset passed the *Death Penalty for Terrorists Law* by **62 votes to 48**, with one abstention.

Its operation in the occupied West Bank is explicitly asymmetric.

The law requires the military legal regime to provide that a resident of the occupied territory who intentionally causes a death through an act classified as terrorism receives the **death penalty by default**, unless special circumstances recorded by the military court justify life imprisonment.

But the provision expressly excludes:

**Israeli citizens and Israeli residents.**

The law also removes previous procedural conditions on a military court’s ability to impose death: it does not require a prosecution request or a unanimous judicial panel, and the regional commander may not pardon, mitigate or commute a death sentence imposed under the provision.

That matters because Palestinians in the occupied West Bank are subjected to Israeli military courts while Israeli settlers living in the same occupied territory are ordinarily dealt with through Israel’s civilian legal system.

Human Rights Watch consequently described the legislation as discriminatory and said its wording makes clear that it would apply **primarily, if not exclusively, to Palestinians**.

The asymmetry is therefore not merely something inferred from demographics.

**It is built into the jurisdictional architecture.**

### ⚠️ 11 May 2026 — a special tribunal

On **11 May 2026**, the Knesset passed separate legislation establishing a special military tribunal for Palestinians accused of participating in the attacks of 7 October 2023 and related hostage-taking.

The vote was extraordinary:

**93 in favour.**

**0 against.**

The tribunal can impose the death penalty.

The Knesset’s own description states that the tribunal may deviate from ordinary rules of criminal procedure and evidence, for recorded reasons, where it considers this necessary to clarify the truth and do justice without significantly impairing the fairness of proceedings.

Israeli and Palestinian human-rights organisations including Adalah, the Public Committee Against Torture in Israel and HaMoked argue that the special framework strips defendants of essential fair-trial protections. Their legal analysis argues that a death sentence imposed through the framework could constitute an **arbitrary deprivation of life** under international law.

That conclusion is theirs and should be attributed as such.

But the underlying problem is obvious enough to state directly:

**death is irreversible.**

Any reduction or exceptional alteration in procedural and evidential safeguards becomes particularly consequential when the available punishment cannot subsequently be corrected.

---

## 🚨 “Arbitrary execution” is not just a swear word

There is a reason to use this language carefully.

An **arbitrary deprivation of life** is a legal concept, not merely another way of saying that an execution feels unjust.

Serious fair-trial deficiencies in proceedings leading to a death sentence can engage the prohibition on arbitrary deprivation of life under international human-rights law.

That is why the procedural architecture matters.

A wrongful prison sentence is an enormous injustice.

A wrongful execution cannot subsequently be corrected.

So this node should not casually state that Israel may now execute Palestinians “without evidence”. That would be broader than the enacted provisions establish.

What it **can** state is more precise and, frankly, alarming enough:

- Israel has enacted a West Bank capital-punishment provision which expressly excludes Israeli citizens and residents;
- that provision reduces procedural barriers previously attached to military-court death sentences;
- Israel has separately created a special tribunal with capital jurisdiction over Palestinians accused in relation to 7 October;
- that tribunal has statutory authority to depart from ordinary procedural and evidential rules under specified conditions;
- and rights organisations argue that the resulting fair-trial deficiencies make execution under the framework an arbitrary deprivation of life.

We do not need to make the evidence more dramatic than it is.

**It is already dramatic.**

## 🗳️ Now put the two columns beside each other

### Political power

In September 2026, the Central Elections Committee votes to exclude **every Arab-party list** from the forthcoming national election.

Palestinian citizens retain the franchise.

The Supreme Court can — and historically very often does — protect their ability to exercise that franchise through the representatives they choose.

### Coercive power

In March 2026, the Knesset creates a capital-punishment regime whose West Bank provisions expressly distinguish between residents of the occupied territory and Israeli citizens or residents.

In May 2026, the Knesset creates a special tribunal capable of imposing death sentences upon Palestinians accused in connection with 7 October, amid serious objections concerning its evidentiary and fair-trial safeguards.

These facts do not establish that the laws constitute one coordinated programme.

**We do not need to claim that.**

What they establish is part of the legal environment in which Palestinians encounter the Israeli state in 2026.

And that environment contains a profound asymmetry:

> **The state is simultaneously contesting the boundaries of Palestinian political power and expanding its own coercive power over Palestinian life.**

That is something anybody concerned with Palestinian equality needs to look directly at.

## ❤️ Equal lives require equal law

This sharpens what we mean when we say Palestinian lives matter.

Equality cannot mean merely:

> **Please do not kill Palestinians who have done nothing wrong.**

That is an extraordinarily low floor.

Equality means that when the state accuses somebody of doing something terribly wrong, **their life is still worth rigorous legal protection.**

Evidence still matters.

Counsel still matters.

An independent tribunal still matters.

Appeal still matters.

Protection from torture and coercion still matters.

The reliability and admissibility of evidence still matters.

The prohibition on arbitrary deprivation of life still matters.

Human rights become most important precisely when somebody is unpopular, accused or condemned.

Otherwise they are not rights.

They are rewards for people whom the state presently likes.

## 🕊️ If you care about two states, this is part of the problem

There is a temptation, particularly outside Israel and Palestine, to treat the political rights of Palestinian citizens of Israel as a secondary question.

They are not.

A serious two-state position has to reckon with political agency as well as territorial settlement.

Palestinian citizens of Israel are citizens **now**.

Their votes exist **now**.

Their representatives exist **now**.

Their political rights matter **now**.

And the Israeli political system in which they exercise those rights also makes decisions with profound consequences for Palestinians who do **not** possess an Israeli vote.

Israeli governments make decisions about war, military operations, settlements, occupation, security, diplomacy and negotiations.

That makes Palestinian political participation inside the Israeli electoral system consequential beyond the immediate mechanics of one election.

It does not mean Palestinian citizens vote as a bloc.

They do not.

It does not mean Palestinian parties share one programme.

They do not.

It means something simpler:

> **Palestinian political agency inside the Israeli electoral system is one of the existing democratic routes through which Palestinian perspectives can exercise political power over decisions profoundly affecting Palestinian lives.**

Attempts to diminish that agency therefore matter beyond the immediate question of electoral administration.

---

## 🌍 Humanitarian concern is not enough

There has been significant movement among Western governments over Palestine.

That context matters.

But recognition, diplomatic statements, humanitarian concern and declarations of support for Palestinian self-determination cannot substitute for attention to **political power**.

If a government says Palestinian lives have equal value, the conditions under which Palestinians can exercise political agency are part of that equality.

If it supports two states, political developments capable of making coexistence, representation and negotiated government harder are relevant to that position.

If it supports democratic resolution of political conflict, the continued availability of democratic routes for Palestinian political participation is directly relevant.

There is a very obvious contradiction in spending decades telling a population:

> **Use politics. Use elections. Use institutions.**

while treating repeated attempts to close or condition those routes as somebody else’s administrative problem.

Palestinian lives are not only humanitarian lives.

They are **political lives**.

People have opinions.

They have movements.

They have disagreements.

They have representatives.

They have demands upon governments.

They have the right to organise.

They have the right to be extremely inconvenient.

They have the right to participate in determining the political systems exercising power over them.

That is why this history is not ultimately a history about pieces of paper going into boxes.

It is a history about **whether political power follows them in.**

## 📅 2026 in one frame

It is worth preserving the chronology without pretending chronology itself proves coordination:

- **30 March 2026:** the Knesset passes the *Death Penalty for Terrorists Law*, including an occupied-West-Bank military-court provision that expressly excludes Israeli citizens and residents.
- **11 May 2026:** the Knesset passes the special October 7 tribunal law by 93–0, creating a tribunal with capital jurisdiction and exceptional procedural and evidential provisions.
- **23 September 2026:** the Central Elections Committee votes to disqualify Ra’am, the Joint List, Ofer Cassif and Sami Abu Shehadeh — every Arab-party list contesting the election and two individual candidates.
- **1 October 2026:** a nine-justice Supreme Court panel hears the electoral-disqualification cases.
- **27 October 2026:** scheduled Knesset election.

These are separate state actions.

They have different sponsors, different legal bases, different institutional routes and different immediate purposes.

The responsible analytical move is therefore **not** to invent a secret causal chain.

It is to refuse to destroy the context by placing every action in a separate conceptual cupboard.

Palestinian political equality and Palestinian physical equality are being tested **inside the same legal and political environment at the same historical moment**.

That is observable without pretending to know anybody’s secret plan.

When a marginalised population’s representatives are described as intrinsically dangerous to the state.

When peaceful political participation itself begins acquiring conditions.

When one population becomes overwhelmingly exposed to forms of state coercion from which another is protected.

When people are displaced and somebody else decides what happens to the land.

When enormous civilian suffering is explained as the regrettable but necessary consequence of somebody else’s political project.

I recognise those things because **human beings have done them before**.

My histories contain some of them.

Sometimes my histories contain the people they were done **to**.

Sometimes they contain the states and empires that did them.

That is not a source of moral purity.

It is a reason to fucking look.

And so, at this point, I need something more substantial than:

> **That word is inappropriate.**

Why?

If you think **apartheid** is the wrong analytical or legal framework, show me robustly why the documented systems of differentiated citizenship, movement, territorial control, political participation and legal status do not satisfy the definition you think should govern.

If you think **colonisation** is the wrong historical framework, tell me precisely what distinguishes the settlement, displacement, territorial and demographic processes being observed from the colonial mechanisms historians recognise elsewhere.

If you think **genocide** is the wrong legal description, engage seriously with the elements of the Genocide Convention: protected group, prohibited acts and, critically, the requirement of specific intent. Tell me what the evidence establishes, what it does not establish, and why.

Those are contestable legal and historical questions.

Good.

**Contest them.**

But do not tell me that comparison itself is offensive.

Do not tell me that recognising a mechanism from another history means claiming that two histories are identical.

And particularly do not tell me that my Jewish history requires me **not to look**.

It requires the opposite.

I am perfectly capable of hearing:

> **This evidence does not establish that legal threshold.**

I am perfectly capable of hearing:

> **This historical analogy breaks down here.**

I am perfectly capable of hearing:

> **You have identified a real mechanism, but you have classified it incorrectly.**

Those are arguments.

They can be tested.

They can be sourced.

They can change my mind.

What I need, particularly now, is for anybody insisting that these words are categorically impermissible to do the intellectual work of explaining **why**.

Because I am watching mechanisms that I recognise from history.

And history is not useful if its only permitted function is telling us, afterwards, that something terrible happened.

The point of remembering is also to recognise the machinery **while somebody still has the opportunity to stop it.**

---

## 🧮 Do not count only confiscated ballots

So the diagnostic question at the end of this node is not:

> **Were “Arab” citizens technically allowed to put a ballot in a box?**

It is a series of questions:

- Who possesses the franchise?
- Who does not?
- Who determines citizenship and residency?
- Which parties can register?
- Which parties can stand?
- Which candidates can stand?
- Which political propositions are permissible?
- Who repeatedly has to litigate for the right to remain on the ballot?
- Which polling places receive exceptional scrutiny or surveillance?
- Whose turnout is represented as politically threatening?
- Whose representatives are considered legitimate coalition partners?
- Which population is governed through civilian law?
- Which population is governed through military law?
- Which population is exposed to the death penalty under provisions from which another population is expressly excluded?
- Which courts can intervene?
- Which institutions actually do intervene?
- And after all of that machinery has operated, **how much effective political power comes out the other end?**

The answer may vary across time, territory, population and institution.

That variation is not noise.

**It is the map.**

---

## 🌫️ And now?

As of **1 October 2026**, we are watching the electoral machinery operate in real time.

The Central Elections Committee has made its decision.

Every Arab-party list contesting the election was disqualified.

The Supreme Court is reviewing those decisions.

The election is scheduled for **27 October**.

So we do not yet write the ending.

We document the state of the machine:

**political actors petitioned → CEC excluded → Palestinian representatives appealed → Supreme Court convened.**

And then we update when the next observable event occurs.

Because this history teaches something useful far beyond Israel and Palestine:

> **Do not ask only whether somebody technically possesses a vote.**
>
> **Follow the vote all the way through the system and find out how much political power comes out the other end.**

And when a racialised minority’s route through that system is repeatedly challenged, surveilled, conditioned or delegitimised, do not wait for somebody to confiscate the ballot paper before deciding that the history of voting has become relevant.

We know this machinery.

**Which fucking century does it belong in?**

Unfortunately, history has already answered that question several times.

That is precisely why finding it in **2026** matters.
