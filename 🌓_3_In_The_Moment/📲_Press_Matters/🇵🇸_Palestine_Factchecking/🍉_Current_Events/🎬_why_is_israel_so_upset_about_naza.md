# 🎬 Why Is Israel So Upset About *NAZA*?  
**First created:** 2026-09-15 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-18  
*An evidence-heavy explainer on* NAZA, *AI-assisted targeting, civilian-harm estimation, remote-war trauma, genocide prevention, whistleblowing, and the rules we may all eventually have to live under.*  

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

*NAZA* has produced an unusually fierce response in Israel. The 2026 documentary by Israeli filmmakers **Yuval Abraham and Rachel Szor** is based on testimony from 24 anonymised Israeli military and intelligence insiders and on a longer body of investigative reporting about the systems used to identify, locate and attack targets in Gaza. It was produced by the Guardian and James Wilson of JW Films, executive-produced by **Jonathan Glazer**, premiered in competition at the Venice International Film Festival, and won the Special Jury Prize.

The political response has included denunciations from senior Israeli officials, accusations of "blood libel", discussion of legal action, and threats to the filmmakers' citizenship. That response is itself newsworthy. It is not, however, the whole subject of this node.

This node proceeds from the position that **there is a credible and serious risk of genocide in Gaza and that the question is properly before the International Court of Justice (ICJ)**. That is not the same thing as claiming that the ICJ has already made a final finding that Israel has committed genocide: it has not. In January 2024 the Court found that at least some of the rights claimed by South Africa under the Genocide Convention were plausible, found a real and imminent risk of irreparable prejudice to those rights, and imposed binding provisional measures. The merits remain a separate question.

Readers who reject the genocide characterisation do not need to accept it before continuing.

That is important because *NAZA* is not principally a film asking an audience to decide the Genocide Convention case. It is a film about a **method of warfare**: how intelligence becomes a target; how a target becomes a location; how civilian presence is estimated; how expected civilian casualties are represented; how humans interact with machine-generated recommendations; and how lethal decisions are distributed across an institution.

Those questions remain if the eventual legal answer is *not genocide*.

They also extend far beyond Israel. The United States has spent years developing AI-assisted intelligence and targeting systems of its own. American remotely piloted aircraft personnel have been studied for PTSD, sleep disturbance, psychological distress, burnout and moral injury for more than a decade. Russia, Ukraine and Iran operate increasingly sophisticated drone systems. Commercial cloud and AI infrastructure is becoming entangled with national-security systems. The technological direction is not going away because the argument about Gaza is uncomfortable.

The larger question is therefore not simply whether one trusts Israel.

It is:

> **What evidence, safeguards, human authority and accountability would we require before accepting machine-assisted lethal targeting by Israel, the United States, Britain, Ukraine, Russia, Iran --- or anybody else?**

## ✨ Key Features

- Separates the **film**, the **underlying investigations**, and the **legal characterisation of the war**.
- Maps Lavender, Gospel/Habsora, *Where's Daddy?*, civilian-presence estimation, NAZA/collateral-damage assessment and operational planning as distinct functions rather than one mysterious "AI weapon".
- Compares publicly documented Israeli systems with the United States' **Project Maven** without claiming technical equivalence.
- Treats "human in the loop" as a governance question: did the human have enough information, time, authority and cognitive capacity to disagree?
- Explains why a pre-strike civilian-casualty estimate matters to **foreseeability and proportionality** without inventing a universal lawful casualty number.
- Places *NAZA* inside a much older documentary and research history of remote warfare, including *National Bird* and US Air Force mental-health studies.
- Treats PTSD as only one possible outcome alongside distress, burnout, sleep disruption and moral injury.
- Gives the historical concept of **blood libel** its specificity rather than using it as a synonym for any deeply hostile allegation concerning Jews or Israel.
- Takes the strongest serious argument against the genocide characterisation seriously while asking what that argument leaves unresolved.
- Centres the Genocide Convention's **preventive** function as well as the eventual adjudication of responsibility.
- Treats Israeli whistleblowers as alarms requiring investigation, not as automatically infallible witnesses or disposable traitors.
- Ends with a reciprocity test: **would we accept these rules if the system belonged to an adversary targeting us?**

## 🎬 What Is *NAZA*?

The Venice Biennale described *NAZA* as a documentary by Yuval Abraham and Rachel Szor, two of the four co-directors of the Oscar-winning *No Other Land*. It was produced by the Guardian and James Wilson of JW Films and executive-produced by Jonathan Glazer. Wilson and Glazer previously worked together on *The Zone of Interest*.

The Guardian's reporting describes the film as the product of nearly three years of work and interviews with **24 Israeli military and intelligence insiders**. Their identities are concealed. The film was shot partly on Tel Aviv rooftops and is concerned with systems through which civilian deaths could be anticipated, processed and authorised within military operations.

The title itself matters. "NAZA" is described by the filmmakers and reporting around the film as an Israeli military/intelligence term for anticipated collateral damage --- expected civilian casualties associated with a proposed strike.

The film therefore starts in a place that is analytically more difficult than "the machine did not know".

Its question is closer to:

> **What if the system was built to know?**

What if intelligence could identify a person, locate a phone, estimate the occupancy of a building, calculate expected civilian harm and put that information before a human decision-maker --- and the strike could proceed anyway?

That is not yet a legal conclusion. It is an evidentiary and governance question.

### 🎥 Why Jonathan Glazer Belongs In The Context

Glazer's involvement should not be treated as celebrity decoration. *The Zone of Interest* was concerned with bureaucratic normality, domestic life and organised mass violence existing in physical and psychological proximity. That does **not** make Gaza identical to the Holocaust, and *NAZA* should not be flattened into *The Zone of Interest II*.

The continuity is thematic rather than equivalential: what does organised violence look like from inside systems whose participants can continue to experience themselves as ordinary people doing ordinary work?

That is also why the film's Tel Aviv setting matters. Gaza is geographically close enough for ordinary life and extraordinary destruction to occupy neighbouring realities.

## 🕰️ *NAZA* Is Not A Bolt From The Blue

The central allegations did not first materialise at Venice in September 2026.

In December 2023, reporting about **The Gospel/Habsora** examined algorithmic target generation and the IDF's dramatically increased capacity to produce targets.

On 3 April 2024, +972 Magazine and Local Call published Yuval Abraham's investigation into **Lavender**, based on six Israeli intelligence officers who said they had first-hand involvement in the system during the war. The investigation alleged that Lavender had been used to mark tens of thousands of Palestinians as suspected members of Hamas or Palestinian Islamic Jihad, that human scrutiny could be extremely abbreviated, and that tracking systems including *Where's Daddy?* were used to identify moments when designated targets were at locations including family homes.

The IDF disputed important parts of that account. It said its information systems were auxiliary tools, that no AI system itself determined that a person was a terrorist, and that analysts were required independently to verify that a target satisfied relevant legal requirements.

In September 2024, **Human Rights Watch** published a technical and legal assessment of four digital systems used by the Israeli military in Gaza: an evacuation-monitoring tool, Gospel, Lavender and *Where's Daddy?*. HRW could not verify every reported technical detail or determine precisely how extensively the systems were combined with other intelligence. It nevertheless concluded that the tools raised serious risks because of faulty data, inexact approximations, machine-learning opacity and the danger of automation bias.

By 2026, *NAZA* brought a much larger body of Israeli insider testimony into documentary form.

So a useful chronology is:

``` text

        ↓
APRIL 2024
Lavender / Where's Daddy? investigation

        ↓

        ↓
2024–2026

        ↓
SEPTEMBER 2026

```

A legitimate question can be asked about the timing of any film, publication or election.

That is not the same question as:

> **When were the underlying concerns first raised?**

## 🧠 AI Warfare For People Who Do Not Work In AI

"AI targeting" can sound as though a computer looks at Gaza, chooses a human being and presses a fire button.

That is not a useful description of most of the systems being discussed.

Modern militaries have an information problem. They can collect more material than human analysts can inspect manually:

``` text
drone video
satellite imagery
signals intelligence
intercepted communications
mobile-device data
radar
human intelligence
databases
movement patterns
previous targeting information
battlefield reports
imagery recognition
```

Machine-learning and other computational systems can help classify, search, prioritise and cross-reference that information.

That can be extremely useful. It can also change the **scale and tempo** of lethal decision-making.

The governance question therefore begins before any weapon is fired:

> **What does the machine make visible, what does it hide, what does it classify as suspicious, how does it represent uncertainty, and how many decisions is a human now expected to process?**

## 🪻 Lavender --- Who?

The April 2024 +972/Local Call investigation described Lavender as a machine-learning system used to assign people in Gaza scores associated with suspected membership of Hamas or Palestinian Islamic Jihad.

According to six intelligence sources interviewed for the investigation, Lavender played a major role in the early months of the war and was used to mark as many as **37,000** people as potential targets. Sources said a sample had been assessed at approximately **90% accuracy** before broad operational reliance.

One source described spending around **20 seconds** reviewing a target, largely to establish whether the person was male, and described the human role as adding little beyond a stamp of approval.

Those are allegations from anonymous Israeli intelligence sources, not independently observable facts.

The IDF disputes the characterisation. It has said that no AI system itself determines that a person is a terrorist, that databases and information systems help analysts cross-reference intelligence, and that analysts are required independently to verify that a proposed target meets relevant legal definitions.

That dispute gives us a testable governance question:

> **What did "independently verify" mean operationally?**

A system can contain a human and still provide very little meaningful human judgement.

``` text
MACHINE RECOMMENDATION
        ↓
substantive human investigation
```

is not the same process as:

``` text
MACHINE RECOMMENDATION
        ↓
brief human confirmation
```

even if both can truthfully be described as having a human "in the loop".

The deeper issue is that a **statistical classification is not the same thing as a lawful-target determination**.

A model can tell an analyst that a person resembles the data pattern associated with a category. International humanitarian law asks a different question: may this person lawfully be attacked in these circumstances?

## 📖 Gospel --- What?

Human Rights Watch describes **The Gospel/Habsora** as an algorithmic system that processes surveillance information to generate lists of structures or other objects for potential attack.

Public reporting has associated Gospel with a dramatic increase in target-production capacity. Former IDF chief Aviv Kohavi described a system capable during the 2021 Gaza conflict of generating roughly **100 targets per day**, compared with a much smaller human-only target-production rate previously.

That difference matters.

The obvious engineering interpretation is:

> **the machine solved a bottleneck.**

The governance interpretation is:

> **what was the bottleneck doing besides slowing things down?**

Human analysis consumes time because humans are slow. Sometimes that is inefficiency.

Sometimes it is verification.

Sometimes it is uncertainty.

Sometimes it is disagreement.

Sometimes it is the time required to notice that a category does not fit a human reality.

Target throughput is therefore not merely a productivity metric when the product is a proposed lethal action.

---

## 🏠 *Where's Daddy?* --- Where?

*Where's Daddy?* is analytically different again.

It is not principally reported as deciding **who** is a target. It is reported as helping determine **where an already-designated target is**.

Human Rights Watch said it was able to confirm that the Israeli military uses a tool with the reported functionality of notifying operators when a designated person enters a particular location, while being unable to verify other specific technical details.

The +972/Local Call investigation alleged that targets were often tracked to family homes because they were easier to locate there. The IDF has disputed allegations that it operated a policy of routinely killing tens of thousands of people in their homes.

The technical problem is brutally simple:

> **A phone is not a person.**

Phones change hands. SIM cards change. Devices are lost. Networks fail. Cell-site location and GPS have different limitations. Gaza's telecommunications infrastructure was heavily damaged.

HRW concluded that mobile-phone location information **alone** is not sufficiently precise to establish that a particular human being is at a particular place at a particular time for a precision attack.

That creates a second proxy layer:

``` text
LAVENDER

behaviour / associations / data
              ↓
inference about PERSON

WHERE'S DADDY?

device / location data
              ↓
inference about PERSON'S LOCATION
```

Stacking proxies does not remove uncertainty.

It can merely make uncertainty harder to see.

## 🗺️ Who Else Is There?

Civilian presence is not merely something discovered after an attack.

It can itself become an input to the targeting system.

HRW examined an Israeli evacuation-monitoring tool using mobile-phone location data to estimate population movement. Journalists had previously described Israeli command-centre screens dividing Gaza into **620 blocks**, coloured according to estimated evacuation levels.

In May 2024 HRW also discovered what appeared to be operational information accidentally exposed in source code associated with an Israeli military evacuation website. The data included Gaza's 620 blocks, population information, civilian movement, Israeli military presence, cumulative attacks and other information. HRW found some of the military-presence data consistent with the state of the Israeli incursion around mid-November 2023, while stating that it could not definitively establish the data's origin and use.

This is an unusually important piece of evidence because it is not solely whistleblower testimony.

It is a technical artefact.

And it creates another question:

> **How reliable does an estimate of civilian absence need to be before a military acts as though civilians are absent?**

## 🧮 NAZA --- How Many Civilians Are Expected To Die?

The most disturbing part of the architecture may also be the least technologically glamorous.

Before a strike, a military may estimate the number of civilians expected to be killed or injured.

That is not inherently sinister. A military **should** think about civilian harm before attacking.

The question is what happens next.

The +972/Local Call sources alleged that during parts of the early war broad collateral-damage tolerances were authorised for categories of targets, including figures of roughly 15 or 20 civilians for some junior militants and much higher numbers for senior targets. The IDF disputes the allegation that it used categorical "kill quotas" and says proportionality is assessed individually.

*NAZA* reportedly includes testimony concerning a case in which an expected civilian-death figure of **500** was approved for a Hamas target.

That claim requires careful verification. It is extraordinary precisely because it is extraordinary.

But the conceptual point exists regardless of the ultimate number:

> **If civilian deaths were estimated before the strike, they were foreseeable enough to be represented in the decision.**

A collateral-damage estimate does **not** by itself establish that an attack was unlawful.

It does, however, create a record of what the decision-maker was being told might happen.

That makes the following questions unavoidable:

- How was the estimate generated?
- What data fed it?
- How current was that data?
- Did the model express uncertainty?
- How did predicted casualties compare with actual casualties?
- What military advantage was anticipated?
- What alternatives were available?
- Who authorised the attack?
- What happened when predictions were repeatedly wrong?
- Was the model recalibrated?
- Did the rules change?

## 🧩 Proxy Upon Proxy --- Epistemic Compression

A machine-assisted targeting system can turn a messy world into a sequence of clean-looking outputs.

Polaris calls the danger here **epistemic compression**.

``` text
behaviour
    ↓
inferred affiliation

device
    ↓
inferred person

device location
    ↓
inferred human location

population model
    ↓
inferred civilian presence

modelled occupancy
    ↓
predicted casualties

predicted casualties
    ↓
proportionality input
```

Every arrow contains assumptions and uncertainty.

But the interface at the end may display:

`TARGET CONFIDENCE: HIGH`

`LOCATION: CONFIRMED`

`NAZA: 17`

The world has not necessarily become more certain.

The uncertainty has become **less visible**.

That is a governance problem because humans are vulnerable to automation bias: the tendency to over-trust outputs that appear systematic, numerical and machine-generated.

## ⚙️ Twenty Seconds Is Not A Conscience

"Human in the loop" is not a magic phrase.

Meaningful human control requires more than the physical existence of a human somewhere in the chain.

A human needs:

``` text
sufficient INFORMATION
        +
sufficient TIME
        +
authority to CHALLENGE
        +
authority to REJECT
        +
capacity to understand UNCERTAINTY
        +
institutional protection for ESCALATION
        +
cognitive capacity to exercise JUDGEMENT
```

If those conditions are absent, the human may become less a decision-maker than a ceremonial confirmation mechanism.

This is where the language of the **human bottleneck** deserves scrutiny.

In engineering, bottlenecks are usually things to remove.

In lethal decision-making, some bottlenecks may be safety features.

> **Sometimes the thing slowing the kill chain down is not an engineering defect. Sometimes the friction is the safeguard.**

## ⚖️ Proportionality Is Not A Body-Count Exchange Rate

International humanitarian law does not provide a universal numerical table stating that one combatant permits the death of five civilians, ten civilians or fifty civilians.

The proportionality rule concerns whether expected incidental civilian harm would be **excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated**.

That means a casualty estimate is neither automatically lawful nor automatically unlawful.

The legal and operational questions include:

- Was the target a lawful military objective?
- What concrete and direct military advantage was expected?
- What civilian harm was expected?
- How reliable was that expectation?
- Were feasible precautions taken?
- Was another timing, weapon or method available that would reduce civilian harm?
- Was the expected civilian harm excessive relative to the anticipated military advantage?

This is why the debate cannot responsibly collapse into:

> "Other wars killed civilians too."

Civilian casualties occur in war. That fact does not answer whether **this attack**, using **this information**, against **this target**, with **this expected harm**, complied with the applicable rules.

Nor does a very high civilian death toll automatically establish genocide.

Different legal questions require different evidence.

## 🔍 Audit The Claim

The IDF has repeatedly said that it complies with international law and takes extensive precautions to minimise civilian harm.

Good.

That is an auditable proposition.

A serious audit could sample a sufficiently large and representative set of strikes and preserve the decision chain:

``` text
TARGET IDENTIFIED
        ↓
evidence supporting target status

CIVILIAN PRESENCE ESTIMATED
        ↓
source and freshness of data

COLLATERAL DAMAGE ESTIMATED
        ↓
predicted deaths / injuries + uncertainty

MILITARY ADVANTAGE
        ↓
recorded justification

HUMAN REVIEW
        ↓
who reviewed?
what did they see?
how long?
what objections?

WEAPON SELECTED
        ↓
munition + alternatives considered

STRIKE
        ↓

BATTLE-DAMAGE ASSESSMENT
        ↓
was target present?
was target killed?
who else was harmed?

AUDIT
        ↓
prediction versus outcome

FEEDBACK
        ↓
did doctrine / model / threshold change?
```

If such an audit demonstrated that targets were individually verified, civilian estimates were reliable, analysts frequently rejected machine recommendations, alternatives were genuinely considered, expected harm was repeatedly reduced at operational cost, and procedures were corrected when predictions failed, **that evidence would matter**.

That is the point of an audit.

Accountability is not a process designed so that Israel can only lose.

## 🔬 What Would Falsify The Allegations?

A journalist-facing investigation should be capable of discovering that its starting hypothesis was wrong.

For the principal *NAZA*/Lavender allegations, useful records could include:

- contemporaneous target files;
- model-validation reports;
- Lavender threshold and rejection-rate records;
- documented false-positive rates;
- analyst review requirements;
- records of analyst disagreement;
- location-confirmation procedures;
- civilian-presence methodology;
- collateral-damage estimates;
- proportionality assessments;
- munition-selection records;
- battle-damage assessments;
- civilian-casualty investigations;
- model recalibration;
- command orders;
- legal reviews;
- disciplinary or criminal referrals;
- evidence of changes after identified failures.

Whistleblower testimony can point investigators towards those records.

It should not replace them where the records can be obtained.

## 🇺🇸 Israel Did Not Invent The Problem

One danger in discussing Gaza is accidentally producing an Israeli exceptionalism in which technologically accelerated warfare appears to have been invented uniquely for Palestinians.

It was not.

The United States has spent decades developing persistent surveillance, remotely piloted aircraft, computer vision, geospatial intelligence and systems intended to reduce the time between sensing something and acting on it.

That history does not excuse Israeli conduct.

It gives us a comparator.

And the comparator is useful because the United States openly describes the objective as **speed and scale**.

---

## 🛰️ Project Maven --- The American Comparator

The US National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency describes **NGA Maven** as the Pentagon's flagship AI project, established in 2017 and now integrated into military analytic workflows.

Its computer-vision systems automatically detect, identify, characterise, extract and attribute features and objects in imagery and video. NGA describes Maven as producing GEOINT "at speed and scale".

In 2025 NGA director Vice Admiral Frank Whitworth told Congress that Maven had reduced some targeting-workflow timelines by as much as **80%**, with one exercise reducing intelligence-to-engagement timelines from **hours to minutes**.

That is technologically impressive.

It also gives us exactly the right question:

> **Which parts of those hours were computational waste, and which parts were humans thinking?**

The Israeli and American systems should not be treated as technically equivalent.

Public information suggests different functions, datasets, doctrines and organisational structures.

But they inhabit the same strategic trajectory:

``` text
MORE SENSORS
      ↓
MORE DATA
      ↓
MACHINE CLASSIFICATION
      ↓
FASTER PRIORITISATION
      ↓
MORE TARGETING CAPACITY
      ↓
SHORTER HUMAN DECISION CYCLES
```

The problem is not that speed is inherently bad.

The problem is that **judgement also takes time**.

## 🧪 The Palestine Laboratory Question

Antony Loewenstein's *The Palestine Laboratory* argues that Israeli surveillance, security and military technologies have been developed and refined through the occupation of Palestinians and then exported internationally, with operational experience functioning as part of their commercial value.

That is the book's thesis. It should be treated as a thesis to investigate rather than a magic sentence that resolves every technology question.

But the underlying relationship between occupation, operational experience and export is a serious research subject. Israeli defence technology is internationally significant; Israeli systems have been marketed using operational experience; Western governments procure Israeli technology; US and European technology flows into Israeli systems; and military, intelligence and commercial relationships run in more than one direction.

The better map is therefore not:

``` text
ISRAEL
  ↓
invents technology on Palestinians
  ↓
WEST buys it
```

It is closer to:

``` text
WESTERN / GLOBAL SECURITY DEMAND
              ↕
US / EUROPEAN MILITARIES + INDUSTRY
              ↕
ISRAELI DEFENCE ESTABLISHMENT
              ↕
ISRAELI DEFENCE-TECH INDUSTRY
              ↕
OCCUPIED PALESTINIAN TERRITORY
              ↓
OPERATIONAL EXPERIENCE / ITERATION
              ↓
PRODUCTS / DOCTRINE / EXPERTISE
              ↓
INTERNATIONAL CUSTOMERS
              ↺
```

Every arrow requires evidence.

But tracing the arrows is more useful than pretending that responsibility belongs neatly to one national box.

## ☁️ Nimbus Is Infrastructure, Not Necessarily A Weapon

Project Nimbus is a large Israeli government cloud-computing project involving Google Cloud and Amazon Web Services.

Google's public description says the agreement provides cloud services across Israeli government entities and was designed around public-sector transformation. Reporting and Israeli government material have also established defence-establishment involvement. Google has said Nimbus is not directed at highly sensitive classified workloads relevant to weapons or intelligence services.

The important discipline is:

> **Do not infer the technical architecture from the commercial contract. Map it.**

There is no evidentiary basis for simply writing:

`Nimbus → Lavender → strike`

unless evidence establishes that relationship.

But contemporary algorithmic warfare requires:

``` text
storage
compute
databases
authentication
communications
model execution
cross-referencing
redundancy
interfaces
dissemination
```

So commercial infrastructure can become relevant to the **kill-chain question** without necessarily being part of the verified kill chain.

That creates a broader governance problem for hyperscale providers: what responsibility attaches to general-purpose infrastructure when its customer is a military and the provider may not have complete visibility into the operational significance of every workload?

The uncomfortable technological question is what happens when **the companies are small cogs too**.

## 🌍 The Reciprocity Test

The most useful rule for evaluating emerging military technology may be to imagine that the system belongs to somebody who does not think we are the good guys.

Suppose Iran develops a system that:

- analyses intercepted communications and social networks;
- scores suspected Israeli military personnel;
- tracks their devices;
- alerts an operator when they return home;
- estimates how many family members are present;
- allows a short human confirmation;
- calculates anticipated civilian casualties;
- and authorises a strike.

What safeguards would Israel want Iran required to observe?

Accuracy?

Meaningful human verification?

Current location information?

Civilian-presence verification?

A demanding proportionality assessment?

Limits on attacks at family homes?

Auditable records?

Accountability for model error?

Those are strong candidates for safeguards Israel should be willing to observe too.

The same test applies to the United States, Britain, Russia, Ukraine and everybody else.

International humanitarian law is not supposed to be a collection of restraints we invent for our enemies.

It is supposed to constrain belligerents.

## 🛰️ The Warning Accompanied The Technology

Remote warfare also carries a human history that is easy to lose when attention remains fixed on hardware.

The warning that remote warfare could psychologically affect its operators did not arrive in 2026.

US military and clinical research has examined the issue for years.

A 2014 study of **1,084 US Air Force drone operators** found that **4.34%** met DSM-IV PTSD symptom criteria. Operators who had spent 25 months or more on station and those working 51 hours or more per week were at greater risk. Among those who did not meet the PTSD threshold, **32% reported sleep problems**.

A 2019 study of 715 remotely piloted aircraft personnel who had experienced at least one real-time traumatic event found **6.15%** met DSM-5 PTSD symptom criteria. Particularly relevant here, witnessing civilian bystanders being killed and feeling shared responsibility for civilian injury or death were significant predictors.

A systematic review published in September 2026 found probable PTSD prevalence across the literature of approximately **3.3--6.7%**, broadly comparable with other military populations, while also identifying operational, organisational and moral stressors.

That is important because it prevents exaggeration.

The evidence does **not** show that every drone operator develops PTSD.

It shows that:

> **physical remoteness from the battlefield does not eliminate psychologically consequential combat exposure.**

That gives *NAZA* a documentary predecessor.

Not because American and Israeli programmes are identical.

Because documentary filmmakers have already had to ask:

> **What happens to the human beings inside technologically mediated killing systems?**

A fixation on PTSD prevalence can hide the rest of the psychological burden.

- PTSD symptoms;
- subthreshold trauma symptoms;
- psychological distress;
- depression;
- anxiety;
- burnout;
- emotional exhaustion;
- sleep disturbance;
- occupational stress;
- moral injury.

The 2026 systematic review is particularly useful because it places organisational stressors beside traumatic exposure rather than treating the operator as a disembodied brain reacting only to violent imagery.

Long hours, unstable shifts, understaffing, role conflict and sleep disruption can matter.

So can what the person believes they participated in.

## 🪞 Commission, Omission And Betrayal

The moral-injury literature helps refine the question further.

Potentially morally injurious experiences can include:

**commission** --- *I did something that violated my values.*

**omission** --- *I failed, or was unable, to prevent something I believed I should prevent.*

**betrayal** --- *an institution or authority I trusted violated what I believed it stood for.*

A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis found associations between potentially morally injurious experiences and PTSD, depression and suicidality, while cautioning that the evidence base was still limited.

This does not mean that every person who witnesses civilian harm develops moral injury.

It does mean that **inability to intervene**, perceived responsibility and institutional betrayal are recognised psychological questions rather than inventions designed specifically for Gaza.

That matters for an architecture in which a person may simultaneously be told:

> **Your role is technical.**

and:

> **A human remained responsible for the decision.**

## 🩺 Israeli Force Resilience Is Part Of The Story

The welfare of Israeli military personnel should not be treated as a rhetorical afterthought.

Some gives somebody enough time to experience doubt.

## 🩸 What Is A Blood Libel?

The historic **blood libel** was a specific antisemitic fabrication: the false accusation that Jews murdered Christians, often children, for ritual purposes, including allegations involving blood and Passover.

Medieval England is central to its history. The death of William of Norwich in the twelfth century became one of the foundational European ritual-murder accusations. Variants spread across Europe and contributed to persecution, executions and pogroms.

The falsity matters.

A blood libel is not simply:

> **an allegation that Jews killed non-Jews.**

It is an antisemitic fabricated accusation with a particular historical genealogy.

The term is also sometimes used metaphorically for an allegedly monstrous false accusation against Jews or Israel. That wider usage exists, but it can create conceptual problems if the metaphor itself is allowed to decide whether a contemporary factual allegation is false.

## 🪦 Why The Genocide Accusation Can Feel Profoundly Antisemitic

That does not mean Jewish reaction to the allegation is incomprehensible.

For Jews whose historical memory includes ritual-murder accusations, pogroms, the Holocaust and the understanding of Israel as a refuge from persecution, an accusation that the Jewish state is intentionally destroying another people can feel existentially hostile.

In September 2026 historian Simon Schama told a Holocaust Educational Trust dinner that the Gaza genocide claim was a **"blood libel"**, framing the allegation through the experience of Jews --- victims of the Holocaust --- being accused of perpetrating genocide.

That reaction deserves to be understood rather than sneered at.

But two propositions remain distinct:

**A.** *This allegation lands on an ancient and terrifying Jewish historical nerve.*

**B.** *The contemporary allegations about Israeli conduct are fabricated.*

A does not establish B.

The second proposition requires evidence.

## ⚖️ The Serious Case Against The Genocide Characterisation

The strongest legal argument against concluding that genocide has been established is not:

> **Nothing terrible happened.**

It is principally an argument about **specific intent**.

Article II of the Genocide Convention concerns specified acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group **as such**.

Israel's position before the ICJ has included the arguments that its military objective is the defeat of Hamas and protection of Israelis rather than destruction of Palestinians as a group; that civilian casualties in urban warfare can occur without genocidal intent; that Israeli precautions and humanitarian measures militate against an inference of genocidal intent; and that inflammatory statements cited by South Africa do not represent government policy.

Those are legally intelligible arguments.

The ICJ's January 2024 provisional-measures order did **not** decide that Israel had committed genocide. The Court explicitly said its order did not prejudge the merits.

A person can therefore coherently say:

> *I believe there may have been unlawful attacks, grave civilian harm and serious failures, but I do not believe the evidence establishes the specific intent required for genocide.*

That is a position to engage with, not a position to caricature.

## ⏳ The Evidentiary Clock Did Not Stop

The genocide-intent question cannot be frozen permanently at one evidentiary moment.

Statements, policies, military conduct, humanitarian conditions and evidence concerning decision-making continue to accumulate.

That does **not** mean every later inflammatory statement proves genocidal intent.

It means later evidence belongs in the record.

The same applies to ceasefire-period violence. A ceasefire does not make every subsequent death proof of genocide. Nor can a continuing pattern of lethal incidents simply be erased from analysis because a ceasefire exists on paper.

The relevant question remains:

> **What is happening now, under what authority, under what rules, and with what mechanisms for preventing prohibited conduct?**

## ⚖️ The Convention Says Prevention As Well As Punishment

The instrument is the **Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide**.

Article II defines genocide and requires the relevant intent.

Article I separately commits states to prevent and punish it.

The ICJ's 2007 *Bosnia and Herzegovina v Serbia and Montenegro* judgment is particularly important for prevention. The Court held that a state's duty to act arises when it learns, or should normally have learned, of a **serious risk** that genocide will be committed. What a state can reasonably be expected to do depends partly on its capacity to influence the actors concerned, including political and other links.

That does not mean that every state capable of influencing Israel is automatically legally responsible for failing to prevent genocide.

It gives us a serious research framework:

- What did allied governments know?
- When did they know it?
- What risk did they understand?
- What means of influence did they possess?
- What did they do with those means?
- What legal obligations applied to them?
- What happened as the evidentiary picture changed?

Prevention necessarily operates under uncertainty.

Waiting for a final judicial finding that genocide **has already occurred** is not a prevention strategy.

## 🚩 Self-Defence Does Not End The Intent Inquiry

Israel has a legitimate security interest in protecting its population from armed attack and in addressing the Hamas-led attack of 7 October 2023 and the taking of hostages.

That fact matters.

It does not logically terminate an inquiry into genocidal intent.

A professed protective or military purpose does not by itself determine whether prohibited intent also existed.

Equally, catastrophic destruction does not by itself establish the specific intent required by the Convention.

The evidentiary work is precisely in the middle:

**conduct, patterns, policy, statements, command decisions, civilian treatment, alternative explanations, institutional response and the relationship between them.**

That is why this node takes the genocide risk seriously without pretending the merits judgment has already been written.

## 📣 Whistleblowers Are Alarms, Not Verdicts

The correct institutional response is therefore neither:

> **They embarrassed us, so they are traitors.**

> **Investigate the alarm.**

That is particularly important where the allegations concern a distributed system. No individual source may understand the whole architecture. One analyst may know Lavender. Another may know location tracking. Another may know collateral-damage calculations. Another may know command rules.

The investigation has to reconstruct the system.

## 🌱 Accountability Is Not Hostility

If somebody believes that Israel is frequently misrepresented in international media, they should have a strong reason to support good audits.

## 🌍 The Rules We Would Be Willing To Live Under

This node does not proceed from the assumption that the possibility of genocide in Gaza is too offensive, too politically dangerous or too historically painful to investigate.

The risk is credible enough to require serious legal, journalistic and institutional attention. The International Court of Justice has not made a final merits finding that genocide occurred, and reasonable disagreement remains over whether the evidence establishes the Convention's specific-intent requirement.

That disagreement cannot be where the investigation stops.

If the eventual answer is **not genocide**, there remains an enormous account to be given of civilian death, destruction, targeting methodology, collateral-damage tolerances, continuing violence, whistleblower testimony, military psychological injury and the effectiveness of Israeli safeguards.

"Not genocide" does not itself establish that those things were lawful, proportionate, necessary, inevitable or well governed.

Nor is this solely Israel's problem.

The United States and other technologically sophisticated militaries have participated in building the surveillance, remote-warfare, computational and targeting environment from which these questions emerge. Commercial technology companies increasingly provide parts of the infrastructure. Russia, Iran, Ukraine and others will not politely decline the same technological possibilities.

The relevant question is therefore larger than whether we trust Israel.

> **It is whether these are rules we would be willing to live under when the person operating the system does not regard us as the good guys.**

If a safeguard is only necessary when Iran operates the system, it is not much of a safeguard.

If meaningful human control matters when Russia targets Ukrainians, it matters when allies target their enemies.

If civilian life matters when our civilians are under the model, it matters when somebody else's children are represented by the collateral-damage number.

And if the answer is that machine-assisted warfare is going to be part of the future regardless, then this is precisely the moment to decide which forms of friction are not defects.

Some friction protects civilians.

Some protects soldiers.

Some protects evidence.

Some protects institutions.

Some gives somebody enough time to say:

> **No. Check that again.**
