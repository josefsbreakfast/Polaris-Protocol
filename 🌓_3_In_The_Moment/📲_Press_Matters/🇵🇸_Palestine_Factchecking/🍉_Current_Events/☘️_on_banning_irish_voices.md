# ☘️ On Banning Irish Voices
**First created:** 2026-10-15 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-16  
*On the peculiar British habit of restricting Irish voices, the consequences of counterterrorism law reaching a novelist's books, and the public's reasonable desire to know what its government thinks it is protecting.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

I started with a fairly ordinary question: why, in October 2026, are people in Britain having trouble buying new copies of Sally Rooney's novels? I have somehow arrived at Gerry Adams, Stephen Rea, BBC governors, Aunt Sarah, Granda Joe, a man on helium and an IRA prisoner's sausage rolls.

This is, apparently, British constitutional history.

The point is not that the Troubles and the war in Gaza are the same conflict. They aren't. Nor are the 1988 broadcasting restrictions and the 2025 proscription of Palestine Action the same legal instrument. They aren't either. The comparison concerns the *behaviour of restrictions*: what the state says they are intended to prevent, what institutions actually do in response, what the public experiences, and whether anyone can still explain the distance between the objective and the outcome.

I don't think governments can never restrict anything. I think that if a government is going to restrict people's lives, it should be able to explain why it considers that restriction reasonable. The public does not have to agree. It does need enough information to understand the claim, challenge it, and distinguish what is known from what is merely being asserted.

A legal justification being available somewhere is not the same thing as the public having been given a comprehensible explanation.

## ☘️ 1. The Irishman whose face arrived before his voice

I was born in 1990, on the British mainland. Britain's restrictions on broadcasting certain Irish political voices were lifted in September 1994. I was not conducting a mature constitutional analysis of the BBC at the age of four. My sense of the period is a mixture of fragments, later cultural references and the strange recognition of Gerry Adams as a familiar face whose actual voice can feel unexpectedly unfamiliar.

The Republic of Ireland had its own earlier restrictions under Section 31 of the Broadcasting Authority Act. Britain's October 1988 directions were separate measures, introduced in a different legal jurisdiction. Both belong to the history of governments attempting to control how political organisations associated with violence reached mass audiences.

On 19 October 1988, Home Secretary Douglas Hurd told Parliament that the BBC and Independent Broadcasting Authority would be required not to broadcast direct statements by representatives of specified organisations, or statements supporting them, subject to qualifications including election coverage. The words could often still be reported. The *original voice* was the problem. [1]

So broadcasters employed actors to read the words instead.

The state had not made the political argument disappear. It had given it a different accent.

## 🎭 2. Aunt Sarah's national-security assessment

In *Derry Girls*, Aunt Sarah offers a more entertaining explanation for the dubbing: Gerry Adams's own voice is dangerously sexy. Granda Joe, apparently in possession of authoritative intelligence, likens it to fine whiskey. Gerry, who has unwisely asked his family a question, discovers there is no safe exit from this conversation.

Aunt Sarah's explanation is not historical evidence. It is a joke about an actual, extraordinary broadcasting practice. And it works because the real-world arrangement was already peculiar: audiences could see a politician's face and hear his words, provided somebody else delivered them.

Meanwhile, *The Day Today* gave us a pseudo-Gerry Adams inhaling helium to diminish the credibility of his statements. Two different comic theories of the voice as a national-security threat. One too seductive; the other apparently requiring emergency balloon intervention.

Both parodies reveal the same underlying assumption: *the sound of a political voice can change the effect of the information it carries*. That is not an absurd assumption in itself. Voice conveys confidence, nerves, evasiveness, accent and emotion. The absurdity lies in imagining that replacing it is a clean operation, as though information were merely words in a bucket.

## 📺 3. Television was a political machine, not just a box in the corner

Television was already an established mass medium by the 1970s. But its near-universal reach, its relatively small number of broadcasters, and its ability to bring political figures into domestic spaces made it an unusually concentrated channel of political information.

That concentration matters. A state could direct a handful of broadcasters much more readily than it can control every cross-border route by which information travels online. The older broadcasting controversy is therefore not a quaint version of a modern social-media moderation dispute. Its institutional architecture was different.

The conflict was also real. People were killed and injured during the Troubles. Governments had legitimate responsibilities to protect life, and serious questions existed about whether broadcasting could be exploited by organisations associated with political violence. But identifying a legitimate objective does not settle whether a particular intervention was necessary, effective or proportionate.

## 🍼 4. The BBC discovers the national-security implications of babies

Before the formal British voice restrictions, the BBC had already experienced a confrontation over *At the Edge of the Union*, a 1985 documentary featuring Martin McGuinness and Gregory Campbell, including aspects of their domestic lives. Government objections and disputes within the BBC centred partly on the concern that presenting controversial political figures as ordinary people could normalise them. The planned August transmission was stopped; journalists staged a one-day strike; an edited version was eventually broadcast in October.

There is a genuine editorial problem here. Humanising somebody does not mean endorsing their actions. Nor does showing family life automatically provide sufficient scrutiny of their political commitments. Journalism has to decide how to show the whole person without obscuring the consequences of their actions.

But the government trying to decide what the audience should be permitted to see creates another problem altogether.

The babies were not, so far as I can establish, a paramilitary organisation.

## 🎬 5. Stephen Rea joins the voice-replacement department

Actors supplied replacement voices for interviews and reports. Stephen Rea was among those reported to have voiced Gerry Adams. A retrospective account suggested that Rea's gentler delivery may even have made Adams sound more appealing than the original voice would have done. That is an interpretation, not demonstrated evidence of audience persuasion.

Still, the information mechanism is important. Replacing a voice doesn't subtract its persuasive properties and leave a neutral remainder. It substitutes another performance, with another set of cues. If an interviewer presses somebody about violence and the original speaker hesitates, the hesitation may matter. Tony Hall later argued that dubbing could deprive audiences of exactly that kind of information.

We have attempted to remove a politician's voice and introduced the interpretive qualities of a professional actor.

**Task failed successfully** is perhaps too cheerful a description of a serious constitutional dispute. It is nevertheless difficult to resist.

## 🥐 6. The sausage rolls have become political

Implementation generated stranger cases. Reports of the period describe a complaint by an IRA prisoners' food spokesman about the size of prison sausage rolls being revoiced; historical and documentary footage also created difficult editorial decisions. Broadcasters had to determine what counted as a prohibited direct statement, when exceptions applied, and whether material already recorded or created for other purposes was caught.

This is a general problem with rules administered through institutions. The legal text is one thing. The compliance department's interpretation is another. Editors may take the cautious route, especially where a mistake has serious consequences. The practical restriction can spread beyond the situation that originally motivated it.

The sausage rolls are not evidence that terrorism was harmless. They are evidence that the mechanism used to address it could become administratively surreal.

The Home Office, regrettably, has lost control of the catering situation.

## 📰 7. A restriction changes the reporting, not only the broadcast

The British directions did not merely change the soundtrack. They affected what broadcasters could commission, edit and transmit. Contemporary critics argued that uncertainty and additional production requirements discouraged coverage, and that organisations affected by the ban could use it as a reason to avoid difficult interviews. Defenders argued that preventing direct access to broadcasting limited publicity for groups connected with violence.

Both effects can exist: a restriction can reduce some original reporting while generating publicity about the restriction itself. Not every ban creates a backlash; not every controversy increases the circulation of the underlying message. There is no universal rule that censorship always fails.

The useful question is more demanding: *what did this particular restriction achieve, what did it cost, and what other effects did it create?*

## 🌊 8. The Irish Sea is not an information firewall

Ireland's Section 31 restrictions ended in January 1994; Britain's continued until September. The difference made a political voice available through some broadcasters while still restricted through others. Gerry Adams's appearances abroad likewise demonstrated the limits of a nationally bounded broadcasting rule.

Internationally, British journalists reported that officials in other countries invoked the Sinn Féin ban when challenged about their own censorship. John Simpson's account of Iraqi officials doing so is particularly revealing. The analogy offered by an authoritarian government need not be accurate to be rhetorically useful to it.

Domestic restrictions travel. Sometimes as law, sometimes as example, sometimes as excuse.

The British restrictions ended on 16 September 1994, following the IRA ceasefire of 31 August. That sequence does not establish that broadcasting policy caused the ceasefire or the peace process. It does remind us that measures described as necessary at one moment can later be reassessed.

Solicitor Paul Chinnery's dry reaction to the lifting of the ban was to welcome the government's apparent new confidence that the public could be trusted to hear Gerry Adams for themselves.

Quite.

## 🍉 9. And now, the Irish novelist

On 14 October 2026, Faber announced that it would stop supplying Sally Rooney's books in the United Kingdom, in any format, with Rooney's agreement. Faber said that, under the current legal circumstances, it could not pass UK earnings on to her and would not sell her work without paying its author. It said it would continue selling her books in other territories. Rooney linked the problem to her publicly stated intention to support Palestine Action, which the UK proscribed in July 2025 but which is not proscribed in the Republic of Ireland. [2–5]

That is more precise than saying that the British government has directly banned *Normal People*. It has not prohibited the novel's text. The reported problem arises from the legal and financial consequences of proscription, Rooney's position, and the publisher's resulting decision. Existing copies and other routes of circulation are a separate question from Faber supplying new stock.

But imagine approaching this as an ordinary reader.

You wanted a novel. You are now required to understand the Terrorism Act, the legal meaning of support, royalties, cross-border jurisdiction, publisher risk, and the difference between a book ban and a book becoming unavailable through its usual supply chain.

*Respectfully, I was trying to buy a Sally Rooney book. Why am I doing a counterterrorism module?*

The formal distinction matters. So does the practical outcome. Explaining one without the other does not resolve the public-information problem.

## ⚖️ 10. What, exactly, does the government believe it is preventing?

Palestine Action was proscribed following government concerns about its activities, including an incident at RAF Brize Norton in June 2025. The lawfulness and application of the proscription have been contested in court. As of 15 October 2026, the UK Supreme Court hearing is scheduled for **4–5 November 2026**; the appeal permission is reported as limited to whether the Home Secretary followed her published policy. That is not the same thing as the Supreme Court rehearing every political and factual dispute about the organisation. [6]

There are distinct questions here. Did the organisation meet the relevant statutory criteria? Did the minister follow the applicable decision-making policy? Was proscription a necessary and proportionate intervention? How are the consequences for protest, expression and publishing being handled? A judicial ruling on one issue may not settle every other issue of public accountability.

I do not presume the government has no justification. I want to know what it considers the justification to be, why ordinary criminal law and more targeted responses were considered insufficient, and what it believes the public is being protected from.

That explanation need not disclose operational intelligence. It should, however, distinguish between material that genuinely cannot be released and questions that simply have not been answered in a useful way.

### 🧾 A public explanation ought to answer

| Question | What would make the answer useful? |
| --- | --- |
| What harm is being prevented? | Describe the conduct and threat rather than rely only on the label attached to it. |
| Why proscription? | Explain the additional protection it offers over existing offences and enforcement powers. |
| What alternatives were considered? | Identify less restrictive options and the reasons they were rejected. |
| Where are the boundaries? | Distinguish criminal conduct, political advocacy, journalism, ordinary protest and legally defined support. |
| What effects were anticipated? | Address foreseeable consequences for publishers, authors, civil society and public participation. |
| What effects have emerged? | Acknowledge unanticipated consequences, including the Rooney supply problem. |
| Who can scrutinise confidential evidence? | Identify independent oversight and routes of legal challenge. |
| What would change the decision? | Explain review, reconsideration and deproscription routes. |

Some answers may already exist across parliamentary records, departmental statements and court papers. The task is to gather them, identify what they actually establish, and show where the gaps remain. The absence of an accessible synthesis is itself different from the absence of any underlying evidence.

## 🕊️ 11. You cannot remove Gaza from the public's understanding of the decision

The proscription is being interpreted amid sustained controversy over the war in Gaza, the conduct of Israel's military campaign, British arms-export and military relationships, ceasefire arrangements, alleged war crimes and South Africa's ongoing case against Israel under the Genocide Convention at the International Court of Justice.

These issues require precise treatment. An allegation is not a final judicial finding; an ICJ case in progress is not a completed merits judgment. The status of ceasefires, export licences and military cooperation must be dated and sourced. Nor does opposition to government policy automatically justify every tactic used in protest against it.

But context matters. People are being asked to assess restrictions on political activity connected with a conflict while also trying to understand their government's conduct in relation to that conflict. It is unsurprising that they evaluate these questions together, even when the legal tests are distinct.

The government may have a defensible security case. If so, the public should be able to understand it as a *case*, rather than be expected to infer it from the existence of the restriction.

## 🛩️ 12. When even an RAF incident arrives as competing theories

One of the things that struck me about discussion of incidents involving RAF facilities was how quickly some people moved from the initial report to speculation about who had really done what, whether the event had been misdescribed, and what interests might be served by a particular account.

Those suspicions are not evidence. The exact incident and examples of public reaction need verification before any claims about their prevalence can be made. Nor was suspicion of official security narratives invented in 2026: the Iraq War and its intelligence controversies alone make that untenable.

What interests me is the information process. A report arrives. The official account may be incomplete, or still developing. Speculative explanations circulate immediately. People encounter those explanations beside ordinary reporting, with no guarantee that the interface distinguishes evidence from conjecture.

When confidence is low, even a truthful but vague official answer may fail to reduce uncertainty. That is not proof of a cover-up. It is a reason to take the quality of public explanation seriously.

## 📰 13. A truthful answer is not necessarily an informative answer

I've been reading parliamentary questions and answers. Sometimes the response is probably accurate, legally cautious and entirely in keeping with the departmental style. But did it answer the question?

Suppose an MP asks what assessment a department has made of a policy's consequences, and the reply says the government remains committed to all relevant obligations. The statement may be true. It doesn't tell the reader whether an assessment was made, what it covered, or what was found.

Three tests matter:

- **Truthfulness:** is the statement accurate?
- **Responsiveness:** does it address the question actually asked?
- **Informativeness:** does it reduce the uncertainty the question was intended to resolve?

One vague answer might have a perfectly reasonable explanation. Repeated vague answers, combined with differently phrased ministerial interviews and press releases, can leave people unable to establish a stable account of policy. Some differences reflect genuine changes or different audiences. If so, say that. Explain what changed.

**More communication is not necessarily more information.**

## 🛒 14. Politicians are not supposed to be nothing but salesmen

I understand that political communication involves persuasion. Governments have policies; ministers defend them. But explaining a decision and selling it are not the same task.

Selling asks: *How do we get people on board?*

Explaining asks: *What do people need to know to evaluate whether this decision is reasonable?*

A useful explanation includes costs, limits, alternatives, uncertainties and consequences, not only the headline benefits. If people keep receiving polished reassurance where they asked for an account of the mechanism, it is hardly surprising if they begin to feel marketed to.

That doesn't mean every politician is lying. It means the style of communication can itself make the truth harder to locate.

## 📺 15. I would quite like the six o'clock news, please

This isn't nostalgia for an age when television had no bias. Newspapers and broadcasters have always had institutional perspectives, editorial judgments and blind spots. The point is the desire for a comprehensible, recognisable account of what happened today.

What do we know? What don't we know? What does the government say? What can independent reporting establish? What is disputed? Which claim has changed since yesterday?

Six o'clock: what happened. Ten o'clock: perhaps a bit spicier, now the children have gone to bed.

The contemporary feed doesn't organise information this way. Eyewitness footage, government statements, speculation, jokes, advertisements, investigative reporting and propaganda can all arrive in the same format. Users are expected to perform the evidentiary sorting themselves, continuously.

We can ask whether online regulation is adequate without pretending that the UK regulates only television. Broadcasting rules and online-safety law exist, but differ in scope, purpose and enforcement. The public also deserves a clear explanation of what regulators can actually do, what they cannot do, and where cross-border companies or technical realities limit the state's power.

If the answer is *we cannot effectively regulate that aspect of the platform*, that is information too. Say so.

## 🫀 16. Every device has pop-up malware, except it's people dying

I grew up in the political aftermath of 9/11. Security language has been part of my adult information environment for as long as I can remember. But the difference between that and the world of the 1988 broadcast directions is not just a change of vocabulary.

People can now be looking at a recipe, a cat or a photograph and suddenly see footage of somebody being killed. Then an advert for moisturiser. Then a joke. Then another war.

*It's like every digital device is infected with pop-up malware, but it's people violently dying.*

The information may be important evidence. The people recording atrocities may be doing essential work. None of that establishes that every viewer has chosen to encounter graphic footage at that moment. Access to information and involuntary exposure to it are different matters.

Nor does becoming accustomed to seeing violence necessarily mean becoming indifferent to the people suffering. Someone can cease to be surprised by the appearance of death in a feed while still being deeply affected by it.

Earlier generations experienced enormous violence, including directly. They were not sheltered innocents. But the routine, unscheduled delivery of distant killings into ordinary domestic digital activity is a different information arrangement. It asks people to witness far more than they can meaningfully respond to.

**Information is not simply transmitted. It is experienced by bodies.**

## 🛰️ 17. America, Scotland has been known to have opinions

There is a wider security-confidence problem here. Reports and disputes over Starlink access in Ukraine raise questions about the dependence of wartime communications on privately controlled infrastructure and American decision-making. The precise incident matters: a SpaceX decision, a technical limitation, a request from US officials and an order from the US government are not interchangeable claims. They must not be casually collapsed.

Britain also operates its own nuclear deterrent while relying on significant US cooperation within the Trident system. Its submarine and associated infrastructure is based in Scotland. Scottish independence would raise difficult constitutional, logistical and strategic questions about those arrangements; it would not automatically make Britain's deterrent vanish.

But, respectfully, America: we could always ask Scotland again. Scotland has opinions. The submarine arrangements do not exist outside politics.

The joke is not a proposal to use Scottish democracy as a bargaining chip. It is a reminder that national-security assumptions rest on relationships, infrastructure and political consent. A public trying to evaluate one security policy does not place everything else it knows about government competence and allied reliability in a sealed compartment.

These matters do not prove anything about Palestine Action. They are part of the environment in which official claims of security necessity are received.

## ♻️ 18. The public sees the outputs, not the machinery

A reader sees a story about an RAF base. Another about Gaza. Another about British arms policy. Another about Ukraine's communications. Another about a court appeal. Then the news that Sally Rooney's publisher will no longer supply her novels in Britain.

Some of these stories are legally or causally connected. Others are only connected by the wider question of whether public institutions are explaining themselves. The danger is pretending they form one secret master narrative. The equally unhelpful alternative is to insist that people must interpret every event without reference to the surrounding loss of confidence.

An incomplete official explanation leaves space for inference. Some inference is careful; some is wrong; some becomes conspiracy. Calling all of it misinformation does not answer the questions that generated it.

There is a possible feedback loop:

1. A consequential restriction is imposed.
2. People encounter effects they did not expect.
3. The official explanation is fragmented or hard to understand.
4. Speculation supplies simpler stories.
5. Officials respond with more polished repetition.
6. The repetition does not resolve the original question.
7. People become less confident that anyone is explaining the decision plainly.

The corrective is not necessarily a better slogan. It may be a better answer.

## 🏛️ 19. Explain the restriction; don't just announce it

A state has responsibilities to protect people from violence. It also has responsibilities to account for the restrictions it imposes. Those responsibilities can coexist, even when some evidence must remain confidential.

Public agreement is not the test of whether an explanation has been given. People can understand the government's reasoning and still disagree. They can accept that a threat exists while disputing the necessity of a particular power. They can support prosecution of criminal damage while questioning the wider consequences of proscription. They can defend the right to report atrocities while asking not to have footage of death thrust unexpectedly into their feeds.

And the government should distinguish three possibilities: that it has reasoning it has not communicated well; that some reasoning depends on genuinely sensitive material; or that the reasoning itself is open to serious challenge. Those are different problems requiring different remedies.

Once the Supreme Court proceedings have clarified the legal position, there should be an opportunity to give the public a structured account of what the decision was intended to achieve, what the courts decided, what the government can and cannot disclose, and what it intends to do about consequences such as the Rooney publishing problem. The hearing may not settle all these questions, and a judgment will not automatically make confidential material public. That is precisely why the public explanation should not be left to emerge accidentally from litigation alone.

I don't need the government to convince every person that it is right. I do think it should be capable of showing people **why it believes it is acting within reason**.

## ☘️ 20. Back to the bookshop

We began with an Irish novelist whose books people are having difficulty obtaining through their ordinary British supply channels.

We went backwards through another period when the British state attempted to manage the public's encounter with Irish political voices. We met the BBC governors, journalists on strike, actors with microphones, the West Belfast Bond, helium Gerry and a national-security sausage roll.

The two episodes are not legally equivalent. But they both reveal something about the distance between a restriction's formal target and its public consequences.

A person trying to buy a Sally Rooney novel is unlikely to begin by asking whether the Home Secretary correctly applied a published proscription policy. They're going to ask why the bookshop can't order the book.

And if the answer is a chain involving political support, a statutory prohibition, royalty payments and publisher compliance, somebody needs to explain that chain properly. Then somebody needs to explain how the consequence relates to the harm the state intended to prevent, and whether that consequence was anticipated or considered acceptable.

The government may have an answer.

**I'd quite like to hear it.**

Granda Joe, meanwhile, maintains that Gerry Adams has the voice of a fine whiskey. Aunt Sarah believes she has solved the entire original security problem. The helium department is unavailable for comment.
