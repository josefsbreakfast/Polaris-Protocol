# ⚖️ UKLFI, Which Planet Are You On?
**First created:** 2026-09-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-17  
*What Britain should ask before allegations concerning individual humanitarian workers become an argument for treating Médecins Sans Frontières as a terrorist organisation.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

In September 2026, UK Lawyers for Israel (UKLFI) asked the British state to contemplate an extraordinary proposition.

According to public reporting, UKLFI wrote to the Home Office, Charity Commission and Fundraising Regulator concerning alleged connections between people associated with Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) and Palestinian armed groups, and urged the Home Secretary to consider proscribing MSF under the Terrorism Act 2000 in a manner compared in reporting with the proscription of Palestine Action.

That is not a small request.

A complaint to a regulator is not proscription.

An investigation is not a finding.

An allegation against an employee is not an allegation against an organisation.

Evidence establishing that an employee belonged to a proscribed organisation does not, without more, establish that their employer knew.

Evidence of failed vetting does not automatically establish institutional participation in terrorism.

And the Terrorism Act is not a particularly large hammer kept beside the Charity Commission for occasions when ordinary regulation feels insufficiently dramatic.

The Home Office's published test concerns whether **the organisation itself is concerned in terrorism**, followed by a proportionality assessment. The legal question is therefore not simply whether something serious may have happened somewhere within a vast international organisation.

It is:

> **What is the evidential bridge between serious allegations concerning particular people and the conclusion that Médecins Sans Frontières itself is an organisation concerned in terrorism?**

This node does not assume that allegations concerning individual MSF personnel are false.

One of the principal allegations — concerning Fadi Al-Wadiya — has acquired significant subsequent corroboration and should be treated seriously.

Nor does this node canonise MSF. MSF's own colonial inheritances, internal power structures, inequalities between internationally and locally recruited staff, safeguarding failures and previous allegations concerning misconduct deserve scrutiny in their own right. There are people far better placed than this author to describe those problems, particularly people from the communities in which international humanitarian organisations operate.

Humanitarian status does not create immunity from law.

But imperfection does not convert humanitarian medicine into terrorism either.

If Britain is being asked to exercise one of its most consequential counter-terrorism powers against one of the world's largest emergency medical organisations, the evidential, legal and public-interest assessment should be commensurate with the power being requested.

That assessment does not belong exclusively to UKLFI.

It belongs to Britain.

## ⚖️ The Legal Instrument Is Not Decorative

Proscription is not simply governmental condemnation.

It changes the legal status of an organisation.

The Home Office explains that offences associated with proscription can include belonging to a proscribed organisation, inviting support for it, arranging certain supportive meetings and other conduct falling within the statutory scheme. The consequences can include serious criminal penalties.

That is why the evidential sequence cannot simply be:

```text
MSF employee allegedly associated with armed group
                    ↓
           therefore MSF knew
                    ↓
      therefore MSF assisted terrorism
                    ↓
        therefore proscribe MSF
```

The actual sequence should be:

```text
What is alleged about the individual?
                    ↓
How reliable is the underlying evidence?
                    ↓
Was the individual actually affiliated?
                    ↓
What did MSF know, and when?
                    ↓
Did MSF knowingly facilitate prohibited activity?
                    ↓
Is this individual concealment, institutional failure,
or institutional participation?
                    ↓
Does MSF itself satisfy the Terrorism Act test?
                    ↓
If so, is proscription necessary and proportionate?
```

Every arrow matters.

The existence of a serious allegation at the top of the chain cannot be used to erase the evidential work required lower down it.

## 🧮 Use The Legal Tool Designed For The Problem

Britain possesses more than one legal instrument.

| Alleged problem | Mechanism which should at least be considered |
|---|---|
| Individual committed a terrorism offence | Criminal investigation and prosecution where jurisdiction permits |
| Individual belonged to a proscribed organisation | Relevant Terrorism Act investigation |
| Employee concealed an affiliation | Employment, safeguarding and internal investigation |
| Sanctions breach | UK sanctions enforcement |
| Charitable funds diverted | Financial/criminal investigation |
| Trustee or governance failure | Charity Commission |
| Inadequate vetting | Governance and regulatory scrutiny |
| Institutional participation in terrorism | Organisational proscription may become relevant |

The government should therefore ask UKLFI a very simple question:

> **Why this instrument?**

Not merely:

> Is the underlying allegation serious?

It may be.

The question remains whether organisational proscription is the legal tool designed for the conduct actually evidenced.

Counter-terrorism legislation is not a super-regulator for every serious organisational failure.

## 🔬 Three Dead Men, Three Separate Evidence Files

The people at the centre of the principal public allegations are dead.

They cannot be interviewed by British investigators.

They cannot answer allegations.

And evidence concerning one cannot be used as free corroboration for another.

### Fadi Al-Wadiya

Fadi Al-Wadiya was an MSF physiotherapist.

He was killed in an Israeli strike on 25 June 2024 while on his way to work at an MSF clinic in Gaza City. Israel subsequently alleged that he was a significant Palestinian Islamic Jihad operative.

At the time, MSF said it had received no prior direct communication from Israeli authorities alleging such activity and that no proof had been shared with it. MSF called for an independent investigation.

The evidential position later changed materially. Palestinian Islamic Jihad itself subsequently identified Al-Wadiya as one of its members. That means it would now be misleading to describe his apparent PIJ affiliation simply as an unsupported Israeli allegation.

But two propositions remain different:

**Al-Wadiya was affiliated with PIJ.**

and

**MSF knew Al-Wadiya was affiliated with PIJ and knowingly facilitated prohibited activity.**

Evidence supporting the first does not automatically establish the second.

If UKLFI possesses evidence establishing the second proposition, that is the evidence Britain needs to see.

### Nasser Hamdi Abdelatif Al-Shalfouh

Nasser Al-Shalfouh joined MSF as a driver in March 2023.

MSF says he had not been working since the war began because its activities in northern Gaza had been severely disrupted. He suffered shrapnel injuries in Jabalia on 8 October 2024 and died at Kamal Adwan Hospital on 10 October.

Israel has alleged that Al-Shalfouh was associated with Hamas and acted as a sniper.

At the time of this drafting, no independent corroboration equivalent to PIJ's subsequent acknowledgement concerning Al-Wadiya has been identified here.

That does not establish that the allegation is false.

It means that the underlying evidence should be examined rather than the allegation itself being treated as corroboration.

### Dr Mahmoud Abu Nujaila

Dr Mahmoud Abu Nujaila was an MSF doctor working at Al-Awda Hospital.

He was killed on 21 November 2023 when the hospital was struck, alongside MSF doctor Ahmad Al-Sahar and Dr Ziad Al-Tatari.

MSF says it had repeatedly communicated the hospital's location and the presence of its personnel to the parties to the conflict, including sharing GPS coordinates with Israeli authorities.

Separate allegations concerning Abu Nujaila have circulated concerning militant or political affiliations and social-media activity. Those claims need to be traced to primary evidence rather than repeated through an advocacy chain until repetition itself starts looking like corroboration.

Again:

**investigate the allegation.**

Do not launder evidence concerning one person into evidence concerning another.

And do not silently convert evidence concerning individuals into evidence of institutional MSF knowledge.

## 🕰️ When Did Anybody Know?

This may be one of the most important factual questions in the entire case.

MSF has said that Israeli authorities did not identify relevant staff to MSF as alleged militants before their deaths.

If that account is correct, Britain should want to know:

- When did Israeli authorities first identify each person?
- What intelligence existed before their deaths?
- How reliable was it?
- Was any warning supplied to MSF?
- If not, why not?
- Could a sanitised warning have been supplied without compromising sources and methods?
- Did Israel ask MSF to investigate or remove any of these employees?
- What did MSF ask Israel after allegations became public?
- What information was subsequently supplied?
- When did UKLFI obtain its evidence?
- What evidence does UKLFI possess which has not been made public?
- What changed between the deaths, later corroborating material and the September 2026 request?
- Why organisational proscription?
- Why now?

There may be entirely legitimate answers.

The point is to obtain them.

## 🧿 Intelligence Provenance Is Not Optional

UKLFI should expect questions about the provenance of the material it has submitted.

That is not an accusation of covert direction.

It is due diligence.

If material derives from Israeli military, intelligence, diplomatic or governmental sources, Britain should know that.

If UKLFI independently corroborated the material, Britain should know how.

If information originated with third-party advocacy organisations, that should be identifiable.

If UKLFI communicated with Israeli governmental bodies about MSF before making its British submission, that is relevant context for assessing provenance.

The Home Office should establish:

- what information originated with Israeli authorities;
- what originated with UKLFI;
- what originated with other organisations;
- what was independently verified;
- what remains intelligence which cannot publicly be disclosed;
- whether UK agencies have independently assessed that intelligence;
- and whether any relevant relationships or interests accompanied the representation.

An Israeli intelligence assessment does not become a British intelligence assessment because a British advocacy organisation includes it in correspondence to a minister.

Britain has its own intelligence services.

Britain has its own ministers.

Britain has its own law.

That is what sovereignty is for.

## 💰 Show Your Working

The same principle applies to UKLFI itself.

An organisation asking the British state to exercise extraordinary coercive power should expect reasonable scrutiny of the provenance of its request.

That means establishing, from public records where possible:

- UKLFI's organisational and legal structure;
- the distinction between UKLFI and related charitable entities;
- governance;
- trustees and directors;
- published accounts;
- disclosed funding;
- institutional partners;
- relevant relationships with Israeli organisations or state institutions;
- commissioned work;
- whether any external actor funded or commissioned the MSF project specifically;
- and what declarations of interest accompanied the representations.

The categories must remain separate:

**established**

**disclosed**

**unknown**

**alleged**

Unknown does not mean secret.

A question is not evidence of its own answer.

But the fact that a question has not yet been answered does not make it improper to ask.

## 🩺 You Have Chosen Médecins Sans Frontières

There is an additional proportionality problem.

This is Médecins Sans Frontières.

That statement does not mean:

**MSF is good, therefore MSF cannot break the law.**

It means:

**understand the object of the proposed state action before assessing the consequences of acting against it.**

MSF is not a Gaza-specific political campaign.

It is a transnational emergency medical organisation involved in trauma medicine, surgery, infectious disease, maternal healthcare, malnutrition, displacement, water and sanitation, and medical care during armed conflict and disaster.

Its work is deeply connected to international healthcare systems and to clinicians, researchers, donors and institutions in Britain.

Proscription would follow the organisation.

It would not remain politely inside the evidential controversy which generated it.

## 🪞 MSF Is Not Above Criticism

None of this requires sanitising MSF.

International humanitarian medicine has its own history of colonialism.

MSF has been criticised over power imbalances between European headquarters and locally recruited staff, racialised employment structures, paternalism, safeguarding failures and allegations of sexual exploitation and abuse.

Those conversations matter.

People from the countries in which international NGOs operate have repeatedly asked why organisations claiming humanitarian universalism can reproduce racial and economic hierarchies they profess to oppose.

Those criticisms should not disappear merely because MSF is under attack from somewhere else.

Indeed, the principle should run in both directions.

A medical charity does not become innocent of every institutional failure because somebody makes an excessive allegation against it.

And institutional failures do not become terrorism because somebody dislikes the charity's politics.

We should be capable of holding both thoughts simultaneously.

## 🦠 Ebola Does Not Care About Your Culture War

The timing makes the whole-system assessment considerably more serious.

The Democratic Republic of the Congo is experiencing a major Ebola outbreak caused by Bundibugyo virus.

On 10 September 2026, WHO reported that, as of 7 September, the DRC had recorded **6,757 confirmed cases and 3,267 deaths**, across 61 health zones in six provinces. WHO described continued geographical expansion, sustained increases in some areas and a crude case fatality ratio of 48.3 per cent. Cases had also been detected outside the DRC, including Uganda, Germany and France.

MSF is one of the organisations contributing medical capacity to Ebola response in the DRC.

That creates another entirely legitimate government question:

> **Has anybody asked DHSC and UKHSA what the consequences of proscribing MSF could be for international outbreak response?**

This is not an Ebola exemption from counter-terrorism law.

If MSF institutionally satisfies the statutory test, government must assess the evidence accordingly.

But proportionality means considering consequences.

And national security includes infectious disease.

We have already lived through a pandemic.

It should not require another one to remember:

> **Pathogens do not stop at the British border to have their passports checked.**

## 🌍 Whose Lives Count In The Risk Assessment?

There is an anti-racist governance question here which does not require establishing racist intent.

MSF's international operations sustain healthcare for populations which are disproportionately African, Asian, Middle Eastern, displaced, colonised, formerly colonised, impoverished or living amid conflicts whose international causes and financing frequently extend far beyond their borders.

In the present Ebola emergency, many of the people relying upon the medical system MSF is helping to sustain are Black Congolese people.

That fact matters.

Britain is simultaneously undergoing another period of public reckoning with its own involvement in the trafficking and enslavement of African people, including new work tracing British investment and benefit from the transatlantic slave trade.

This node does **not** claim that UKLFI selected MSF because it wishes to harm Black people.

It asks something different:

> **If a proposed British action foreseeably affects healthcare infrastructure protecting thousands of Black lives, were those lives included in the assessment?**

Were patients in Congo considered?

Were patients in Sudan considered?

Were patients in South Sudan considered?

Were African healthcare professionals working alongside MSF considered?

Were international epidemic consequences considered?

If not, why not?

At what point does repeatedly failing to count the people most likely to bear a policy's external costs become an anti-racist governance problem regardless of whether anybody consciously intended racial harm?

Britain should know better than to make African lives disappear into the footnotes of somebody else's geopolitical argument.

## 🪞 Jewish Is Not The Opposite Of Black

There is another problem with any political framework which imagines this argument as a negotiation between **Jewish interests** on one side and the interests of racialised communities somewhere else.

Some British Jews are Black.

Some are mixed-race.

Some are Mizrahi or Sephardi.

Some are Asian.

Some are queer.

Some are disabled.

Some are migrants.

Some belong to several of those categories simultaneously.

Britain has organised Black Jewish and Jews-of-Colour communities and alliances. Some explicitly participate in Palestine solidarity; others do not. Mainstream and progressive Jewish institutions have themselves increasingly acknowledged that Black Jews and Jews of Colour have often been poorly represented in communal structures.

This is not demographic trivia.

It changes the political question.

A policy cannot be assumed to represent **Jewish safety** merely because a Jewish or pro-Israel organisation advocates for it, particularly where its foreseeable consequences fall heavily upon Black people, Palestinians, migrants, queer people or other communities to which Jewish people themselves also belong.

Those people do not stop being Jewish when another part of their identity becomes politically inconvenient.

Nor should disagreement with a particular communal institution be mistaken for departure from Jewish communal life.

There is no reason to assume every Jewish participant arrived there through precisely the same political journey.

Nor is there evidence here for a neat demographic claim that marginalised Jews must be more likely to attend such demonstrations.

But there is an obvious structural question.

If public institutions understood to represent British Jewish opinion are disproportionately shaped by particular historical, political, denominational or demographic experiences, what happens to Jewish people whose lives do not fit comfortably within that representation?

Black Jews find each other.

Queer Jews find each other.

Anti-Zionist Jews find each other.

Jews of Colour find each other.

Disabled Jews find each other.

Mizrahi and Sephardi Jews build spaces around histories which cannot simply be absorbed into an Ashkenazi-European template.

People occupying several of those categories find one another.

They build religious spaces, political organisations, friendship networks, mutual-aid structures and protest communities in which they do not have to choose which part of themselves is permitted into the room.

That is not evidence that every marginalised Jew is anti-Zionist.

It would be equally flattening to claim that.

The point is about **representation and selection**.

The existence of those communities is evidence against treating organised Jewish opinion as synonymous with Jewish opinion.

## 📚 Baldwin's Uncomfortable Question

James Baldwin's 1967 essay *Negroes Are Anti-Semitic Because They're Anti-White* is uncomfortable reading precisely because it refuses an uncomplicated story about relations between two historically persecuted populations.

Baldwin was writing about the United States, not Britain or Israel, and his argument should not simply be exported across borders as though racial systems are identical.

But the problem he identified remains useful.

A community can possess a genuine history of racialised persecution while some of its members simultaneously acquire access to social structures which disadvantage another racialised population.

The fact that antisemitism exists does not prevent anti-Black racism existing among Jews.

The fact that anti-Black racism exists does not make antisemitism imaginary.

The difficult political work begins when neither community is required to deny its own experience in order to acknowledge the other's.

Post-war and post-civil-rights Black–Jewish relations have generated an entire literature because this is not an imaginary tension.

It concerns whiteness.

Class.

Migration.

Neighbourhoods.

Religion.

Political coalition.

Memory.

Assimilation.

Colonialism.

Civil rights.

And the complicated process through which populations once excluded from a racial hierarchy can acquire conditional access to parts of it without the hierarchy itself disappearing.

That is precisely why saying simply **“Jewish people are a minority”** cannot complete an anti-racist analysis.

It starts one.

## 🐆 Israel Had Black Panthers Too

Israel itself supplies an important example.

In 1971, young Mizrahi Jews in Jerusalem's Musrara neighbourhood established the Israeli Black Panthers.

Many were children of Jewish families originating in Morocco and elsewhere in North Africa and the Middle East.

Their complaint was not that Israel had failed to create a Jewish state.

They were living inside one.

Their complaint was that a Jewish state could itself contain profound inequalities between Jews.

The Panthers protested poverty, discrimination, housing conditions, unequal access to resources and the marginalisation of Mizrahi communities within an Israeli society whose political and institutional establishment was heavily Ashkenazi.

Golda Meir's government responded antagonistically. After meeting Panther representatives in 1971, Meir notoriously described them as **“not nice”**.

Their demonstrations were heavily policed.

The Panthers nevertheless became one of the most important challenges to the idea that Jewish national unity had resolved ethnic and class hierarchy among Jews.

Some developed relationships with the radical Israeli left and with Palestinian politics.

That history matters.

Because the proposition that marginalised Jews might understand their own liberation as connected with Palestinian liberation is not a twenty-first-century social-media invention.

Mizrahi activists were making versions of that argument inside Israel more than fifty years ago.

Their politics were not identical.

Their later trajectories were not identical.

Mizrahi Jews themselves subsequently moved in many different political directions.

But the historical existence of the Israeli Black Panthers destroys one particularly convenient fiction:

> **A Jewish state automatically resolves racial hierarchy between Jews.**

It did not.

## 🧳 Zionism Has Baggage Too

None of this discussion begins in 2023.

Nor did Jewish political thought spend two thousand years waiting patiently for everybody to agree upon the State of Israel.

Modern Jewish history contains arguments about what Jews are, what a Jewish nation means, what diaspora means, whether Jewish emancipation requires territory, whether political sovereignty protects Jews, whether nationalism itself is dangerous, whether Jewish cultural autonomy can exist without a state, whether assimilation is possible or desirable, and whether Jewish liberation can meaningfully exist separately from the liberation of the people among whom Jews live.

Those arguments did not disappear because one political project eventually produced a state.

Zionism became extraordinarily consequential.

It did not become the only Jewish answer retroactively.

That matters when contemporary institutions speak as though disagreement with a particular Israeli political settlement represents departure from Jewish political history itself.

It does not.

Jewish disagreement about nationalism is part of Jewish political history.

## 🧩 Zionism Does Not Automatically Dismantle The Racism That Produced Antisemitism

This is where the author's political position should be stated rather than smuggled in as neutral historical fact.

> **My objection to Zionism includes the fact that I do not think relocating Jewish national security outside Europe dismantles the racial order which made European antisemitism possible.**
>
> It changes the proposed solution to Jewish vulnerability.
>
> It does not necessarily dismantle the system which produced that vulnerability.

A Zionist reader may disagree.

There are Zionist traditions which understand Jewish sovereignty itself as an anti-racist response to Jewish racial persecution.

There are liberal and socialist Zionist traditions which have attempted to reconcile Jewish national self-determination with universal equality.

There are contemporary Zionists who oppose occupation, settlement expansion and anti-Palestinian racism.

Their existence matters too.

But none of that makes Zionism an automatic solvent for racism.

No nationalism is granted that property merely because the people constructing it have themselves experienced persecution.

A movement can resist one hierarchy while reproducing another.

A state created partly as an answer to racial persecution can contain racialised inequalities of its own.

The Israeli Black Panthers already demonstrated that problem within the Jewish population itself.

Palestinian experience raises it much more profoundly beyond it.

## 🐚 Zionism Itself Was Not Always Geographically Inevitable

Even the territorial history is more complicated than retrospective national mythology sometimes suggests.

In *Der Judenstaat* in 1896, Theodor Herzl explicitly considered both Palestine and Argentina as possible locations for the political project he proposed.

Herzl later supported consideration of Britain's East Africa proposal following the Kishinev pogrom. It became popularly known as the **Uganda Scheme**, although the territory under consideration lay principally in what is now Kenya.

The proposal produced ferocious disagreement within the Zionist movement and was eventually rejected.

None of this means Jewish historical, religious and cultural relationships with the Land of Israel were invented.

They plainly were not.

It means something more specific:

> **Modern political Zionism itself contained arguments about what territory, sovereignty and Jewish safety were actually for.**

The eventual answer should not be projected backwards as though nobody ever asked the question.

## 🐭 Herzl's Mauschel Problem

Nor should early Zionist thought be sanitised when discussing race and Jewish belonging.

In 1897, Herzl published the polemic *Mauschel* under the pseudonym Benjamin Seff.

“Mauschel” was constructed as a degraded Jewish countertype using characteristics recognisable from contemporary antisemitic representations of Jews, and the text has become an uncomfortable object in scholarship about racialising and antisemitic imagery within early Zionist discourse.

That does not mean:

**Herzl was simply an antisemite and therefore Zionism is antisemitism.**

History is not improved by making it stupid.

It means that a movement created partly in response to European antisemitism could itself absorb categories, hierarchies and representations produced by the European racial environment in which its founders lived.

That is precisely the baggage.

Movements do not leave history behind merely because they intend to escape it.

## 🧭 There Were Other Jewish Maps

There were other answers.

Bundism imagined Jewish political life through diaspora, socialism, Yiddish culture and *doikayt* — hereness — rather than territorial sovereignty in Palestine.

Jewish autonomists developed models of national-cultural rights within multinational states.

Religious anti-Zionists objected for theological reasons.

Assimilationists rejected Jewish nationalism differently.

Territorialists considered territories outside Palestine.

Cultural Zionists imagined a Jewish national centre without necessarily beginning from the same conception of state sovereignty.

Binational currents imagined Jewish and Palestinian political futures differently again.

These traditions disagreed profoundly with one another.

The point is not to turn them into a harmonious anti-Zionist coalition that never existed.

The point is that **Jewish political imagination was plural**.

It still is.

Daniel Boyarin's *The No-State Solution: A Jewish Manifesto* deliberately returns to that older problem. Boyarin attempts to separate Jewish nationhood from state sovereignty and recover the possibility of a diaspora nation. Significantly for this node, the book includes a chapter titled **“Judaïtude/Négritude”**, placing Jewish peoplehood into conversation with Black diasporic thought.

The Black diaspora is not identical to the Jewish diaspora.

It does not need to be.

The comparison opens the possibility that nationhood can exist across territory rather than being made legitimate only when borders are drawn around it.

That is one contemporary Jewish answer.

It is not the only one.

Which is exactly the point.

## 🕯️ Actual Antisemitism Exists

That distinction is particularly important because antisemitism is not imaginary.

Jews do face threats.

Far-right antisemitism exists.

Conspiracy movements recycle ancient antisemitic structures through new vocabulary.

Contemporary fascist organisation deserves attention.

Antisemitism can also appear in spaces which describe themselves as pro-Palestinian, anti-war or anti-racist.

Acknowledging those things does not require accepting every political claim made in the name of Jewish safety.

There is a difference between protecting Jewish people and allowing Jewish vulnerability to become a general-purpose solvent for arguments about Palestine.

The author's position here is deliberately first-person:

> **I know what antisemitism feels like.**
>
> I have experienced it.
>
> I do not need Palestinian children's artwork removed from a hospital to make me safe.
>
> I do not need doctors treating Palestinians turned into objects of suspicion to make me safe.
>
> I need institutions to recognise actual antisemitism when it appears and protect Jewish people from people who actually wish us harm.
>
> **Do not spend the political capital of Jewish safety on this.**

## 🏥 This Is Not UKLFI's First Intervention Into British Healthcare

In February 2023 — before 7 October — UKLFI complained about a display at Chelsea and Westminster Hospital incorporating designs attributed to Palestinian children from UNRWA schools in Gaza.

UKLFI said Jewish patients had felt vulnerable and victimised by the display and objected to political and geographical representations within it.

The hospital subsequently removed the artwork.

This does not establish UKLFI's motivation in the MSF case.

It establishes that the MSF intervention is not the first occasion on which UKLFI has intervened concerning Palestine-related material within a British healthcare environment.

And it raises a useful vocabulary problem.

**Discomfort is not identical to offence.**

**Offence is not identical to distress.**

**Distress is not identical to intimidation.**

**Intimidation is not identical to discrimination.**

**Discrimination is not identical to threat.**

Each can matter.

They should not become interchangeable merely because Jewish safety is invoked.

## 🕯️ Tzedakah Is Mine To Give

The timing adds another layer.

The public controversy emerged during Tishrei and the High Holy Day period.

No claim is made here that this timing was deliberate.

But timing has meaning independent of intention.

The High Holy Days are a period of reflection, repentance, return and responsibility.

Tzedakah is not merely a synonym for feeling generous.

For many Jews, charitable giving forms part of the ethical landscape through which responsibility towards other people is understood.

Jewish donors may give to Israeli organisations.

They may give to Palestinian organisations.

## ⚖️ The Genocide Convention Is Preventive For A Reason

The legal context requires precision.

South Africa instituted proceedings against Israel under the Genocide Convention on 29 December 2023.

The ICJ held public hearings on provisional measures in January 2024.

On 26 January, the Court found prima facie jurisdiction and found that at least some of the rights claimed by South Africa were plausible, including the right of Palestinians in Gaza to be protected from acts of genocide and related prohibited acts. It ordered provisional measures including measures relating to prevention and humanitarian assistance.

That was **not** a final judicial determination that genocide had occurred.

But neither was it legally equivalent to nothing having happened.

On 28 March 2024, the Court strengthened humanitarian requirements, including the unhindered provision at scale of urgently needed assistance such as food, water, electricity, fuel, shelter, sanitation, **medical supplies and medical care**.

The Court again addressed provisional measures in May.

The author of this node describes the destruction of Gaza as genocide. That political and legal characterisation remains contested in international proceedings and should not be confused with a final ICJ merits judgment which has not yet occurred.

The policy question for Britain can nevertheless be stated without pretending the merits have already been decided:

> **Given the ICJ proceedings, the provisional protection of Palestinians' plausible Genocide Convention rights, Britain's own prevention obligations, and the Court's repeated attention to medical and humanitarian provision, what assessment has Britain made of the foreseeable effect of interfering with a major medical provider serving Palestinians in Gaza?**

That does not predetermine the answer.

It requires the answer to exist.

## 🌊 Humanitarian Capacity Cannot Always Be Restored Later

UNRWA provides a warning about sequencing.

In early 2024, Israel made serious allegations concerning UNRWA employees.

The United Nations investigated them.

Donor states suspended or paused substantial funding in the immediate aftermath.

Subsequent investigations produced differentiated findings rather than one clean answer: some cases lacked sufficient evidence; other cases were considered serious enough for employment consequences.

Britain later resumed funding after reviewing independent work and reform plans.

The MSF allegations are not the UNRWA allegations.

But the temporal lesson matters.

Humanitarian capacity can be interrupted very quickly.

Restoring it later does not restore what happened in between.

You cannot retrospectively perform an operation that never occurred.

You cannot retrospectively provide antibiotics before sepsis.

You cannot retrospectively provide clean water before infection.

You cannot retrospectively reverse a preventable death.

Precaution therefore runs in both directions.

Investigate security risks.

And investigate the risks created by the response.

## 🚑 What A Checkpoint Means When You Need A Doctor

The humanitarian discussion cannot remain abstract.

In 2026, OCHA and WHO have continued documenting the effects of movement restrictions on Palestinian healthcare in the West Bank.

OCHA reported that, between 28 February and 3 March alone, WHO recorded nine incidents in which ambulances were obstructed. Reports included ambulances held at gunpoint at checkpoints, searches, forced diversions and delays. In one case in the Salfit area, a woman in labour reportedly had to cross on foot because an ambulance was prevented from reaching her.

In July 2026, Palestinian health workers reported that a 28-year-old woman from Qaryout, six months pregnant and suffering severe bleeding, was transported towards Rafidia Hospital. Road closures forced detours. At the Awarta checkpoint the ambulance encountered a closed gate and delay. Her baby did not survive an emergency delivery.

Human Rights Watch reviewed video from the ambulance and reported that the woman's life was also endangered. The Israeli military disputed aspects of the account, saying the emergency vehicle had not completed required coordination and that the security check lasted only a few minutes.

That disagreement should be reported.

So should the outcome.

A critically ill pregnant woman was trying to reach hospital through a system of military movement controls.

Her baby died.

WHO has documented dozens of delays in medical care in the West Bank during 2026.

This is what occupation means when the thing on the other side of the checkpoint is an obstetric theatre.

It is not merely a map.

It is time.

And medicine is full of situations in which time is tissue, time is oxygen, time is blood and time is life.  

And while Britain is being invited to scrutinise Palestinian medical workers through the language of terrorism, it should remember **Dr Hussam Abu Safiya**, paediatrician and director of Kamal Adwan Hospital. Israeli forces detained him during the hospital raid of 27 December 2024; nearly twenty-one months later, he remains detained without charge or trial under Israel's Unlawful Combatants Law. Amnesty International reported in June 2026 that his detention had again been extended, until at least October, and that he had been held in solitary confinement while being denied adequate healthcare.

That belongs inside any serious assessment of the chilling effect upon Palestinian medicine. Britain is being asked to consider extraordinary action against an international medical organisation while one of Gaza's most prominent doctors remains imprisoned without charge. **If the subject before us is whether Palestinian healthcare has become securitised, Dr Abu Safiya cannot sensibly be left outside the frame.**

## ♟️ Sometimes The Legal Environment Is Part Of The Outcome

Journalists should also resist assuming that the significance of legal advocacy can be measured only by whether the applicant ultimately wins.

Strategic litigation, threatened litigation and repeated regulatory intervention can have effects **before a court reaches the merits — and sometimes without proceedings being issued at all**.

A credible legal challenge can require an institution to obtain advice, preserve records, reconsider its reasoning, conduct additional risk assessment, delay a decision or change the way future decisions are documented. Repeated interventions can also create an environment in which policymakers, charities, universities, banks, hospitals, journalists and other institutions anticipate legal or regulatory challenge before acting.

None of that establishes that creating such an environment is UKLFI's purpose in the MSF case.

But it means journalists should distinguish between at least three possible measures of a legal intervention:

1. **Did the organisation win the legal argument?**
2. **Did the intervention produce the requested policy outcome?**
3. **Did making the intervention itself alter the institutional risk environment?**

The third question matters particularly here.

MSF does not have to be proscribed for a proscription request to become searchable adverse media.

The Home Secretary does not have to agree with UKLFI for another institution to notice that the Home Secretary has been asked.

A regulator does not have to make an adverse finding before a compliance department asks whether the existence of the complaint changes its risk assessment.

And a legal proposition does not have to succeed in court before officials begin anticipating that similar decisions may generate further litigation.

This is not peculiar to UKLFI. Strategic and public-interest litigation is used across political movements precisely because law can operate upon **institutions as well as judgments**. Palestinian rights organisations, environmental organisations, civil-liberties groups, businesses and campaigning organisations all use litigation and threatened litigation to test, constrain or alter government behaviour.

The journalistic mistake would therefore be to report each intervention as an entirely isolated contest:

> **Organisation makes allegation → institution responds → organisation wins or loses.**

The larger reporting question is:

> **What environment does a sustained pattern of legal and regulatory intervention create around the people making decisions?**

That question can be investigated empirically without claiming to know anybody's private intention.

Map the interventions.

Map the recipients.

Map the legal mechanisms.

Map the remedies requested.

Map which proceedings were actually issued.

Map which succeeded.

Map which failed.

Map which institutions changed their behaviour anyway.

Then the pattern, if there is one, can speak for itself.

**Sometimes the judgment is the outcome. Sometimes the changed risk calculation is an outcome too.**

## 📯 Terrorism Is Not Ordinary Political Vocabulary

Alleging terrorism is profoundly serious.

English defamation law has its own tests and defences, and this node does not determine whether any particular article or statement is defamatory.

But journalistic precision matters independently of litigation.

There is a substantial difference between reporting:

> UKLFI has asked the Home Secretary to consider proscription.

and reporting:

> Israel alleges that an employee had a militant affiliation.

and establishing:

> independent evidence corroborates that employee's affiliation.

and asserting:

> MSF knowingly supports terrorism.

and concluding:

> MSF is a terrorist organisation.

Those statements are not synonyms.

The seriousness of an allegation increases rather than diminishes the obligation to explain exactly **who says what, about whom, and on what evidence**.

## 💳 The Financial System Has Already Heard The Allegation

There is also a much less philosophical reason the UKLFI intervention matters **before any government decision has been made**.

Financial risk systems do not wait for philosophy seminars.

Banks, payment processors, insurers, compliance teams, institutional donors, grant-makers, employers and counterparties operate through risk classification.

Terrorism allegations are therefore capable of producing effects before proscription.

That does **not** mean every bank will automatically terminate its relationship with MSF because UKLFI made an allegation.

It means the allegation can become information which compliance systems must decide how to treat.

An institution may ask:

- Has a terrorism allegation been made?
- By whom?
- Is a government considering it?
- Is a regulator examining related concerns?
- Are there sanctions implications?
- Is enhanced due diligence required?
- Does a transaction require manual review?
- Does an institutional relationship now create reputational risk?

The answers can generate friction without anybody formally being found guilty of anything.

This phenomenon is already familiar in humanitarian finance.

Banks' attempts to avoid sanctions, counter-terrorism and money-laundering exposure can produce **de-risking**: restricting services to organisations, regions or populations perceived as unusually expensive or dangerous to service.

For humanitarian organisations operating in conflict zones, that can mean delayed transfers, rejected transactions, increased compliance costs and difficulty moving money to precisely the places where legitimate humanitarian work is hardest to perform.

So the policy question begins **now**.

Not on the hypothetical day MSF is proscribed.

## 💳 What Happens To The Person Who Donated Ten Years Ago?

A person who lawfully donated to MSF ten years ago does not become retrospectively guilty of supporting terrorism merely because somebody later makes an allegation or because an organisation were hypothetically proscribed years afterwards.

That is not how criminal liability should be described.

But criminal liability is not the only way contemporary financial systems produce consequences.

Historical relationships can appear within due-diligence data.

Institutions can retain transaction records.

Customers can be asked about previous relationships.

Risk models can incorporate adverse media.

Enhanced due diligence can create questions which would never otherwise have been asked.

That makes careless public allegations consequential even where they never produce prosecution.

The right response is not panic.

It is **data discipline**.

Banks, screening providers and compliance teams should distinguish:

**allegation**

from

**regulatory investigation**

from

**government consideration**

from

**proscription**

from

**criminal conviction**.

If those categories collapse into one another inside automated or semi-automated risk systems, a legal distinction carefully preserved by British law can disappear inside a database.

That is itself a governance problem.

*UKLFI, Which Planet Are You On?* is a living fact-checking and governance node of the **Polaris Protocol**.

Its purpose is not to prevent scrutiny of humanitarian organisations. It is to preserve the evidential boundary between allegations concerning individuals, institutional governance failures and the much more serious legal conclusion that an organisation itself is concerned in terrorism.
