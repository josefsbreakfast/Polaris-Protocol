## 🛰️ Orientation

This folder is the external-power branch of **🇵🇸 Palestine Factchecking**.

It exists because Israel and Palestine do not exist inside a sealed bilateral system.

Questions about the conflict routinely depend on the actions of:

- the United States;
- the United Kingdom;
- European states;
- the European Union;
- Iran;
- Egypt;
- Jordan;
- Qatar;
- Saudi Arabia;
- the United Arab Emirates;
- Turkey;
- Russia;
- China;
- international organisations;
- military alliances;
- intelligence relationships;
- banks;
- investors;
- defence companies;
- technology companies;
- public-relations firms;
- and other transnational actors.

Some influence is obvious.

Some is financial.

Some is diplomatic.

Some is military.

Some is legal.

Some is informational.

Some is infrastructural.

Some is indirect.

The purpose of this branch is to identify **who is doing what, through which mechanism, with what evidence, and with what degree of effect**.

## 🧭 Start With The Mechanism

“Country X supports Israel” is too vague to factcheck properly.

Support can mean:

- diplomatic recognition;
- military aid;
- intelligence cooperation;
- weapons sales;
- export licences;
- political cover;
- votes in international institutions;
- financial guarantees;
- technology transfer;
- trade;
- investment;
- sanctions relief;
- public diplomacy;
- mediation;
- humanitarian aid;
- legal intervention;
- or private-sector activity tolerated or encouraged by the state.

Likewise:

> **Country X supports Palestine**

can describe very different things.

Define the mechanism before evaluating the claim.

---

## 🧬 Separate State Policy From Private Activity

A company headquartered in a country is not automatically acting on behalf of that government.

A bank financing a company does not automatically establish state policy.

A lobbying firm working for a foreign client may be registered and regulated rather than covert.

A university partnership is not the same thing as a diplomatic treaty.

A charitable donation is not the same thing as state aid.

Where the question is about foreign power, distinguish:

- government action;
- state-owned enterprise;
- publicly funded institution;
- regulated private actor;
- contractor;
- lobbyist;
- investor;
- NGO;
- and independent civil society.

Do not widen attribution beyond the evidence.

## 📊 Money Needs Accounting Verbs

For financial claims, preserve whether money was:

- announced;
- authorised;
- appropriated;
- allocated;
- contracted;
- committed;
- obligated;
- disbursed;
- received;
- loaned;
- guaranteed;
- invested;
- divested;
- or spent.

These are not synonyms.

A headline claiming a state “gave” a certain sum may conceal several different financial mechanisms.

## 🧿 Influence Is Not Control

This principle should recur throughout the branch.

A powerful state may have:

- leverage;
- dependency;
- alliance relationships;
- financial influence;
- intelligence access;
- diplomatic weight;
- or military supply relationships.

None automatically establishes control over another actor's every decision.

Use the narrowest defensible term:

**relationship → support → influence → leverage → coordination → direction → control**

Each step requires more evidence.

---

## 🧬 Coordination Needs Evidence

Visible policy alignment can result from:

- common interests;
- alliance structures;
- shared intelligence;
- economic incentives;
- diplomatic habit;
- ideological affinity;
- legal obligations;
- or direct coordination.

Do not jump straight to secret direction.

Where coordination is alleged, look for:

- meetings;
- agreements;
- communications;
- operational planning;
- shared documents;
- contract structures;
- or other direct evidence.

---

## 📈 Material Effect Matters

A state can issue strong statements with little practical effect.

Another may say very little while supplying:

- weapons;
- finance;
- intelligence;
- diplomatic protection;
- or logistical support.

This branch should therefore distinguish:

> **rhetorical position**

from:

> **material contribution.**

Both can matter.

They are not the same.

---

## 🪿 Global Power Is Experienced Locally

Foreign policy can feel abstract.

Its effects are not.

A veto can affect diplomatic options.

An export licence can affect weapon availability.

A banking decision can affect financial flows.

A border decision can affect movement.

A sanctions regime can affect everyday transactions.

A public-diplomacy campaign can affect what information becomes visible.

A cloud or surveillance contract can affect infrastructure.

This is where Global Powers connects to **🪿 Embodying The Information Ecology** and the wider Polaris concern with systems becoming lived conditions.

## 🚧 Common Global-Power Failure Modes

Watch for:

- treating “support” as one undefined category;
- confusing authorisation with delivery;
- confusing delivery with operational use;
- treating alliance as control;
- treating influence as command;
- attributing private corporate activity directly to a state without evidence;
- treating state rhetoric as evidence of material action;
- ignoring quiet material support because it lacks dramatic statements;
- treating one vote as a complete foreign-policy position;
- assuming all EU or Arab states act as blocs;
- counting gross contract values as money already spent;
- treating lobbying registration as proof of covert influence;
- assuming financial exposure proves political agreement;
- and inferring causation merely because a powerful actor had the capacity to influence an outcome.
