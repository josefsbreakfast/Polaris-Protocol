# 🧵 Identity & Language

**Status:** scaffold / awaiting terminology, translation and source review  
**First created:** 2026-08-20 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-20  
*Factchecking identity claims, political vocabulary, translation, naming and contested language around Palestine without pretending that every dispute is either “just semantics” or solvable by dictionary.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

This folder is the identity-and-language branch of **🇵🇸 Palestine Factchecking**.

It exists because some of the most persistent questions about Israel and Palestine are questions about words:

- Who is Palestinian?
- Who is Israeli?
- Who is Jewish?
- Who is Arab?
- What does Zionism mean?
- What does anti-Zionism mean?
- Is anti-Zionism antisemitic?
- What does indigeneity mean here?
- What does settler mean?
- What does colonisation mean?
- What is a refugee?
- What does diaspora mean?
- What does *intifada* mean?
- What does *Nakba* mean?
- What does *from the river to the sea* mean?
- What does a particular Arabic or Hebrew slogan actually say?
- Is a translation accurate?
- Is a historical term being used as though its meaning never changed?
- Is a legal term being used politically?
- Is a political identity being mistaken for citizenship, ethnicity, religion or ancestry?

These questions can contain factual components.

They can also contain:

- history;
- self-identification;
- law;
- translation;
- political theory;
- religious meaning;
- social convention;
- and normative disagreement.

The task is not to force all of that into one dictionary definition.

The task is to identify **which kind of claim is actually being made**.

---

## 🧭 Identity Categories Are Not Interchangeable

Do not casually collapse:

- nationality;
- citizenship;
- ethnicity;
- religion;
- ancestry;
- language;
- political ideology;
- geographic origin;
- legal status;
- communal membership;
- and self-identification.

A person can belong to several categories at once.

A category may also be externally imposed rather than personally chosen.

Questions such as:

> **Is this person Israeli?**

and:

> **Is this person Jewish?**

and:

> **Is this person a Zionist?**

are not three versions of the same question.

Neither are:

> **Palestinian**

> **Arab**

> **Muslim**

These distinctions should survive editing.

---

## 🧵 Self-Identification Matters

Where the question concerns how an identifiable person or community describes itself, self-identification is evidence.

It is not necessarily the only relevant evidence.

Legal nationality, census classification, historical terminology and external social classification may differ.

A careful node should distinguish:

> **how someone identifies**

from:

> **how a state classifies them**

from:

> **how a historian describes the population**

from:

> **how an opponent labels them.**

Do not silently substitute one for another.

---

## ⏳ Words Have Histories

Political vocabulary changes over time.

A term used in:

- 1880;
- 1917;
- 1948;
- 1967;
- 1993;
- 2023;
- or 2026

may not carry exactly the same political or social meaning.

Historical quotations should therefore be read in historical context.

Ask:

- Who used the term?
- When?
- In which language?
- For which audience?
- What alternatives existed?
- Was the meaning contested at the time?
- Are we importing a later definition backwards?

A word's present political charge does not tell us exactly what an earlier speaker meant.

---

## 🌍 Translation Is Evidence Handling

Translation is not a decorative final step.

It can determine what proposition the reader believes was expressed.

For consequential material, preserve where practical:

- original-language text;
- speaker or writer;
- date;
- source;
- surrounding sentence or passage;
- literal translation;
- idiomatic translation;
- meaningful ambiguity;
- and translator or translation method.

A viral subtitle should not become canonical merely because it is already in English.

---

## 🗣️ Literal And Idiomatic Meaning Are Different

A literal translation can be technically accurate and communicatively misleading.

An idiomatic translation can communicate meaning well while obscuring important lexical choices.

Where the distinction matters, show both.

For example, a useful translation note may ask:

- What does each word literally mean?
- What would a fluent speaker hear?
- Is the phrase idiomatic?
- Is it formal, colloquial, religious, military, insulting, humorous or archaic?
- Does the expression carry political associations not visible in English?

Translation should preserve register as well as vocabulary where possible.

---

## 🔤 Transliteration Is Not Translation

Arabic and Hebrew can be represented in Latin characters in several ways.

Different spellings may refer to the same word or name.

Do not infer substantive difference merely from transliteration.

A node may need to record:

- original script;
- common English spelling;
- alternative transliterations;
- pronunciation where relevant;
- and why a particular form is used in the archive.

Search strategy matters here too.

A source may be missed because the researcher searched only one English spelling.

---

## 🪞 Naming Is Not Neutral — But Neither Is Every Name A Verdict

Places, wars, political movements and historical events can have multiple names.

Examples may differ because of:

- language;
- historical period;
- political perspective;
- legal status;
- community usage;
- colonial administration;
- religious tradition;
- or later memory.

The archive should not pretend naming choices are meaningless.

It should also avoid treating every use of one conventional name as proof of a speaker's entire political programme.

Where naming is contested, explain the alternatives.

---

## 🗺️ Place Names Need Dates And Context

A place may have:

- Arabic names;
- Hebrew names;
- English exonyms;
- Ottoman-era forms;
- Mandate-era forms;
- modern municipal names;
- historical village names;
- and renamed or transliterated variants.

For historical work, preserve the name appropriate to the period where useful.

For current work, choose wording that allows the reader to identify the place without quietly erasing relevant historical naming.

Aliases can be useful evidence.

---

## ✡️ Jewish Identity Is Not A Single Political Position

Jewishness can refer to overlapping dimensions of:

- religion;
- peoplehood;
- ethnicity;
- ancestry;
- culture;
- community;
- and self-identification.

It does not establish a person's position on:

- Zionism;
- the Israeli government;
- Palestinian rights;
- war;
- occupation;
- settlements;
- or any other political question.

Do not use:

> **Jewish**

as shorthand for:

> **Israeli**

or:

> **Zionist**

or:

> **supporter of the Israeli government.**

Likewise, criticism of Israel should not be automatically generalised to Jews.

The distinctions matter both factually and for antisemitism analysis.

---

## 🇵🇸 Palestinian Identity Is Not A Single Political Position

Palestinian identity likewise does not establish:

- religion;
- citizenship;
- residence;
- factional affiliation;
- support for Hamas;
- support for Fatah;
- support for armed resistance;
- views on a two-state or one-state settlement;
- or any other political position.

Palestinians may be:

- Muslim;
- Christian;
- members of other communities;
- religious;
- secular;
- citizens of different states;
- refugees;
- residents under different legal regimes;
- members of the diaspora;
- or people with multiple overlapping identities.

Do not turn nationality or peoplehood into a political questionnaire.

---

## 🇮🇱 Israeli Identity Is Not Synonymous With Jewish Identity

Israeli citizenship and Jewish identity overlap for many people.

They are not identical categories.

Israel includes citizens and residents from multiple:

- ethnic;
- religious;
- linguistic;
- and political communities.

When a proposition concerns:

- Israeli citizens;
- Israeli Jews;
- Jewish people globally;
- Hebrew speakers;
- Israeli government policy;
- or Zionists,

use the category actually supported by the evidence.

Do not widen it casually.

---

## 🧿 Zionism Requires Definition

“Zionism” has referred to multiple ideological traditions and political projects over time.

Questions about Zionism may concern:

- historical political Zionism;
- religious Zionism;
- labour Zionism;
- revisionist Zionism;
- cultural Zionism;
- support for Jewish national self-determination;
- support for the existence of Israel;
- support for particular territorial claims;
- or contemporary political identities using the label.

A node should therefore ask:

> **Which Zionism?**

and:

> **Whose definition?**

Do not use one activist, governmental, academic or dictionary definition as though the term has never been internally contested.

---

## 🧿 Anti-Zionism Requires Definition Too

Anti-Zionism can likewise refer to different positions, including opposition to:

- historical Zionist movements;
- ethnonationalism;
- a specifically Jewish state;
- the present constitutional structure of Israel;
- territorial expansion;
- or Zionism as understood by the speaker.

Those positions are not necessarily identical.

A factcheck should identify the proposition rather than infer it from the label alone.

---

## 🕯️ Antisemitism Questions Need Precision

Questions about antisemitism can involve:

- explicit hostility to Jews;
- stereotypes;
- conspiracy narratives;
- collective blame;
- Holocaust denial or distortion;
- discriminatory exclusion;
- coded language;
- rhetoric about Israel;
- rhetoric about Zionism;
- or disputes over definitions and institutional guidance.

Do not assume:

> **criticism of Israel = antisemitism.**

Do not assume:

> **language about Israel can never be antisemitic.**

Context, wording, target, inference and pattern matter.

Where competing definitions or frameworks are relevant, identify them rather than pretending one contested document has magically ended the argument.

---

## 🧿 Anti-Palestinian Racism And Dehumanisation Need Precision Too

Palestinians may be subjected to:

- racialisation;
- collective suspicion;
- dehumanising language;
- assumptions of terrorist affiliation;
- denial of national or historical identity;
- discriminatory exclusion;
- and collective blame.

Again, context matters.

Criticism of a Palestinian political organisation is not automatically hostility to Palestinians.

Treating Palestinians as inherently violent or collectively responsible is a different proposition.

The archive should preserve that distinction.

---

## 🏺 Indigeneity Is Not A One-Line Gotcha

Claims about indigeneity can involve:

- historical continuity;
- ethnogenesis;
- language;
- culture;
- ancestral connection;
- displacement;
- colonial relationships;
- legal frameworks;
- political theory;
- and contemporary identity.

Different academic and legal frameworks may use the concept differently.

The fact that two populations have deep historical connections to the same land is not logically impossible.

A node on indigeneity should therefore define the framework before attempting the conclusion.

Do not use the word merely as a synonym for:

> **the people I believe have the stronger political claim.**

---

## 🏘️ Settler, Settlement And Settler Colonialism Are Different Claims

These terms can operate at different analytical levels.

### Settler

May describe a person residing in a settlement, but context matters.

### Settlement

May have a specific geographic, administrative or legal meaning.

### Settler colonialism

Is a historical and theoretical framework used to analyse particular forms of political and territorial structure.

Using one does not automatically establish all the others.

Where the question is legal, route to `⚖️_Law/`.

Where it is historical, route to `🫒_History/`.

Where it is theoretical or ethical, say so.

---

## 🗝️ Refugee Is A Legal, Administrative And Social Term

“Refugee” can refer to:

- a legal status;
- registration with a particular agency;
- displacement history;
- inherited or family status under particular institutional systems;
- or ordinary-language description.

Questions about Palestinian refugees often require careful distinction between:

- historical displacement;
- present registration;
- legal status;
- nationality;
- residence;
- descendants;
- and claims concerning return or compensation.

Do not solve those questions by assuming one everyday definition controls every legal and institutional context.

---

## ⚔️ Terrorism, Militancy, Armed Group And Resistance

These terms can contain:

- legal classification;
- government designation;
- journalistic convention;
- political judgement;
- self-description;
- and moral evaluation.

When an organisation is formally designated under a particular jurisdiction, identify:

- the jurisdiction;
- the designation;
- the date;
- and the legal consequence relevant to the question.

Do not silently universalise one state's designation into a timeless global definition.

Likewise, the term **resistance** can describe a political self-conception without resolving whether particular conduct is lawful or ethical.

Language does not substitute for conduct analysis.

---

## 🕊️ Civilian And Combatant Are Not Vibes

These terms have legal significance.

Do not infer combatant status merely from:

- sex;
- age;
- political opinion;
- location;
- family relationship;
- clothing;
- or social-media rhetoric.

Likewise, civilian status does not mean a person is politically neutral or morally admirable.

Legal status and political identity are different questions.

Route detailed classification questions to `⚖️_Law/`.

---

## 📣 Slogans Need Context

Political slogans are compressed language.

Their meaning can depend on:

- speaker;
- audience;
- language;
- historical origin;
- surrounding speech;
- movement;
- time period;
- and intended political programme.

A slogan may also be deliberately broad enough to permit different speakers to mean different things.

Later nodes may need to examine recurring phrases individually.

A useful slogan factcheck should ask:

1. What is the phrase?
2. What language is it in?
3. What is the literal translation?
4. What is its historical usage?
5. Who uses it?
6. What meanings do users attribute to it?
7. What meanings do opponents hear in it?
8. Is one interpretation demonstrably universal?
9. Does context narrow the meaning in the specific instance being checked?

Do not factcheck a slogan by choosing the most alarming possible interpretation and declaring it the dictionary definition.

Do not choose the most benign possible interpretation either.

## 🧬 Identity Claims Can Be Historical Claims

Questions such as:

> **Did Palestinians exist before 1948?**

or:

> **Were Jews indigenous to the region?**

or:

> **When did Israeli identity emerge?**

cannot be resolved by modern identity documents alone.

They require historical evidence concerning:

- terminology;
- institutions;
- self-description;
- communal continuity;
- political movements;
- population;
- and changing identities.

Route the historical evidence to `🫒_History/` and cross-link rather than duplicating the entire argument here.

---

## ⚖️ Identity Claims Can Be Legal Claims

Some identity words also carry legal consequences.

Examples may involve:

- citizenship;
- nationality;
- refugee status;
- protected characteristics;
- minority rights;
- residency;
- discrimination;
- indigenous rights frameworks;
- and proscribed organisations.

Where the dispute turns on legal status, route to `⚖️_Law/`.

The social meaning of an identity and its legal classification may differ.

---

## 📊 Identity Categories Can Distort Statistics

Quantitative claims depend on category definitions.

Ask:

- Who counts as Palestinian?
- Who counts as Israeli?
- Are residents and citizens distinguished?
- Are East Jerusalem Palestinians classified separately?
- Are settlers included in a territorial population?
- What does a census category mean?
- How are refugees counted?
- What age or sex categories are used?
- Does the category change across datasets?

Route methodological disputes to `📊_Statistics/`.

A demographic category is not self-explanatory.

---

## 🤖 AI And Search Can Flatten Contested Language

Search engines and generative AI often prefer a single concise definition.

That can be dangerous when the accurate answer is:

> **the term has several historically and politically important meanings.**

Highly indexed institutional definitions may dominate retrieval.

Public-relations and SEO campaigns may also affect which formulations become easy to encounter.

This does not mean every AI definition is manipulated.

It means language questions should be followed to inspectable:

- dictionaries;
- historical sources;
- legal texts;
- academic work;
- community usage;
- and primary statements

rather than treating fluent synthesis as lexical authority.

---

## 🚧 Common Identity And Language Failure Modes

Watch for:

- treating citizenship, ethnicity, religion and ideology as interchangeable;
- assigning political beliefs from identity alone;
- using one definition of Zionism or anti-Zionism for every historical period;
- treating every criticism of Israel as antisemitic;
- treating antisemitism as impossible when rhetoric is framed as anti-Zionism;
- treating every criticism of Palestinian politics as anti-Palestinian racism;
- treating Palestinians collectively as responsible for an armed group;
- treating Jews collectively as responsible for the Israeli state;
- assuming one translation is uncontested;
- stripping slogans of speaker and context;
- using legal terminology as ordinary political insult;
- using ordinary political terminology as though it were already a legal finding;
- projecting modern identities backwards without evidence;
- treating transliteration differences as different words;
- and using dictionary definitions to erase historical or community usage.
