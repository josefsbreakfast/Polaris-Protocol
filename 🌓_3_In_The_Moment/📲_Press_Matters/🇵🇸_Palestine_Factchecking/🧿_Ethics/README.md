# 🧿 Ethics

**Status:** scaffold / awaiting ethical-question and source map  
**First created:** 2026-08-20 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-20  
*Ethical reasoning routes for Palestine: separating moral judgement from factual and legal claims while taking suffering, responsibility, power, dignity and political choice seriously.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

This folder is the ethical branch of **🇵🇸 Palestine Factchecking**.

It exists because some of the most important questions people ask about Israel and Palestine are not fully answerable by finding one more source.

Questions such as:

- Is this justified?
- What do civilians owe one another?
- Can violence ever be legitimate?
- What counts as collective punishment morally, beyond the legal test?
- What responsibility does a population bear for its government or armed groups?
- What responsibility does a state bear for foreseeable civilian harm?
- What do powerful allies owe people affected by their support?
- Is neutrality ethical?
- What is complicity?
- What does proportionality mean outside a courtroom?
- What does justice require after mass violence?
- What does self-defence permit morally?
- Can resistance remain morally defensible if some tactics are not?
- What is owed to hostages, detainees, displaced people and survivors?
- What does reconciliation require?
- What happens when one group's safety is repeatedly framed as incompatible with another group's freedom?

These are not merely factual disputes.

But facts still matter enormously.

Ethical reasoning built on false history, wrong numbers, invented quotations or inaccurate legal claims is still bad reasoning.

This branch therefore sits **after** the evidential work, not instead of it.

---

## 🧿 What This Folder Is For

This branch should eventually help readers distinguish:

- empirical claims;
- legal claims;
- moral claims;
- political strategy;
- emotional reaction;
- and ethical principle.

It should make visible where an argument moves from:

> **this happened**

to:

> **therefore this was justified**

or:

> **this was unlawful**

to:

> **therefore every response to it is morally permissible**

or:

> **this population suffered**

to:

> **therefore this population bears no responsibility for anything done in its name.**

Those transitions need arguments.

They should not be hidden inside factual language.

---

## ⚖️ Law And Ethics Are Not The Same Thing

Something can be:

- lawful and morally wrong;
- unlawful and morally understandable;
- legally unresolved and ethically horrifying;
- legally permitted but politically reckless;
- legally prohibited but motivated by circumstances that still matter to moral analysis.

The law provides important constraints and concepts.

It does not exhaust ethics.

Likewise, moral certainty does not alter the content of law.

Route legal classification to `⚖️_Law/`.

Use this folder for the normative question that remains.

---

## 📊 Numbers Inform Ethics — They Do Not Decide It

Casualty figures matter.

So do:

- age;
- civilian status;
- displacement;
- starvation;
- destruction;
- captivity;
- injury;
- disability;
- and duration.

But ethics cannot be reduced to arithmetic.

A lower death toll does not automatically make a policy just.

A higher death toll does not automatically answer every question about intent, responsibility or permissible response.

Numbers constrain moral arguments.

They do not replace them.

Route quantitative disputes to `📊_Statistics/`.

---

## 🧭 State The Moral Proposition

Before debating an ethical question, make the normative claim explicit.

Compare:

> **Civilians were killed.**

with:

> **Killing those civilians was morally impermissible.**

with:

> **Any action foreseeably risking civilian death was morally impermissible.**

with:

> **The action was permissible because it pursued a sufficiently important military objective.**

with:

> **The objective itself was illegitimate.**

These are different arguments.

Do not let moral premises hide inside verbs such as:

- deserved;
- necessary;
- inevitable;
- proportionate;
- justified;
- defensive;
- retaliatory;
- or reasonable.

---

## 🧬 Moral Responsibility Has Levels

Ethical reasoning should distinguish between:

- individual responsibility;
- command responsibility;
- institutional responsibility;
- state responsibility;
- voter responsibility;
- bystander responsibility;
- ally responsibility;
- corporate responsibility;
- and collective identity.

These are not interchangeable.

A government's action does not automatically make every citizen morally responsible.

An armed group's action does not automatically make every civilian who shares its nationality, religion or territory responsible.

Voting, support, silence, fear, coercion, age, political power and available alternatives all matter.

Collective blame is usually where moral reasoning becomes lazy very quickly.

---

## 🧍 Civilians Are Not Moral Blank Pages

Civilian protection is not dependent on political innocence.

A civilian may hold:

- hateful views;
- nationalist views;
- militarist views;
- racist views;
- extremist views;
- or views the author finds morally repellent.

That does not automatically erase civilian status or make harm morally trivial.

Likewise, being a civilian does not mean a person has no agency, politics or responsibility for anything.

The ethical question is more precise than:

> **good person or bad person?**

---

## ⚔️ Armed Resistance

This branch should eventually distinguish between:

- a claimed right to resist;
- the moral legitimacy of resisting domination or occupation;
- the permissibility of particular tactics;
- the choice of targets;
- treatment of captives;
- foreseeable civilian harm;
- and strategic consequences.

It is possible to believe resistance can be morally legitimate while condemning particular acts.

It is also possible to believe a particular act was morally understandable without believing it was justified.

Do not collapse cause, motive, tactic and moral permission into one category.

---

## 🛡️ Self-Defence

Claims of self-defence likewise need decomposition.

Questions may include:

- self-defence by whom?
- against what threat?
- immediate or long-term?
- individual or collective?
- defensive objective or retaliatory objective?
- what alternatives existed?
- what harms were foreseeable?
- who bears those harms?
- what limits should apply even when the defensive claim is legitimate?

The existence of a real threat does not automatically justify every response.

The existence of civilian harm does not automatically prove that no defensive action was morally permissible.

The hard work is in the limits.

---

## 💥 Retaliation And Punishment

Retaliation can refer to:

- deterrence;
- punishment;
- revenge;
- coercion;
- restoration of credibility;
- incapacitation;
- or emotional demand for equivalence.

These are morally distinct.

A policy justified publicly as deterrence may function partly as punishment.

A demand for punishment may be rhetorically reframed as security.

This branch should ask what moral purpose is actually being defended.

---

## 🧯 Collective Punishment As A Moral Problem

Even where legal analysis belongs elsewhere, collective punishment also raises a basic ethical problem:

> **When is harm imposed on people for actions they did not individually choose or control?**

Questions include:

- what choices were realistically available?
- what degree of collective agency existed?
- who had political power?
- who could leave?
- who was a child?
- who opposed the relevant actor?
- who was coerced?
- who had no meaningful control over events?

A moral argument that assigns responsibility to millions of people at once needs unusually careful justification.

---

## 🪞 Intent Matters — Outcomes Matter Too

Ethical disputes often polarise around:

> **They intended harm**

versus:

> **They did not intend harm.**

Intent matters.

So do foreseeable consequences.

A policy can be morally reckless without the worst outcome being its explicit goal.

A good intention does not erase predictable harm.

A harmful outcome does not automatically prove malicious intent.

The ethical analysis should preserve both.

---

## 🔬 Foreseeability

A central moral question is often:

> **What could a reasonable decision-maker have expected to happen?**

This may depend on:

- prior incidents;
- intelligence;
- warnings;
- infrastructure conditions;
- geography;
- civilian density;
- evacuation feasibility;
- health-system capacity;
- food availability;
- and known patterns of displacement.

Foreseeability is an evidential question feeding a moral one.

Do not infer it from hindsight alone.

---

## 🚪 Choice Under Constraint

People act under unequal conditions.

Ethical analysis should ask what alternatives were genuinely available.

This applies to:

- civilians;
- soldiers;
- political leaders;
- hostages;
- detainees;
- aid workers;
- protesters;
- journalists;
- foreign governments;
- and institutions.

The existence of an abstract alternative does not mean it was practically available.

Conversely, constraint does not erase all agency.

Avoid both romanticising powerlessness and exaggerating freedom.

---

## 🌍 Power Changes Responsibility

The capacity to cause harm matters morally.

So does the capacity to prevent it.

Actors with:

- military superiority;
- border control;
- financial leverage;
- diplomatic power;
- intelligence capability;
- technological access;
- or institutional authority

may have responsibilities unavailable to weaker actors.

That does not mean weaker actors have none.

It means moral responsibility should be sensitive to actual capacity.

---

## 💰 Complicity And Material Support

This branch should eventually examine moral questions around:

- arms;
- finance;
- investment;
- banking;
- insurance;
- technology;
- intelligence;
- logistics;
- diplomatic cover;
- public diplomacy;
- and institutional partnerships.

Important questions include:

- How direct is the contribution?
- How foreseeable is the downstream harm?
- How replaceable is the contribution?
- What knowledge did the actor possess?
- What alternatives existed?
- What leverage did the actor have?
- Was support conditional?
- What happened after credible warnings emerged?

Legal complicity is a separate question.

Moral complicity may be broader, narrower or differently structured.

---

## 🧿 Neutrality

Neutrality can mean several things:

- refusing partisan alignment;
- applying equal evidential standards;
- refusing to intervene;
- remaining institutionally impartial;
- declining to condemn;
- or preserving space for mediation.

Those are not identical.

Sometimes neutrality protects fairness.

Sometimes it protects power.

Sometimes taking sides corrupts evidence.

Sometimes refusing to name an obvious asymmetry becomes a political choice of its own.

This branch should avoid slogans such as:

> **neutrality is always complicity**

or:

> **neutrality is always objectivity**

without specifying the context.

---

## 🕊️ Peace And Justice Are Not Synonyms

A reduction in violence is morally important.

It does not necessarily resolve:

- dispossession;
- occupation;
- impunity;
- hostage-taking;
- detention;
- inequality;
- displacement;
- political exclusion;
- or historical grievance.

Likewise, a demand for justice can become a justification for endless violence if no imaginable settlement is ever sufficient.

This branch should eventually ask:

> **What kind of peace?**

and:

> **What kind of justice?**

and:

> **for whom?**

---

## 🗝️ Return, Security And Competing Needs

Some ethical questions involve genuinely competing goods.

Examples may include:

- refugee return;
- collective security;
- political self-determination;
- minority protection;
- territorial continuity;
- freedom of movement;
- demilitarisation;
- sovereignty;
- and protection from displacement.

A serious ethics branch should not pretend every conflict between goods is fake.

It should ask whether a proposed resolution:

- distributes risk fairly;
- protects rights reciprocally;
- entrenches domination;
- or requires one population to permanently absorb costs for another's security.

---

## 🧵 Identity Does Not Settle Morality

Being:

- Jewish;
- Palestinian;
- Israeli;
- Arab;
- Muslim;
- Christian;
- Zionist;
- anti-Zionist;
- refugee;
- survivor;
- soldier;
- settler;
- or activist

does not automatically make an ethical argument correct.

Identity can provide experience, context and stakes.

It does not confer moral infallibility.

Route definitional questions to `🧵_Identity_And_Language/`.

---

## 🕯️ Trauma Explains Without Automatically Justifying

Historical and personal trauma matter.

So do:

- genocide memory;
- displacement;
- massacre;
- occupation;
- persecution;
- pogroms;
- terrorism;
- imprisonment;
- and intergenerational fear.

Trauma can help explain political behaviour.

It does not automatically justify harming others.

This principle should apply in every direction.

Understanding is not acquittal.

---

## 🧬 Historical Wrong Does Not Create Unlimited Moral Credit

A population can have suffered severe historical injustice.

That history can create:

- claims to repair;
- obligations on others;
- legitimate fear;
- and political context.

It does not create an unlimited account from which future harms can be morally withdrawn.

Likewise, acknowledging later wrongdoing does not erase earlier victimisation.

History can contain more than one real injury.

Route factual historical claims to `🫒_History/`.

---

## 🧿 Dehumanisation Is An Ethical Event

Language that turns people into:

- animals;
- vermin;
- disease;
- demographic threats;
- disposable populations;
- or undifferentiated enemy collectives

matters morally even before it becomes operational policy.

Dehumanisation can:

- lower inhibitions against harm;
- widen acceptable targets;
- erase individual distinction;
- and make suffering easier to dismiss.

Not every insult is evidence of an atrocity plan.

But dehumanising language should not be treated as harmless background noise either.

---

## 🧠 Moral Injury And Participation

This branch may later need to consider the moral burden carried by:

- soldiers;
- medics;
- journalists;
- aid workers;
- civil servants;
- lawyers;
- contractors;
- protesters;
- and institutional employees

who are asked to participate in systems they believe may cause serious harm.

Questions may include:

- duty;
- refusal;
- whistleblowing;
- complicity;
- institutional loyalty;
- professional ethics;
- and moral injury.

These are not unique to Israel and Palestine.

The conflict makes them unusually visible.

## 🪿 Ethics Is Embodied

Moral judgement is affected by:

- fear;
- grief;
- rage;
- humiliation;
- proximity;
- trauma;
- group identity;
- repeated imagery;
- social pressure;
- and perceived existential threat.

That does not make moral reasoning impossible.

It means ethical argument occurs inside bodies and information environments.

This is one reason to cross-link with `🪿_Embodying_The_Information_Ecology/`.

## 🧭 Moral Symmetry And Asymmetry

Applying one moral standard to all people does not require pretending all actors possess equal power, capability or responsibility.

Likewise, recognising asymmetry does not mean stronger actors are automatically wrong and weaker actors automatically right.

A useful ethics node should distinguish:

> **equal moral worth**

from:

> **equal power**

from:

> **equal responsibility**

from:

> **equal vulnerability**

Those are different propositions.

---

## ⚖️ Universalism Requires Actual Universality

If a principle is invoked, test whether it survives reversal.

For example:

> **Civilians should not be deliberately targeted.**

Does the principle still apply when the civilians belong to the other population?

> **People have a right to resist domination.**

What limits apply when resistance harms civilians?

> **States may defend their populations.**

What limits apply when defence imposes catastrophic harm on another population?

A principle that only activates for one group is not functioning as a universal principle.

It may still be a political preference.

Name it honestly.

---

## 🧪 The Reversal Test

Before publishing a strong moral argument, ask:

> **Would I accept this principle if the identities of the actors were reversed?**

If not, ask why.

Sometimes there is a legitimate asymmetry:

- power;
- role;
- legal status;
- capability;
- prior obligation.

Sometimes there is only team loyalty.

The test is not mechanically decisive.

It is useful friction.

---

## 🧿 The Child Test

Another useful ethical check:

> **Would this argument still sound acceptable if the person bearing the consequence were a child with no meaningful control over the political act being punished or deterred?**

This does not resolve every question.

It is particularly useful when rhetoric has begun to collapse whole populations into political actors.

---

## 🧬 Means And Ends

A morally valuable end does not make every means permissible.

Likewise, morally compromised means do not automatically prove the underlying political goal illegitimate.

Separate:

- goal;
- tactic;
- actor;
- foreseeable consequence;
- and alternative.

This matters for:

- resistance;
- state security;
- hostage recovery;
- deterrence;
- political liberation;
- territorial control;
- and diplomatic pressure.

---

## 🪦 Grief Does Not Need Political Permission

The archive should preserve the moral legitimacy of grief across political boundaries.

Acknowledging:

- Israeli civilian suffering;
- Palestinian civilian suffering;
- hostage suffering;
- detainee suffering;
- bereavement;
- fear;
- or trauma

does not require adopting the politics of the person who suffered.

Human recognition is not a concession.

---

## 🌱 Repair

Longer-term ethical questions may include:

- restitution;
- return;
- compensation;
- truth-telling;
- accountability;
- amnesty;
- prosecution;
- memorialisation;
- institutional reform;
- reparations;
- guarantees of non-repetition;
- and coexistence.

These questions will not have purely factual answers.

But they require factual history.

Repair built on denial is unstable.

So is reconciliation demanded without accountability.

---

## 🚧 Common Ethical Failure Modes

Watch for:

- treating legal and moral conclusions as interchangeable;
- using casualty numbers as complete moral arguments;
- assigning collective guilt;
- treating trauma as unlimited justification;
- treating victimhood as moral infallibility;
- treating powerlessness as moral innocence;
- treating power as automatic guilt;
- confusing motive with justification;
- confusing explanation with excuse;
- ignoring foreseeable consequences;
- assuming intentions erase outcomes;
- applying principles selectively by identity;
- using “self-defence” or “resistance” as conclusion words rather than arguments;
- treating civilians as politically blank;
- treating civilians as legitimate targets because of politics;
- calling every compromise unjust because it is incomplete;
- and using moral horror as evidence for factual propositions.
