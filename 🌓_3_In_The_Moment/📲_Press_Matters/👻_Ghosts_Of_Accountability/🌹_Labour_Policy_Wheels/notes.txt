This folder examines how Labour policy is developed, financed, inherited, communicated, rebranded and held accountable.

It is rooted in a specific sequence of events: the unusually large May 2026 King’s Speech; the later presentation of many closely related proposals as part of Andy Burnham’s distinctive political programme; ongoing questions about Labour donations, private access and the outsourcing or privatisation of policy development; and the Farage, Count Binface and red-fox electoral spectacle, which showed both the value and the limits of satirical anti-authoritarian politics.

The central issue is not simply whether one politician copied another. Policies have long and often obscured lineages. They may emerge through government departments, councils, trade unions, think tanks, consultancies, donors, campaign groups and earlier party programmes before becoming attached to a particular leader or political brand.

The folder therefore asks:

* what had already been proposed, and when;
* who helped develop, finance or promote it;
* what genuinely changes when a policy is reintroduced;
* whether power, money and delivery are actually redistributed;
* who receives credit for workable policy;
* who inherits responsibility for failure;
* and which obvious material problems remain unaddressed by a party claiming to represent working people.

The analysis is not intended to displace opposition to Farage or other authoritarian political projects. Satire, electoral disruption and anti-authoritarian contest remain important. But they must be able to coexist with serious scrutiny of Labour’s leadership, donors, policy networks and institutional choices. Attention is not the same as accountability, and one spectacular political event should not be allowed to erase slower questions about policy authorship and public power.

The folder uses disability justice, anti-racism and broader anti-discrimination principles as part of its basic policy test. It rejects a narrow image of the working class composed only of continuously employed, non-disabled workers. Disabled people, carers, migrants, tenants, precarious workers, unemployed people, pensioners and people performing unpaid labour all belong within the material analysis.

Its approach to growth is similarly selective and pro-social. “Degrowth” does not mean managed destitution, service withdrawal or green austerity. It means shrinking extraction, waste, rents, pollution and unnecessary private monopoly while expanding housing, care, health, accessibility, public transport, clean energy, education and collective security.

The governing question is therefore broader than who owns the policy wheel:

Who designed it, who paid for access, who is permitted to steer it, what is meant to grow or shrink, whose life becomes easier, and whose security is still being used as the balancing item?
