# 🧷 Language For Different Evidence Levels  
**First created:** 2026-07-14 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-14  
*Precision should distinguish proof, inference, pattern and sequence without laundering observable coercion into false neutrality.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

This node provides a controlled vocabulary for reporting the STFU apparatus.

Its purpose is to prevent three common failures:

- overstating what the evidence proves;
- understating what the evidence plainly shows;
- collapsing different kinds of connection into one vague claim.

The central rule is:

> **Use the strongest language the evidence supports—and no stronger.**

That requires distinguishing:

- direct evidence;
- strong documentary inference;
- pattern evidence;
- temporal association;
- unverified claims;
- unknown connection.

Precision is not timidity.

Qualifiers should reflect evidence, not fear of naming an observable pattern.

---

## 🧭 The Evidence Ladder

Use a six-level ladder.

### Level 1 — Direct Evidence

A record establishes instruction, referral or coordination.

### Level 2 — Strong Documentary Inference

Records strongly indicate a connection without fully documenting command.

### Level 3 — Pattern Evidence

The incident resembles recurring methods, target classes or outputs.

### Level 4 — Temporal Association

One event followed or coincided with another.

### Level 5 — Unverified Claim

An allegation has been made but not publicly substantiated.

### Level 6 — Unknown

The available record does not establish how or whether the actions connect.

Each level requires different verbs.

---

## 🔴 Direct Evidence

Use direct language only where the record supports it.

Suitable terms include:

- ordered;
- directed;
- instructed;
- formally referred;
- documented coordination;
- communicated with;
- jointly conducted;
- approved;
- authorised.

Possible sources include:

- executive orders;
- signed memoranda;
- referral letters;
- recorded instructions;
- emails;
- meeting records;
- sworn testimony;
- judicial findings.

---

## 🧾 “Ordered”

Use **ordered** where a person or institution issued a binding or explicit command.

Example structure:

> The president ordered the department to conduct the review.

Do not use **ordered** where the evidence shows only:

- public demands;
- hostile rhetoric;
- political expectation;
- later aligned action.

---

## 🧭 “Directed”

Use **directed** where the record shows authoritative guidance toward a particular action or outcome.

This may be appropriate where:

- the instruction was explicit;
- implementation details were delegated;
- the recipient retained limited discretion.

Do not use it merely because an official expressed a preference.

---

## 📬 “Formally Referred”

Use **formally referred** where one institution transmitted a matter to another through an official process.

Example:

> The White House formally referred the allegation to the department for review.

This establishes routing.

It does not establish:

- guilt;
- predetermined outcome;
- unlawful motive.

---

## 🔗 “Documented Coordination”

Use **documented coordination** only where records show:

- communication;
- joint planning;
- shared operational activity;
- case discussion;
- agreed division of responsibility.

Avoid using **coordinated** where actions merely resemble one another.

---

## 🟠 Strong Documentary Inference

Use this level where records strongly indicate a connection but do not establish direct command.

Suitable formulations include:

- appears to have prompted;
- records indicate a connection;
- was handled through an unusual process involving;
- used the same allegation and followed shortly after;
- the available documents strongly suggest;
- the sequence and records indicate;
- appears to have been routed through.

This language should identify the supporting evidence.

---

## 🧠 “Appears To Have Prompted”

Use where:

- one event preceded another;
- records show institutional reaction;
- direct instruction is not public.

Example:

> The public demand appears to have prompted an internal review, according to correspondence released in the litigation.

The phrase should not stand alone without the evidential basis.

---

## 📂 “Records Indicate A Connection”

Use where documents reveal:

- overlapping communications;
- shared allegations;
- unusual process;
- linked decision-making.

Example:

> Records indicate a connection between the political referral and the later personnel action.

State what the records contain.

---

## 🛤️ “Handled Through An Unusual Process Involving”

Use where:

- ordinary channels were bypassed;
- senior appointees intervened;
- a special team was created;
- career officials were excluded.

Example:

> The matter was handled through an unusual process involving senior political appointees and a newly created review team.

This identifies process without automatically asserting retaliation.

---

## 🟡 Pattern Evidence

Pattern language connects an incident to recurring structures without claiming direct coordination.

Suitable formulations include:

- fits a broader pattern;
- resembles earlier cases;
- used a method previously applied to;
- formed part of a cumulative pressure campaign;
- repeated a target category seen across;
- followed a familiar routing sequence;
- produced the same practical output as.

Pattern claims should name:

- the prior cases;
- the shared method;
- the relevant difference.

---

## 🕸️ “Fits A Broader Pattern”

Use where several cases share meaningful features.

Example:

> The action fits a broader pattern in which public designation is followed by administrative pressure on employers or clients.

Do not rely on superficial similarity.

The repeated features should be specified.

---

## 🪞 “Resembles Earlier Cases”

Use where the analogy is useful but incomplete.

Example:

> The route resembles earlier cases involving clearance action against named critics, although no common referral has been established.

This formulation preserves both similarity and uncertainty.

---

## 🧰 “Used A Method Previously Applied To”

Use where a specific technique recurs.

Examples include:

- grant freezes;
- visa review;
- contract pressure;
- public naming;
- law-firm targeting;
- supporter amplification.

The method should be concrete.

---

## 🔗 “Formed Part Of A Cumulative Pressure Campaign”

Use this only where the cumulative record is established.

If direct coordination is not proven, qualify it:

> Formally separate actions formed part of a cumulative field of pressure around the same target.

Avoid **campaign** where the evidence shows only coincidence.

---

## ⚪ Temporal Association Only

Temporal language should describe sequence without implying causation.

Suitable terms include:

- followed;
- came after;
- coincided with;
- occurred shortly after;
- preceded;
- unfolded during the same period.

Add where needed:

> **No direct connection has been established.**

---

## ⏱️ “Followed”

**Followed** means only that one event came later.

Example:

> The investigation followed several weeks of public criticism from administration officials.

This does not mean the criticism caused the investigation.

---

## 🕰️ “Came After”

Use for straightforward chronology.

Example:

> The employer’s decision came after the public designation and before any formal finding.

The timing may be significant.

Causation remains a separate question.

---

## 🔄 “Coincided With”

Use where events overlap.

Example:

> The contract review coincided with a wider campaign against firms representing administration critics.

This supports context, not coordination.

---

## 🚫 “No Direct Connection Has Been Established”

Use this where:

- chronology is clear;
- alignment is visible;
- no instruction, referral or communication is public.

This phrase should not be used to erase pattern evidence.

It should define its limit.

---

## ⚫ Unverified Claims

Use careful language where allegations lack publicly available proof.

Suitable terms include:

- alleged;
- claimed;
- asserted;
- claimed without publicly available evidence;
- the administration has not disclosed its evidential basis;
- the allegation remains unsubstantiated in the public record;
- no supporting records have been released.

The source of the allegation should always be named.

---

## 🗣️ “Alleged”

Use **alleged** for:

- unproven conduct;
- disputed factual claims;
- claims under investigation.

Do not use it to describe an event already established by documents or admission.

---

## 🕳️ “Claimed Without Publicly Available Evidence”

Use where an official has made a serious allegation but has not produced support.

Example:

> The administration claimed the organisation had foreign links without providing publicly available evidence.

This is stronger and clearer than merely repeating the claim.

---

## 🔒 “Has Not Disclosed Its Evidential Basis”

Use where:

- the process may be classified;
- the institution refuses detail;
- a public basis is absent.

Example:

> The department has not disclosed its evidential basis for treating the speech as a security concern.

This states the public-record limitation.

---

## ❔ Unknown Connection

Sometimes the honest conclusion is:

- connection unknown;
- records unavailable;
- process undisclosed;
- no conclusion currently possible.

Suitable formulations include:

- the available record does not establish;
- it is not known whether;
- no public records show;
- the relationship between the actions remains unclear;
- the institution declined to explain whether.

Unknown is an evidential category.

It is not an invitation to speculate.

---

## 🏷️ Terms For People

Do not collapse different legal and procedural statuses.

### Target

A person or institution selected for political, administrative or public pressure.

### Subject

A person within the scope of an investigation or review.

### Suspect

A person whom investigators suspect of an offence.

### Defendant

A person formally accused in legal proceedings.

### Convicted Person

A person found guilty through a completed legal process.

Use the narrowest accurate term.

---

## 🎯 “Target”

Use **target** carefully.

It may mean:

- publicly designated person;
- intended object of pressure;
- formal investigative target.

Where ambiguity exists, specify:

- political target;
- public target;
- investigative target.

Do not imply formal investigative status without evidence.

---

## 🔍 “Subject”

Use **subject** where the person is within the scope of an inquiry but has not necessarily been accused.

This is often safer than **suspect**.

---

## 🚔 “Suspect”

Use only where:

- investigators describe the person as suspected;
- the legal status supports it;
- evidence concerns a specific offence.

Political opponents are not suspects merely because officials attack them.

---

## ⚖️ “Defendant”

Use only after formal proceedings name the person as a defendant.

Do not use it for:

- review;
- inquiry;
- referral;
- investigation.

---

## 🧷 Terms For Processes

Keep these processes separate.

### Review

An examination that may be preliminary or administrative.

### Inquiry

A broader fact-finding process.

### Investigation

A structured process examining possible wrongdoing.

### Referral

Transmission of a matter to another institution.

### Prosecution

Formal legal proceedings alleging an offence.

### Administrative Action

A non-criminal decision affecting status, access, employment, funding or regulation.

The distinction matters because **review** does not mean **investigation**, and **investigation** does not mean **prosecution**.

---

## 🔍 “Investigation”

Use only where the institution has opened an investigative process.

Do not upgrade:

- political demands;
- preliminary checks;
- routine review;

into an investigation without confirmation.

---

## 📬 “Referral”

A referral is an act of transmission.

It does not mean the receiving body accepted:

- the allegation;
- the legal theory;
- the requested outcome.

---

## ⚖️ “Prosecution”

Use only when formal legal proceedings have begun.

Do not treat:

- public accusation;
- investigation;
- charge recommendation;

as prosecution.

---

## 🏛️ “Administrative Action”

Use for:

- visa decisions;
- clearance changes;
- grant freezes;
- contract exclusions;
- personnel actions;
- licence decisions.

Administrative action can impose serious punishment without criminal proceedings.

---

## 🧭 Terms For Political Misuse

These terms overlap but are not interchangeable.

### Retaliation

Action taken in response to protected conduct, criticism or opposition.

### Selective Enforcement

A rule is applied unevenly to comparable actors.

### Viewpoint Discrimination

Action depends on the perspective or opinion expressed.

### Partisan Enforcement

Enforcement systematically advantages one political side.

### Political Effect

An action has political consequences regardless of proven motive.

Use the term that matches the evidence.

---

## ⚡ “Retaliation”

Use where evidence connects the action to:

- criticism;
- opposition;
- protected speech;
- prior official conflict.

Strongest support includes:

- judicial finding;
- direct statement;
- documented sequence and motive evidence.

Do not use retaliation merely because the target is political.

---

## ⚖️ “Selective Enforcement”

Use where meaningful comparator evidence shows different treatment.

A valid comparison should involve:

- similar conduct;
- similar power;
- similar institutional level;
- similar evidential threshold;
- similar potential consequence.

---

## 🗣️ “Viewpoint Discrimination”

Use where the distinction turns on what position was expressed.

This may be a legal conclusion.

Attribute it to:

- court finding;
- complaint;
- legal analysis;

where appropriate.

---

## 🟥 “Partisan Enforcement”

Use where the pattern consistently advantages one political faction.

This requires broader evidence than one case.

---

## 🎭 “Political Effect”

Use where the consequence is political but motive remains unproven.

Example:

> Whatever the agency’s motive, the decision had the political effect of deterring other non-citizens from joining the protest.

This preserves effect without asserting intent.

---

## 🕸️ Language For Cumulative Systems

Useful formulations include:

> **Formally separate actions produced a combined coercive effect.**

> **The available evidence does not establish a single command structure.**

> **The same target category appeared across several agencies.**

> **Several institutions acted on the same public allegation through different legal and commercial routes.**

> **The actions were institutionally distinct but cumulative in their effect on the target.**

> **No direct coordination has been shown, although the sequence created a reinforcing field of pressure.**

These formulations preserve structure and uncertainty.

---

## 📣 Language For Public Signalling

Useful formulations include:

> **The remarks made the preferred outcome legible.**

> **Officials acted after repeated public demands, although no direct instruction is public.**

> **The target was publicly designated before the formal process began.**

> **The statement supplied a political allegation later used by other actors.**

> **The public demand may have shaped institutional risk calculations even without a formal order.**

Public signalling is a distinct mechanism.

Do not describe it as command without evidence.

---

## 🧠 Language For Anticipatory Compliance

Useful formulations include:

> **The institution acted before receiving a formal order.**

> **Internal records show concern about exposure to government contracts.**

> **The employer described the decision as independent but cited risks created by the administration’s action.**

> **The firm withdrew after the government made continued representation commercially costly.**

> **The available evidence indicates anticipatory compliance rather than direct instruction.**

---

## ⚖️ Language After Judicial Findings

Judicial findings should change the language.

Before judgment:

- alleged retaliation;
- appears selective;
- challenged as viewpoint discrimination.

After a finding:

- the court found;
- the judge concluded;
- the order was held unconstitutional;
- the court identified retaliatory motive;
- the action was enjoined.

State:

- jurisdiction;
- procedural stage;
- whether the ruling is final;
- whether an appeal is pending.

Do not convert an interim ruling into a final universal conclusion.

---

## 🔄 Language After Reversal Or Appeal

Use:

- the ruling was reversed;
- the injunction was stayed;
- the judgment remains on appeal;
- the court did not reach the motive question;
- the case was dismissed on procedural grounds.

Update earlier characterisations where necessary.

The evidential vocabulary should move with the record.

---

## 🚫 Terms To Avoid

Avoid:

- obviously;
- clearly, where evidence is contested;
- undoubtedly;
- proven, where only allegation exists;
- secret police, as an unsupported standalone label;
- ordered, where only rhetoric exists;
- coordinated, where actions merely align;
- conspiracy, where the evidence shows a network or pattern;
- innocent, where the process remains unresolved;
- guilty, before adjudication.

Overstatement creates easy rebuttals.

---

## ⚠️ “Clearly” And “Obviously”

These words often substitute confidence for evidence.

Replace them with:

- records show;
- the court found;
- the chronology indicates;
- the available evidence supports;
- no public evidence establishes.

---

## 🚨 “Secret Police”

Do not use **secret police** as a standalone insult.

Where justified, define the functional claim:

> The apparatus performed several political-police functions, including enemy classification, selective investigation, pressure on associates and wider deterrence.

The label should follow the analysis.

It should not replace it.

---

## 🔗 “Coordinated”

Use only where evidence shows coordination.

Where actions align without proof, use:

- converged;
- reinforced one another;
- used the same allegation;
- occurred in sequence;
- produced cumulative pressure;
- independently aligned.

---

## 🧱 Precision Without False Neutrality

Precision becomes false neutrality where reporters:

- repeat unsupported official claims without noting the absence of evidence;
- treat motive denial as equal to documentary proof;
- refuse to describe a repeated pattern;
- use “controversial” to conceal coercive effect;
- qualify established facts into ambiguity.

A qualifier should narrow the claim accurately.

It should not drain it of meaning.

---

## 🧾 Attribution Discipline

Attribute:

- allegations to the speaker;
- legal conclusions to the court;
- motive claims to the evidence or claimant;
- estimates to their source;
- private accounts to named or described witnesses.

Examples:

> The administration alleged...

> The court found...

> Internal correspondence indicates...

> The target said...

> No public records establish...

Attribution protects clarity.

## 🟢 Colour-Coding Option

An internal system may use colour coding:

- red — direct evidence;
- orange — strong documentary inference;
- yellow — pattern evidence;
- blue — anticipatory compliance;
- grey — temporal association;
- white — unknown.

Colour must never be the only indicator.

Every visual system needs text labels and a legend.

## 📝 Model Sentences

### Direct Evidence

> The memorandum directed the department to review the named firms.

### Formal Referral

> The White House formally referred the allegation to the agency on 4 March.

### Strong Inference

> Released correspondence indicates that the public demand prompted an unusual senior-level review.

### Pattern

> The action fits a broader pattern of using contract exposure against institutions associated with administration critics.

### Temporal Only

> The review began three days after the president’s remarks; no direct instruction has been made public.

### Independent Alignment

> The employer described its decision as independent, although it relied on the same allegation advanced by administration officials.

### Unknown

> The available record does not establish whether the agency communicated with the White House before opening the matter.

### Cumulative Effect

> Formally separate actions produced a combined coercive effect through legal cost, employment loss and increased security risk.

## 🧪 What Would Require Stronger Or Weaker Language?

Strengthen language when:

- direct records emerge;
- a court makes a factual finding;
- an institution admits communication;
- a referral becomes public;
- comparator evidence is established.

Weaken or revise language when:

- records contradict the chronology;
- an inference loses support;
- a court rejects the factual claim;
- a supposed referral did not occur;
- private actors document unrelated reasons;
- an appeal reverses the relevant finding.

Language should be revisable.

---

## ⚠️ Evidential Boundaries

This node does not claim that:

- qualifiers always improve accuracy;
- every connection can be neatly classified;
- direct evidence is required to report a pattern;
- pattern evidence proves motive;
- temporal sequence is irrelevant;
- official denials deserve automatic deference;
- strong language is inherently irresponsible.

It makes a narrower methodological claim:

> **Reliable apparatus reporting depends on a vocabulary that distinguishes instruction, referral, documentary inference, recurring pattern, public signalling, anticipatory compliance, temporal sequence, unverified allegation and unknown connection—while still naming cumulative coercive effects where the evidence supports them.**
