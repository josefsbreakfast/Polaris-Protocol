## 🪻 Moving Forwards — discussion notes

- Proposed subcluster:
  - `🌹🧸_Your_Baby_Does_Not_Just_Disappear/🪻_Moving_Forwards/`
- This is the constructive part of the cluster:
  - not simply documenting atrocity;
  - not pretending there is one simple policy fix;
  - asking what the accumulated history teaches us about how to design better systems.

### 🕸️ Stop treating each case as an isolated scandal

- There is now enough historical and international evidence that we do not need to keep beginning with:
  - *How could something like this possibly happen?*
- The conceptual disbelief barrier has substantially weakened.
- We know that:
  - children can be systematically exploited;
  - institutions can facilitate abuse;
  - governments, churches, charities, families and professionals can all participate in harmful systems;
  - children can be transferred between institutions and jurisdictions;
  - apparently respectable administrative processes can conceal coercion or exploitation;
  - vulnerable children can be disbelieved even where formal safeguarding systems exist.
- This does **not** mean every allegation should automatically be believed without evidence.
- It means the *category of phenomenon* no longer has to be proved conceptually from scratch every time.

### 📦 The problem with "scandals"

- Naming individual scandals helps establish:
  - what happened;
  - where;
  - when;
  - who was responsible.
- But it can also fragment the larger dataset.
- We end up studying:
  - Tuam;
  - Bessborough;
  - Magdalene laundries;
  - forced adoption;
  - child migration;
  - children's homes;
  - institutional sexual abuse;
  - grooming;
  - trafficking;
  as separate phenomena.
- This risks repeatedly fixing one manifestation while leaving the conditions capable of producing another largely intact.
- The next analytical stage may therefore be:
  - preserve the specificity of each history;
  - **then put the datasets beside one another and look for recurring structural conditions.**

### 🌍 Patterns do not require central command

- Ordinary migration provides a useful analogy.
- Large-scale migration can produce highly recognisable routes and patterns without somebody centrally instructing:
  - X number of people must move from A to B.
- Patterns emerge through:
  - incentives;
  - existing networks;
  - laws;
  - opportunities;
  - transport;
  - information;
  - social conditions;
  - previous movement.
- Likewise, recurring patterns in the movement or exploitation of vulnerable children do not require one centrally coordinated trafficking conspiracy.
- Important distinction:
  - **coordination ≠ centralisation.**
- Multiple actors can produce a coordinated or patterned system while:
  - pursuing different interests;
  - controlling only one part of the process;
  - describing their own activity differently;
  - having no single central controller.

### 🕳️ The QAnon/conspiracy distinction

- Conspiracy narratives often recognise the real existence of child exploitation but imagine that its scale requires:
  - one secret organisation;
  - one controlling group;
  - one centrally coordinated trafficking system.
- The historical evidence suggests a less fantastical but potentially more important problem:
  - exploitation does **not** require one master organisation.
- There can instead be:
  - demand;
  - vulnerable populations;
  - intermediaries;
  - institutional pathways;
  - incentives;
  - weak oversight;
  - insufficient friction;
  - low likelihood of accountability.
- Different systems can become interoperable without becoming one organisation.
- This suggests a more useful question than:
  - *Who secretly controls the entire system?*
- Ask:
  - **What conditions repeatedly make vulnerable children accessible and transferable?**

### 🧸 Children as equally important people

- Children do not necessarily have the same:
  - cognitive capacity;
  - legal autonomy;
  - decision-making ability;
  as adults at every developmental stage.
- But this should not imply that they have **less human importance or value**.
- Reduced decision-making capacity should not mean reduced consideration.
- In many circumstances it should create:
  - **greater duties upon the people exercising power on the child's behalf.**
- This raises a fundamental design question:
  - **Who gets to make decisions for a child, and who scrutinises those decisions?**

### ⚖️ Children's rights have developed over time

- Modern child protection did not arrive as one complete system.
- Recognition of children as individual rights-holders developed incrementally.
- Protections accumulated through repeated recognition of different harms and failure modes.
- Broad historical movement includes increasing recognition of:
  - limits upon child labour;
  - education rights;
  - protection from cruelty and neglect;
  - welfare obligations;
  - regulation of institutions;
  - adoption safeguards;
  - sexual abuse;
  - children's participation and procedural rights;
  - trafficking and exploitation;
  - grooming;
  - newer forms of online exploitation.
- This history therefore does not require the conclusion:
  - *everybody in the past possessed our complete safeguarding framework and deliberately ignored it.*
- Nor does historical context justify:
  - *they didn't know any better.*
- People resisted.
- Survivors objected.
- Families objected.
- Reformers campaigned.
- Contemporary criticism often existed.
- The more useful conclusion is:
  - **safeguarding itself has been an iterative process of discovering additional ways power over children can be abused.**

### 🔧 We should not assume safeguarding is finished

- There is no particular reason to believe that the present generation has finally designed the perfect child-protection system.
- Previous generations also operated systems they considered protective or benevolent.
- Therefore:
  - identify failures;
  - compare failures;
  - identify recurring conditions;
  - redesign;
  - observe outcomes;
  - continue adapting.
- It is acceptable to say:
  - **we have not finished designing this yet.**

### 🌱 Root causes rather than endless patches

- There is a parallel here with violence against women and girls.
- It is possible to prohibit individual harmful acts while retaining much of the environment capable of generating them.
- For example:
  - extreme power asymmetry;
  - dependency;
  - stigma;
  - weak external visibility;
  - disbelief of vulnerable people;
  - institutional self-protection;
  - information asymmetry;
  - poor accountability;
  - low costs for institutional failure.
- If those underlying conditions remain substantially unchanged:
  - one manifestation may be prohibited;
  - another can emerge through a different pathway.
- Therefore the question is not only:
  - **What act should we prohibit?**
- It is also:
  - **What conditions keep making these acts possible?**

### 🕸️ Recurring structural questions

- Across otherwise different histories, ask:
  - Who is believed?
  - Who gets to make decisions?
  - Who controls information?
  - Who controls movement?
  - Who can leave?
  - Who has independent advocacy?
  - Who can complain safely?
  - Who is economically dependent?
  - Who is socially isolated?
  - Who receives the benefit of institutional doubt?
  - Whose sexuality is policed?
  - Whose behaviour is interpreted as evidence that they are unreliable?
  - Who possesses records about someone else's life?
  - Does responsibility actually follow the exercise of power?
  - What happens when the organisation responsible for safeguarding someone is itself the source of harm?

### 🚸 Social transferability

- One potentially useful structural variable:
  - **How socially transferable is this child?**
- Not merely:
  - *Can this child legally be moved?*
- But:
  - Who notices if they disappear from their present environment?
  - Who has standing to object?
  - Is there somebody outside the institution who knows where they are?
  - Can the child independently contact somebody?
  - Does the child possess or have access to information about their own identity?
  - Can one organisation transfer them to another?
  - Is the destination independently checked?
  - Can family members find them?
  - Can they leave?
  - If they report abuse, will somebody believe them?
- Vulnerability can therefore partly arise from having too few independent relationships capable of resisting somebody else's control.

### 🌹🧸 Follow the person, not merely the institution

- Traditional scandal analysis asks:
  - *What happened inside this organisation?*
- A joined-up approach can additionally ask:
  - *What happened to this person as they moved through the system?*
- Follow:
  - origin;
  - vulnerability;
  - acquisition of authority;
  - separation;
  - transfer;
  - destination;
  - records;
  - subsequent treatment;
  - later accountability.
- Institutional boundaries should not automatically become analytical boundaries.

### 👧 VAWG and child protection overlap

- Girls can sit at the intersection of:
  - child vulnerability;
  - misogyny;
  - sexual policing;
  - sexual exploitation;
  - economic vulnerability;
  - institutional power.
- Histories involving unmarried pregnant girls make this particularly visible.
- But the larger framework should remain capable of explaining:
  - boys who were exploited;
  - adult women;
  - care-experienced children;
  - disabled children;
  - racialised populations;
  - LGBT+ children;
  - poor families;
  - other populations whose vulnerability takes different forms.
- Gender matters enormously without needing to become the sole explanatory variable.

### 🪻 Why "Moving Forwards"

- Initially considered:
  - `🪻_Cybernetic_Solutions`
- Replaced with:
  - `🪻_Moving_Forwards/`
- "Solutions" risks suggesting that we already possess the final answer.
- "Moving Forwards" better captures:
  - learning;
  - adaptation;
  - humility;
  - redesign;
  - continuing improvement.
- The cybernetic principle can remain underneath it without needing to dominate the title:

  **observe failure → learn → change conditions → observe again → adapt**

- The purpose of collecting these histories is therefore not simply to construct an ever-larger archive of horror.
- They are accumulated **failure data**.
- Enough different Pandora's boxes have now been opened that their contents can be placed on the same table.
- The opportunity is to ask:
  - **What keeps recurring?**
  - **Why?**
  - **What would have interrupted it?**
  - **What can we change upstream so that fewer people ever reach the point where they require rescue, redress or retrospective justice?**

### 🪻 Central proposition

- We have repeatedly become better at protecting children because somebody identified a failure that existing systems did not adequately recognise.
- There is no reason to assume that process is complete.
- The objective is not to discover one universal explanation for every historical atrocity.
- It is to become better at recognising **recurring architectures of vulnerability and power** across otherwise distinct cases.
- Then build systems around what the combined evidence teaches us.

> **We do not have to keep being surprised by the same structural failure wearing different clothes.**
