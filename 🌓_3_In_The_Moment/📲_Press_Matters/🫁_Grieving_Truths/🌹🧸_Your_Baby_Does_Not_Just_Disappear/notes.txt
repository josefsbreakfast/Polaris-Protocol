# 🌹🧸 Your Baby Does Not Just Disappear

## Core framing

- New folder:
  - `🌹🧸_Your_Baby_Does_Not_Just_Disappear`
- Intended to sit within the broader grief / atrocity / institutional-scandal area.
- This cluster is specifically concerned with the historical and continuing systems surrounding:
  - Magdalene laundries;
  - mother-and-baby institutions;
  - unmarried pregnant girls and women;
  - forced or coerced adoption;
  - separation of children from their families;
  - child migration;
  - institutional deaths of babies and children;
  - unidentified / unrecorded / inadequately recorded burials;
  - the continuing struggle by survivors and families to establish what happened.

## 🌹 Why not simply call this "mother and baby homes"?

- "Mother and baby" is itself an institutional category.
- It can produce a misleading mental image:
  - an adult woman;
  - perhaps in her twenties;
  - straightforwardly understood as a "mother";
  - voluntarily inhabiting that social role.
- That does not adequately describe the population involved.
- Some were women.
- Some were very young women.
- Some were literally girls.
- Pregnancy could itself exist in contexts of:
  - sexual abuse;
  - coercion;
  - exploitation;
  - profound inequalities of power.
- Calling everyone simply "mothers" can therefore obscure what had happened to them before they ever entered the institution.
- The institutional response could then add another layer of violence.
- The folder should not reproduce the administrative language through which those experiences were flattened.

## 🧸 Why "Your Baby Does Not Just Disappear"

- The title deliberately changes register from institutional classification to the experience of the person whose child was taken.
- **Your baby does not just disappear.**
- A baby cannot administratively vanish by itself.
- If a child disappears from someone's life, there is a material chain behind that disappearance.
- Questions immediately follow:
  - Who took the child?
  - Who authorised it?
  - Who transported them?
  - Who received them?
  - Who arranged an adoption?
  - Who altered, withheld or created records?
  - Who knew where the child had gone?
  - If the child died, who recorded the death?
  - Who buried them?
  - Where were they buried?
  - Who knew where they were buried?
  - What documentation was created?
  - Where is that documentation now?
- The phrase therefore refuses **institutional disappearance**.
- It also speaks back to the expectation placed upon girls and women that they should accept the disappearance and continue with their lives.

## 🌹🧸 Symbolism

- `🌹🧸` emerged as the visual marker.
- **🌹 Rose**
  - mourning;
  - remembrance;
  - love;
  - graves;
  - women and girls as well as children;
  - also connects with the roses and garden memorial practices at Tuam.
- **🧸 Teddy**
  - recognisably signifies a child rather than an abstract administrative category;
  - childhood;
  - vulnerability;
  - tenderness;
  - the ordinary human life concealed by institutional statistics.
- Together they are deliberately tender rather than bureaucratic:
  - `🌹🧸`
- The tenderness sitting beside:
  - `Your_Baby_Does_Not_Just_Disappear`
  makes the title considerably harder rather than softer.

## 🇮🇪 Tuam

- Current film:
  - *The Lost Children of Tuam* (2026).
- Centres on Catherine Corless's investigation of the former Bon Secours Mother and Baby Home at Tuam.
- 796 children were recorded as having died there.
- Corless's work exposed the absence of corresponding conventional burial records and helped force investigation of the site.
- The Tuam story remains materially unfinished:
  - excavation;
  - identification;
  - recovery of remains;
  - records;
  - questions from surviving relatives.
- Important framing:
  - this is not simply a completed historical scandal now being dramatised by a film;
  - work arising from what happened is continuing in the present.

## 🌼 Bessborough / Cork

- Separate but related contemporary struggle around the former Bessborough Mother and Baby Institution in Cork.
- Hundreds of children died in connection with the institution.
- Burial locations for the overwhelming majority have not been conclusively established.
- Campaigning has involved:
  - survivors;
  - relatives;
  - preservation of potential burial areas;
  - opposition to development;
  - demands for records;
  - archaeological evidence;
  - calls for inquests;
  - memorialisation.
- One proposed memorial treatment has been preservation as a **wildflower meadow**.
- This provides an existing connection between flowers and contemporary campaigning rather than flowers being imposed purely as a Polaris aesthetic.

## 🕯️ Tuam ↔ Bessborough

- There has also been contemporary commemorative movement connecting the two sites.
- A 2026 walk travelled from Tuam to Bessborough.
- A lantern was carried between them.
- Children's names were spoken.
- Flowers were offered at Bessborough.
- The lantern remained there.
- This creates a living commemorative connection between sites rather than treating each as an entirely isolated historical event.

## 🇬🇧 Forced adoption

- Related UK history should be included without pretending it is identical to the Irish institutional system.
- Keir Starmer delivered a formal UK parliamentary apology in July 2026 concerning historic forced adoption practices in England and Wales.
- The apology concerned the coercion and pressure experienced by women whose children were removed for adoption.
- Northern Ireland has its own distinct institutional and inquiry history involving:
  - mother-and-baby institutions;
  - Magdalene laundries;
  - workhouses.
- Important distinction:
  - Ireland ≠ Northern Ireland ≠ England and Wales.
- They can be studied together without collapsing their different:
  - laws;
  - institutions;
  - churches;
  - governments;
  - timelines;
  - administrative systems.

## 🚢 British child migration

- Another related but distinct system:
  - British child migration programmes.
- Children were transported overseas, particularly to:
  - Australia;
  - Canada;
  - New Zealand;
  - Southern Rhodesia.
- Government bodies, charities and other institutions participated.
- Some children experienced:
  - institutionalisation;
  - exploitation;
  - child labour;
  - physical abuse;
  - sexual abuse;
  - separation from their families;
  - false or misleading information about whether their parents were alive.

## 🎬 Margaret Humphreys / *Oranges and Sunshine*

- The film initially being remembered was most likely:
  - *Oranges and Sunshine* (2011).
- Emily Watson plays British social worker Margaret Humphreys.
- The date confusion makes sense because the events depicted began considerably earlier.
- Humphreys began investigating child migration in the 1980s.
- Her work led to the Child Migrants Trust.
- Her book:
  - *Empty Cradles* (1994).
- The later film adaptation:
  - *Oranges and Sunshine* (2011).
- So the remembered chronology is roughly:
  - **1980s investigation**
  - → **1993: *The Leaving of Liverpool***
  - → **1994: *Empty Cradles***
  - → **2011: *Oranges and Sunshine***
- *The Leaving of Liverpool* is another important cultural intervention:
  - BBC/Australian drama;
  - broadcast in 1993;
  - generated enormous public response to the child-migration issue.

## 🕸️ The common mechanism

- These histories should **not** be presented as one identical programme.
- Nevertheless, there is a recurring institutional architecture worth examining.

- Rough sequence:

  **policing girls' and women's sexuality**
  → pregnancy becomes grounds for institutional intervention
  → isolation / confinement / stigma
  → reduced power to resist authority
  → separation of girl/woman and child
  → institutional control over the child's identity and destination
  → adoption / migration / institutionalisation / death
  → incomplete, inaccessible, misleading or absent records
  → decades of survivors and relatives trying to reconstruct what happened

- The child is not necessarily incidental to the punishment imposed upon the girl or woman.
- Control over the child can itself become part of the mechanism of punishment and control.

## 🗃️ Information as part of the mechanism

- Strong connection to Embodied Information Ecology.
- Administrative information is not merely evidence left behind after violence.
- **Control over information can help make the separation possible and durable.**
- Relevant information includes:
  - birth certificates;
  - names;
  - parentage;
  - baptismal records;
  - adoption documentation;
  - institutional files;
  - migration records;
  - passenger records;
  - death certificates;
  - burial registers;
  - grave locations;
  - correspondence with families;
  - access to archives.
- Fundamental asymmetry:
  - **the institution may know something about your child that you do not.**
- That asymmetry can persist for decades.
- A child can therefore be made to "disappear" socially and informationally while remaining entirely traceable inside institutional systems.

## 🌹 The continuing problem

- Avoid framing this as:
  - *terrible things happened historically and society subsequently fixed them.*
- The original institutional systems may have ended or changed.
- Their consequences have not.
- Survivors are alive.
- Families are alive.
- Some still do not know:
  - what happened to relatives;
  - where children were sent;
  - whether somebody survived;
  - what name they subsequently lived under;
  - where somebody was buried.
- Records are still being sought.
- Remains are still being recovered.
- Sites are still contested.
- Excavations are happening.
- Campaigns are active.
- Legal and political processes remain active.
- Films such as *The Lost Children of Tuam* are therefore entering an **ongoing process of recovery and accountability**, not merely commemorating a closed historical episode.

## 🌹🧸 Central proposition

> **Your baby does not just disappear.**

- If a child disappeared, something happened.
- Someone made decisions.
- Someone exercised authority.
- Someone produced or failed to produce records.
- Someone knew something.
- The fact that the person from whom the child was taken was denied that information does not mean the information never existed.
- Recovering that chain is simultaneously:
  - historical work;
  - archival work;
  - survivor work;
  - family work;
  - forensic work;
  - and accountability work.

“Yes. I think that is a much bigger argument than historical redress, because unresolved historical abuse helps establish the practical boundaries of what institutions learn they can survive.

There are a few layers to it:

* Justice establishes the seriousness of the underlying act, not merely compensation for the past. If institutions can systematically confine girls and women, separate them from their children, conceal or lose the destinations of those children, leave families unable to locate graves, and then largely allow responsibility to dissolve through time, that communicates something about how seriously violence against women and girls is actually treated when accountability becomes difficult.
* Non-resolution creates precedent culturally even where it creates no formal legal precedent. People learn what society ultimately tolerates. Institutions learn which scandals can be survived. Officials learn that delay is extraordinarily effective: deny → contest → investigate → wait → records disappear → perpetrators die → say contemporary standards are different → apologise after meaningful individual accountability has become almost impossible.
* That matters to present-day VAWG policy, because you cannot entirely separate prevention from retrospective accountability. Prevention says: we regard this conduct as sufficiently serious that we will intervene to stop it. Justice says: we meant that.
* And these cases are particularly stark because many of the usual ambiguities invoked around abuse are substantially reduced. A child objectively existed. An institution took responsibility for that child. If the child subsequently died, was adopted, was transported overseas, or was otherwise separated from their family, there was a material sequence of events. 🌹🧸_Your_Baby_Does_Not_Just_Disappear gets directly at that.
* There’s therefore an uncomfortable credibility problem when a state simultaneously announces increasingly sophisticated strategies against violence against women and girls while families are still trying to establish where children taken from girls and women by institutions actually went. The contemporary policy can be worthwhile and the historical failure can weaken its moral and institutional credibility. Those aren’t contradictory propositions.
* And I think your correction from “punish” to “seek justice” is important. Justice doesn’t necessarily mean finding an elderly individual and putting them in prison. Depending on evidence and circumstances it can mean criminal accountability, civil liability, disclosure, access to records, correction of records, excavation, identification, inquests, institutional findings, reparations, memorialisation, preservation of sites, and formally establishing responsibility.

The question underneath it becomes quite severe:

If this does not create an obligation to find out what happened, what threshold of violence against women and children would?

And there’s a further VAWG connection I think is particularly important: historical justice tells living victims whether disclosure is worth the cost.

If somebody experiencing abuse now looks backwards and sees women spending fifty years trying to get an institution simply to acknowledge where their children went, that’s information. Conversely, demonstrating that institutions will continue investigating serious abuse despite embarrassment, elapsed time, institutional prestige or administrative difficulty is also information.

So justice isn’t only backwards-facing. The way a society handles old violence forms part of the information environment in which somebody experiencing violence today decides whether anybody will believe them, whether reporting is worth it, and whether institutions will actually persist when accountability becomes inconvenient.

That feels extremely at home in this folder, because it turns 🌹🧸 from a historical archive into an argument about why unfinished historical justice has present-tense consequences.”
