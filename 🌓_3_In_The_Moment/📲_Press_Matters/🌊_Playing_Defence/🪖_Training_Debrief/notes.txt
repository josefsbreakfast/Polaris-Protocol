## 🩺 Existing Presenting Complaint

- September 2026 reporting indicated that the Army was looking for approximately **£30 million of in-year savings**.
- Major collective-training activity was reportedly among the expenditure being curtailed.
- Training directly connected to:
  - deployments;
  - readiness;
  - rapid-response requirements;
  - operational commitments;
  was being protected.
- The Army has **not stopped training**.
- The important question has always been:
  - what training has stopped?
  - what capability did it generate?
  - what is replacing it?
  - what operational/readiness risk is created by removing it?
  - who assessed that risk?
  - who formally accepted it?

- This matters because:
  - individual competence is not collective competence;
  - collective competence has to be practised;
  - combat skills fade;
  - collective training progresses through increasingly complex levels;
  - sections/platoons/companies/battlegroups/brigades do not become competent merely because their individual members are competent.

- Existing useful formulation:

  > **The price of an activity and the value of that activity are different things.**

- Another existing formulation:

  > **The casualty that never happens because a unit rehearsed the situation properly has no invoice.**

- Preparedness frequently produces **absent events**.
- Those absent events are difficult to count in a budget.

## 🪖 What Collective Training Actually Generates

- Collective training potentially generates:
  - command practice;
  - team cohesion;
  - communications practice;
  - logistics rehearsal;
  - equipment integration;
  - combined-arms integration;
  - familiarity between units;
  - identification of failure points;
  - adaptation to changing doctrine;
  - repeated practice;
  - trust;
  - tacit knowledge;
  - understanding of how other people actually behave under pressure;
  - opportunities to make mistakes without making them operationally.

- Military competence contains knowledge that is:
  - procedural;
  - relational;
  - embodied;
  - contextual;
  - partially tacit.

- Soldiers need opportunities to discover:
  - how other people move;
  - how commanders behave;
  - where communications break;
  - how equipment interacts;
  - how plans fail;
  - how rapidly mistakes propagate;
  - what another person is likely to do without requiring a lengthy explanation.

## 🤖 Technology Is Not The Enemy Here

- The Training Debrief already explicitly rejects a false choice between:
  - modern/synthetic/AI-assisted training;
  - live and collective training.

- AI, simulation and virtual training may:
  - increase repetitions;
  - expose personnel to more scenarios;
  - improve decision training;
  - allow experimentation;
  - reduce some costs;
  - allow some activities that would otherwise be impractical.

- The 2025 Strategic Defence Review itself uses an **augmentation model**:
  - advanced simulation can make training more effective and efficient;
  - live firing remains essential to assuring fighting capability;
  - training must be a strategic and institutional priority for restoring readiness.

- Existing Training Debrief question:

  > **Is technology augmenting collective competence, or financially substituting for activities whose important functions it cannot reproduce?**

- That requires evidence.
- We should not assume either:
  - synthetic training can replace everything;
  - or synthetic training is inherently inferior.

## 💷 September 2026 Training Restriction — New Evidence

- Parliamentary questioning has made the original reporting somewhat firmer.
- Ben Obese-Jecty asked about:
  - the scope of the directive affecting collective training;
  - cancellation of Royal Tank Regiment live-firing;
  - whether the Defence Secretary made a decision on **3 September 2026** to stop collective training above sub-unit level.

- MOD subsequently confirmed that the Royal Tank Regiment exercise was cancelled following a **wider Defence direction** concerning training priorities.

- The government has not clearly answered:
  - the complete scope of that direction;
  - whether "above sub-unit level" accurately describes it;
  - whether the Defence Secretary personally made the 3 September decision;
  - the complete decision chain;
  - the complete readiness assessment;
  - who formally accepted the resulting risk.

- The ministerial answer instead emphasised:
  - spending prioritisation;
  - greatest operational effect;
  - protection of readiness;
  - operational commitments;
  - safety-critical activity;
  - NATO commitments.

- Politically/communicatively, that answer does **not acknowledge the training reduction as an obvious problem requiring a detailed explanation**.
- Its basic institutional stance is:
  - prioritisation is occurring;
  - important activity is protected;
  - the system is therefore functioning as intended.

- That does not establish that there is no training deficit.
- It means the government has not yet publicly demonstrated:
  - what capability has been lost;
  - what replaces it;
  - why the trade-off is acceptable.

## 💷 Wider Political Problem

- Defence policy says:
  - readiness matters;
  - training matters;
  - warfighting readiness must improve;
  - Britain must adapt to lessons from Ukraine;
  - technology must modernise;
  - NATO requirements must be met.

- Meanwhile:
  - collective training has been curtailed in response to affordability pressure.

- The important question is therefore not simply:

  > **Is Britain spending enough on Defence?**

- It is:

  > **What does Britain expect the Armed Forces to do, and what system is actually required to produce that capability reliably?**

- Then:
  - what does that system cost?
  - what happens when one apparently flexible component is removed?
  - are savings being made because an activity is strategically least valuable?
  - or because it is administratively easiest to stop?

- Existing hypothesis:

  > **financial flexibility becoming capability vulnerability**

- Still a hypothesis.
- Needs evidence about:
  - delegated budgets;
  - spending categories;
  - contractual commitments;
  - alternatives considered;
  - Army Command advice;
  - ministerial decisions.

# 🛸 DRONE WARS

## 🛸 What Happened

- British Army held a **Drone Wars** competition at Sandhurst.
- Organised through / closely associated with **16 Air Assault Brigade**.
- 16 Air Assault Brigade has a significant role in developing Army practice for operating **with and against drones**.
- Reporting differs slightly on participation:
  - *Sunday Times*: 24 teams of four;
  - Forces News: 23 teams entered.
- Preserve that discrepancy rather than silently harmonising it.

- Teams came from across the existing Army.
- Reported participants included:
  - Gurkhas;
  - paratroopers;
  - Royal Tank Regiment drone specialists;
  - Infantry Battle School personnel;
  - Ranger Regiment personnel;
  - other Army teams.

- Overall winner reported as a combined **2nd and 4th Battalion Ranger Regiment** team.

- Important:
  - this was not a newly created class of Future Drone People™;
  - existing soldiers were being exposed to new tactical problems and technologies.

---

## 🏃 Exercise Structure

- Forces News compared it to **Cambrian Patrol with drones**.
- Teams followed an approximately **six-hour patrol route**.
- There were **six missions**.
- Reported structure:
  - approximately 30 minutes to move between tasks;
  - approximately 30 minutes to complete each task.

- This introduces:
  - physical movement;
  - time pressure;
  - tactical decision-making;
  - fatigue;
  - survivability;
  - teamwork;
  - technology employment.

- It therefore wasn't simply:

  > **Can you fly a drone?**

- It was closer to:

  > **Can you use a drone to solve a military problem while still behaving like a soldier?**

---

## 🎮 Reported Tasks

- Reported tasks included:
  - flying a drone into a bunker;
  - aerial reconnaissance ahead of an infantry attack;
  - FPV attack against a moving vehicle;
  - ISR-related tasks;
  - concealment;
  - deception;
  - avoiding detection;
  - operating around thermal detection / signatures.

- Exact public descriptions of all six tasks should be preserved if we can locate them.
- Do not assume every task has yet been fully reported.

---

## 🌳 Deliberately Making Drone Operation Difficult

- The exercise did not appear designed around ideal hobby-drone conditions.
- Operators worked:
  - from concealment;
  - around trees/obstacles;
  - with interference to links/video;
  - while needing to manage their own detectability.

- This matters because:
  - technical piloting proficiency is only one component;
  - military drone employment occurs inside a hostile environment.

- The problem becomes:
  - fly;
  - observe;
  - interpret;
  - communicate;
  - remain concealed;
  - manage signatures;
  - complete the tactical task;
  - survive.

---

## ☂️ Low-Tech Adaptation Still Matters

- Reporting described improvised solutions including **umbrellas used in relation to thermal concealment**.

- Useful conceptual point:
  - future warfare does not mean every useful adaptation is high-tech;
  - new technological environments can make very simple physical adaptations valuable.

- Competition potentially creates space for:
  - improvisation;
  - experimentation;
  - bottom-up problem-solving.

- Useful vibe:

  > **Have we tried... umbrella?**

---

## 🚙 Moving Vehicle / BRDM Task

- One reported task used a full-size mock-up of a **BRDM-type Soviet-designed armoured reconnaissance vehicle**.
- Built around a Unimog chassis.
- Reportedly included:
  - fibreglass vehicle body;
  - cage protection representative of anti-drone protection;
  - modified exhaust arrangements;
  - attempts to provide representative optical/thermal signatures.

- Purpose:
  - give FPV operators something more representative than a static cardboard target.

- Reported scoring:
  - **4 points** — preferred target area;
  - **2 points** — vehicle hit;
  - **0 points** — miss.

- At the point Forces News observed:
  - all 11 teams that had attempted the task had reportedly hit the vehicle;
  - five had hit the preferred target area.

- Useful distinction:
  - successful contact with a target is not necessarily equal performance;
  - exercise design can discriminate between levels of tactical/technical proficiency.

---

## 🎯 What Drone Wars Was Actually Testing

- Public language emphasised:
  - speed;
  - precision;
  - lethality.

- But the exercise appears to test a wider bundle:
  - drone piloting;
  - FPV control;
  - reconnaissance;
  - target identification;
  - tactical judgement;
  - concealment;
  - deception;
  - signature management;
  - teamwork;
  - adaptation;
  - time management;
  - degraded communications;
  - decision-making under pressure.

- Useful ladder:

  > **technical proficiency ≠ tactical proficiency ≠ collective military capability**

- Drone racing can develop:
  - speed;
  - precision;
  - control.

- Drone Wars tries to place those technical abilities inside:
  - tactical problems;
  - contested environments;
  - military objectives.

---

## 🧠 Competition As A Learning Mechanism

- Brigadier Ed Cartwright's explanation is important.
- Competition is being used deliberately to **accelerate adaptation**.
- Logic appears to be something like:

  > **competition → pressure → experimentation → visible variance → identify talent/good practice → organisational learning**

- The useful output is therefore not simply:
  - who won?

- Better questions:
  - who solved particular problems unusually well?
  - which techniques emerged?
  - which approaches failed?
  - what did different Army cultures/units do differently?
  - which adaptations were transferable?
  - which depended on particular individuals?
  - what did organisers learn that they did not already expect?
  - how are lessons captured?
  - how are they disseminated?

- Do **not** infer:
  - Ranger Regiment won therefore Rangers are objectively the Army's best drone soldiers.
- It was:
  - one competition;
  - six particular tasks;
  - one scoring architecture;
  - one set of participants;
  - one moment in a rapidly changing field.

- Their performance raises questions rather than closing them.

---

## 🧑‍🏫 Talent Discovery

- This directly connects to our earlier discussion.

- The Army already contains:
  - experienced soldiers;
  - tactically skilled personnel;
  - engineers;
  - technically minded personnel;
  - people with civilian technical skills;
  - reservists with relevant civilian experience;
  - people with aptitude that may not have been visible under previous training systems.

- New capability does not necessarily require:
  - abandoning existing personnel;
  - treating previous military experience as obsolete;
  - inventing an entirely separate technological warrior caste.

- Alternative model:

  > **Find the talent already inside the system. Give people the new tools. Train them. See who adapts. Develop them further. Spread what works.**

- Drone Wars appears broadly compatible with that model.

## 🪖 Existing Soldiering Skills Still Matter

- Drone warfare does not abolish:
  - fieldcraft;
  - concealment;
  - reconnaissance;
  - tactical judgement;
  - communication;
  - leadership;
  - logistics;
  - teamwork;
  - terrain understanding;
  - adaptation under pressure.

- It changes the environment in which those skills operate.

- Persistent aerial observation makes:
  - concealment more difficult;
  - movement more observable;
  - signatures more dangerous;
  - mistakes potentially more rapidly punishable.

- Therefore the adaptation problem is often:

  > **existing skill + changed environment + new tool**

- Not:

  > **old soldier bad; drone boy good.**

## 🔄 The Important Next Step

- Drone Wars appears to move soldiers from:
  - technical proficiency;
  toward
  - tactical proficiency.

- The Training Debrief question then becomes:

  > **How does that knowledge become collective competence?**

- Finding 4 excellent people does not automatically generate:
  - a competent platoon;
  - company;
  - battlegroup;
  - brigade.

- Knowledge has to travel.

- Questions:
  - Do winners/participants become instructors?
  - Are lessons codified?
  - Do units receive revised training packages?
  - Does doctrine change?
  - Are techniques trialled elsewhere?
  - Does 16 Air Assault Brigade act as a dissemination hub?
  - Are training establishments receiving the lessons?
  - How rapidly can useful practice spread?
  - How do they prevent useful improvisation becoming rigid doctrine too early?
  - How do they distinguish genuinely transferable practice from something that merely scored well in the competition?

## 🌍 Possible Future Expansion

- Reporting indicates an ambition to develop future Drone Wars iterations toward:
  - tri-service participation;
  - eventually international participation.

- Potential learning architecture:

  > **Army units → inter-service competition → allied competition → comparison of different training/doctrinal cultures**

- Potential value:
  - expose different assumptions;
  - reveal different solutions;
  - identify transferable techniques;
  - accelerate adaptation;
  - create shared understanding with allies.

- But:
  - competition design matters;
  - scoring determines behaviour;
  - participants may optimise for the exercise rather than war;
  - lessons therefore still require validation elsewhere.

# 🔗 Where Drone Wars Meets The Training Deficit

- The correct connection is **not**:

  > training is being cut but look, they're wasting time playing with drones.

- Drone Wars appears potentially useful.

- The connection is:

  > **Successful technological modernisation creates additional training and integration requirements.**

- New drones create requirements for:
  - operators;
  - instructors;
  - maintainers;
  - commanders who understand their use;
  - reconnaissance integration;
  - fires integration;
  - airspace management;
  - communications;
  - electronic-warfare understanding;
  - counter-UAS;
  - concealment/signature management;
  - logistics;
  - doctrine;
  - collective practice.

- Therefore:

  > **Modernisation does not necessarily reduce the training requirement. It can increase its complexity.**

- This is why:
  - drone acquisition is not drone capability;
  - individual drone qualification is not tactical capability;
  - tactical drone proficiency is not formation-level competence.

- Eventually everybody has to practise together.

## 🏞️ Training Estate

- Training estate belongs in the same system.
- MOD has itself described suitable and accessible training estate as an enabler of:
  - force generation;
  - readiness;
  - Regular training;
  - Reserve training.

- Modernisation can change estate requirements rather than eliminate them.

- Drone-era training may require environments suitable for:
  - longer-range observation;
  - contested movement;
  - signature management;
  - realistic target systems;
  - counter-UAS;
  - EW;
  - live/synthetic integration;
  - combined-arms activity.

- MOD has been adapting parts of the estate to contemporary warfare, including Ukraine-style trench infrastructure at Salisbury Plain and STANTA.

- Therefore:

  > **modernisation requires estate; it does not abolish estate.**

- Separate investigation needed into:
  - estate availability;
  - utilisation;
  - disposals;
  - access;
  - capacity;
  - geographic suitability;
  - infrastructure;
  - competing civilian/military pressures.

# 🧿 Current Working Assessment

- Drone Wars currently looks like a **potentially sensible experimental training intervention**.
- It appears to:
  - use existing Army talent;
  - expose personnel to emerging battlefield problems;
  - reward adaptation;
  - combine technical and tactical proficiency;
  - allow bottom-up experimentation;
  - create observable differences in performance.

- It does **not** answer the wider collective-training problem.

- Instead it gives us a particularly clear example of why that problem matters:

  > **Finding the best solution in an experiment is only useful if the institution can learn it, distribute it, practise it and integrate it.**

- Or, less politely:

  > **Cool. You found the good drone lads.**
  >
  > **Now train the fucking Army.**
