British Army training cuts / Defence settlement — extensive working notes

STATUS: WORKING NOTES
PURPOSE: Capture the discussion so far before further research / drafting.
CENTRAL THREAD: The reported ~£30m Army training saving is interesting not simply as a budget cut, but as a possible symptom of a recurring British defence problem: protecting large programmes and easily quantified assets while allowing harder-to-measure human capability, collective training and readiness to absorb financial pressure.

⸻

🪖 Immediate issue: Army collective training cuts

* Reported measure:
    * British Army required to find approximately £30m of savings.
    * Major/non-essential collective training activity reportedly suspended or reduced.
    * Essential training and operational requirements continue.
    * Navy and RAF appear to have experienced smaller or differently structured reductions rather than an equivalent suspension of collective activity.
* Immediate question:
    * Why has the Army apparently taken the most visible/readiness-sensitive hit?
* Important distinction:
    * this may reflect an Army-specific in-year shortfall;
    * it may reflect what expenditure Army Command can actually switch off quickly;
    * it may reflect higher-level prioritisation;
    * it may reflect protected/ringfenced programmes elsewhere;
    * it may reflect ministerial/departmental communication failures;
    * we do not yet know which.
* The important question is therefore not merely:
    * “Who cut Army training?”
* It is:
    * At what level was somebody first told that the financial settlement would result in reduced collective training, and what happened when that information travelled upwards?

⸻

💷 £30m is small in state-budget terms

* £30m is meaningful to the Army training programme.
* It is extremely small relative to:
    * total Defence expenditure;
    * the Defence Investment Plan;
    * major procurement programmes;
    * the nuclear enterprise;
    * Dreadnought.
* Therefore:
    * the question is not whether £30m exists somewhere within British government;
    * the question is why this particular £30m could not be found, moved, negotiated, deferred or otherwise accommodated.
* This does not mean budgets are completely fungible.
* Capital expenditure, RDEL, committed programmes and contractual obligations cannot simply be exchanged casually.
* But:
    * if the budgeting architecture makes a strategically valuable £30m activity easier to cancel than comparatively marginal expenditure elsewhere,
    * the architecture itself deserves scrutiny.

⸻

☢️ Dreadnought comparison

* July 2026:
    * government announced approximately £8.4bn for the next stage of the Dreadnought SSBN programme.
* Wider Dreadnought programme:
    * approximately £31bn programme + £10bn contingency.
* £30m is roughly:
    * 0.36% of £8.4bn;
    * the £8.4bn commitment is about 280 times larger.
* This is not an argument to cancel Dreadnought to fund exercises.
* The budgets/programmes are structurally different.
* The useful comparison is political and institutional:
    * a state capable of managing an £8.4bn strategic commitment should be capable of finding a solution to a £30m readiness problem.
* Particularly awkward message to Army personnel:
    * enormous strategic/capital programmes remain protected;
    * comparatively small expenditure on people actually practising warfare is vulnerable.

⸻

🧮 Protected programmes versus flexible activity

* Potential structural problem:
    * large programmes may be:
        * contractually committed;
        * politically protected;
        * ringfenced;
        * strategically designated;
        * difficult to alter quickly.
* Training is comparatively:
    * discretionary in accounting terms;
    * schedulable;
    * cancellable;
    * capable of producing immediate in-year savings.
* This can create a perverse rule:
    * the expenditure most capable of being changed absorbs the shock, rather than the expenditure with the least strategic value.
* Flexibility can therefore become a liability.
* Relevant question:
    * Was Army training cut because it was the least important thing, or because it was the easiest thing to stop?
* Those are radically different explanations.

⸻

🏗️ Contracting and lock-in

* Government often describes major contracts as effectively fixed commitments.
* But contractual lock-in runs in both directions:
    * suppliers benefit enormously from long-term government contracts;
    * losing a major government customer can be highly undesirable for the contractor.
* Therefore, during genuine affordability pressure:
    * government can negotiate;
    * seek temporary price restraint;
    * restructure delivery schedules;
    * renegotiate increments;
    * spread savings across multiple suppliers/programmes.
* This does not guarantee £30m could easily have been extracted from contractors.
* But it raises a legitimate political question:
    * was serious commercial negotiation attempted before cutting human training capability?
* Wider concern:
    * procurement expenditure creates concentrated commercial constituencies;
    * training expenditure largely does not.
* A defence contractor has:
    * executives;
    * lobbyists;
    * shareholders;
    * political relationships;
    * jobs attached to constituencies;
    * announcements and ribbon-cuttings.
* A casualty prevented by better training:
    * has no invoice;
    * no lobbying firm;
    * no press release;
    * and is inherently difficult to count.

⸻

🏋️ Why Army collective training is different

Collective competence is not individual competence

* An Army is not simply:
    * X individually competent soldiers;
    * plus X functioning vehicles;
    * plus equipment.
* Collective warfare depends upon:
    * teams;
    * sections;
    * platoons;
    * companies;
    * battlegroups;
    * brigades;
    * supporting arms;
    * logistics;
    * communications;
    * command relationships;
    * rehearsed responses.
* People need to learn:
    * how other people move;
    * how commanders behave;
    * how units respond under pressure;
    * how errors propagate;
    * how communication fails;
    * how equipment interacts;
    * how to recognise other people’s intentions rapidly.
* Much of this knowledge is:
    * tacit;
    * embodied;
    * relational;
    * difficult to teach through classroom instruction.
* Useful football analogy:
    * having the best individual players does not automatically produce the best team;
    * they need repeated time playing together.
* Collective training therefore generates human interoperability.

⸻

🤖 VR / AI cannot simply replace collective training

* AI, simulation and VR can be extremely useful.
* They can:
    * increase repetitions;
    * expose personnel to varied scenarios;
    * simulate threats;
    * assist decision training;
    * reduce some costs;
    * support equipment familiarisation.
* But they cannot fully reproduce:
    * physical exhaustion;
    * terrain;
    * weather;
    * equipment failure;
    * real interpersonal uncertainty;
    * unit movement;
    * physical logistics;
    * stress generated by actual bodies moving around each other;
    * trust developed through repeated shared activity.
* Therefore:
    * simulation should augment collective training rather than becoming an accounting substitute for it.
* Historical irony:
    * Defence is simultaneously promoting more AI/digital/VR training while reducing funding for some of the physical collective training those systems are meant to complement.

* Relevant present-day lesson:

* Therefore 2026 training decisions land against deep institutional memory, not a blank slate.

🏚️ Training reform after Iraq / Afghanistan

Andrew Kennett / Land Warfare reform

* February 2008:
    * Andrew Kennett appointed Director General Land Warfare.
* Responsible for preparing British troops for operations.
* Reform already underway under Graham Lamb.
* Accelerated under David Richards’ Operation Integrity programme.
* Approximately £65m over 12 months provided to transform training.
* Political/institutional context:
    * Iraq proved harder than expected;
    * British forces became bogged down;
    * casualties were mounting in Iraq and Afghanistan.
* Useful comparison:
    * £65m was regarded as a substantial training-reform injection in 2008.
    * Current £30m saving is therefore not trivial when measured against historical Army training interventions.

⸻

BATUS reform

* Training at BATUS moved beyond conventional pop-up tank targets.
* Added:
    * improvised three-dimensional villages;
    * drone technology;
    * battle-casualty simulation;
    * multi-stage dismounted fighting;
    * less predictable orders of advance;
    * more varied enemy formations.
* Important:
    * drone integration into Army training is not a post-Ukraine invention.
    * British training was incorporating drones many years ago.
* Therefore current rhetoric about “new technology” should not imply:
    * drones/technology replace collective training;
    * the Army has only recently discovered technological warfare.
* Historically:
    * technological integration was part of making physical collective training more realistic, not a replacement for it.

🥾 OPTAG

Initial problem

* Operational Training and Advisory Group prepared personnel for theatre.
* Heritage linked to Northern Ireland Training and Advisory Team.
* Richard Wesley had gone through OPTAG before Afghanistan.
* His battalion:
    * was on public duties;
    * received relatively short notice for Afghanistan;
    * had roughly eight weeks to convert to Bowman radios and prepare.
* Wesley found OPTAG:
    * under-resourced;
    * poorly focused;
    * although flexible.
* He later took command and sought major reform.

⸻

⏳ Eight weeks is tight

* Important distinction:
    * there is no magical universal “six-week neuroplasticity law”.
* But:
    * complex procedural/motor/team learning requires repetition and consolidation.
* Eight weeks to:
    * learn/convert equipment;
    * prepare tactically;
    * adapt collectively;
    * prepare personally/family-wise for deployment;
    * enter a war zone;
    * is plainly a compressed schedule.
* Transformation cannot always be accelerated simply by adding information.
* Some competence requires time spent doing the thing repeatedly.

⸻

👨‍🏫 Instructor credibility

* Wesley found instructors preparing troops for Afghanistan who had never served there.
* He regarded this as inadequate.
* OPTAG was not considered a prestigious posting.
* Best NCOs often preferred:
    * Brecon;
    * Sandhurst.
* Wesley worked to raise OPTAG’s institutional status.
* Important organisational lesson:
    * institutions do not simply allocate expertise rationally;
    * prestige hierarchies affect where talented people choose to serve.
* Theatre-specific training benefits from:
    * current operational experience;
    * credible instructors;
    * rapid feedback from deployed personnel.

🏘️ Representative training infrastructure

Hay bales versus proper compounds

* Wesley’s battalion had previously practised compound clearance using hay bales at Sennybridge.
* He estimated approximately £20m was needed for improved representative facilities.
* Projects developed at:
    * Lydd/Hythe;
    * Longcross;
    * Thetford.
* Key point:
    * “cheap” training infrastructure can be falsely economical.
* Hay bales:
    * degrade;
    * need sourcing;
    * need transport;
    * need replacement;
    * require disposal;
    * do not reproduce real structures particularly well.
* Proper facilities:
    * cost capital;
    * but can produce better repeated training over time.
* Infrastructure is itself part of training capability.

🩸 Catastrophic injury and invisible costs

Successful rehabilitation does not erase injury

* Derenalagi’s later athletic success is extraordinary.
* But a public story such as:
    * catastrophic injury → rehabilitation → Paralympics
    * can accidentally make the outcome look narratively complete.
* In general, major traumatic amputation can involve:
    * residual-limb pain;
    * phantom-limb pain;
    * repeated prosthetic fitting;
    * skin breakdown;
    * altered biomechanics;
    * fatigue;
    * musculoskeletal problems;
    * accessibility limitations;
    * psychological trauma;
    * grief;
    * major identity/body adaptation;
    * lifelong clinical needs.
* Do not attribute any specific complication to Derenalagi without evidence.
* The point is about the possible burden associated with injuries of this class.

⸻

Acute physiological severity

* Catastrophic blast injuries involving traumatic limb loss carry obvious risk of:
    * massive haemorrhage;
    * shock;
    * coagulopathy;
    * cardiac arrest;
    * death.
* Modern major-haemorrhage management:
    * haemorrhage control;
    * blood products / balanced resuscitation;
    * careful avoidance of excessive crystalloid.
* Historical/austere environments may constrain what is immediately available.
* Important conceptual point:
    * survival itself may require overcoming a massive physiological insult before rehabilitation even begins.

⸻

The state acquires a lifetime obligation

* When someone is catastrophically injured in military service, consequences may include decades of:
    * rehabilitation;
    * prosthetics;
    * prosthetic replacement;
    * specialist healthcare;
    * pain management;
    * mental-health support;
    * housing adaptations;
    * compensation;
    * pensions;
    * employment/retraining;
    * family/carer effects.
* These should not be framed as “the injured veteran costs money”.
* Rather:
    * the state created the occupational exposure and therefore acquired the obligation.
* Prevention is therefore valuable both:
    * morally;
    * operationally;
    * economically.

🦾 Defence technology should include rehabilitation technology

    * less political glamour around rehabilitation engineering.
* Defence-adjacent AI could have major value in:
    * adaptive prosthetic control;
    * sensor fusion;
    * gait recognition;
    * myoelectric control;
    * terrain adaptation;
    * rehabilitation robotics;
    * potentially neural interfaces.
* Useful principle:
    * if the state invests heavily in technology that sends humans into danger, it should invest comparably seriously in technology that restores capability when those humans return injured.
* Weapons technology and rehabilitation technology belong in the same moral accounting system.

🧠 Training realism and stress inoculation

Tightening live-fire arcs

* Purpose:
    * accustom personnel to rounds passing closer;

* Some personnel felt OPTAG left them excessively primed:

* Wesley’s position:

    * commanders could subsequently bring that level down.
* Relevant psychological question:
    * optimal stress inoculation versus excessive hypervigilance.
* This sounds like an area where:
    * the basic principle was defensible;

🎯 OPTAG’s success produced another problem: overfitting

* Akam’s particularly important argument:

🚁 CASEVAC / casualty behaviour

* Stuart Nicholson at Brecon in 2012 observed Afghanistan/Iraq veterans responding automatically to simulated casualties.
* Their reflex:
    * casualty-in-contact procedures;
    * prepare helicopter landing site;
    * get casualty evacuated.
* Completely rational within Afghanistan.
* But in general/peer war:
    * Britain may not control the air;
    * helicopter evacuation may not be available;
    * CASEVAC may be delayed;
    * stopping the attack may create additional casualties.
* Brecon instructors therefore had to re-teach a brutal alternative.

Preserve the source wording

“Fucking leave him and come back for him!”

* Do not sanitise to:
    * “Leave him and come back for him.”
* The profanity matters.

🧠 Unlearning is training too

* Training is not only:
    * acquiring new knowledge.
* It can require:
    * suppressing previously successful automatic responses;
    * recognising when context has changed;
    * developing conditional judgement;
    * replacing reflexes.
* This strengthens the case for repeated collective training.
* You cannot simply:

    * explain the new doctrine;
    * assume embodied responses change.
* Particularly important for highly experienced veterans:
    * expertise itself can become context-specific.

    * tokenise the veteran;
    * spend their credibility;
    * damage their standing if policy subsequently fails.
* Equivalent problem exists with:
    * one doctor representing NHS;
    * one teacher representing education;
    * one survivor representing all survivors.

📣 Political presentation versus practical policy

* Same practical intervention can be sold through very different vocabularies.
* Examples:
    * “humanitarian maritime rescue”
    * versus
    * “persistent surveillance and rapid response securing Britain’s maritime frontier”.
* Or:
    * “spend money supporting asylum seekers”
    * versus
    * “process cases quickly so taxpayers stop paying indefinite accommodation contracts”.
* Or:
    * “improve soldiers’ conditions”
    * versus
    * “improve retention and preserve expensive trained capability”.
* Political lesson:
    * people do not always disagree about the intervention; sometimes they disagree about the moral vocabulary surrounding it.
* Useful prosocial technique:
    * package decent policy in language different constituencies can accept.
* Operation Fortress comedy:
    * aggressive anti-migration branding accidentally contains substantial SAR/safeguarding capacity.
* Hence:
    * Nigel Farage accidentally invents Search and Rescue Premium™.

🏚️ Defence estate / nominal versus usable assets

* MOD owns substantial land/infrastructure.
* But:
    * “MOD owns it”
    * does not mean
    * “it is immediately usable”.
* Training land may be constrained by:
    * maintenance;
    * safety;
    * contamination;
    * environmental restrictions;
    * infrastructure condition;
    * access;
    * range availability.
* Barracks/accommodation may require:
    * heating;
    * plumbing;
    * electrical work;
    * fire-safety work;
    * asbestos remediation;
    * damp treatment;
    * sanitation;
    * kitchens;
    * transport.
* Therefore:
    * empty ≠ available;
    * available ≠ habitable;
    * habitable ≠ appropriately located.
* Relevant to political proposals to house asylum seekers on former military sites.
* Strong practical test:
    * politician proposing accommodation should spend a wet November weekend there without ministerial car.
* Transport matters:
    * many military sites are isolated;
    * no car can mean poor access to:
        * services;
        * shops;
        * healthcare;
        * community.

📚 JSP burden

* JSPs / compliance architecture can consume substantial time.
* Not an argument against:
    * law;
    * safety;
    * proper procedure.
* Rather:
    * systems can become so administratively cumbersome that they consume capacity from already stretched personnel.
* Potential reform question:
    * which JSP requirements genuinely manage risk and which represent accumulated administrative sediment?
* Worth investigating separately rather than assuming all bureaucracy is useless.
* Possible productivity gain:
    * simplifying compliance could free substantial personnel time without reducing front-line capability.

🏥 Public-service analogy

* Strong analogy with healthcare:
    * administrators see a line item;
    * clinicians see downstream human consequences.
* A hospital bed is not healthcare capacity without:
    * staff;
    * equipment;
    * diagnostics;
    * pharmacy;
    * cleaning;
    * logistics.
* Similarly:
    * land is not training capacity;
    * a soldier is not collective readiness;
    * a tank is not a functioning armoured formation.
* General principle:
    * the state repeatedly confuses nominal assets with usable systems.
* When support layers have been hollowed out for years:
    * another small cut can produce nonlinear effects.

📉 £30m does not equal £30m of capability

Central formulation:

£30m is not necessarily the value of what has been cut. £30m is merely what it costs to purchase that activity this year.

Value potentially includes:

* readiness;
* cohesion;
* interoperability;
* operational learning;
* trust;
* command practice;
* logistics practice;
* equipment integration;
* mistakes avoided;
* casualties avoided;
* equipment losses avoided;
* institutional learning.

Therefore:

* financial saving and capability loss cannot be assumed to be equal.

👻 The casualty that never happens has no invoice

Potential key Polaris line:

A tank has a price. A drone has a price. A contract has a price. An exercise has a price. The casualty that never happens because a unit rehearsed the situation properly has no invoice.

* This is the core measurement problem.
* Prevention produces:
    * absent events.
* Absent events are difficult to count.
* Therefore prevention can look less valuable than visible procurement.
* Same problem appears in:
    * medicine;
    * safeguarding;
    * public health;
    * cybersecurity;
    * policing;
    * disaster preparedness.

Current evidence supports a narrower proposition:

That is enough to investigate.

🧠 Bigger institutional thesis

The Akam material suggests a recurring British Army problem:

1. capability becomes constrained;
2. conflict exposes deficiencies;
3. people are injured/killed;
4. resources suddenly become available;
5. training/equipment reform accelerates;
6. operational learning improves;
7. immediate conflict recedes;
8. fiscal pressure returns;
9. hard-to-quantify readiness activity becomes vulnerable again.

Potential Polaris question:

Does Britain repeatedly require operational failure before it remembers why preparedness costs money?

⸻

🔁 Accountability thesis

Potential cybernetic formulation:

An institution cannot learn reliably if negative feedback changes procedures but never changes power.

* If:
    * junior personnel absorb consequences;
    * training establishments absorb lessons;
    * senior decision-makers remain unaffected;
* then:
    * the organisation may adapt tactically without adapting institutionally.

Potential companion:

The system was wrong, but apparently nobody sufficiently senior was wrong.

⸻

🪖 Core human argument

The sharpest version is not:

Give the Army whatever money it asks for.

Nor:

Generals always know best.

Nor:

Training cuts inevitably kill people.

It is:

When people whose occupation requires them to accept extraordinary physical risk repeatedly explain which forms of preparation materially affect their ability to manage that risk, government should require an exceptionally good reason before overriding them for a comparatively small fiscal saving.

Civilian control remains essential.

But civilian control creates responsibility:

If you have never personally borne the risk you are allocating, you have a particularly strong obligation to understand the evidence of those who have.

⸻

🧿 Potential bottom line

The current £30m Army training dispute matters because it exposes how Britain values military capability. Expensive platforms, contracts and technologies are easy to count. Collective competence is not. A soldier who survives because a unit rehearsed the right response does not appear as a Treasury saving. A casualty that never occurs generates no invoice. Yet Iraq and Afghanistan repeatedly demonstrated that realistic preparation, current operational learning and collective competence have human consequences. The question for ministers is therefore not simply why £30m had to be saved. It is why this was judged the least damaging £30m to lose — and who accepted that judgement.

This should give us a very fat working base.
