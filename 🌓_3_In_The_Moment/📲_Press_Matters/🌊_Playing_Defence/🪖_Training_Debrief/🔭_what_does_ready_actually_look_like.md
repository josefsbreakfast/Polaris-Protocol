# 🔭 What Does Ready Actually Look Like?

**First created:** 2026-09-07 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-20  
*Define readiness by military function, time, sustainment and regenerative depth — then test whether the whole system can actually produce it.*

---

## 🔭 Orientation

Britain says it wants Armed Forces which are:

> **ready to deter, fight and win.**

Good.

Ready to do what?

At what scale?

At what notice?

For how long?

Against what level of resistance?

With which allies?

Using what logistics?

With what casualty system?

What happens when equipment breaks?

What happens when communications fail?

Who produces the second force?

How quickly can losses be replaced?

How quickly can the force recover afterwards?

And:

> **When was any of that last demonstrated?**

Those questions matter because readiness is easy to describe badly.

A Defence budget is not readiness.

A platform is not readiness.

A soldier is not readiness.

An exercise is not readiness.

A stockpile is not readiness.

A software programme is not readiness.

All may contribute to readiness.

The useful definition is functional:

> **Can the force perform the required military task, under the expected conditions, at the required scale and notice, for the required duration, while absorbing plausible disruption?**

And now one further condition needs to be explicit:

> **Can it recover, replace losses and generate what comes next?**

Readiness is not a possession.

It is:

> **a perishable system state.**

---

## 1. 🎯 Start With The Function

Before discussing:

- force size;
- spending;
- platforms;
- exercises;
- personnel numbers;
- procurement programmes;

ask:

> **What does Britain expect its Armed Forces to do?**

Possible functions include:

- defend the United Kingdom;
- defend sovereign territories;
- contribute to NATO collective defence;
- maintain the nuclear deterrent;
- protect UK airspace;
- protect maritime approaches;
- secure critical infrastructure;
- provide intelligence and surveillance;
- provide expeditionary capability;
- support allies;
- sustain prolonged operations;
- respond to domestic emergencies;
- regenerate forces after losses.

Not every task requires the same force.

Not every task requires the same notice.

Not every task can necessarily be performed simultaneously.

That is precisely why the requirement must come first.

A political commitment is not yet military capability.

Translate:

```text id="nyx8dp"
political commitment
        ↓
military task
        ↓
required function
        ↓
required force
        ↓
notice
        ↓
movement
        ↓
operational capability
        ↓
sustainment
        ↓
reinforcement
        ↓
recovery
        ↓
regeneration
```

Until that translation happens:

> **commitment**

and:

> **capability**

can live comfortably in separate documents.

War will eventually introduce them.

It would be preferable if government did so first.

## 2. 📐 Every Readiness Requirement Needs Dimensions

“Ready” is meaningless without dimensions.

For every required function specify:

### Function

What must actually happen?

### Scale

How much force is required?

### Notice

How quickly must it act?

### Duration

How long must meaningful activity continue?

### Conditions

Against what plausible disruption or resistance?

### Concurrency

What else must Britain be able to do at the same time?

### Sustainment

What keeps the force operating?

### Recovery

How quickly must the force return to readiness afterwards?

### Regeneration

How quickly can losses and depleted capability be replaced?

Without these:

> **ready**

can mean anything from:

> **can deploy tomorrow**

to:

> **we could probably reconstruct this given sufficient warning and several miracles.**

Those are different capabilities.

## 3. 🧩 Readiness Is A System Property

At a deliberately simplified level:

```text id="kj6qpu"
mission
+
people
+
equipment
+
collective competence
+
command
+
communications
+
intelligence
+
logistics
+
maintenance
+
medicine
+
estate
+
movement
+
industry
+
stocks
+
allies
+
time
=
usable military capability
```

No individual component owns readiness.

Readiness emerges from the relationships between them.

That means:

> **a system can possess excellent components and still fail as a system.**

A sophisticated platform can exist without:

- trained operators;
- trained teams;
- maintenance;
- ammunition;
- logistics;
- communications;
- command;
- collective rehearsal.

A large establishment can exist without the scarce specialists required for a particular task.

An alliance can exist without interoperability.

A headquarters can exist without being able to command effectively under disruption.

So the relevant question is not:

> **What does Britain own?**

It is:

> **What military function can Britain generate from what it owns?**

---

## 4. 🪜 Readiness Exists At Several Organisational Levels

Readiness can exist at one level and fail at another.

### Individual

Can the person perform their role?

### Crew / team

Can the small group operate together?

### Unit

Can the battalion, squadron, ship or equivalent perform its task?

### Formation

Can multiple units operate together?

### Joint force

Can land, maritime, air, cyber, space and enabling functions integrate?

### Allied force

Can Britain actually operate inside the intended coalition?

Individual competence does not automatically aggregate.

Suppose:

- soldiers are qualified;
- vehicles exist;
- radios function;
- ammunition exists.

That does not prove a formation can:

- manoeuvre;
- communicate;
- coordinate effects;
- sustain itself;
- evacuate casualties;
- adapt under pressure;
- fight alongside allies.

A formation is not:

> **a spreadsheet containing individually competent components.**

It is:

> **a relationship between them.**

That relationship has to be practised.

For every major military function, map what has to work.

             ↓

│ EQUIPMENT                                   │
│ COLLECTIVE COMPETENCE                       │

│ MEDICINE                                    │

> **Which dependency fails first?**

That question often tells us considerably more than:

> **How many things have we bought?**

## 6. 🔧 Availability Chains Matter

Headline inventories are particularly dangerous because they collapse several different states into one number.

### Equipment

```text id="47b6z9"
owned
  ↓
in service
  ↓
serviceable
  ↓
allocated
  ↓
available
  ↓
crew available
  ↓
supported
  ↓
usable for required function
```

A ship can exist while being:

- in maintenance;
- short of crew;
- awaiting certification;
- undergoing repair.

An aircraft count does not tell us:

- serviceability;
- crew availability;
- sortie generation;
- weapon stocks;
- tanker support;
- maintenance;
- airfield resilience.

The glamorous platform cannot escape the workshop.

### Personnel

Likewise:

```text id="ml7cz6"
establishment
      ↓
person present
      ↓
trained
      ↓
trade trained
      ↓
medically deployable
      ↓
not committed elsewhere
      ↓
available at required notice
```

So:

> **strength ≠ availability.**

And:

> **inventory ≠ capability.**

And:

> **owned ≠ usable.**

## 7. 👷 Scarce Skills Can Constrain Large Forces

A large organisation can be constrained by a relatively small number of:

- engineers;
- pilots;
- maintainers;
- medics;
- linguists;
- cyber specialists;
- nuclear specialists;
- instructors;
- specialist logisticians.

Therefore readiness needs:

> **critical-skill mapping.**

For each major function ask:

- which scarce trades are required?
- how many are actually available?
- how long do replacements take to train?
- what else are those people supporting?
- what happens if several are unavailable simultaneously?

Retention therefore becomes readiness.

If a specialist takes:

> **years to train**

and leaves rapidly:

the force-generation system may become unstable long before the headline establishment visibly collapses.

People policy is not separate from military capability.

---

## 8. ⏱️ Time Is Capability

A force available in:

> **six months**

is different from one available in:

> **six days.**

If the requirement is 30 days and the force can reliably generate the function after 180:

the force may still be useful.

But:

> **it does not satisfy that requirement.**

Readiness therefore needs a clock.

A useful conceptual distinction is:

### Immediate readiness

Can the required force act now or at the required notice?

### Sustained readiness

Can it continue operating for the required duration?

### Regenerative readiness

Can Britain:

- replace losses;
- rotate personnel;
- repair equipment;
- replenish stocks;
- retrain;
- generate the next force?

This distinction matters enormously.

A system can preserve immediate readiness while quietly degrading:

> **the machinery that produces tomorrow's readiness.**

---

## 9. 🔁 The Second Force Is Part Of The First Readiness Calculation

If the first formation deploys:

- who replaces casualties?
- who rotates exhausted personnel?
- who repairs equipment?
- who replenishes ammunition?
- who trains replacements?
- who prepares the next formation?
- what happens to every other commitment while this occurs?

Readiness is not merely:

> **first contact.**

It includes:

> **what Britain can still do afterwards.**

So:

```text id="j5s7wu"
initial force
+
sustainment
+
replacement
+
repair
+
retraining
+
recovery
=
persistent capability
```

Without regeneration:

> **readiness is consumable.**

---

## 10. 🎓 Regeneration Infrastructure Is Readiness

If Britain needs to expand or rebuild capability, the limiting factor may not be:

> **people willing to serve.**

It may be:

- instructors;
- ranges;
- training areas;
- accommodation;
- equipment;
- administrative throughput;
- maintenance;
- medical capacity;
- industrial capacity.

Instructor capacity is therefore:

> **mobilisation capability.**

Estate is:

> **operational infrastructure.**

Maintenance is:

> **capability persistence.**

Industry is:

> **replacement capacity.**

A country cannot regenerate trained military capability faster than the system capable of producing it.

That is why a short-term saving which damages the force-generation machinery can have effects much larger than the activity initially removed.

---

## 11. 📦 Stocks, Production And Repair Form One Material System

Stockpiles answer:

> **What do we possess?**

Production answers:

> **What can we replace?**

Repair answers:

> **What can we return to use?**

So material readiness is better understood as:

```text id="v1hh4z"
stored capacity
+
production capacity
+
repair capacity
=
material persistence
```

For critical stocks ask:

> **At plausible rates of use, how long does the force remain functional?**

Potential constraints include:

- ammunition;
- missiles;
- fuel;
- medical supplies;
- spare parts.

Exact quantities may legitimately remain sensitive.

But Defence itself must know whether:

> **planned operational duration and available material depth agree.**

A large stockpile without replenishment eventually becomes a countdown.

---

## 12. 🩸 Medicine Is Part Of The Fighting System

A credible military force requires:

- point-of-injury care;
- evacuation;
- surgery;
- rehabilitation;
- mental-health support;
- casualty replacement planning.

Medical constraints affect:

- operational tempo;
- acceptable risk;
- force preservation;
- unit cohesion;
- replacement requirements.

Medicine is not:

> **aftercare.**

It is part of operational capability.

For any serious force plan, somebody needs to have asked:

> **What casualty burden are we planning to absorb?**

Then:

- can medical systems handle it?
- can personnel be replaced?
- can units remain coherent?
- can families be supported?
- can the force regenerate?

The exact number may remain protected.

The arithmetic cannot remain undone.

---

## 13. 🤝 Integration Is A Capability

Britain's strategy is deliberately alliance-heavy.

That can provide enormous capability.

But every allied dependency should specify:

> **What exactly is the ally expected to provide?**

Then:

- has it agreed?
- is it available on the same clock?
- could it be required elsewhere?
- have the forces practised together?

Alliance is not a magic multiplier.

It is:

> **an operational relationship.**

Likewise:

> **NATO membership is not interoperability.**

Two allied forces do not automatically possess:

- compatible communications;
- aligned logistics;
- interoperable ammunition;
- rehearsed command relationships;
- common procedures.

Those properties require practice.

And the same applies inside Britain's own integrated force.

A future effect may require:

```text id="ck4fp6"
sensor
  ↓
intelligence
  ↓
command
  ↓
air / maritime / land / cyber / space
  ↓
logistics
  ↓
effect
```

If those relationships have not been exercised:

> **integration remains partly theoretical.**

## 14. 📡 Command And Communications Must Survive Degradation

A force can retain weapons while losing coordination.

So communications should be tested under:

- electronic attack;
- cyber disruption;
- infrastructure failure;
- degraded satellite access;
- equipment loss.

Test:

- alternative communications;
- degraded-mode procedures;
- redundancy;
- operator competence.

Likewise headquarters need:

- trained staff;
- information;
- authority;
- communications;
- rehearsed decision processes.

The question is:

> **Can the command system still make useful decisions at the required speed when the preferred system stops working?**

Not:

> **Does the headquarters exist?**

---

## 15. 🌧️ Readiness Means Surviving Friction

Perfect conditions are not the standard.

A useful readiness test deliberately introduces:

- communications failure;
- delayed intelligence;
- equipment loss;
- casualties;
- logistics pressure;
- fuel constraint;
- cyber disruption;
- delayed allies;
- destroyed plans;
- missing specialists.

Then ask:

> **What function remains?**

A resilient force becomes:

> **less capable.**

It does not immediately become:

> **incapable.**

This is why exercises should sometimes break things.

The purpose is not humiliation.

It is:

> **to discover whether the function survives contact with friction.**

## 16. 🌧️ The Tuesday Roster Test

Every readiness claim eventually encounters:

> **Who is doing this on Tuesday?**

Actually.

Which people?

Which equipment?

Which specialist trades?

Which headquarters?

Which instructors?

Which transport?

Which medical support?

Which logistics?

Which ally?

If the answer requires:

- everyone present;
- nothing broken;
- nobody sick;
- nobody on leave;
- no simultaneous commitment;
- every contractor available;
- every ally arriving precisely on time;

the capability may exist.

But it is fragile.

Then make Tuesday worse.

- bad weather;
- broken equipment;
- missing specialist;
- delayed ally;
- communications disruption;
- another emergency.

Does the function survive?

That is resilience.

And concurrency eventually faces the same test.

Policy documents can describe Mission A and Mission B separately.

Reality may reveal:

> **they share the same Tuesday roster.**

---

## 17. 🧪 Readiness Must Be Demonstrated

A spreadsheet saying:

> **should work**

is not validation.

Major functions should periodically be demonstrated through combinations of:

- realistic exercises;
- mobilisation tests;
- logistics rehearsals;
- degraded communications;
- allied exercises;
- medical exercises;
- technical testing;
- operational performance.

The closer the test resembles the required function:

> **the stronger the evidence.**

Collective training therefore does two things.

It:

### Produces readiness

through rehearsal.

And:

### Measures readiness

through performance.

That means reducing collective training can affect both:

> **the capability**

and:

> **the sensor used to determine whether the capability exists.**

---

## 18. 🩻 Record Near Misses And Workarounds

Suppose an exercise succeeds only because:

- one unusually experienced person improvises;
- spare equipment happens to exist;
- weather remains favourable;
- a supplier accelerates delivery;
- personnel work excessive hours.

Record it.

The event succeeded.

The system may still be fragile.

Readiness evidence should therefore include:

> **how close success came to failure.**

Likewise repeated workarounds can become readiness debt.

If personnel continually compensate for:

- missing staff;
- broken equipment;
- poor software;
- housing problems;
- logistics delays;

the output may remain green while the underlying system deteriorates.

Ask:

> **What invisible human effort is keeping this capability working?**

The better people become at coping with a broken system:

> **the longer the broken system can look functional.**

---

## 19. 🧠 Ask The People Doing The Job

Central data is necessary.

It is not sufficient.

Ask:

- operators;
- crews;
- instructors;
- engineers;
- logisticians;
- medics;
- junior personnel.

Not merely:

> **Do you feel ready?**

Ask:

> **What stops you doing the task?**

> **What takes too long?**

> **What repeatedly fails?**

> **What do you have to work around?**

> **What are you compensating for manually?**

Quantitative evidence may tell us:

> **how often something fails.**

Professional testimony may tell us:

> **why.**

A strong readiness assessment combines:

- metrics;
- technical evidence;
- exercise performance;
- professional judgement;
- after-action reporting.

Neither:

> **dashboard**

nor:

> **anecdote**

should govern alone.

## 20. 🔬 Readiness Evidence Has A Hierarchy And A Shelf Life

Not all evidence tells us the same thing.

### Stronger direct evidence

- realistic exercise performance;
- validated operational performance;
- demonstrated mobilisation;
- demonstrated sustainment.

### Useful partial evidence

- partial exercises;
- technical testing;
- recent comparable performance;
- validated modelling tied to observed performance.

### More indirect evidence

- administrative readiness;
- declared availability;
- modelling without recent validation.

### Insufficient alone

- spending increased;
- platform purchased;
- programme announced;
- strategy published.

The question is always:

> **What proves the function exists?**

And evidence decays.

A capability demonstrated five years ago may have changed because:

- personnel changed;
- equipment changed;
- doctrine changed;
- allies changed;
- threats changed.

Every readiness claim should therefore know:

> **When was this last validated?**

And:

> **Has the system changed materially since then?**

If yes:

retest.

## 21. 🪜 Use Functional State And Confidence Separately

A readiness assessment should distinguish:

> **what state we think the capability is in**

from:

> **how strong the evidence for that judgement is.**

### Functional state

**RESILIENT**  
Can perform the required function and absorb substantial plausible disruption.

**READY**  
Can perform the required function at required notice, scale and duration.

**CONSTRAINED**  
Can perform it, but with material limitations.

**FRAGILE**  
Can perform only under favourable assumptions or with little margin.

**NOMINAL**  
Capability exists principally on paper or cannot presently be demonstrated at required scale.

**ABSENT**  
Required function cannot currently be generated.

### Confidence

**HIGH**  
Recent, relevant and sufficiently realistic validation exists.

**MODERATE**  
Useful evidence exists but contains material gaps.

**LOW**  
Assessment relies heavily on indirect or stale evidence.

**UNTESTED**  
Required function has not recently been validated at the necessary scale or conditions.

This allows an important distinction.

A function can be:

> **READY / LOW CONFIDENCE**

because evidence is stale.

Another can be:

> **CONSTRAINED / HIGH CONFIDENCE**

because a recent exercise demonstrated exactly where the constraint lies.

The second capability is worse in state.

But better understood.

Do not manufacture certainty because the dashboard demands a colour.

## 22. 📉 Readiness Debt Needs A Balance Sheet

Operational commitments can consume:

- training time;
- personnel;
- maintenance;
- equipment life;
- ammunition;
- family stability;
- instructor capacity.

If recovery does not follow:

> **the force accumulates readiness debt.**

Possible signs include:

- deferred maintenance;
- cancelled training;
- repeated deployments;
- disrupted leave;
- depleted instructor cadres;
- delayed recovery.

So after deployment or major activity ask:

> **How long until the force is genuinely ready again?**

Include:

- personnel recovery;
- maintenance;
- restocking;
- leave;
- retraining;
- equipment replacement.

A force kept permanently at nominal high readiness without recovery can eventually become:

> **permanently tired instead.**

Readiness therefore needs:

> **a balance sheet as well as a snapshot.**

## 23. 🚚 Movement Is Capability

Being ready in barracks is insufficient if the force cannot reach the task.

Assess:

- sealift;
- airlift;
- rail;
- roads;
- ports;
- customs;
- host-nation support;
- loading;
- unloading;
- onward movement.

One of Defence's least glamorous questions is:

> **How does it actually get there?**

Which is exactly why it is useful.

Domestic commitments need the same discipline.

Personnel used for civil contingencies are:

> **not additional people.**

For every domestic assumption ask:

> **Which military capability supplies them?**

And:

> **What happens if an overseas crisis occurs simultaneously?**

Supporting government has an opportunity cost.

Count it.

## 24. 🧠 Readiness Requires Multiple Professional Lenses

No single professional specialism can see the entire readiness system equally well.

An engineer may see:

- equipment availability;
- maintainability;
- technical integration;
- capability architecture.

A land operator may see:

- collective competence;
- tactical cohesion;
- physical endurance;
- command relationships.

A logistician may see:

- fuel;
- transport;
- stocks;
- throughput.

A medic may see:

- casualty assumptions;
- evacuation;
- treatment capacity;
- rehabilitation.

A Treasury official may see:

- affordability;
- opportunity cost;
- fiscal sustainability.

A minister may see:

- political commitments;
- alliances;
- public legitimacy;
- competing national priorities.

None is sufficient alone.

The answer is not:

> **leaders with no specialism.**

That merely produces:

> **people who know less about everything.**

The principle is:

> **Specialisation is necessary. Counter-specialisation is governance.**

## 25. 🛩️ Somebody Needs Permission To Confiscate The Catalogue

Some capability is unusually visible.

You can point at:

- aircraft;
- ships;
- vehicles;
- missiles;
- satellites;
- factories;
- digital systems.

They persist.

They have:

- programmes;
- contracts;
- milestones;
- acquisition costs.

Collective competence behaves differently.

You cannot buy it once.

You repeatedly manufacture it through:

- people;
- equipment;
- instructors;
- exercises;
- logistics;
- command;
- rehearsal.

Then:

> **it decays.**

That makes perishable capability particularly vulnerable in institutions whose strongest administrative machinery is organised around durable assets.

Every senior specialist therefore needs somebody authorised to say:

> **The programme is impressive.**

> **The equipment is excellent.**

> **The transformation case is strong.**

> **But this boring recurring activity is what turns those assets into a fighting force.**

That is not obstruction.

That is governance.

Like shiny things all you want.

> **Just make sure somebody senior is authorised to confiscate the catalogue occasionally.**

## 26. 🔀 Integration Must Preserve Disagreement

Central Defence governance needs to integrate:

- land;
- maritime;
- air;
- strategic;
- medical;
- logistics;
- intelligence;
- personnel;
- technical;
- financial;

judgement.

Integration should mean:

> **joint judgement after specialist evidence has been heard.**

Not:

> **specialist evidence disappears until everybody agrees.**

Service-specific professional evidence is therefore part of the sensor system.

If one part of Defence assesses:

> **this damages the function I am responsible for generating**

that signal should remain recognisable when it reaches the centre.

Senior disagreement may itself be useful readiness information.

Record:

- the competing assessments;
- assumptions;
- evidence;
- final decision;
- residual risk owner.

A system in which senior professionals disagree is not automatically broken.

A system in which they:

> **cannot disagree safely**

may be.

## 27. 🧠 Protect The Negative Channel

A readiness system needs a protected route for saying:

> **No. This is below minimum credible readiness.**

That message needs to reach people with authority without becoming:

> **serious capability loss**

then:

> **readiness pressure**

then:

> **delivery challenge**

then:

> **manageable constraint**

then:

> **broadly on track.**

This is where:

[`⚙️_the_feedback_machine.md`](./⚙️_the_feedback_machine.md)

becomes part of readiness architecture.

A strong centre is only as intelligent as:

> **the signals it receives.**

## 28. 🧾 Define The Requirement Before Affordability

Long periods of financial pressure can teach organisations to pre-compress their own requirements.

Professionals become skilled at:

- prioritising;
- compromising;
- deferring;
- making do;
- anticipating refusal.

Those are useful skills.

But the institution may eventually stop presenting:

> **what the function actually requires**

and instead present:

> **what it believes has some chance of being funded.**

Then the requirement has already been austerised before political choice begins.

The better sequence is:

```text id="4qvk3p"
strategic requirement
       ↓
required military function
       ↓
minimum credible readiness
       ↓
force-generation requirement
       ↓
cost
       ↓
political affordability decision
       ↓
explicit residual risk
```

Not:

```text id="y3ofj6"
budget
  ↓
available force
  ↓
whatever that force can currently do
  ↓
redefine that as the requirement
```

That is budgeting.

It is not strategy.

This is why [`🧾_the_blank_cheque_exercise.md`](./🧾_the_blank_cheque_exercise.md) exists.

## 29. 📐 Define The Minimum Credible State

For each major function distinguish:

### Ideal

The level professionals would prefer with substantial resilience.

### Minimum credible

The lowest state at which the task can still be performed with tolerable risk.

### Failure threshold

Below this, stop describing the function as reliably available.

Then political choice becomes visible.

For example:

> Function X requires READY.

> Current force generates CONSTRAINED.

> Reaching READY requires interventions A, B and C.

> Government has chosen not to fund all of them.

> Residual risks are X and Y.

That is not military government.

It is:

> **civilian control with the military consequence visible.**

## 30. 🎯 Objectives Before Indicators

Start with:

> **Generate the military function required by strategy.**

Then define the evidence required to show it exists.

For example:

- formation achieves required collective competence;
- equipment availability supports the task;
- logistics sustain required duration;
- command system is validated;
- allied integration is demonstrated;
- personnel fill supports deployment and recovery;
- regeneration can produce the second force.

Then:

> **choose KPIs.**

Not before.

Useful indicators may include:

- equipment availability;
- trained personnel;
- exercise outcomes;
- sortie generation;
- repair times;
- stock depth;
- mobilisation speed;
- retention;
- allied certification.

But:

> **the dashboard is the instrument panel.**

It is not:

> **the engine.**

A green metric can coexist with a broken system if the wrong thing is being measured.

## 31. 💷 Inputs Need Operational Endpoints

Spending is an input.

> **£X billion spent**

proves:

> **£X billion was spent.**

Procurement is an input.

Instead of:

> **50 vehicles delivered**

ask:

> **What military function can now be generated that could not previously be generated?**

Instead of:

> **contract awarded**

ask:

> **When does usable capability appear?**

Training activity is also an input.

Instead of:

> **exercise completed**

ask:

> **What competence was demonstrated?**

Instead of:

> **5,000 personnel trained**

ask:

> **What can they now do?**

Instead of:

> **synthetic training increased**

> **What validated function resulted?**

## 33. 🚦 If You Want Colours, Make Them Mean Something

If Defence wants red, amber and green:

fine.

But define them.

### Green

Required function can be performed at required notice and duration with planned resilience.

### Amber

Function remains available but with meaningful constraint or reduced margin.

### Red

Stated requirement cannot currently be met.

Then show confidence separately.

For example:

> **Sustainment: AMBER / MODERATE CONFIDENCE**

because:

> **the last sufficiently large validation occurred eighteen months ago.**

Now the reader knows both:

- the assessed state;
- the evidential strength.

Do not let:

> **green**

mean:

> **nobody has successfully proved otherwise recently.**

## 34. 🪟 Public Readiness Does Not Require Publishing The War Plan

Specific formation readiness may legitimately remain classified.

The public does not need:

- precise stock levels;
- unit vulnerabilities;
- detailed mobilisation schedules;
- exploitable readiness gaps.

But public reporting can still provide:

- aggregate trends;
- whether planned exercises occurred;
- recruitment and retention;
- estate condition;
- broad availability trends;
- major recovery programmes;
- significant known bottlenecks;
- categories of readiness evidence.

If government says:

> **readiness has improved**

it should be possible to ask:

> **What category of evidence shows that?**

Possible answers include:

- better exercise performance;
- improved equipment availability;
- greater stock depth;
- faster mobilisation;
- stronger personnel fill;
- validated interoperability.

No sensitive number necessarily needs publication.

But:

> **readiness improved because government says readiness improved**

is circular.

The public needs:

> **the strategic map.**

Not:

> **the exploitable variables.**

That is bounded transparency.

## 35. 💷 Apply The Model To The £30 Million Case

The Army training dispute should not be framed only as:

> **Did £30 million disappear?**

Ask:

> **Which readiness functions depended upon the affected activity?**

Then:

- at what organisational level?
- when were those functions last validated?
- what replaces the affected activity?
- has replacement been validated?
- how quickly does competence decay?
- when is it next exercised?
- what regeneration is required?
- what scarce instructors, estate or equipment does recovery need?
- what other commitments use the same capacity?
- what did the Army assess?
- what did integrated Defence governance assess?
- what residual risk was accepted?
- who owns it?

That converts:

> **financial saving**

into:

> **military consequence.**

And it gives [`💷_thirty_million_pounds.md`](./💷_thirty_million_pounds.md) somewhere useful to land.

> **we need to know what success means.**

## 37. 🔁 The Governing Readiness Test

The whole node can therefore be reduced to one test.

For every strategically required military function ask:

> **What must Britain be able to do?**

> **At what scale?**

> **By when?**

> **For how long?**

> **Under what conditions?**

> **While doing what else?**

> **Which people, equipment and relationships produce it?**

> **Which dependency fails first?**

> **What happens when something breaks?**

> **What produces the second force?**

> **How long does recovery take?**

> **When was the function last demonstrated?**

> **At what scale?**

> **How confident are we in that evidence?**

> **What remains fragile?**

> **Who owns that risk?**

Then:

> **ready**

finally means something.
