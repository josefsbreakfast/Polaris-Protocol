# 🍖 Whips, Chains And BBQ Skewers
**First created:** 2026-10-02 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-02  
*Put the politics down for five minutes. Why is it apparently so easy to get into things Britain says are important to national security?*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

For the purposes of this note, put the politics to one side.

You do not need to agree about Palestine.

You do not need to agree about Israel.

You do not need to agree about Elbit Systems.

You do not need to agree about Palestine Action.

You do not need to agree about whether direct action is legitimate, whether criminal damage can ever be justified, whether proscription was proportionate, or where the legal boundary of terrorism ought to sit.

There are other places to have those arguments.

This note has a much stupider question.

> **Why is it apparently so easy to get into things Britain says are important to national security?**

Because there is a point at which the politics of the intruder stops being the most interesting thing about a successful intrusion.

If someone gets through the fence, reaches the aircraft, gets into the factory, reaches the sensitive equipment or manages to keep security sufficiently occupied for somebody else to do so, there is another system in the room.

**The security system.**

And reality has just given it an exam.

This is particularly important when the response to an intrusion is that the intruders were not merely protesters or vandals but increasingly dangerous, organised, potentially violent actors presenting a national-security or terrorism concern.

Okay.

Take that assessment seriously.

Then the obvious next question is:

> **Why the fuck did they get in?**

---

## 🧪 1. Feynman At The Fence

There is a Feynman problem here.

A system is not demonstrated to work because its description sounds sophisticated.

A security architecture may have:

- policies;
- procedures;
- threat assessments;
- CCTV;
- fences;
- guards;
- control rooms;
- access controls;
- reporting structures;
- security contractors;
- intelligence relationships;
- escalation plans;
- impressive acronyms;
- extremely serious meetings.

Lovely.

Then somebody tries the door.

That is the experiment.

The important question is not whether the security model looked convincing before contact with reality.

The important question is what happened when reality arrived.

A successful penetration does not automatically mean the entire system was incompetent. No realistic physical-security architecture can guarantee that no human being will ever cross an outer perimeter.

The interesting questions are more granular:

- Was the intrusion detected?
- How quickly?
- How far did the intruders penetrate?
- Were they contained?
- Could they reach critical assets?
- Could they move laterally?
- Were workers kept safe?
- Was reinforcement available?
- Did the incident-response plan work?
- Did the system degrade gracefully when one layer failed?
- What did the institution learn afterwards?

A breach is not merely an embarrassment.

**It is experimental data.**

---

## 🕰️ 2. Britain Has Seen Protesters Before

Politically motivated intrusion onto British defence sites is not a new threat class.

It is not even particularly new within living memory.

Greenham Common generated repeated questions about perimeter penetration and the distinction between preventing every possible incursion and rapidly containing people who did get through.

Anti-nuclear movements have targeted military infrastructure.

Anti-war movements have targeted military infrastructure.

During the Iraq War period, the Ministry of Defence was recording physical incursions across the defence estate involving protesters, criminals and vandals.

There have been actions around other conflicts.

There have been actions against defence companies.

There have been actions against bases.

There will presumably be actions around future conflicts that nobody currently knows anything about.

Defence does not need to predict the politics.

It needs to recognise the **behavioural threat class**.

Someone may approach a sensitive facility because they are:

- protesting;
- committing criminal damage;
- stealing;
- conducting reconnaissance;
- attempting industrial espionage;
- sabotaging equipment;
- acting on behalf of organised crime;
- acting on behalf of a hostile state;
- preparing violence;
- trying to make a very loud political point;
- trying very hard not to make any point at all.

The fence does not need to understand their manifesto.

## ✈️ 3. Filton

On 6 August 2024, activists entered an Elbit Systems facility at Filton near Bristol.

The operation involved a prison van, entry into the site, significant property damage and confrontation with security workers.

The criminal proceedings arising from Filton have subsequently been divided across multiple trials.

That matters.

Evidence has therefore entered the public domain in pieces.

The first major trial concentrated heavily on people associated with the entry and property-damage side of the operation.

The October 2026 trial concerns defendants whom the prosecution alleges belonged to a separate **black**, or **covert**, team.

That trial is ongoing.

The allegations discussed below are therefore **prosecution allegations unless and until established by evidence and verdict**.

This distinction is not decorative.

It is the entire point of having a trial.

---

## 🕳️ 4. There Was Already A Fucking Hole In The Fence

One detail requires particular attention.

Evidence from the earlier Filton proceedings indicated that a hole had been discovered cut in the perimeter before the main action.

Additional security was reportedly deployed.

Then the action happened.

This is where the security question becomes considerably more interesting than:

> Why did nobody know somebody somewhere had bought a whip?

Someone purchasing an unusual object is an ambiguous signal.

People have hobbies.

People perform.

People own tools.

People buy fireworks.

People own vans.

People have kitchens.

Some people have extremely confusing cupboards.

A deliberately interfered-with perimeter at a repeatedly targeted defence-industrial facility is rather further down the funnel.

At that point the question becomes:

> **What threat assessment followed from discovering that somebody had physically interfered with the perimeter?**

Who was told?

What was recorded?

Was the site searched?

Was the opening repaired?

Was surveillance increased?

Were police informed?

Were staff warned?

Was access to critical areas changed?

Was reinforcement placed nearby?

Was the possibility considered that the interference was preparation rather than vandalism?

Was the security posture changed sufficiently?

We do not currently have public answers to all of those questions.

That is precisely why they belong here.

---

## ⚫ 5. Enter: The Black Team

The prosecution in the current Filton trial alleges that the operation contained differentiated teams.

The **red/overt team** was intended to enter the facility and damage Elbit property.

The **black/covert team**, according to the Crown, had a different function: distracting, intimidating or otherwise occupying security so that the overt operation could proceed.

The prosecution alleges that members of this group used or carried items including:

- fireworks or flares;
- hammers;
- whips;
- large metal skewers.

It further alleges organised roles, preparation, coordination and withdrawal arrangements.

Again:

**ongoing trial.**

The jury has not yet determined the truth of the prosecution case.

But for the purposes of examining security, something useful happens if we temporarily take the strongest version of that allegation seriously.

Assume they were organised.

Assume the teams were deliberate.

Assume the equipment was deliberate.

Assume the security-distraction role was deliberate.

Assume there was meaningful preparation.

Assume some participants were prepared to threaten or use violence.

Fine.

> **Why was the security system unable to prevent that operation reaching the facility?**

The more sophisticated and dangerous the alleged adversary becomes, the more important that question becomes.

---

## 🪢 6. Whose Fucking Whip Was It?

Unfortunately, the whip has become analytically important.

Earlier reporting and evidence generated considerable confusion because a security guard was subsequently seen possessing a whip.

Evidence from the earlier proceedings indicates a more complicated sequence in which a whip was apparently present among activist equipment, subsequently came into the possession of security, and later changed hands again during the confrontation.

Exactly what particular people did with it was disputed.

The current prosecution case now places whips more explicitly within the alleged black-team operation.

This is an excellent little lesson in evidential provenance.

A photograph showing:

> **MAN HOLDING WHIP**

establishes that a man was holding a whip at that moment.

It does not automatically establish:

- whose whip it originally was;
- who brought it;
- why it was brought;
- who used it first;
- whether it was used as a weapon;
- whether it struck anyone;
- how dangerous the particular implement was;
- how skilled the person holding it was;
- what happened immediately before the photograph;
- what happened immediately afterwards.

Objects have histories.

**Chain of possession matters.**

At present, the public evidence appears stronger for the proposition that the whip originated with the activist side than for the early online interpretation that Elbit security simply turned up independently armed with a whip.

The exact original purchaser or owner is a different question.

We await further evidence.

The Whip remains under investigation.

## 🎪 7. The Author Has Been Around Whips

The author has been around whips.

This is all the biographical information required here.

Whips can absolutely injure people.

Some can produce nasty injuries.

This does not mean that the presence of an object called a **whip** tells us everything necessary to assess the actual threat presented during a particular confrontation.

There are different designs.

Different lengths.

Different weights.

Different environments.

Different levels of skill.

Different distances.

Different ways of using them.

A long whip can be difficult to control effectively.

A shorter implement presents a different range problem.

Space matters.

Distance matters.

Training matters.

Actual use matters.

Actual injury matters.

Video matters.

None of this establishes that the Filton whips were harmless.

It establishes something considerably more boring:

> **SHOW YOUR WORK.**

If a whip formed part of a serious coordinated attempt to violently suppress security workers, evidence can establish that.

If it was primarily being cracked around as part of an extremely confusing improvised circus performance, evidence can establish that too.

At present:

**ongoing trial.**

---

## 🎪 8. Fine. Show Us The Circus.

There is a wider public-confidence problem here.

The Filton cases sit inside an intensely contested political environment.

The government's later proscription of Palestine Action substantially increased the importance attached to what these actions meant.

The public argument consequently cannot rest forever on:

> **WHIPS. SKEWERS. TERRORISM. TRUST US.**

That does not mean prejudicial evidence should be dumped into the public domain during a live criminal trial.

It means that, once proceedings permit, the evidential record matters.

Where lawful and safe to release, that may include:

- judgments;
- judicial findings;
- agreed facts;
- sentencing remarks;
- appeal judgments;
- CCTV;
- body-worn video;
- contemporaneous communications;
- planning documents;
- equipment provenance;
- official reviews;
- security lessons learned.

If the black team really did perform the world's most threatening circus routine, then eventually:

**show us the circus.**

Not because violence is funny.

Because evidence is better than adjectives.

---

## 👷 9. The Last Control Measure Is Dave

There is another person being lost in the national-security framing.

The security worker.

If a security system deteriorates until an individual guard is physically wrestling someone carrying a sledgehammer, dealing with pyrotechnics, confronting somebody holding a whip or trying to prevent several coordinated people moving through a facility at once, that worker is downstream of a large number of management decisions.

They did not necessarily:

- design the perimeter;
- choose the staffing level;
- allocate the security budget;
- configure the CCTV;
- design the alarm system;
- decide how quickly reinforcement could arrive;
- write the contingency plan;
- decide what happened after the hole appeared in the fence;
- determine police liaison;
- choose the training package;
- assess the intelligence;
- decide whether the site should remain operational under the existing posture.

Eventually every beautiful security architecture reaches a human being.

If enough earlier layers fail, the final security protocol becomes:

> **Dave has encountered the threat.**

Dave has a spine.

Dave has knees.

Dave would presumably like to retain both.

**Dave is not a bollard.**

## 🦴 10. The Employment Tribunal From Hell

There is therefore an occupational-safety question sitting underneath the national-security one.

What were workers actually being asked to deal with?

Was staffing safe?

Was there sufficient backup?

Were people appropriately trained?

Could they withdraw?

Could they summon support?

Were there clear escalation procedures?

Was there a control room with adequate situational awareness?

Was physical intervention expected?

Under what circumstances?

Were workers trained in de-escalation?

Were they equipped for the risks identified by the employer?

Had foreseeable scenarios actually been exercised?

What happened after the perimeter was found interfered with?

If the employer believed a serious coordinated attack was plausible, were workers resourced accordingly?

Because perhaps the most frightening document after an incident is not:

> **COUNTER-TERRORISM THREAT ASSESSMENT**

Perhaps it is:

> **My employer knew there was a foreseeable risk and gave me nothing adequate to work with.**

---

## 🕊️ 11. De-Escalation Is Security

Security is not synonymous with winning fights.

It should generally be designed to reduce the number of situations in which anybody has to fight at all.

The useful sequence looks more like:

**detect → assess → communicate → contain → protect critical areas → summon appropriate support → de-escalate where possible → intervene proportionately where necessary → preserve evidence → recover**

The exact sequence will differ between facilities.

The principle does not.

A worker physically overpowering an intruder may sometimes be necessary.

But:

> **Hire A Bigger Man**

is not a serious substitute for protective-security architecture.

Nor should workers be placed into unnecessary physical confrontations because staffing, detection, perimeter security or reinforcement arrangements were inadequate.

Keeping shiny toys safe includes keeping the people guarding them safe.

## ✈️ 12. Brize Norton

Then we get to RAF Brize Norton.

In June 2025, Palestine Action activists penetrated RAF Brize Norton and reached Voyager aircraft.

The incident triggered a security review across the defence estate.

The Ministry of Defence subsequently acknowledged that the Strategic Defence Review had already identified defence sites including Brize Norton as requiring further investment.

After the breach, Defence increased patrols and guard readiness, checked CCTV, gates and barriers, maintained an enhanced-vigilance posture and introduced further technical security measures.

That response matters.

It tells us something very important.

**The security architecture itself was considered capable of improvement.**

This does not determine the criminal or political questions surrounding the people who exploited it.

It establishes that:

> **Arresting the person who demonstrated a vulnerability and fixing the vulnerability are different jobs.**

You may need to do both.

Doing one does not magically accomplish the other.

---

## 🕰️ 13. This Is Not Britain's First Protest

There is a historical danger in treating every contemporary incident as unprecedented.

Greenham Common alone should cure us of that.

In June 1983, around 80 demonstrators penetrated the perimeter at five separate points.

The interesting thing is what the contemporary defence analysis did with that information.

It recognised that the perimeter was particularly difficult to defend and distinguished between:

**preventing every possible incursion**

and

**swiftly containing incursions that occurred.**

That is systems thinking.

The Ministry of Defence later recorded dozens of physical incursions annually across the defence estate during the 2000s, including protester, criminal and vandal activity.

Many arrests in the relevant period related to anti-nuclear and anti-Iraq-war protest.

So:

> **politically motivated attempts to enter British defence facilities are not an unforeseeable new phenomenon discovered during the Gaza war.**

The politics changes.

The security problem persists.

---

## 📊 14. Are We Seeing The One-Offs That Got Through?

There is an important limitation.

We do not presently have enough public data to establish whether Filton and Brize Norton demonstrate generally poor security across British defence infrastructure.

Successful breaches are visible.

Successful prevention often is not.

There may be:

- attempted incursions detected before arrival;
- people stopped at outer perimeters;
- suspicious activity identified early;
- operations abandoned because security worked;
- intelligence-led interventions never publicised;
- incidents contained without reaching sensitive assets.

Those matter to the denominator.

So the question is not:

> **Two breaches happened, therefore everything is fucked.**

The question is:

> **What is the actual failure rate?**

Historically, Defence has counted physical incursions.

Can contemporary figures tell us:

- attempted penetrations;
- successful outer-perimeter penetrations;
- penetrations reaching inner security layers;
- access to critical assets;
- protest-related incidents;
- criminal incidents;
- suspected hostile reconnaissance;
- response times;
- changes over time?

Without denominators, spectacular incidents can distort perception.

But without transparency, institutions can also hide systemic weakness behind the claim that each spectacular incident was exceptional.

**Show the denominator.**

---

## 🕵️ 15. What The Fuck Is Counter-Terrorism Doing?

This question needs more care than:

> Why didn't MI5 see somebody Googling tactical whips?

We currently have no public evidence establishing that MI5 knew about the Filton operation beforehand and failed to intervene.

Nor should intelligence be imagined as an omniscient machine watching every strange purchase in Britain.

There are lawful reasons to own:

- whips;
- fireworks;
- metal skewers;
- tools;
- black clothing;
- vans.

A person buying a whip somewhere in Britain is a noisy signal.

Someone cutting a hole in the perimeter of a repeatedly targeted defence facility is a rather different one.

The useful question is therefore:

> **What did the protective-security system know, who knew it, and what did they do?**

That system may include different layers:

**company security  
→ management  
→ defence-industry security reporting  
→ local policing  
→ protective-security functions  
→ national policing  
→ counter-terrorism policing  
→ intelligence agencies where appropriate**

Failures can occur at the seams.

Information can exist without reaching the right person.

Warnings can be insufficiently specific.

Responsibilities can be fragmented.

Budgets can be inadequate.

Threat assessments can be wrong.

People can correctly identify a risk and then inadequately respond to it.

That is what needs examining.

## 🧠 16. Intent Is Not Capability Is Not Vulnerability Is Not Consequence

Four variables keep being collapsed.

They are not the same thing.

### Intent

What does the actor want to achieve?

### Capability

What can the actor actually do?

### Vulnerability

What does the target allow them to do?

### Consequence

What happens if they succeed?

A highly motivated adversary may accomplish very little against strong security.

A relatively unsophisticated adversary can produce a serious outcome against a highly vulnerable target.

A terrifying legal label does not automatically establish capability.

A successful breach does not automatically establish extraordinary attacker sophistication.

Sometimes the adversary is brilliant.

Sometimes the door was open.

Sometimes both things are true.

**Find out which.**

## 🥷 17. Replace The Protester

This is the point at which the Palestine Action argument can be removed almost completely.

Take exactly the same vulnerabilities.

Replace the person approaching them.

What if the next person wants:

- theft;
- espionage;
- covert sabotage;
- hostile-state reconnaissance;
- organised criminal access;
- lethal violence;
- terrorism;
- proxy activity?

Do not ask whether those actors are morally equivalent.

They are not being compared morally.

They are being passed through the **same security architecture**.

That is the point.

A publicity-seeking direct-action movement may actually provide an unusually noisy adversary.

Matching clothing.

Video.

Political statements.

Property destruction.

Pyrotechnics.

Publicity afterwards.

Perhaps even, God help us, **WHIP TEAM**.

A genuinely clandestine hostile actor may want the opposite.

No spectacle.

No announcement.

No confrontation.

No arrest.

No viral footage.

Ideally, nobody even realises that access occurred.

So:

> **If the system struggled with the noisy adversary, what happens when the quiet one arrives?**

## 🧱 18. Terrorism Is Not A Security Control

There is a legal and political argument about whether particular actions or organisations meet the statutory definition of terrorism and what legal powers should follow.

That argument can happen elsewhere.

For this node, there is a simpler proposition.

> **Calling somebody a terrorist after they penetrate the perimeter does not retroactively strengthen the perimeter.**

If the government's strongest threat assessment is correct, then protective security should be calibrated to a serious threat.

If the pre-incident operational assessment was substantially less alarming, then the difference between that assessment and later rhetoric deserves examination.

Either way:

**what security measures followed from the actual threat assessment?**

Because there is an uncomfortable circular argument available otherwise:

> These people penetrated sensitive defence infrastructure because they are terrifyingly capable.
>
> Their successful penetration proves how terrifying they are.
>
> Please do not ask why the sensitive defence infrastructure was penetrable.

No.

Ask.

## 🏗️ 19. Business Continuity Means Assuming Someone Will Try

Defence companies operate in politically contested environments.

Wars produce protest.

Weapons produce protest.

Military alliances produce protest.

Foreign policy produces protest.

None of this requires an organisation to agree with the protesters.

It requires the organisation to notice that protesters exist.

Business continuity therefore has to consider:

- repeated targeting;
- physical intrusion;
- critical-asset segregation;
- personnel safety;
- incident response;
- recovery;
- redundancy;
- communications;
- police liaison;
- previous actions;
- changing threat environments.

If a facility manufactures or develops technology regarded as important to national defence, the question becomes more serious.

A failure of physical security at one facility does **not** prove that the defence technology manufactured there is defective.

Those are separate questions.

But resilience of the defence-industrial base is itself a defence concern.

If you sell shiny toys:

**keep shiny toys safe.**

## 🔧 20. Reality Has Marked The Exam

There is a simple institutional response to a successful breach.

> We believed control X would prevent outcome Y.
>
> Outcome Y occurred.
>
> Therefore something in our model requires examination.

Maybe X failed.

Maybe X worked but another layer failed.

Maybe the threat model was wrong.

Maybe staffing was wrong.

Maybe detection was too slow.

Maybe communication failed.

Maybe responsibility was unclear.

Maybe management received a warning and underestimated it.

Maybe the system performed reasonably and this was simply the residual risk that no feasible architecture could entirely eliminate.

That is possible too.

**Find out.**

What you do not do is protect the model from the experimental result.

Reality does not care how much paperwork supported the original assumption.

Reality has marked the exam.

## 🍖 21. Keep Shiny Toys Safe

The United Kingdom possesses:

- military aircraft;
- weapons;
- defence technology;
- research facilities;
- sensitive information;
- critical infrastructure;
- extremely expensive equipment;
- people who know extremely interesting things.

Some of these things are shiny.

Some are classified.

Some are both.

Some would be extremely useful to somebody Britain would prefer not to have them.

So:

> **KEEP SHINY TOYS SAFE.**

Not:

> prosecute whoever touched the shiny toy and declare the security problem solved.

The prosecution may be completely appropriate.

It is still not a fence.

## 👷 22. Keep Dave Safe Too

The shiny toy is not actually the most valuable thing in this note.

The people are.

Security workers.

Police officers.

Military personnel.

Emergency responders.

Cleaners.

Engineers.

Researchers.

Everybody who happens to be at work when the security architecture meets reality.

They should not be expected to absorb institutional failure through their bodies.

The final control measure should not routinely be:

> **Dave will physically deal with it.**

Dave has a spine.

Dave has knees.

Dave has a family.

Dave has employment rights.

Dave is not a bollard.

And if management knew there was a foreseeable risk, failed to provide adequate staffing, planning, training, reinforcement or protection, then perhaps the most frightening thing waiting at the end of the incident is not the terrorism prosecution.

Perhaps it is the employment tribunal asking:

> **What, precisely, did you give this worker to work with?**

## 🧭 23. The Actual Question

This node does not conclude that Palestine Action was harmless.

It does not conclude that the current Filton defendants are guilty.

Their trial is ongoing.

It does not conclude that MI5 failed.

There is presently insufficient public evidence to establish that.

It does not conclude that Elbit's entire security operation was incompetent.

It does not conclude that British military bases are generally undefended.

It does not conclude that every breach can be prevented.

It asks for something much less exciting.

**Show the system.**

Show:

- what the threat assessment was;
- what warnings existed;
- what controls existed;
- what failed;
- what worked;
- how far penetration actually went;
- how workers were protected;
- what lessons were identified;
- what changed afterwards;
- whether similar vulnerabilities exist elsewhere;
- whether repeated breaches represent exceptional failures or a wider pattern.

Because regardless of what anybody thinks about the politics:

> **if people Britain describes as serious national-security threats can repeatedly reach things Britain describes as important to national security, the security architecture itself is part of the story.**

And if the next person approaching the fence is quieter, better trained and considerably less interested in posting the results on social media:

**we would rather have fixed it after the people with the BBQ skewers.**
