# 🏛️ White House Snitches — Drafting Order + Node Plan
## 0. `README.md`
### Job
Front door to the whole cluster.
### Core argument
This folder does **not** argue that any particular White House official is a spy, foreign asset, or source of a leak.
It argues that:
> **“Who leaked it?” is not a complete information-security threat model.**
A reported failure of security assurance around somebody close to senior government decision-making raises questions about access, devices, communications, personnel security, compartmentation, hostile collection and institutional resilience — whether or not anybody deliberately disclosed anything.
### Needs to contain
- Why the folder exists.
- Very short explanation of the triggering reporting.
- Explicit evidential disclaimer.
- Difference between:
  - exposure;
  - compromise;
  - deliberate disclosure;
  - insider threat;
  - espionage;
  - attribution.
- Explanation that the folder is primarily a **press-literacy resource**.
- Quick route for readers who only have time for five nodes:
  - ☎️
  - 🪪
  - 📱
  - 🇮🇷
  - ✍️
- Full reading order.
- Links into relevant Iran/Data Wars material.

# PART I — WHAT ARE WE ACTUALLY TALKING ABOUT?
## 1. `🦑_security_language_for_normal_people.md`
### Job
Give journalists/readers enough vocabulary to understand the rest of the folder without requiring a security background.
### Core argument
Security terminology describes **different events and levels of evidence**. Treating everything as a “leak”, “hack”, “spy” or “breach” destroys useful distinctions.
### Needs to contain
Plain-language distinctions between:
- leak;
- unauthorised disclosure;
- exposure;
- compromise;
- breach;
- intrusion;
- vulnerability;
- exploit;
- insider risk/threat;
- intelligence collection;
- espionage;
- elicitation;
- recruitment;
- asset/source/agent;
- attribution;
- clearance;
- access;
- need-to-know;
- compartmentation;
- metadata.
Central rule:
> **Words are evidence labels. Do not promote the evidence by changing the noun.**

PART II — WHAT THE FUCK IS THE SECURITY SYSTEM FOR?

3. 🪪_clearance_is_not_a_lanyard.md

Job

Explain why vetting and clearance exist without turning them into moral judgments.

Core argument

"I trust her"
        ≠
"We have assessed and appropriately controlled
the risks associated with her access."

Needs to contain

* Clearance ≠ Good Person Certificate.
* No clearance ≠ spy.
* Vetting as personnel-security assurance.
* Role + access + environment + threat + controls.
* Interim/temporary arrangements.
* Compensating controls.
* Continuing obligations.
* Why an assurance gap matters even without evidence of compromise.
* How proper security processes protect the employee as well as the institution.
* Why applying established requirements is not a witch hunt.

Central proposition:

This is the whole fucking point of clearance.

4. 👀_access_is_more_than_opening_the_document.md

Job

Destroy the binary idea that access means “was formally handed classified paperwork.”

Core argument

People can acquire sensitive or intelligence-useful knowledge through proximity and context, not merely document permissions.

Needs to contain

Possible access through:

* meetings;
* screens;
* overheard conversations;
* physical spaces;
* schedules;
* calendars;
* communications;
* draft documents;
* travel;
* meeting attendance;
* changes in routine;
* knowing what senior people are suddenly interested in;
* knowing who is talking to whom.

Distinguish:

classified-document access
        ≠
total information exposure

Central question:

What information environment did the role actually expose the person to?

5. 📱_the_phone_is_part_of_the_security_boundary.md

Make endpoint/device security intuitively obvious.

Needs to contain

* Phones.
* Laptops.
* Accounts.
* Messaging applications.
* Authentication.
* Cloud backups.
* Notifications.
* Contacts.
* Calendars.

Central lines:

And:

Your phone does not acquire a security clearance through osmosis.

6. 🧬_the_attack_surface_has_more_than_one_strand.md

Job

Join the previous nodes into one systems model.

Core argument

The attack surface is not “cyber” OR “human”.

It is:

human
+
technical
+
physical
+
social
+
organisational
+
informational

Needs to contain

* Why these strands interact.
* Human → credential → account → network.

* Physical access → observation → inference.
* Organisational weakness → excessive privilege.
* Why attackers can move between strands.
* Why siloed security teams can miss cross-domain pathways.

Central proposition:

People and machines form one security environment.

7. 🧅_compartmentation_exists_for_a_reason.md

Job

Explain why good security assumes eventual failure.

Core argument

Security does not require perfect people.

It limits the consequences when imperfect people, devices and controls inevitably fail.

Needs to contain

* Need-to-know.
* Least privilege.
* Compartments.
* Defence in depth.
* Blast-radius reduction.
* Separation of duties.
* Access review.
* Why even cleared people don’t automatically get everything.
* Why “but we trust them” misunderstands the architecture.
* Why one compromised account/person/device should not expose the entire system.

Central proposition:

The purpose of compartmentation is not to predict which person will fail. It is to survive whichever one does.

Teach aggregation and inferential disclosure.

Core argument

Nobody necessarily has to disclose sensitive proposition X if an outside observer can reconstruct X from fragments.

Needs to contain

A
+
B
+

+
existing knowledge
=
X

Examples involving:

* calendars;
* personnel movements;
* meeting attendance;
* public statements;
* timing;
* travel;
* changes in routine;
* apparently innocuous disclosures.
* Why intelligence collectors already possess contextual knowledge.
* Why journalists themselves routinely use aggregation.

Central line:

The collector is allowed to know things already.

PART IV — THIS IS A SYSTEMS PROBLEM

13. 🦠_porosity_is_a_security_failure.md

Job

Move from individual blame to institutional architecture.

Core argument

When enough interfaces are poorly characterised or controlled, the meaningful problem becomes systemic porosity.

Needs to contain

* Multiple exposure pathways.
* Unclear access.
* Weak logging.
* Excess privileges.
* Device/account uncertainty.
* Physical proximity.
* Organisational workarounds.
* Informal communications.
* Why porosity complicates later attribution.
* Why finding one culprit may leave every structural vulnerability untouched.

Central question:

If Naughty Dave disappeared tomorrow, could the same thing happen again?

If yes:

Dave was not your entire security problem.

15. _useful_old_school_defence_expertise.md

Job

Call the traditional defence/security establishment back onto the fucking pitch.

Core argument

Some people already know these fundamentals.

They may have assumed everybody else did too.

Apparently not.

Old-school defence, protective-security and counterintelligence expertise can therefore perform an important contemporary defensive function by re-normalising basic security discipline and explaining why these controls exist.

Needs to contain

Useful institutional muscle memory around:

* personnel assurance;
* need-to-know;
* compartmentation;
* hostile collection;
* secure environments;
* reporting unusual approaches;
* information discipline;
* treating access as a security property;
* accepting inconvenient rules;
* preparing for failures before they happen;
* protecting personnel as well as secrets.

Public education as defensive activity

People with institutional credibility can explain:

Clearance is not an accusation.

Need-to-know is not an insult.

Loyalty does not secure a telephone.

Being targeted does not make somebody a traitor.

An annoying security requirement may exist because somebody has already modelled a threat you have never had reason to think about.

The rules still apply when you’re important.

Important limitation

Do not romanticise the old establishment.

Old-school security can also bring:

* racial profiling;
* excessive secrecy;
* overclassification;
* technological illiteracy;
* institutional prejudice;
* groupthink;
* obsolete threat models.

The answer is not:

RETURN TO 1983.

It is:

Combine durable counterintelligence fundamentals with contemporary cyber, information-security and threat-intelligence expertise.

Central ending:

Some of you already know this. Please clock in.

PART V — NOW REPORT THE FUCKING THING PROPERLY

16. ✍️_questions_journalists_should_actually_ask.md

Job

Turn the entire cluster into usable journalism.

Core argument

A reporter does not need to become a counterintelligence professional.

They need to stop prematurely collapsing uncertainty.

Needs to contain

Personnel assurance

* What clearance/vetting was required?
* What was actually completed?
* Was interim access authorised?
* What compensating controls existed?
* Who authorised them?
* How long did the arrangement persist?

Access

* Which systems?
* Which spaces?
* Which meetings?
* Which information?
* Which communications?
* Which contextual knowledge?

Technical

* Which devices/accounts were authorised?
* Were personal endpoints permitted?
* Has credential/device compromise been investigated?
* What logs exist?

Information pathways

* Could aggregation explain the disclosure?
* Could metadata reveal it?
* Could multiple sources independently reconstruct it?
* Was deliberate disclosure actually established?

Threat environment

* Which actors had plausible collection requirements?
* What capabilities were relevant?
* Had the threat environment changed?
* Was foreign collection considered?

Attribution discipline

Always distinguish:

possible pathway
        ↓
evidence of exposure
        ↓
evidence of exploitation
        ↓
evidence of compromise
        ↓
attribution
        ↓
evidence of knowing cooperation

Final question

If the alleged leaker disappeared tomorrow, would the same security failure still be possible?

If yes, keep reporting.
