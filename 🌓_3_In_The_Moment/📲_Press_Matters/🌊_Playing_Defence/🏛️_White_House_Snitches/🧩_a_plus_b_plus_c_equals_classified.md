# 🧩 A Plus B Plus C Equals Classified
**First created:** 2026-08-21 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-21  
*Why individually limited facts can combine into a sensitive inference even when nobody directly discloses the final proposition.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Sometimes nobody says the secret.

That does not necessarily mean the secret remains unavailable.

Imagine:

```text
A = individually unremarkable fact
B = another individually unremarkable fact
C = observable change
D = existing contextual knowledge

A + B + C + D
        ↓
sensitive inference X
```

Nobody has to say **X**.

Nobody necessarily has to possess a document containing **X**.

Nobody even has to understand that their own fragment helps reveal **X**.

An informed observer can assemble the pieces.

This is the aggregation problem.

Or, in the deliberately blunt shorthand of this node:

> **A + B + C can equal classified.**

Not literally in every legal or classification sense.

But informationally, several pieces that look limited in isolation can combine into a conclusion considerably more sensitive than any one of them.

That matters for security.

It also matters for journalism, because journalists already know exactly how this works.

They do it for a living.

---

## 🧩 Nobody Actually Said X

The classic leak model looks like this:

```text
PERSON KNOWS X
        ↓
PERSON TELLS OUTSIDER X
        ↓
OUTSIDER NOW KNOWS X
```

That happens.

But consider another pathway:

```text
PERSON 1 → A
PERSON 2 → B
PUBLIC RECORD → C
OBSERVATION → D
                ↓
        INFORMED OUTSIDER
                ↓
                X
```

Now imagine asking everybody involved:

> **Did you disclose X?**

They may all truthfully answer:

> **No.**

Because none of them did.

The final proposition was reconstructed outside the protected environment.

That is not semantic trickery.

It is a different information mechanism.

And if an investigation assumes that somebody must have directly transmitted X, it may spend enormous effort searching for a disclosure that never occurred in that form.

## 🧠 Context Is Doing Some Of The Work

Information does not have the same value to every observer.

Suppose somebody sees:

```text
A
B
C
```

A novice may see three unrelated facts.

Someone who understands the institution may see:

```text
NORMAL BASELINE
        +
A
        +
B
        +
UNUSUAL C
        ↓
SOMETHING HAS CHANGED
```

Context can include:

- knowledge of ordinary routines;
- institutional structure;
- previous events;
- specialist expertise;
- relationships between actors;
- expected timelines;
- public reporting;
- or long-term observation.

This means the informational value of a fragment depends partly upon what the recipient already knows.

A meeting title may mean nothing to one person.

To another, it identifies the only committee that convenes before a particular kind of decision.

A cancelled journey may look mundane.

To somebody who understands the principal's normal behaviour, it may be a significant deviation.

The fragment did not change.

The **context around the fragment** changed what could be inferred from it.

## 📚 Sensitive Inference Is Not The Same As Formal Classification

The title of this node is intentionally punchier than the underlying legal proposition.

So the distinction needs to be explicit.

```text
FORMALLY CLASSIFIED INFORMATION
        ≠
EVERY SENSITIVE CONCLUSION
THAT CAN BE INFERRED
```

Formal classification is governed by particular legal and administrative systems.

Aggregation is an information-security problem.

Those categories can overlap.

They are not interchangeable.

Several unclassified, public, administrative or otherwise individually limited facts may sometimes permit an observer to infer something that the institution itself treats as highly sensitive.

That does **not** mean the component facts retroactively acquire a particular formal classification merely because somebody combines them.

The point is narrower:

> **The sensitivity of the conclusion cannot always be assessed by examining each fragment in isolation.**

That is why security has to care about the whole information environment.

## 📊 Aggregation Is A Process

Aggregation can involve several related operations.

### Accumulation

One fragment may reveal little.

Repeated fragments may reveal a pattern.

```text
A
A
A
A
A
        ↓
BASELINE
```

Once a baseline exists, deviation becomes easier to recognise.

### Correlation

Two datasets or observations may become more informative when connected.

```text
WHO
+
WHEN
        ↓
RELATIONSHIP PATTERN
```

### Inference

Available facts may support a conclusion that was never explicitly stated.

```text
A + B + C
        ↓
probably X
```

### Pattern Recognition

Repeated activity can expose routine.

Routine makes unusual activity visible.

### Contextual Interpretation

Existing expertise can make an otherwise mundane fragment meaningful.

None of these operations requires magic.

They are ordinary reasoning.

That is precisely why they matter.

## 📞 Metadata Can Supply The Shape

Content is only one source of fragments.

Metadata can contribute:

```text
who
when
how often
for how long
in what sequence
what changed
```

Imagine that nobody can read a conversation.

But an observer can see:

```text
normal contact
normal contact
normal contact

SUDDEN BURST OF CONTACT

meeting

travel change

public announcement
```

That sequence does not prove what was discussed.

It may nevertheless become analytically useful.

Metadata can therefore become one component in a wider aggregation.

```text
metadata
+
public information
+
institutional context
        ↓
inference
```

See [📞 Metadata Can Tell The Story](./📞_metadata_can_tell_the_story.md) for the fuller distinction between content and structure.

## 👀 Access To Fragments Is Still Access

This is why the access map cannot stop at:

> **Who could open the final document?**

Someone may have access only to:

- a calendar;
- a room;
- a meeting title;
- a visitor list;
- a communications pattern;
- a draft;
- a partial briefing;
- an administrative system;
- or the visible consequences of a decision.

None of those necessarily gives them the final proposition.

But:

```text
FRAGMENT A
+
FRAGMENT B
+
FRAGMENT C
        ↓
WIDER PICTURE
```

The security question is therefore not merely whether somebody could retrieve **X** directly.

It is also whether their access, combined with other available information, could materially contribute to reconstructing X.

See [👀 Access Is More Than Opening The Document](./👀_access_is_more_than_opening_the_document.md).

## 🧅 Why Compartmentation Exists

This is one reason security separates information.

If every person, account and system can see every relevant fragment, then a single point of compromise may expose the entire picture.

Compartmentation can interrupt composition.

```text
COMPARTMENT 1 → A

COMPARTMENT 2 → B

COMPARTMENT 3 → C

LIMITED CROSS-ACCESS
        ↓
HARDER TO ASSEMBLE X
```

This is not an argument for classifying everything.

Overclassification creates its own problems.

Nor is it an argument for preventing legitimate information-sharing.

It is an argument for understanding that **unnecessary concentration of information increases the potential blast radius of a failure**.

See [🧅 Compartmentation Exists For A Reason](./🧅_compartmentation_exists_for_a_reason.md).

## 📰 Journalists Already Know How To Do This

There is something slightly strange about explaining aggregation risk to journalists.

Journalism routinely depends upon it.

A reporter may combine:

- one source confirming a meeting;
- another confirming a disagreement;
- a public filing;
- a diary change;
- a photograph;
- an official refusal to comment;
- and prior knowledge of the institution.

No source necessarily hands over the completed story.

The reporter builds it.

That is not a criticism.

It is often excellent reporting.

But the same cognitive operation does not cease to exist when the observer is:

- another government;
- a commercial actor;
- a criminal group;
- an activist;
- a political opponent;
- or simply somebody with sufficient curiosity and context.

If reporters understand how much can be reconstructed from fragments, they are unusually well placed to understand why security professionals care about aggregation.

The relevant conceptual move is simply:

> **Other observers can do journalism-shaped inference too.**

## 🕸️ This Is One Way You Do Not Need A Leaker

Aggregation also explains why the appearance of sensitive information outside an institution does not automatically prove that somebody directly leaked the final proposition.

There may have been:

```text
deliberate fragment disclosure
```

or:

```text
accidental fragment exposure
```

or:

```text
technical access to limited information
```

or:

```text
public information
+
observation
+
context
```

or some combination of them.

The final outcome can therefore be:

```text
OUTSIDER KNOWS X
```

without the mechanism being:

```text
INSIDER SAID X
```

That distinction is developed fully in [🕸️ You Do Not Need A Leaker](./🕸️_you_do_not_need_a_leaker.md).

## ⚖️ Inference Is Not Proof

Aggregation analysis needs brakes.

The ability to construct a plausible conclusion does not establish that the conclusion is true.

```text
CAN INFER X
        ≠
X IS TRUE

X IS PLAUSIBLE
        ≠
X IS CONFIRMED
```

Nor does the existence of an aggregation pathway establish that anybody actually used it.

```text
FRAGMENTS COULD REVEAL X
        ≠
SOMEBODY RECONSTRUCTED X
```

And if somebody did reconstruct X:

```text
SOMEBODY INFERRED X
        ≠
THEY OBTAINED X
FROM A SECRET SOURCE
```

This distinction matters enormously in both intelligence and journalism.

A strong inference may justify further investigation.

It does not become direct evidence merely because the inference is clever.

The same discipline should apply when reconstructing how an information incident occurred.

## 🔬 Investigate The Pieces, Not Only The Final Proposition

Suppose sensitive proposition X appears in public.

A narrow investigation asks:

> **Who knew X?**

That is useful.

A wider investigation also asks:

- What component facts could support X?
- Which of those facts were already public?
- Which were observable?
- Which were available through administrative systems?
- Which were accessible to different people?
- Which could have been exposed technically?
- Which fragments were individually legitimate disclosures?
- What contextual knowledge would be required to assemble them?
- Was X directly disclosed at all?
- What evidence distinguishes direct disclosure from reconstruction?

This changes the investigation from:

```text
FIND PERSON WHO SAID X
```

to:

```text
RECONSTRUCT INFORMATION PATHWAY
```

Sometimes those exercises identify the same mechanism.

Sometimes they do not.

## 🧿 The Actual Lesson

Sensitive information does not always travel as a complete package.

Sometimes it travels as pieces.

```text
A
+
B
+
C
+
CONTEXT
        ↓
X
```

Nobody necessarily says X.

Nobody necessarily writes X.

Nobody necessarily possesses X in a single document.

And nobody contributing one fragment necessarily understands the conclusion somebody else can build from the whole.

That is why aggregation matters.

It is why access mapping matters.

It is why metadata matters.

It is why compartmentation matters.

And it is why the appearance of a sensitive conclusion outside an institution does not, by itself, tell you how that conclusion got there.

> **Sometimes nobody leaks the secret.**

> **The system leaks enough pieces for somebody else to build it.**
