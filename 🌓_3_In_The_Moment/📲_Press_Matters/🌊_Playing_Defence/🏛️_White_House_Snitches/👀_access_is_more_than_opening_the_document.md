# 👀 Access Is More Than Opening The Document
**First created:** 2026-08-21 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-21  
*Why sensitive access is a map of permissions, proximity, observation and inference — not a yes/no question about who physically opened a file.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

There is a sensible question journalists ask when reporting on a sensitive political environment:

> **Did this person have access to classified information?**

Good question.

It is not yet a sufficiently precise one.

Public discussion often imagines access through a **document model**:

```text
person receives document
        ↓
person opens document
        ↓
person reads secret
        ↓
person now "has access"
```

That is one kind of access.

It is not the only kind.

A senior political environment also contains meetings, screens, calendars, conversations, buildings, systems, visitors, travel, draft documents, changes in routine, relationships and patterns.

A person may therefore be able to learn something without ever being formally handed the document on which the fact is written.

Equally, being near sensitive activity does **not** prove that somebody actually acquired sensitive information.

The useful task is neither to minimise nor sensationalise access.

It is to map it.

> **Access is not a yes/no property. It is a map.**

---

## 📄 The Document Model Of Access

The document model is attractive because it gives us a clean boundary.

```text
HAS THE FILE
      =
HAS ACCESS

DOES NOT HAVE THE FILE
      =
NO ACCESS
```

Unfortunately, institutions do not operate entirely through discrete files.

Consider a hypothetical senior office.

One person receives the formal briefing.

Another schedules the meeting.

Another controls access to the room.

Another sees who attends.

Another hears the discussion through legitimate proximity.

Another sees the resulting change in the principal's diary.

Another notices that a previously routine activity is abruptly cancelled.

Another has access only to an administrative system containing titles, participants and timing.

None of those facts necessarily means that any of those people possesses the underlying protected information.

But neither does:

> **They never opened the document.**

settle the access question.

The document is only one object inside a larger information environment.

## 👀 Five Kinds Of Access

For reporting purposes, it is useful to separate at least five kinds of access.

### Formal Access

```text
what the rules authorised
```

This is the access somebody is officially entitled to possess.

It may be defined by:

- role;
- clearance;
- authorisation;
- need-to-know;
- compartment;
- or specific institutional permissions.

Formal access tells us what **should** be available.

It does not necessarily tell us what was practically available.

### Technical Access

```text
what systems actually permitted
```

A person's credentials, account permissions or device configuration may allow them to retrieve information.

Technical access can match formal access.

It can also exceed it.

Conversely, somebody may be formally entitled to information but technically unable to obtain it through a particular system.

That distinction becomes important after an incident:

> **What did the policy permit, and what did the system permit?**

Those are separate questions.

### Physical Access

```text
where somebody could actually go
```

Physical proximity can create access to:

- rooms;
- desks;
- screens;
- documents;
- conversations;
- visitors;
- secure equipment;
- or patterns of activity.

Again:

```text
could physically encounter
        ≠
did acquire information
```

But physical access is part of the exposure architecture.

### Observational Access

```text
what somebody could see or hear
```

This is the category the document model most easily forgets.

A person might observe:

- a name on a screen;
- a briefing title;
- who enters a meeting;
- which official is suddenly summoned;
- a message notification;
- a document left visible;
- a conversation conducted nearby;
- or the fact that a particular issue is consuming senior attention.

No file transfer is required.

The information environment is doing the exposing.

### Contextual / Inferential Access

```text
what somebody's position allows them to work out
```

Sometimes the sensitive fact is never directly observed.

Instead, somebody possesses enough surrounding context to infer it.

```text
A + B + C + institutional knowledge
        ↓
probably X
```

This is particularly important around senior political offices because repeated exposure to routine activity teaches people what **normal** looks like.

Once you understand normal, deviation itself can become information.

## 🚪 Being In The Room Is Information

Suppose somebody is permitted to enter a room where senior officials regularly meet.

That does not mean they know everything discussed there.

It may nevertheless expose them to contextual information.

They may know:

- who attended;
- who did not;
- when the meeting began;
- how long it lasted;
- whether people were unexpectedly summoned;
- whether documents were carried in;
- whether the meeting displaced something else;
- whether the same participants reconvened later.

Each fact may be innocuous.

Together they may reveal considerably more.

This is why the useful question is not:

> **Was she handed the secret?**

It is:

> **What information could the role reasonably expose somebody to directly, indirectly or contextually?**

That is an access question.

It is not an accusation.

## 📅 The Schedule Can Be Access

Administrative information is easy to dismiss because it does not look dramatic.

Calendars are not stamped **TOP SECRET** across the front.

But schedules can reveal:

- relationships;
- priorities;
- unusual activity;
- travel;
- recurring contacts;
- meeting sequences;
- changes in tempo;
- and the emergence of issues before they become public.

Imagine:

```text
ordinary schedule
        ↓
unexpected meeting inserted
        ↓
particular officials attend
        ↓
travel changes
        ↓
public announcement two days later
```

The calendar did not contain the announcement.

It may still have contained useful information about what was happening.

This is one reason metadata deserves its own analysis.

See [📞 Metadata Can Tell The Story](./📞_metadata_can_tell_the_story.md) for the wider problem of relationships, timing and patterns carrying information independently of message content.

## 🧩 Sometimes You Never Need The Secret

The strongest version of the document model assumes that an outside observer must somehow acquire proposition **X** directly.

But intelligence, journalism and ordinary human reasoning do not work that way.

Suppose:

```text
A = senior official suddenly cancels travel
B = specialist adviser arrives unexpectedly
C = particular department begins late-night activity
D = known diplomatic contact is scheduled
```

Nobody says:

```text
X = a particular decision is imminent
```

An informed observer may nevertheless infer X.

That is why access to fragments can matter.

It is also why security architecture uses concepts such as need-to-know and compartmentation.

The purpose is not merely to prevent one person from physically possessing one highly sensitive document.

It is also to limit how much of the wider picture any single unnecessary access pathway can reveal.

See [🧩 A Plus B Plus C Equals Classified](./🧩_a_plus_b_plus_c_equals_classified.md) for the full aggregation problem.

## ⚖️ Access Is Not Acquisition Is Not Disclosure

Expanding the concept of access creates an obvious danger.

If we are not careful, everybody who could theoretically encounter information becomes a suspect.

No.

Keep the evidential ladder intact:

```text
could access
        ≠
did access

did access
        ≠
noticed significance

noticed significance
        ≠
retained information

retained information
        ≠
disclosed information

disclosed information
        ≠
acted maliciously

malicious disclosure
        ≠
espionage
```

This distinction is essential.

A useful access map identifies **possible pathways**.

It does not establish which pathway was used.

Nor does it establish intent.

The purpose of widening the map is to stop investigators and journalists prematurely narrowing the incident to:

> **Which one of these five people deliberately told the press?**

when twenty people, three systems, two rooms and several contextual pathways may have been relevant to how the information became available.

## 🪪 Why Clearance Questions Need An Access Map

This is where personnel assurance and access control meet.

Suppose somebody has not completed the security process normally expected for their role.

That fact does not tell us what they actually encountered.

Maybe their access was tightly restricted.

Maybe compensating controls existed.

Maybe they were excluded from particular systems and briefings.

Maybe their practical access was far wider than their formal status suggests.

The clearance question therefore needs an access question beside it:

```text
WHAT ASSURANCE EXISTED?
        +
WHAT ACCESS ACTUALLY EXISTED?
```

Neither can substitute for the other.

Likewise:

```text
HAS CLEARANCE
        ≠
HAS ACCESS TO EVERYTHING
```

and:

```text
NO COMPLETED CLEARANCE
        ≠
NO PRACTICAL ACCESS
```

See [🪪 Clearance Is Not A Lanyard](./🪪_clearance_is_not_a_lanyard.md) for the personnel-assurance side of that distinction.

## 📱 Access Can Arrive Through An Interface

There is another complication.

The person does not necessarily have to be the direct access point.

Their devices and accounts may form part of the pathway.

For example, an information environment may expose:

- message notifications;
- calendar entries;
- contact names;
- account relationships;
- authentication prompts;
- document titles;
- photographs;
- cloud synchronisation;
- or other contextual traces.

That means:

```text
PERSON DID NOT OPEN DOCUMENT
        ≠
NO RELEVANT INFORMATION
WAS AVAILABLE THROUGH
THE PERSON'S INTERFACES
```

Again, that does not establish compromise.

It establishes why the endpoint belongs inside the access map.

See [📱 The Phone Is Part Of The Security Boundary](./📱_the_phone_is_part_of_the_security_boundary.md).

## 🧬 Practical Access Can Exceed Organisational Charts

Formal diagrams are useful.

Reality is messier.

An organisational chart may tell you:

```text
A reports to B
B attends meeting C
C handles issue D
```

It may not tell you:

- who actually drafts B's correspondence;
- who manages B's calendar;
- who regularly sits outside the meeting room;
- who troubleshoots B's devices;
- who books the travel;
- who receives the visitor;
- which contractor administers the system;
- who covers the role when somebody is absent;
- which informal relationship routinely bypasses the formal workflow.

That does not make every assistant, technician, contractor or colleague a security problem.

It means the **practical attack surface follows real information flows**, not merely the boxes printed on the institution's diagram.

See [🧬 The Attack Surface Has More Than One Strand](./🧬_the_attack_surface_has_more_than_one_strand.md) for the wider sociotechnical model.

## 🧅 Access Controls Are Supposed To Limit Consequences

Once access is understood as multidimensional, compartmentation starts making considerably more sense.

The objective is not:

> **Find perfectly trustworthy humans and then let them see everything.**

It is closer to:

> **Give people and systems the access necessary for their function while limiting unnecessary exposure and the consequences of inevitable failure.**

That can involve:

- need-to-know;
- least privilege;
- role-based permissions;
- physical separation;
- compartmentation;
- restricted systems;
- logging;
- supervision;
- and periodic review.

The details vary by institution.

The principle does not.

If one account, person or endpoint can reach far beyond what their function requires, the potential blast radius grows.

See [🧅 Compartmentation Exists For A Reason](./🧅_compartmentation_exists_for_a_reason.md) for the containment logic.

## ✍️ Questions Journalists Should Actually Ask

Instead of stopping at:

> **Did this person have access?**

ask what **kind**.

Useful questions include:

- What access was formally authorised?
- What access did technical systems actually permit?
- Which physical spaces could the person enter?
- Which meetings could they attend?
- What could they routinely see or hear?
- What scheduling or administrative information did the role expose?
- What contextual information could reasonably be inferred from that position?
- Did practical access exceed documented access?
- Did the person's access change over time?
- Were permissions periodically reviewed?
- Which devices and accounts interacted with the role?
- Could sensitive conclusions be reconstructed from information that was individually less sensitive?
- If an incident occurred, which possible access pathways were actually examined?

And one particularly useful question:

> **When officials say somebody “did not have access”, which definition of access are they using?**

Because:

> **They could not open the classified file**

may be a perfectly accurate answer to a much narrower question than the one the public thinks has been answered.

## 🏛️ The Actual Lesson

Access is not a magical state acquired when somebody clicks **OPEN**.

It is a relationship between:

```text
person
+
role
+
systems
+
spaces
+
information
+
context
```

Sometimes access is explicit.

Sometimes it is technical.

Sometimes it is physical.

Sometimes it is observational.

Sometimes it exists only because somebody has enough context to understand what everybody else would regard as meaningless fragments.

None of those pathways automatically establishes that information was acquired, retained, disclosed or exploited.

But if you want to understand how information could move through a sensitive institution, you have to map the environment that actually existed.

Not merely the document permissions.

> **Access is not a yes/no property. It is a map.**

And if you want to understand who could know something:

> **stop asking only who opened the document.**
