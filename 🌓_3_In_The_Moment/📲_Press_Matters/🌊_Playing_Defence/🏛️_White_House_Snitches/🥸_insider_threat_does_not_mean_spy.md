# 🥸 Insider Threat Does Not Mean Spy
**First created:** 2026-08-21 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-21  
*Why being inside a security boundary describes access and position — not motive, allegiance or guilt.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Say **insider threat** in public and a particular character tends to appear.

```text
TRUSTED EMPLOYEE
        ↓
SECRETLY WORKING FOR SOMEBODY ELSE
        ↓
STEALS SECRETS
        ↓
SPY
```

That person exists.

They are not the whole category.

Sensitive institutions have to manage risk associated with people who possess legitimate access because humans can create security exposure in many different ways.

They can make mistakes.

They can misunderstand rules.

They can have excessive permissions.

They can become disgruntled.

They can be deceived.

They can be coerced.

Their accounts or devices can be compromised.

They can deliberately misuse access.

And, at the most serious end, they can knowingly disclose information or cooperate with an outside actor.

Those are different security problems.

So the first distinction is simple:

> **“Insider” tells you where somebody sits relative to the security boundary. It does not tell you what they intended.**

---

## 🥸 Insider Is A Position, Not A Character Diagnosis

An insider is not necessarily somebody lurking ominously in a corridor.

The useful security concept begins with **legitimate relationship and access**.

Depending on the institution, that may include people such as:

- employees;
- officials;
- contractors;
- consultants;
- temporary staff;
- support personnel;
- or others legitimately permitted into particular systems or environments.

Their access may be:

- informational;
- technical;
- physical;
- administrative;
- observational;
- or contextual.

The important point is that some access exists because the organisation has allowed it.

That creates a security relationship.

It does not create evidence of wrongdoing.

```text
INSIDER
        ≠
SUSPICIOUS PERSON

LEGITIMATE ACCESS
        ≠
MISUSE OF ACCESS
```

This is why [👀 Access Is More Than Opening The Document](./👀_access_is_more_than_opening_the_document.md) matters.

Before asking whether somebody abused access, establish what access they actually had.

---

## ⚠️ “Threat” And “Risk” Need Careful Language

Security terminology varies between institutions and frameworks.

Some use **insider threat** broadly.

Others distinguish more carefully between insider **risk**, insider **incident**, malicious insiders, negligent insiders and other categories.

Journalists should therefore resist assuming that the phrase has one universal technical meaning.

Ask:

> **What does the institution mean by “insider threat” in this context?**

That matters because ordinary readers may hear:

```text
INSIDER THREAT
        =
SUSPECTED SPY
```

when the institution may be discussing a much wider risk-management problem.

Terminology should clarify the evidence.

It should not silently intensify it.

---

## 🧠 Human-Associated Risk Has More Than One Form

A useful map looks something like this:

```text
HUMAN-ASSOCIATED SECURITY RISK
        │
        ├── mistake / accident
        ├── misunderstanding
        ├── negligence
        ├── inappropriate rule-bending
        ├── excessive or unnecessary access
        ├── deception / manipulation
        ├── coercion or pressure
        ├── grievance-related misuse
        ├── deliberate unauthorised disclosure
        └── knowing collaboration / espionage
```

These are **not stages in a ladder**.

Someone who makes an error is not on a journey toward espionage.

Someone who becomes disgruntled does not thereby become malicious.

Someone who is targeted does not thereby become compromised.

The categories identify different ways human access can interact with security risk.

They require different evidence.

They also require different mitigations.

---

## 🙈 Error Is Not Betrayal

People make mistakes.

That is one of the foundational assumptions of security architecture.

A person might:

- send something to the wrong recipient;
- misunderstand handling requirements;
- expose information on a screen;
- use an inappropriate account;
- fail to recognise the significance of a fragment;
- or follow a workflow that itself contains weak controls.

None of those facts establishes disloyalty.

```text
ERROR
        ≠
MALICE
```

Sometimes the person is responsible for a serious mistake.

Sometimes the institution designed a system in which an ordinary mistake had absurdly large consequences.

Those questions should be separated.

> **A security system that requires perfect humans is an insecure system with excellent branding.**

See [🧅 Compartmentation Exists For A Reason](./🧅_compartmentation_exists_for_a_reason.md).

## 🎯 Targeted Does Not Mean Recruited

Another distinction disappears surprisingly quickly in public discussion.

A person can be **targeted** without cooperating.

An outside actor may be interested in:

- their access;
- their device;
- their account;
- their relationships;
- their role;
- their knowledge;
- or their proximity to somebody else.

That does not mean the target knows.

It certainly does not mean the target agreed.

```text
TARGETED
        ≠
COMPROMISED

COMPROMISED
        ≠
KNOWINGLY COOPERATIVE

KNOWINGLY COOPERATIVE
        ≠
EVERY FORM OF ESPIONAGE
```

A technically compromised device can create information exposure while its owner remains entirely loyal.

A person can be unsuccessfully approached.

They can recognise an approach and report it.

They can be manipulated into revealing something whose significance they do not understand.

Or somebody can simply observe them.

The word **target** describes somebody else's interest.

It does not establish the target's conduct.

## 📱 Sometimes The Account Is The Insider Problem

People and their technical identities are intertwined.

Suppose a legitimate staff member has appropriate access.

Their account is compromised.

From the system's perspective, activity may initially appear to originate through legitimate credentials.

The human is an insider.

The human may also be completely innocent.

```text
LEGITIMATE USER
        +
COMPROMISED CREDENTIAL
        ↓
INSIDER-SHAPED ACTIVITY
WITHOUT INSIDER INTENT
```

This is why reducing an investigation to **which authorised person had access?** can be inadequate.

You also need to ask:

- which account;
- which device;
- which authentication event;
- which permissions;
- which logs;
- and whether the activity can actually be attributed to the human associated with the credential.

See [📱 The Phone Is Part Of The Security Boundary](./📱_the_phone_is_part_of_the_security_boundary.md).

## 🪪 Vetting Is Trying To Manage Risk, Not Discover Evil

This is also why personnel assurance is broader than asking:

> **Is this a good person?**

Security vetting and continuing assurance exist because access creates consequences.

Depending on the relevant system and role, security processes may need to consider whether foreseeable vulnerabilities, pressures, conflicts, access arrangements or other risks require management.

That does not mean:

```text
VULNERABILITY
        =
MORAL DEFECT
```

People have complicated lives.

Institutions know this.

The security question is whether relevant risks have been identified, assessed and managed appropriately for the access involved.

This is why [🪪 Clearance Is Not A Lanyard](./🪪_clearance_is_not_a_lanyard.md) treats clearance as part of a wider assurance architecture rather than a magical declaration of personal goodness.

## 🏛️ Institutions Can Manufacture Insider Risk

Sometimes the organisation itself makes the insider problem worse.

Consider:

- unnecessary access;
- permissions that accumulate over time;
- weak separation of duties;
- poor supervision;
- unclear handling rules;
- unmanaged exceptions;
- inadequate logging;
- poor offboarding;
- personal and official systems bleeding together;
- or a culture in which people routinely bypass controls to get work done.

Now imagine one employee makes a mistake.

If that employee can reach vastly more information than their role requires, the consequences are larger.

```text
ORDINARY HUMAN FAILURE
        +
EXCESSIVE ACCESS
        ↓
SERIOUS SECURITY INCIDENT
```

The person matters.

The architecture matters too.

Sometimes asking only:

> **What was wrong with the insider?**

prevents the institution from asking:

> **What was wrong with the system around them?**

## 💼 Some Roles Are Structurally Close To The Information Spine

This matters particularly for support roles.

A person does not need to be the policymaker, intelligence officer or principal decision-maker to occupy a security-sensitive position.

Some roles may routinely involve proximity to:

```text
calendars
+
communications
+
movement
+
visitors
+
meeting rhythms
+
priority changes
+
documents
+
devices
+
institutional context
```

Executive assistants are an obvious example.

If an executive assistant repeatedly follows the executive, remains close during movement, anticipates requirements and tracks changes in schedule, the first explanation should not be:

> **What an unusually intense personal attachment.**

It should be:

> **That may be the job.**

Occupational proximity can be security-relevant precisely because it is ordinary and legitimate.

```text
OCCUPATIONAL PROXIMITY
        ≠
PERSONAL INTIMACY

SECURITY-SENSITIVE ROLE
        ≠
SUSPICIOUS PERSON
```

The correct question is what access accompanies the role and whether controls match that access.

## 👩‍💼 Feminised Labour Can Become Security-Invisible

There is a wider institutional point here.

Some support roles have historically been strongly gendered.

The work involved can include:

- anticipation;
- coordination;
- memory;
- relational knowledge;
- diary management;
- gatekeeping;
- proximity;
- communication;
- emotional smoothing;
- and maintaining continuity around a senior person.

When performed by women, that work can be narrated as personality:

> **she is devoted**

> **she fusses around him**

> **she follows him everywhere**

> **she is always at his side**

rather than recognised as professional labour.

That creates a peculiar analytical distortion.

```text
FEMINISED SUPPORT ROLE
        ↓
LABOUR READ AS PERSONALITY
        ↓
PROFESSIONAL FUNCTION OBSCURED
        ↓
STRUCTURAL ACCESS UNDERESTIMATED
```

The gendered story can therefore do two things simultaneously:

1. over-personalise the woman; and
2. under-analyse the job.

That is bad gender analysis.

It is also bad security analysis.

> **Sometimes the supposedly “soft” feminised role is sitting directly on the information spine of the institution.**

That does not make the worker suspicious.

It makes the role worth threat-modelling properly.

## 🕸️ Insider-Shaped Outcomes Do Not Prove Insider Disclosure

This connects directly to the previous node.

Suppose information appears outside the institution and only a small number of insiders were believed to know it.

That may make deliberate insider disclosure an important hypothesis.

But it still does not establish the mechanism.

The information might have become available through:

- an insider's deliberate disclosure;
- an insider's mistake;
- a compromised insider account;
- a compromised device;
- observational access;
- metadata;
- aggregation;
- or a mixed pathway.

So:

```text
INSIDERS HAD ACCESS
        ≠
AN INSIDER DELIBERATELY LEAKED
```

See [🕸️ You Do Not Need A Leaker](./🕸️_you_do_not_need_a_leaker.md).

## 🍯 Exploitation Is Broader Than The Spy Movie

Public discussion also tends to imagine human exploitation through one particularly cinematic model:

```text
ATTRACTIVE STRANGER
        ↓
SEDUCTION
        ↓
RECRUITMENT
        ↓
SECRETS
```

That can exist.

But security interest in a person or role can involve much broader questions about access, trust, relationships, technology, social interaction and vulnerability.

And sometimes nobody needs to recruit the person at all.

Their:

- device;
- account;
- observable behaviour;
- occupational proximity;
- contextual knowledge;
- or relationships

may be the relevant surface.

That is where this node hands over to [🍯 Honeypots Are Not Magic](./🍯_honeypots_are_not_magic.md).

## ⚖️ Keep The Evidential Ladder Intact

The easiest way to turn insider-risk reporting into nonsense is to collapse several distinct propositions.

Keep them separate:

```text
PERSON HAS ACCESS
        ≠
PERSON CREATED EXPOSURE

PERSON CREATED EXPOSURE
        ≠
PERSON ACTED DELIBERATELY

PERSON ACTED DELIBERATELY
        ≠
PERSON ACTED FOR AN OUTSIDE ACTOR

PERSON WAS TARGETED
        ≠
PERSON WAS COMPROMISED

PERSON WAS COMPROMISED
        ≠
PERSON KNOWINGLY COOPERATED

PERSON KNOWINGLY COOPERATED
        ≠
ESPIONAGE HAS BEEN ESTABLISHED
```

Each step requires evidence.

Do not let a security term perform that evidential work on its own.

This protects innocent people.

It also improves genuine counterintelligence analysis because the investigator remains interested in what actually happened rather than forcing every anomaly into the most dramatic available story.

## ✍️ Questions Journalists Should Actually Ask

When officials invoke insider threat or insider risk, useful questions include:

- What does **insider** mean in the framework being used?
- What does **threat** mean?
- Is the concern intentional, unintentional or still undetermined?
- What legitimate access did the person or role possess?
- Was that access necessary?
- Were permissions appropriately limited?
- Is the concern about the person, their account, their device or their wider access environment?
- Is there evidence of deliberate misuse?
- Is there evidence of outside targeting?
- If so, is there evidence the targeting succeeded?
- Is there evidence of knowing cooperation?
- What distinguishes error, compromise, deliberate disclosure and espionage in the available evidence?
- Did the institution's own access design increase the consequences?
- Were temporary or unusual arrangements involved?
- Were logs sufficient to attribute the activity to a human rather than merely a credential?
- Is ordinary occupational proximity being misdescribed as unusual personal intimacy?
- Is a gendered reading of a support role obscuring the professional access the role actually carries?

And the simplest question:

> **What fact takes us from “this person was inside the boundary” to the stronger claim being made about them?**

If nobody can answer that, the stronger claim may be running ahead of the evidence.

## 🧿 The Actual Lesson

An insider is somebody with a legitimate relationship to the protected environment.

That can make their access security-relevant.

It does not make them guilty.

Human-associated security risk includes:

```text
error
+
poor judgement
+
excessive access
+
technical compromise
+
targeting
+
coercion
+
grievance
+
deliberate misuse
+
knowing collaboration
```

Those are not synonyms.

And the institution itself helps determine how consequential any one of them can become.

So when somebody says **insider threat**, do not mentally translate it into:

```text
SPY
```

Ask what the term means.

Ask what happened.

Ask what access existed.

Ask what the evidence establishes about intent.

Ask whether the person, the technology or the institution created the pathway.

And remember:

> **“Insider” tells you where somebody sits relative to the security boundary.**

> **It does not tell you what they intended.**
