# 🫀 Grieving Charlie Foxtrots

**First created:** 2026-09-30 \| **Last updated:** 2026-09-30\
*Sometimes it is just a Charlie Foxtrot. Sometimes somebody has to live
with what happened next.*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🛰️ Orientation

**Charlie Foxtrot** is military phonetic-alphabet slang for a
**clusterfuck**.

This folder is not an argument that every institutional fuck-up requires
a truth commission, national mourning, three public inquiries and
somebody solemnly playing a bugle.

Sometimes things go wrong.

Sometimes the plan was stupid.

Sometimes everybody gets wet, somebody loses the paperwork, the
expensive machine refuses to machine, six departments discover that
nobody booked the room, and everyone goes home annoyed.

Congratulations.

Charlie Foxtrot.

This folder is about the other kind.

The ones where something goes badly wrong **and somebody gets hurt**.

People are killed.

Civilians are harmed.

Soldiers are harmed.

People are abandoned by institutions which owed them something.

Wrong information becomes attached to somebody's name.

A population is treated as inherently suspicious.

A preventable failure becomes an institutional secret.

A bad operational decision becomes somebody else's bereavement.

The original harm is compounded by denial, delay, defensiveness, bad
investigation, political cowardice, institutional self-protection, media
failure, or the inability to say:

> **We did this. It was wrong. What happens now?**

Those are **Grieving Charlie Foxtrots**.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🫀 1. Why Grieving?

Because consequences matter.

This is not a catalogue of amusing military incompetence, nor an
argument that mistakes themselves are morally extraordinary.

Human institutions fuck up because human beings fuck up.

Military institutions have the additional problem that some of the
things entrusted to them are extraordinarily dangerous.

A mistake involving stationery is irritating.

A mistake involving armed force can kill somebody.

And once somebody has been seriously harmed, the institutional problem
changes.

The relevant questions are no longer only:

-   What failed?
-   Who made the decision?
-   Which procedure broke?
-   How do we prevent recurrence?

They also become:

-   Who was harmed?
-   What does the institution owe them?
-   Has the institution told the truth?
-   Has responsibility been correctly located?
-   Has somebody innocent been left carrying blame?
-   Who needs to apologise?
-   Who has authority to apologise?
-   What can actually be repaired?
-   What cannot be repaired?
-   What would accountability accomplish?
-   What would accountability **not** accomplish?
-   What did delay make worse?
-   What did the public learn?
-   What did personnel inside the institution learn?
-   What happens to trust afterwards?

That is where grief enters the systems analysis.

Not as decoration.

As information about consequence.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🪖 2. This Is Not A Slate On The British Army

The British cases will probably be disproportionately represented here
for a very simple reason:

**I know the British story better.**

I live inside its information environment.

I recognise more of its references.

I know which events remain culturally legible, which names carry
historical weight, which political arguments are recurring, and which
moments I can remember happening during my own lifetime.

That makes British history a useful starting dataset.

It does not make British institutions uniquely wicked.

Nor does examining British military failures require pretending that the
British Armed Forces have never done anything useful, competent,
courageous or necessary.

Quite the opposite.

One of the propositions being tested throughout this folder is whether
**institutional loyalty can survive institutional honesty**.

It had better.

A military institution cannot sensibly define loyalty as:

> Every action undertaken by anybody wearing our uniform must
> subsequently be defended.

That would make institutional honour dependent upon institutional
infallibility.

No human organisation can meet that standard.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🗺️ 3. Local Knowledge, Transferable Lessons

Some of the cases examined here will be extremely well known in Britain
or Ireland and surprisingly poorly understood elsewhere.

Some will be known by name without the mechanism being understood.

Some will have substantially different cultural salience across England,
Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland.

That unevenness matters.

The point is not to export British history as the universal template for
military failure.

The point is to use histories we can reconstruct properly to ask
questions that travel.

For each case:

**What happened?**

**Why did it happen?**

**What happened afterwards?**

**Who responded well?**

**Who responded badly?**

**What actually helped?**

**What made the original harm worse?**

**What was performative or pointless?**

**What took far too fucking long?**

**Where did responsibility actually sit?**

And finally:

> **What can somebody facing a different Charlie Foxtrot learn from this
> one?**

Analogy supplies questions.

It does not predetermine answers.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🔬 4. No Single Villain Required

These nodes should resist the comforting tendency to compress
institutional failure into one Bad Man.

Responsibility can exist at different levels simultaneously.

Depending upon the case, we may need to examine:

-   individual personnel;
-   immediate commanders;
-   senior military leadership;
-   operational doctrine;
-   intelligence;
-   training;
-   procurement;
-   ministers;
-   Cabinet;
-   departments;
-   Parliament;
-   police;
-   prosecutors;
-   courts;
-   public inquiries;
-   regulators;
-   contractors;
-   journalists;
-   editors;
-   broadcasters;
-   political parties;
-   institutional culture;
-   and the wider information environment.

Those actors do not necessarily possess equal responsibility.

Their responsibilities may also be completely different.

A soldier may be responsible for pulling a trigger.

A commander may be responsible for an order.

A department may be responsible for a defective system.

A minister may bear political responsibility for an institution.

An inquiry may be responsible for establishing an authoritative record.

A prosecutor has to answer a different question under a different
evidential standard.

A newspaper may not have caused the original event at all, while still
affecting what the public subsequently believes happened.

**Map responsibility. Do not puree it.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## ⚖️ 5. What Was Good About The Response?

This matters just as much as identifying failure.

These nodes are not prosecutions looking exclusively for aggravating
evidence.

If something worked, keep it.

If somebody made a good decision, say so.

If an institution eventually developed an effective mechanism for
correction, understand the mechanism.

If a minister took responsibility unusually well, study how.

If journalists uncovered information that institutions had concealed,
preserve the method.

If an inquiry successfully restored an accurate public record, ask why
it succeeded.

If military personnel resisted a bad instruction, learn from them.

If reform reduced recurrence, document it.

If an apology actually mattered, do not become so cynical that we
pretend it didn't.

Institutional learning requires retaining **successful responses to
failure** as well as cataloguing the failure itself.

Otherwise we learn only:

> Don't do bad thing.

Excellent.

Very advanced.

What we actually need is:

> **When the bad thing has already happened, what made the next thing
> better?**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🧱 6. What Made It Worse?

The opposite question matters too.

Common candidates include:

-   denial;
-   premature institutional certainty;
-   closing ranks;
-   blaming victims;
-   treating political association as evidence of culpability;
-   destroying or losing records;
-   misleading investigators;
-   allowing incorrect information to persist;
-   unnecessary secrecy;
-   refusing independent scrutiny;
-   inadequate terms of reference;
-   institutional delay;
-   political delay;
-   legal delay;
-   media amplification of unsupported claims;
-   treating criticism as disloyalty;
-   confusing reputation management with institutional repair.

Sometimes the secondary institutional response becomes part of the
original harm.

That needs recording too.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## ⏳ 7. Time Is Part Of The System

**Eventually** is not a neutral word.

An institution can eventually admit what happened.

Eventually apologise.

Eventually investigate.

Eventually compensate somebody.

Eventually restore a person's name.

But people live during the interval.

Evidence deteriorates.

Witnesses die.

Parents die.

Children grow up.

Incorrect accounts become culturally embedded.

Institutional suspicion becomes historical memory.

Later criminal accountability may become harder precisely because
earlier accountability did not happen.

So these nodes should always ask:

> **How long did correction take, and what happened because it took that
> long?**

Delay is not empty space between the incident and the resolution.

Delay produces consequences.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 8. People Are Not The Security Environment

This will recur.

Dangerous environments are real.

Enemy forces are real.

Terrorist organisations are real.

Insurgencies are real.

Riots are real.

Intelligence uncertainty is real.

Fear is real.

None of those things eliminates the need to establish what **this
individual person** was doing.

Group identity is information.

It is not automatically individual conduct.

Political sympathy is information.

It is not automatically armed status.

Geography is information.

It is not automatically hostile intent.

Association is information.

It is not automatically culpability.

A state using coercive force has enormous power to collapse those
categories.

A professional state must therefore work unusually hard **not to**.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🔁 9. Legitimacy Goes Both Ways

Institutional trust is not something governments are simply entitled to
receive from populations.

Institutions generate evidence about themselves.

People observe what happens.

They update accordingly.

If government wants people to trust government, government has
obligations towards the people whose trust it wants.

That includes what happens **after government gets something terribly
wrong**.

Can it investigate itself?

Can it tolerate independent investigation?

Can it distinguish institutional loyalty from institutional denial?

Can it publicly correct incorrect information?

Can it acknowledge an innocent person's innocence?

Can it identify its own responsibility?

Can it change?

Can it say sorry without treating the apology as a demand for
forgiveness?

A state which answers those questions well may restore some legitimacy.

A state which answers them badly produces information too.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🕯️ 10. Reconciliation Is Not An Administrative Output

Truth.

Responsibility.

Accountability.

Criminal liability.

Apology.

Compensation.

Institutional reform.

Grief.

Forgiveness.

Reconciliation.

These are related.

They are **not synonyms**.

A government may be able to establish an inquiry.

A court may determine legal liability.

A military may change doctrine.

A minister may apologise.

A department may compensate somebody.

None of those institutions possesses a button labelled:

`RECONCILIATION = TRUE`

People harmed by the state retain agency over what its later actions
mean to them.

The institution can create better conditions for reconciliation.

It cannot order reconciliation into existence.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🧰 11. The Charlie Foxtrot Debrief

Where possible, each worked case should eventually answer a common set
of questions.

### Before

-   What was the institution trying to accomplish?
-   What information did it possess?
-   What assumptions was it making?
-   What risks were known?
-   What constraints were real?

### During

-   What actually happened?
-   Where did events diverge from the intended system?
-   Who possessed decision-making authority?
-   What information moved between actors?
-   What failed operationally?

### Harm

-   Who was harmed?
-   How?
-   Was the harm recognised immediately?
-   Was responsibility disputed?
-   Was incorrect information attached to the people harmed?

### After

-   What happened in the first hours, days and weeks?
-   Who investigated?
-   Who controlled the information?
-   What did government say?
-   What did the institution say?
-   What did the media say?
-   What did affected people say?

### Accountability

-   What responsibility belonged to individuals?
-   What belonged to command?
-   What belonged to government?
-   What belonged to systems?
-   What belonged elsewhere?
-   Which processes were criminal, civil, political, administrative or
    investigatory?

### Repair

-   What helped?
-   What did not?
-   What made things worse?
-   What took too long?
-   What could realistically be repaired?
-   What could never be restored?

### Learning

-   What changed afterwards?
-   Did the change address the actual mechanism of failure?
-   Was recurrence reduced?
-   Which useful response should be preserved?
-   Which mistake should not be repeated elsewhere?

## ☘️ 12. First Case: Bloody Sunday

The first worked case is:

`☘️_bloody_sunday_1972.md`

Bloody Sunday is useful here precisely because the British state's later
response contains **both failure and something worth preserving**.

The killings themselves.

The inadequacy of the first official account.

The decades required to replace it.

The evidential and legal consequences of delay.

But also:

the eventual willingness to establish a much larger inquiry;

the production of an authoritative evidential record;

the unequivocal exoneration of the civilian casualties;

the Prime Minister's acceptance of state responsibility;

and the proposition that:

> **you do not defend the British Army by defending the indefensible.**

That is not the end of the story.

It is something worth learning from inside it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🧭 13. The Point

This folder is not here because Britain needs to spend eternity
flagellating itself over every military failure.

It is here because **not learning is a choice**.

When a Charlie Foxtrot hurts nobody, fix the process, take the piss, buy
somebody a pint and carry on.

When people are killed, injured, traumatised, abandoned, wrongly accused
or made to carry the consequences of institutional failure, something
more is required.

Not endless national self-hatred.

Not compulsory forgiveness.

Not pretending every tragedy has a neat reconciliation arc.

Something considerably more practical:

**find out what happened.**

**tell the truth.**

**put responsibility where it belongs.**

**repair what can actually be repaired.**

**preserve what worked in the response.**

**understand what made it worse.**

**and teach the next institution before it produces another grieving
Charlie Foxtrot.**
