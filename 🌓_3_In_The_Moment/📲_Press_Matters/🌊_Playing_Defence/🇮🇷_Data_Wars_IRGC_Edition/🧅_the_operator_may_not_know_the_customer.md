# 🧅 The Operator May Not Know The Customer  
**First created:** 2026-08-01 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-09  
*The person carrying out the task may know only the layer directly above them — and the damage may not be visible until the layers are peeled back.*  

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

Cyber operations are often described as though a state selects an operator, gives them a complete mission, and receives the result.

Sometimes that happens.

Often the structure is much messier.

The person stealing credentials, installing malware, buying access, handling data, running a harassment account, probing a control system, or taking one small paid task may not know who ultimately wants the work done.

They may not know that there is an ultimate customer at all.

A plausible chain may look like:

```text
recruit
→ small task
→ criminal intermediary
→ access broker
→ contractor or proxy
→ state customer
```

Each layer may see only the layer next to it.

That makes the operation:

- cheaper;
- more deniable;
- harder to attribute;
- easier to compartmentalise;
- and easier to replace when one participant is exposed.

The onion matters for another reason too.

Sometimes an onion looks intact from the outside.

The damage becomes visible only when the layers are peeled back.

A cyber operation can work the same way.

The first visible intrusion may look like:

```text
ordinary crime
```

The next layer may reveal:

```text
commercial access brokerage
```

The next:

```text
repeated tasking
```

The next:

```text
proxy or intelligence-linked acquisition
```

And only later may investigators discover how the access, data, or operational effect was actually used.

The visible operator may therefore be real without being the whole explanation.

The visible damage may also be real without being the whole consequence.

---

## 🧅 Why The Onion Matters  

The onion is not just a metaphor for secrecy.

It is a model of **layered visibility**.

At the outside, investigators may see:

- one account;
- one credential theft;
- one intrusion;
- one ransomware payment;
- one contractor;
- one hacktivist claim;
- one exposed controller;
- or one person handling stolen data.

Peeling back the next layer may reveal:

- repeated customers;
- shared infrastructure;
- recurring payment routes;
- common access brokers;
- similar victim selection;
- follow-on exploitation;
- or a wider operational purpose.

The important principle is:

> The first layer discovered should not automatically be treated as the final layer that exists.

But the reverse matters too.

An incomplete chain should not be filled with assumptions.

The pack should preserve:

```text
what is visible
→ what is evidenced
→ what is suspected
→ what remains underneath
```

without pretending that every onion contains the same thing.

## 🪜 Recruitment Can Begin Below The Threshold Of Alarm  

People are not always recruited into cyber operations by being asked to commit a spectacular attack.

They may begin with:

- testing whether a password works;
- forwarding a message;
- opening an account;
- verifying a phone number;
- buying a domain;
- translating a document;
- scraping public information;
- locating one person;
- downloading one file;
- moving cryptocurrency;
- checking whether a system is reachable;
- probing a remote interface;
- or providing access to a system they already use.

The first task may look minor.

The payment may look disproportionate enough to feel flattering, but not alarming enough to trigger refusal.

The recruit may be told that the target is:

- a fraudster;
- an enemy;
- a security threat;
- a bad employer;
- a dishonest partner;
- a political extremist;
- a corrupt institution;
- or simply someone who deserves to be embarrassed.

That story reduces friction.

It gives the recruit a moral explanation before they are given enough information to examine the real purpose.

---

## 🕳️ The Funnel Can Work Like Grooming  

The recruitment funnel can resemble grooming because commitment is built in stages.

```text
small favour
→ reward
→ secrecy
→ slightly larger task
→ shared risk
→ dependency
→ coercion
```

The recruit may become entangled through:

- money;
- praise;
- belonging;
- ideology;
- fear;
- debt;
- blackmail;
- criminal exposure;
- status;
- access to a community;
- or the belief that they are already too involved to leave safely.

By the time the scale becomes visible, the operator may have:

- accepted several payments;
- committed offences;
- shared personal details;
- exposed another person;
- compromised a system;
- enabled a more serious intrusion;
- or become dependent on the network providing the work.

The system does not need perfect loyalty.

It needs enough compliance to complete the next task.

---

## 🎭 Several Labels Can Be True At Once  

Public reporting often asks whether an actor is:

- a state operator;
- a proxy;
- a hacktivist;
- a criminal;
- a contractor;
- an access broker;
- or an independent opportunist.

Those categories can overlap.

A group may behave like a criminal enterprise most of the time and still accept occasional state tasking.

A contractor may serve several customers.

A patriotic hacktivist group may receive:

- access;
- protection;
- infrastructure;
- target suggestions;
- intelligence;
- or stolen material

without becoming a formal state unit.

A state may buy data from criminals after the theft rather than directing the theft itself.

An operator may exaggerate state links to increase prestige.

A state may exaggerate distance from an actor to preserve deniability.

The useful questions are therefore not only:

> Who pressed the button?

They are also:

- Who selected the target?
- Who supplied the access?
- Who found the vulnerability?
- Who paid?
- Who sold the result?
- Who handled the stolen material?
- Who provided follow-on tasking?
- Who amplified the result?
- Who protected the operators?
- Who benefited from the exposure?
- Who used the information afterwards?
- And who knew what at each stage?

The customer may enter the chain late.

That still matters.

---

## 🛒 Access Can Be Bought After The Breach  

A state does not always need to commission the original intrusion.

Cybercrime produces a market in:

- stolen credentials;
- compromised mailboxes;
- remote access;
- leaked databases;
- location data;
- medical information;
- identity documents;
- corporate records;
- privileged accounts;
- virtual private network access;
- exposed remote-management systems;
- and access to industrial or operational systems.

An operator may steal access for money.

An intermediary may sell it to several buyers.

One of those buyers may be state-linked.

In that model, the criminal explanation and the state-interest explanation are not opposites.

The breach may begin as crime and later become:

- intelligence;
- coercion;
- propaganda;
- targeting infrastructure;
- operational access;
- or preparation for later disruption.

This is one reason why the question:

> Was the original attacker directly ordered by Iran?

may be too narrow.

Other questions are:

> Did an Iranian or Iran-aligned actor acquire the access afterwards?

> Did they buy the stolen data?

> Did they reuse the intrusion?

> Did they turn reconnaissance into disruption?

> Did they use the result for a different operation than the one the original intruder intended?

Those are different evidentiary claims.

They should be recorded separately.

---

## 🚰 Access To Machinery Can Be Resold Too  

The access-broker problem becomes more serious where the system is not merely informational.

Access to:

- programmable logic controllers;
- industrial control systems;
- building-management systems;
- remote plant interfaces;
- water treatment systems;
- power systems;
- pumps;
- valves;
- sensors;
- or supervisory control systems

can itself become a commodity.

The person who first discovers the exposed system may have no strategic objective at all.

They may simply know:

> someone will pay for this access.

That creates a possible chain such as:

```text
internet-exposed control system
→ opportunistic discovery
→ credential theft
→ access sale
→ intermediary
→ state-linked buyer
→ operational tasking
```

Or:

```text
criminal intrusion
→ persistence retained
→ access resold
→ later wartime use
```

The original operator may therefore have no knowledge of:

- the war;
- the eventual target selection;
- the physical effect;
- the customer's identity;
- or the legal significance of what follows.

That does not erase their conduct.

It does complicate the attribution chain.

---

## 🧠 Partial Knowledge Is A Feature  

Compartmentalisation is not evidence that no wider strategy exists.

It may be how the strategy is protected.

One participant knows the target.

Another knows the vulnerability.

Another provides access.

Another handles payment.

Another maintains infrastructure.

Another writes the narrative.

Another publishes the material.

Another uses it for pressure.

Another decides whether the access should remain dormant or become disruptive.

No single low-level participant needs to understand the whole operation.

That makes exposure harder because investigators may find genuine ignorance at several points in the chain.

A recruit saying:

> I did not know who wanted it.

may be telling the truth.

A contractor saying:

> We only supplied access.

may also be telling the truth.

An intermediary saying:

> We did not know how the customer intended to use it.

may also be telling the truth.

Several true statements can still sit inside one harmful chain.

That does not identify the customer.

It tells us the onion has more layers.

## 🔁 The Customer May Change  

One of the most important complications is that the final customer may not be present at the beginning.

A chain can change hands.

For example:

```text
criminal steals credentials
→ broker acquires them
→ contractor buys access
→ state-linked actor purchases selected accounts
→ intelligence service exploits them
```

Or:

```text
hacktivist compromises system
→ publishes proof of access
→ another actor notices
→ access is privately transferred
→ wartime operator uses it later
```

This means there may be no single answer to:

> Who was this attack for?

The answer at the moment of initial compromise may be:

> nobody in particular.

The answer later may be:

> whoever bought the access.

The answer later still may be:

> a state actor who recognised its strategic value.

The timeline should therefore distinguish:

```text
INITIAL PURPOSE
LATER CUSTOMER
FOLLOW-ON USE
FINAL OPERATIONAL EFFECT
```

where the evidence allows.

---

## 🧍 The Target May Be A Person, Not Merely A System  

Traditional cyber defence often treats the institution as the primary victim.

The response becomes:

```text
contain
restore
patch
notify
resume
```

That may be necessary.

It is not enough where the stolen material concerns an individual.

The operational purpose may be to:

- expose;
- isolate;
- intimidate;
- recruit around;
- discredit;
- locate;
- profile;
- socially punish;
- or make a person easier to target later.

Once the data leaves state custody, further harm may be carried out by:

- domestic political actors;
- abusive relatives;
- employers;
- journalists;
- stalkers;
- organised crime;
- hostile online communities;
- commercial intermediaries;
- or people who believe they are acting patriotically.

Those later actors may have no direct relationship with the original intruder.

They may not need one.

The original operation only has to make the material available and create a story about what it means.

The network can then outsource the rest to ordinary social cruelty.

---

## 🧨 Domestic Participation Can Extend The Attack  

Foreign information operations do not need to perform every stage themselves.

They can create conditions in which citizens of the target country continue the work.

That continuation may include:

- circulating leaked material;
- humiliating the victim;
- reporting them to employers or authorities;
- inventing allegations around real data;
- attempting contact or recruitment;
- threatening family members;
- narrowing access to work, housing, healthcare, or political participation;
- or treating the leak as public permission to interfere.

This is strategically useful because the later harm appears domestic.

The state may then classify each act separately:

- harassment;
- employment dispute;
- safeguarding concern;
- family conflict;
- reputational damage;
- ordinary cybercrime;
- data-protection failure;
- or local misconduct.

The campaign disappears into administrative categories.

The person experiences one continuous attack.

That is another onion effect.

Each layer may look individually mundane.

The cumulative harm becomes visible only when the layers are read together.

---

## 🇮🇷 What This Means For Iran Analysis  

Iranian state and state-aligned activity has long been understood as capable of operating through overlapping:

- military;
- intelligence;
- political;
- commercial;
- proxy;
- hacktivist;
- and criminal structures.

That does not mean every Iranian-speaking operator, every anti-Western actor, or every criminal using Iranian branding is directed by the IRGC.

It means the analytical model must allow for:

- loose coordination;
- ideological alignment;
- tolerated actors;
- commercial tasking;
- shared infrastructure;
- indirect acquisition;
- access brokerage;
- purchase of stolen material;
- and opportunistic exploitation of access obtained by others.

A decentralised ecosystem can still produce strategic convergence.

The absence of one visible command chain does not prove the absence of:

- state benefit;
- state influence;
- later state acquisition;
- or later state exploitation.

At the same time:

```text
benefit
≠
direction

acquisition
≠
original tasking

ideological alignment
≠
state control

shared target
≠
shared customer
```

The pack should keep those claims separate.

## ⚖️ Layered Tasking Complicates Legal Responsibility  

The onion also matters legally.

Suppose a civilian water-control system is compromised through a chain involving:

```text
criminal operator
→ access broker
→ intermediary
→ state-linked customer
→ disruptive wartime tasking
```

Several different questions follow.

Who performed the original intrusion?

Who knowingly transferred access?

Who knew the target was civilian infrastructure?

Who selected the operational objective?

Who ordered the disruptive action?

Who knew the operation was connected to the armed conflict?

Who intended or accepted the expected physical consequences?

And who can legally be connected to the state?

Those answers may point to different people.

That means:

```text
identifying the hacker
≠
identifying the customer

identifying the customer
≠
proving state responsibility

proving state responsibility
≠
identifying individual criminal responsibility
```

The deeper the legal claim, the more layers may need to be peeled back.

That is one reason public attribution can lag behind technical investigation.

The technically visible operator may be only the outer skin.

---

## 🕸️ Attribution Should Record The Layer Being Attributed  

The pack should avoid writing simply:

```text
ATTRIBUTION: IRAN
```

where the evidence actually supports something narrower.

A better model is:

```text
OPERATOR:
INTERMEDIARY:
ACCESS SOURCE:
CUSTOMER:
BENEFICIARY:
STATE LINK:
PUBLIC ATTRIBUTION:
LEGAL RESPONSIBILITY:
```

For example:

```text
OPERATOR:
Criminal intrusion set.

INTERMEDIARY:
Unknown access broker.

ACCESS SOURCE:
Compromised remote-control credentials.

CUSTOMER:
Unresolved.

BENEFICIARY:
Possible Iranian-aligned actor.

STATE LINK:
Suspected, not established.

PUBLIC ATTRIBUTION:
None.

LEGAL RESPONSIBILITY:
Open.
```

That structure prevents the strongest hypothesis from silently replacing the evidence actually available.

## 🔎 What Evidence Would Help  

Confidence increases where investigators can identify:

- payment flows;
- communications between layers;
- repeated intermediaries;
- shared hosting or infrastructure;
- access brokers serving known state-linked customers;
- recovered tasking;
- matching victim selection;
- coordinated publication;
- common operational timing;
- repeated resale patterns;
- follow-on access by known operators;
- or later use of stolen material by identifiable state or proxy actors.

Useful questions include:

```text
WHO HAD ACCESS FIRST?
WHO DISCOVERED THE VULNERABILITY?
WHO KEPT PERSISTENCE?
WHO SOLD OR SHARED IT?
WHO RECEIVED IT?
WHO PAID?
WHO SELECTED THE TARGET?
WHO PROVIDED FOLLOW-ON TASKING?
WHO PUBLISHED IT?
WHO AMPLIFIED IT?
WHO USED IT AGAINST THE TARGET?
WHO CHANGED THE PHYSICAL PROCESS?
WHO BENEFITED?
```

The answers may not point to one organisation.

They may reveal an ecosystem.

---

## 🧪 What Would Show The Onion Is Deeper Than It First Appeared  

A later development deserves special attention where it reveals that an apparently isolated incident had additional layers.

Examples include:

- criminal access later used by a state-linked actor;
- a previously unknown intermediary appearing across several campaigns;
- identical credentials or infrastructure changing hands;
- a hacktivist claim followed by evidence of professional tasking;
- data stolen in one breach appearing in a separate political or intelligence operation;
- access obtained months earlier becoming operational during wartime;
- an operator shown to have served several customers;
- or a control-system intrusion later linked to a broader campaign.

That should be recorded as:

```text
NEW LAYER DISCOVERED
```

not merely as:

```text
ATTRIBUTION CHANGED
```

because the new evidence may alter the understood architecture of the operation itself.

---

## ⚠️ What This Node Does Not Claim  

This node does not claim that:

- every cybercriminal is secretly a state proxy;
- every recruit is an innocent dupe;
- every leaked database has an intelligence customer;
- every access broker serves a state;
- every domestic act of harassment is coordinated;
- every operational-technology breach is intended for wartime use;
- every decentralised network has one hidden commander;
- or every onion, once peeled, reveals the IRGC at the centre.

It argues for a wider evidentiary frame.

The immediate operator, organiser, access broker, buyer, beneficiary, final user, and legally responsible actor may be different people.

An investigation that stops at the first visible operator may identify a perpetrator while missing the customer.

An investigation that assumes a hidden customer without evidence makes the opposite mistake.

The task is to peel carefully.

---

## 🧭 Working Rule  

The practical rule for this pack is:

> Record the operator, intermediary, access source, buyer, beneficiary, later user, and state link separately wherever the evidence allows.

Do not collapse them into one actor.

Do not assume they are unrelated merely because the chain is incomplete.

And do not assume the centre of the onion merely because the outer layer looks familiar.

The task is to preserve the layers.

That is how the onion becomes visible.
