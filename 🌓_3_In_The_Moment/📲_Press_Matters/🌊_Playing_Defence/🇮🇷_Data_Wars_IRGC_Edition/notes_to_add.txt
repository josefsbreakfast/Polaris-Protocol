The telecommunications effect itself was real: AT&T customers across Texas lost service on **7 September**, with more than 7,000 households affected in the Dallas-area incident and wider outage reporting across several cities. But **AT&T explicitly rejects the cyberattack claim**. Its assessment is that attempted **physical cable theft** caused the Dallas outage, and it says it has found no evidence supporting APT IRAN’s assertion. Service has since been restored. ([fox26houston.com][2])

**Attribution confidence:** effectively **none for the AT&T outage as an Iranian cyberattack** on present evidence. There is a credible Iranian-linked claimant, but the infrastructure owner has supplied a concrete competing cause that fits the observed effect. That should currently be weighted substantially above the Telegram claim.

The **Texas water-utility claim is even less substantiated**. APT IRAN says it penetrated and disrupted an unnamed facility, but there is not yet an independently identified victim, public utility confirmation, regulator statement, technical evidence or confirmed service outage. I would therefore retain it as **unverified actor claim only**, not an incident. ([Threat Beat][1])

This matters because the group is operating against a backdrop of **genuine Iranian-affiliated OT disruption**. U.S. agencies have already confirmed that Iranian-affiliated actors have manipulated internet-exposed PLCs in American water, energy and government facilities since March, producing operational disruption and financial losses. ([U.S. Department of War][3])

### How this changes the trend since 28 February

The new pattern is partly **informational rather than technical**:

**real Iranian-linked PLC attacks**
→ **public threats naming water, energy and telecom**
→ **large unrelated infrastructure outage occurs**
→ **Iran-linked persona rapidly claims it**.

That means **temporal correlation is becoming actively exploitable as propaganda**. An operator no longer needs to cause every outage it claims; during a period of genuine attacks, ordinary equipment failure, physical sabotage or criminal activity can be folded into a narrative of coordinated Iranian reach.

That is analytically important because it creates exactly the attribution problem this watch is designed to avoid. A cluster of outages after Iranian threats is now **less probative by itself**, not more, unless accompanied by victim confirmation, OT telemetry, infrastructure overlap or other technical evidence.

At the same time, the threat should not simply be dismissed as bluff. The group’s sector selection — **water + energy + telecommunications** — matches the broader Iranian wartime targeting trajectory, and the federal April advisory confirms that closely related Iranian-affiliated actors have already caused physical-process disruption through exposed PLCs. ([U.S. Department of War][3])

So the net update is:

> **Iran-linked infrastructure disruption remains real, but the actor ecosystem is now visibly mixing genuine capability with opportunistic claims against unrelated outages.**

That raises the evidentiary bar for future incident attribution and makes **cross-checking physical failure, criminal sabotage and ordinary network faults** increasingly important.

### 🇺🇸 United States — healthcare technology / clinical infrastructure

**supplier compromise → legitimate vendor credential → authorised healthcare API → bulk patient-data extraction**

rather than:

**attacker → direct breach of healthcare provider.**

That matters because recruitment, affiliate activity, compromised contractors and access brokers can make the ultimate operator much harder to see. Here, however, there is no evidence that this complexity conceals a state sponsor; ordinary ransomware remains the much stronger explanation.

vendor or service-provider identity → shared interface/API → healthcare data across downstream organisations.

There is **one meaningful pattern shift worth adding**. It is **not Iran-linked on current evidence**, but it matters to this watch because it shows how rapidly a criminal operator can now create reusable access across education, government and other essential sectors in countries involved in the war.

### 🇺🇸 🇬🇧 🇨🇦 🇫🇷 and others — education / government / healthcare / utilities

GreyNoise disclosed on **9 September 2026** that a likely Russian-speaking operator used hundreds of AI agents to exploit two PaperCut NG/MF vulnerabilities and compromise **at least 440 servers belonging to 395 organisations in 48 countries**. The campaign began on **31 August** and was opportunistic rather than sector-selected. ([greynoise.io][1])

The affected-country distribution is significant for this watch: **98 U.S. victims, 59 UK, 31 French, 24 Canadian, 15 German**, plus smaller numbers across Australia, NATO Europe and other states. The operator harvested credentials at **280 victims**, obtained operating-system or domain secrets from **147**, and reached full domain-administrator control at **12**. ([greynoise.io][1])

**Sector impact:** education dominated with **204 compromised instances**, including seven where domain-admin access was achieved. But the same campaign also penetrated **nine government/public-sector environments, eight healthcare/social-care organisations, 13 manufacturing/industrial/energy/utility organisations, and six financial/insurance organisations**. GreyNoise attributes the education concentration largely to PaperCut’s installed base rather than deliberate targeting. ([greynoise.io][1])

**Operational impact:** there is not yet evidence of widespread public-service outages. The important impact is **access creation and credential theft**. Where the attacker reached domain admin, it used DCSync to extract the organisation’s Active Directory credential database. GreyNoise says it is still unclear whether the actor intends to exploit those accesses directly or **hand them to affiliated actors** for ransomware, data theft or other follow-on activity. ([greynoise.io][1])

**Attribution confidence:** **moderate for a Russian-speaking criminal/operator profile; effectively none for Iran.** GreyNoise describes the actor as likely Russian-speaking and says the campaign looks opportunistic. Its pre-existing country-avoidance list included **Iran itself**, which weighs strongly against reading this as an Iranian-directed campaign. ([greynoise.io][1])

**Competing explanations:** straightforward access brokerage or preparation for ransomware is currently the best fit. There is no evidence of Iranian tasking, and no reason yet to reinterpret the campaign as covert state activity.

### Why it matters to the Iran watch

The meaningful change is the **speed and fungibility of access creation**.

The operator went from an empty workspace to successful remote-code execution against a real victim in **under four hours**, reached its first domain-admin access roughly two hours later, and—once the campaign was running at scale—compromised **11 organisations in 26 seconds**. At one U.S. high school, initial access became full domain-admin control in seven minutes. ([greynoise.io][1])

So the broader environment since **28 February** now has another layer:

**state-linked reconnaissance / OT exploitation**

* **ransomware and access brokers**
* **AI-assisted mass compromise capable of manufacturing hundreds of downstream footholds almost immediately.**

That matters because the question “who originally penetrated this network?” is becoming even less equivalent to “who ultimately uses the access?” GreyNoise explicitly leaves open whether these compromises will be transferred to other actors. ([greynoise.io][1])

It therefore strengthens the part of the model you have been watching around **recruitment, affiliates and obscured tasking**, without supplying any Iran attribution itself. A state actor does not necessarily need to perform the initial intrusion if a criminal ecosystem can generate privileged access at this scale and speed.

I found **no newly confirmed Iran-linked outage or separate essential-service disruption since the previous check** that clears the alert threshold. The development worth recording today is the emergence of **AI-orchestrated, cross-sector access generation at scale**, not a new Iranian strike.
