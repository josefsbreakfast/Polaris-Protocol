# 🇮🇷 Data Wars: IRGC Edition
**First created:** 2026-08-01 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-01  
*Tracking essential-state cyber disruption across the widening Iran war without pretending attribution is cleaner than it is.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

This pack tracks cyber incidents affecting the systems through which states continue to function during the Iran war.

That means more than defence networks.

It includes water, energy, health, education, government administration, policing and justice systems, transport, telecommunications, banking, payments, and the contracted infrastructure that keeps those systems operating.

The war did not begin in cyberspace. It does not remain neatly outside it either.

Iranian and Iran-linked cyber activity is often decentralised, deniable, layered through proxies, criminal operators, access brokers, hacktivist branding, contractors, and people who may not know who ultimately benefits from the task they have been given.

That makes two opposite mistakes especially dangerous:

- attributing every badly timed breach to Iran;
- treating every unattributed breach as strategically meaningless.

This pack does neither.

---

## ⚠️ This Is Not An Attribution Ledger

Some incidents included here have been attributed to Iranian state or Iran-linked actors.

Some are suspected.

Some are actor-claimed.

Some remain unattributed.

Some may ultimately prove unrelated.

Timing, clustering, repeated techniques, target selection, operational effect, and resemblance to known campaigns may justify scrutiny. They do not, by themselves, prove Iranian direction.

Readers should consult the cited official advisories, affected institutions, technical research, and reputable reporting as investigations develop.

Where attribution changes, the timeline should change with it.

## 🧭 What This Pack Is Tracking

The central question is not:

> Has Iran switched off an entire Western country?

The question is:

> Are essential systems across states Iran regards as participating in the war showing a meaningful pattern of intrusion, disruption, data compromise, coercion, or operational degradation?

A modern cyber campaign may not arrive as one spectacular national outage.

It may arrive as:

- local water systems forced into manual operation;
- public records removed from state custody;
- banking disruption that weakens confidence without collapsing payments;
- health or education systems losing access to data;
- transport systems taking weeks to recover;
- repeated breaches that reveal institutional seams;
- or citizens discovering that hostile actors can obtain their data more easily than they can obtain an accountable explanation from their own government.

Small incidents can accumulate into strategic effect.

Ambiguity can be part of the effect.

Institutional exhaustion can be part of the effect.

## 🗺️ The War Map Is Wider Than The Formal War

The United States and Israel are the central direct belligerents.

But Iran may also regard states as participating where they:

- host forces used in operations against Iran;
- provide basing, logistics, refuelling, intelligence, interception, or air defence;
- protect regional infrastructure supporting the opposing coalition;
- join maritime, sanctions, or military support arrangements;
- or constrain Iran's ability to retaliate.

A government may describe its role as defensive support.

Iran may still read that role as participation.

This pack therefore tracks incidents across the wider coalition and support architecture, while keeping each country's degree of involvement distinct.

## 🕸️ Attribution Is Not A Light Switch

Public discussion often collapses attribution into two settings:

```text
Iran did it
Iran did not do it
```

Real cyber attribution is usually slower, graded, and contested.

A useful record separates:

- confirmed state attribution;
- probable or suspected attribution;
- actor claims;
- technical resemblance;
- proxy or criminal overlap;
- unattributed incidents;
- competing explanations;
- and what remains unknown.

No public attribution does not mean no strategic relevance.

Strategic relevance does not mean proven Iranian direction.

Both propositions can be true at once.

## 🧅 The Operator May Not Know The Customer

Cyber operations can be assembled through layers.

A person may begin with a small paid task, credential theft, account access, malware deployment, or data handling without knowing who ultimately commissioned the work.

A plausible chain may look like:

```text
recruit
→ small task
→ criminal intermediary
→ access broker
→ contractor or proxy
→ state customer
```

This resembles a grooming funnel because commitment can be built gradually.

By the time the operator understands the scale or purpose of the work, they may already be financially, socially, technically, or criminally entangled.

This is not unique to Iran.

It is part of the wider ecology through which states, intelligence-linked actors, criminal organisations, and political networks can obtain deniable labour.

## 🏗️ What Counts As State Infrastructure

This pack uses a functional test.

A system belongs here where its failure, compromise, manipulation, or loss would materially interfere with the state's ability to govern or with ordinary social life.

That includes:

- water and wastewater;
- energy and industrial control;
- healthcare and medical supply;
- education and public records;
- banking, payments, and financial confidence;
- transport and logistics;
- telecommunications;
- defence and security systems;
- government administration;
- policing and justice infrastructure;
- and contracted systems performing an essential public function.

A private company may therefore appear where it is operating part of the country's essential infrastructure.

The issue is function, not branding.

---

## 📉 Small Disruptions Can Make A Campaign

Waiting for one dramatic collapse is an outdated model of cyber defence.

A campaign can be strategically significant without producing a single cinematic event.

The pattern may be visible through:

- repeated low-level operational disruption;
- attacks across several sectors;
- recurring use of weakly protected control systems;
- data theft from institutions with public responsibilities;
- incidents clustered around military escalation;
- movement from reconnaissance into machinery;
- or a growing gap between what institutions know internally and what affected people are told.

The campaign effect comes from accumulation.

The state does not need to stop functioning completely for confidence in the state to be damaged.

---

## 🇬🇧 Britain And The Exploitable Seam

Britain presents a specific governance problem.

Its institutions may possess substantial cyber, defence, intelligence, regulatory, and investigative capability while still leaving responsibility fragmented across departments, contractors, police forces, regulators, and national-security refusals.

That fragmentation can become an adversary-facing weakness.

A state that protects systems but cannot provide an affected citizen with:

- a named lead authority;
- a written account;
- a lawful basis;
- a risk assessment;
- a protection pathway;
- and a route of challenge;

is advertising that the seams between institutions can be used to isolate the target.

That is not merely poor administration.

It is part of national resilience.

## 📰 Reporting Rule

The reporting rule for this pack is simple:

> Do not attribute beyond the evidence. Do not erase a developing strategic pattern merely because public attribution is incomplete.

Each incident should be recorded with:

- date;
- country;
- sector;
- affected body;
- operational effect;
- claimed actor;
- official attribution;
- other attribution;
- confidence level;
- relevance to the Iran war;
- rival explanations;
- sources;
- review date;
- and correction status.

Repetition is not corroboration.

Ten articles may still trace back to one advisory, one vendor report, or one unverified actor claim.

The source chain matters.
