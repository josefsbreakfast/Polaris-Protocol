🇳🇱🇧🇪🇺🇸 2. Netherlands, Belgium and United States — multinational food-distribution disruption

Status: 🔴 Confirmed operational disruption; unauthorised intrusion not yet established.

A separate cybersecurity incident began affecting Ahold Delhaize on 11 October.

In its  official statement of 12 October⁠￼, the company confirmed a network-security problem and said it had taken systems offline to protect its infrastructure.

The consequences are now considerably more substantial than website unavailability.

Operational impact

In the Netherlands, Albert Heijn suspended online grocery ordering and deliveries. Websites, applications, customer-service functions and some loyalty systems became unavailable.

More importantly, operations stopped at several distribution centres.

Albert Heijn’s chief executive confirmed that some centres had ceased operating because their supporting systems were shut down. Suppliers were reportedly instructed to redirect deliveries to the remaining functioning distribution infrastructure.

The company warned that fresh-food availability could deteriorate if disruption continued.  

In Belgium, Delhaize experienced digital-service problems, with potential downstream effects on food distribution.

In the United States, websites associated with Food Lion, Stop & Shop, Giant Food, Hannaford and The Giant Company became unavailable. Employees also reported difficulty accessing scheduling and payroll information.

Physical stores remained open, and there is no established nationwide interruption to food availability.  

Attribution and competing explanations

Network-security incident: 🟢 Confirmed.

Precautionary system shutdowns: 🟢 Confirmed.

Distribution-centre disruption: 🟢 Confirmed.

Multinational digital-service disruption: 🟢 Confirmed.

Successful malicious intrusion: 🟠 Suspected, not publicly confirmed.

Ransomware or data theft: ⚪ Not established.

Iranian involvement: ⚪ No evidence.

Cybersecurity specialists consider a malicious intrusion plausible, but an unresolved vulnerability or precautionary containment decision could also explain the shutdowns.  

Crucially, there is no evidence connecting this incident to the French refrigeration attacks.

The incidents occurred within approximately the same period and affected the same broad essential-service sector.

But their demonstrated mechanisms differ:

France: direct manipulation of refrigeration controllers.

Ahold Delhaize: network-security incident → protective IT shutdowns → distribution and administrative disruption.

That is a meaningful cluster for monitoring, not proof of coordination.

Change since 28 February

This introduces another important distinction within food infrastructure.

The French incident demonstrates physical-process compromise.

The Ahold incident demonstrates logistical dependence on shared information systems.

In both cases, essential goods can become unavailable without an attacker directly targeting farms, warehouses or transport vehicles.

Timeline classification: CORE — multinational food-distribution disruption; attribution unresolved.

🧭 Overall assessment

Today’s findings materially strengthen the cyber-to-physical and essential-supply disruption strands.

The French incident is the more important development because it establishes deliberate manipulation of operating equipment across numerous locations.

The Ahold incident demonstrates how an apparently centralised network-security problem can propagate into geographically distributed food logistics.

The coincidence warrants investigation, but does not establish a common campaign.

There is also a separate Iranian attribution development: on 12 October, the British government assessed that the suspected September plot involving RAF Fairford was almost certainly directed by Iran’s Revolutionary Guards. That is significant evidence concerning alleged proxy-directed physical operations, but it is not a cyber incident and should not be added to the cyberattack count. Iran denies involvement.  

For the cyber timeline, the most important comparison now spans three environments:

Water infrastructure: unauthorised industrial-control manipulation.

Food refrigeration: unauthorised physical-process manipulation, now independently confirmed in France.

Shared logistics: information-system disruption propagating into essential-goods distribution.

The first two share a technical class of behaviour. The third shares a potential strategic consequence.

Watch result: two new threshold-crossing incidents; one confirmed cyber-to-physical attack; no increase in Iranian cyber-attribution confidence.

The highest-priority unresolved question is whether the August US military commissary refrigeration failures and the October French attack share any technical mechanism. That comparison is now considerably more important than simply counting another ransomware incident.”
