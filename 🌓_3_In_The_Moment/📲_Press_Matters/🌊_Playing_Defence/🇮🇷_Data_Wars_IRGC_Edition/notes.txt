⚖️ 1. United States — Federal bankruptcy courts disrupted

New disclosure: 15 October 2026 | Sector: Government administration and justice

The US federal judiciary has confirmed that a cybersecurity incident affecting BAE Systems has disabled the Bankruptcy Noticing Center, which the contractor operates for federal bankruptcy courts nationwide.

The affected systems produce and distribute official bankruptcy notices. According to the judiciary, their websites and associated noticing processes were unavailable when it issued its statement.

This is a confirmed interruption to a federal judicial function, not merely a contractor reporting that its computers were compromised.

Attribution confidence: The incident and operational disruption are confirmed. The attacker, intrusion method and any data theft remain undisclosed. There is no established Iranian connection, and no evidence that BAE’s defence programmes were affected.

Competing explanations: Criminal intrusion, ransomware, another state-linked actor or a more limited compromise of the contractor’s judicial-service environment all remain possible.

Trend significance: This strengthens the shared-contractor pattern seen since February. A cyberattack on one commercial provider can interrupt a public function across multiple jurisdictions. The particularly important development is that the failure has propagated into the administration of justice.

Sources:  US Courts — official incident statement, 15 October⁠￼;  BleepingComputer — 16 October report⁠￼.  

🧊 2. France — Refrigeration attacks now confirmed at substantial scale

Incident: approximately 11–12 October | New evidence: 13–15 October | Sector: Food infrastructure and operational technology

This is the most significant new cyber-to-physical development.

France’s national cybersecurity authority has confirmed widespread compromises involving several families of internet-connected refrigeration controllers. Its advisory, first issued on 12 October and expanded on 13 October, identifies AK-SM, AK-SC and XWEB equipment.

By 15 October, reporting based on statements from French officials put the affected total at approximately 700 supermarkets.

Reported consequences include refrigeration temperatures reaching 50°C, food destruction, financial losses and fires. At some stores, attackers reportedly manipulated defrost cycles.

This is no longer simply a case of exposed industrial equipment or an unverified attack claim. The operational and physical effects are established.

Attribution confidence: High for controller compromise and physical disruption; unknown for attacker identity. Investigators had not identified a responsible group, and no credible claim of responsibility or ransom demand had emerged.

Competing explanations: Opportunistic exploitation of exposed, outdated controllers is a strong possibility. Deliberate sabotage is also possible. The lack of an extortion demand is interesting but does not establish state sponsorship.

Iran assessment: The activity is compatible with the broad strategic value of disrupting civilian infrastructure during wartime. However, shared incentives and similar technical effects are not evidence of Iranian responsibility.

Trend significance: Since 28 February, the machinery picture has expanded from water and energy into distributed food-storage systems. Hundreds of ordinary commercial premises can collectively constitute an essential-infrastructure problem when the same control technology is compromised.

Reports of similar effects elsewhere in Europe deserve investigation, but they should not yet be counted as one confirmed multinational campaign. Dutch authorities have also cautioned against assuming a connection with the separate supermarket-network incident there.

Sources:  CERT-FR — official refrigeration-controller advisory⁠￼;  SecurityWeek — 15 October investigation⁠￼;  Reuters — investigators and attribution uncertainty⁠￼.  

🏘️ 3. United Kingdom — Housing Ombudsman’s public-service systems disrupted

Disclosure: 13 October; updated 14 October | Sector: Public administration and complaints infrastructure

The Housing Ombudsman Service has confirmed that an incident involving an external IT supplier affected its case-management system.

Its online complaint form, landlord portal and web chat were taken offline. An initial notification also warned that some contact information might have been copied.

The incident was reported as contained, but the extent of any personal-data compromise remained under investigation.

Attribution confidence: High for the supplier-related incident and interruption to online services. No established attacker identity, method or Iranian connection.

Competing explanations: A criminal intrusion into the supplier, unauthorised access to the case-management environment or another supplier security failure remain possible.

Together with the US judicial incident, this is a meaningful concentration of public-administration disruption through outsourced systems. It does not establish common attackers or coordination.

Source:  Housing Ombudsman — official incident statement⁠￼;  original 13 October notification⁠￼.

Earlier incident disclosed: 14 September | New regulatory detail: 13–15 October | Sector: Energy and information infrastructure

Energy delivery intact ≠ customer information secure.

Confirmed Iranian responsibility for today’s newly identified incidents: None.

Credible new Iranian attribution for the French refrigeration, US courts, UK Ombudsman or Texas utility incidents: None established.

Broader wartime relevance: Significant. These developments demonstrate exploitable infrastructure dependencies in countries exposed to Iranian threats. They do not demonstrate that Iran exploited those dependencies.
