# 👾 Cyber War Crimes  
**First created:** 2026-08-07 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-07  
*Cyber operations do not sit outside the laws of war merely because the weapon is code rather than explosives.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

Cyber activity during an armed conflict can raise international humanitarian law questions.

But not every hostile cyber operation is a war crime.

Several separate questions have to be kept apart:

```text
CYBER INCIDENT
≠
CYBERATTACK FOR IHL PURPOSES
≠
UNLAWFUL ATTACK
≠
WAR CRIME
≠
STATE RESPONSIBILITY
≠
INDIVIDUAL CRIMINAL RESPONSIBILITY
```

This node exists to stop those categories collapsing into one another.

The central questions are:

> What did the cyber operation actually do?

> What object or people did it affect?

> Was the operation connected to an armed conflict?

> Was the target civilian, military, or dual-use?

> What harm was intended, expected, or caused?

> Who can the conduct be attributed to?

> And is there enough evidence to establish individual criminal responsibility?

The answer to one question does not automatically answer the next.

---

## ⚔️ Cyber Does Not Create A Law-Free Zone  

The fact that an operation is digital does not make international humanitarian law disappear.

Where the required armed-conflict nexus exists, familiar rules may become relevant, including:

- distinction;
- proportionality;
- precautions in attack;
- protection of civilians;
- protection of civilian objects;
- protection of medical services;
- and special rules concerning objects indispensable to civilian survival.

The difficult part is often not whether IHL matters.

It is how established legal rules apply to cyber operations whose effects may be:

- temporary;
- distributed;
- reversible;
- invisible;
- delayed;
- dependent on another system;
- or difficult to classify as physical damage.

Those questions remain legally contested in some areas.

The pack should therefore distinguish settled principle from disputed application.

---

## 🧱 First Question: What Did The Operation Actually Do?  

The phrase:

> cyberattack

can conceal many different kinds of conduct.

For example:

```text
credential theft
data theft
espionage
website defacement
service disruption
record manipulation
control-system access
command manipulation
physical-process change
physical damage
civilian injury
```

These do not necessarily have the same legal character.

The pack should first establish the technical event.

Only then should legal analysis begin.

---

## 🪜 Technical Depth Matters  

For operational systems, use the existing machinery ladder:

```text
LEVEL 0 — EXTERNAL RECONNAISSANCE
        ↓
LEVEL 1 — IT / ADMINISTRATIVE ACCESS
        ↓
LEVEL 2 — OT NETWORK VISIBILITY
        ↓
LEVEL 3 — HMI / CONTROL INTERFACE ACCESS
        ↓
LEVEL 4 — CONTROLLER OR CONFIGURATION ACCESS
        ↓
LEVEL 5 — COMMAND / SETTING MANIPULATION
        ↓
LEVEL 6 — OBSERVED PHYSICAL-PROCESS CHANGE
        ↓
LEVEL 7 — SAFETY / SERVICE / PHYSICAL HARM
```

Do not infer the legal significance of Level 6 or 7 from evidence that only establishes Level 2 or 3.

Likewise:

```text
access
≠
attack

capability
≠
use

use
≠
civilian harm

civilian harm
≠
war crime
```

---

## 🧍 Civilians Remain Civilians  

The existence of an armed conflict does not remove civilian protection.

A civilian does not become lawfully targetable merely because:

- they live in a belligerent state;
- they use government services;
- their bank supports the national economy;
- their water comes from public infrastructure;
- or their data is strategically useful.

Likewise:

```text
enemy-state citizen
≠
combatant
```

and:

```text
civilian inconvenience
≠
lawful coercive target
```

The civilian character of the person remains the starting point unless the applicable law provides otherwise on the facts.

---

## 🏗️ State Infrastructure Is Not Automatically A Military Objective  

This cluster uses **state infrastructure** functionally.

That is not an IHL target category.

A system may be:

- publicly owned;
- essential to government;
- economically important;
- strategically valuable;
- or relied upon by the armed forces

without automatically becoming a military objective.

The legal question is narrower and fact-specific.

So:

```text
essential to the state
≠
lawfully targetable
```

and:

```text
part of the battlespace
≠
military objective
```

That distinction applies throughout this pack.

---

## 🚰 Water Requires Special Attention  

Civilian drinking-water infrastructure deserves particular scrutiny.

Water may involve:

- treatment;
- pumping;
- pressure control;
- reservoirs;
- desalination;
- wastewater;
- electricity;
- telecommunications;
- and industrial control systems.

A cyber operation may therefore affect water without visibly destroying a plant.

Possible effects include:

- loss of trusted telemetry;
- forced manual operation;
- altered pressure;
- treatment disruption;
- pump manipulation;
- contamination risk;
- loss of service;
- or inability to maintain safe supply.

Objects indispensable to the survival of the civilian population receive particular protection under IHL.

That means serious interference with civilian drinking-water infrastructure during armed conflict should trigger a legal review.

But:

```text
water system compromised
≠
war crime established
```

The remaining elements still have to be examined.

---

## 🏥 Medical Systems Also Carry Particular Protection  

Cyber operations affecting healthcare may raise especially serious questions.

Relevant systems include:

- hospitals;
- medical records;
- diagnostics;
- pharmacies;
- laboratories;
- blood and transplant systems;
- ambulance coordination;
- medical devices;
- and suppliers essential to care.

A hospital does not need to collapse completely for harm to occur.

Cyber interference may produce:

- delayed treatment;
- cancelled care;
- unsafe workarounds;
- unavailable records;
- unreliable medical information;
- or loss of access to equipment.

Medical services receive particular protection during armed conflict.

That makes confirmed cyber operations against them especially important to preserve carefully for legal analysis.

---

## 🧾 Data And Records Create Harder Questions  

Cyber operations do not always destroy objects.

They may:

- copy data;
- delete data;
- alter records;
- corrupt provenance;
- make records unavailable;
- or create uncertainty over which version is authoritative.

The legal treatment of some purely data-focused operations remains more contested than operations producing clear physical damage or injury.

That means the pack should avoid pretending that every serious data breach automatically fits neatly into the legal category of an **attack**.

But the absence of physical destruction does not make the incident strategically or legally irrelevant.

It may affect:

- care;
- liberty;
- legal process;
- identity;
- safeguarding;
- public administration;
- or civilian protection.

The technical facts should be preserved even where the precise legal characterisation remains open.

---

## 🎯 Distinction Still Applies  

Where an operation qualifies as an attack for IHL purposes, the attacker must distinguish between lawful military objectives and civilians or civilian objects.

That means the pack should ask:

```text
WHAT WAS THE TARGET?
WHAT WAS ITS ACTUAL FUNCTION?
WAS IT CIVILIAN?
WAS IT MILITARY?
WAS IT DUAL-USE?
WHAT MILITARY ADVANTAGE WAS EXPECTED?
```

The existence of some military use does not erase civilian consequence.

Shared infrastructure can support both.

That makes accurate description of the system especially important.

---

## ⚖️ Proportionality Still Matters  

Even where a lawful military objective is involved, expected civilian harm matters.

Cyber operations can create indirect consequences.

For example:

```text
electricity disruption
→ water pumping affected
→ hospital backup systems stressed
→ civilian harm increases
```

Or:

```text
banking disruption
→ public payments delayed
→ essential suppliers affected
```

Those dependencies matter because the relevant harm may not occur inside the first compromised system.

The analysis should preserve both:

```text
DIRECT EFFECT
```

and:

```text
FORESEEABLE REVERBERATING EFFECT
```

where evidence permits.

---

## 🛡️ Precautions Matter Too  

A cyber operation may offer technical opportunities to reduce civilian harm.

That can include:

- limiting the systems affected;
- restricting duration;
- avoiding shared civilian infrastructure;
- choosing less harmful methods;
- stopping where unexpected civilian effects appear;
- or designing operations to affect only the intended function.

The existence of cyber precision does not guarantee lawful behaviour.

But where less harmful alternatives were feasible, that may matter to the legal analysis.

---

## 🕸️ Attribution Is A Separate Question  

Even where the conduct appears legally serious, the next problem is:

> Who did it?

That may require separate attribution at several levels:

```text
TECHNICAL OPERATOR
        ↓
ORGANISATION / PROXY
        ↓
STATE LINK
        ↓
STATE DIRECTION OR CONTROL
```

The pack should not jump from:

> Iran-linked actor

to:

> Iran committed a war crime.

Those are different propositions.

Likewise:

```text
Iranian-affiliated operator
≠
Iranian state responsibility
```

without the evidence required to connect the conduct to the state.

---

## 🧅 The Operator May Not Be The Customer  

The cyber-war-crimes question becomes harder where tasking is layered.

A possible chain is:

```text
criminal operator
→ access broker
→ contractor
→ proxy
→ state-linked customer
→ wartime operational task
```

Different people may know different things.

One person may obtain access.

Another may buy it.

Another may select the civilian target.

Another may order the disruptive action.

Another may understand the expected consequences.

That means legal responsibility may sit at different layers.

The fact that the first hacker did not know the ultimate customer does not resolve responsibility higher in the chain.

The fact that a state benefited from the operation does not by itself prove it directed the operation either.

---

## 👤 War Crimes Concern Individual Criminal Responsibility  

A war crime is not simply a label placed on a state.

War-crimes prosecution ultimately concerns criminal responsibility of individuals.

Relevant questions may include:

- who performed the conduct;
- who ordered it;
- who planned it;
- who facilitated it;
- who knew the relevant facts;
- what intent or knowledge existed;
- and what connection existed to the armed conflict.

That means:

```text
state responsibility
≠
individual criminal responsibility
```

A state may bear international responsibility without investigators yet being able to establish a criminal case against a particular person.

The reverse evidentiary problems can also arise.

The pack should keep the two tracks separate.

---

## 🔐 Public Attribution Creates A Practical Catch-22  

This pack is particularly interested in the gap between private knowledge and public attribution.

A government may privately assess with high confidence that a foreign state conducted an operation.

Public attribution may require exposing:

- intelligence sources;
- technical capabilities;
- partner intelligence;
- covert access;
- or an ongoing investigation.

But without public attribution, the state may have less ability to say publicly:

> this foreign state committed an unlawful wartime attack against our civilians.

The result may look like:

```text
strong classified attribution
+
weak public attribution
=
strong internal threat assessment
+
limited public legal leverage
```

That does not change whether unlawful conduct actually occurred.

It changes what can responsibly be demonstrated in public.

---

## 🗣️ Legal Language Must Not Outrun The Evidence  

Reporting should preserve the legal ladder.

Avoid:

> Iran committed a cyber war crime against US water systems.

where the public record establishes only:

- water-system incidents;
- serious operational effects;
- a known historical Iranian interest in similar systems;
- and an unresolved current attribution.

Better:

> Cyber interference with civilian water infrastructure during the armed conflict raises IHL questions. Attribution remains under investigation.

If attribution strengthens:

> Officials have linked the operation to an Iranian-affiliated actor. Public evidence of state direction and individual criminal responsibility remains incomplete.

Legal seriousness does not require premature certainty.

---

## 📰 A Useful Legal Reporting Ladder  

The pack can describe developing cases through:

```text
CYBER INCIDENT CONFIRMED
        ↓
ARMED-CONFLICT NEXUS IDENTIFIED
        ↓
CIVILIAN / PROTECTED OBJECT AFFECTED
        ↓
IHL ISSUE IDENTIFIED
        ↓
POSSIBLE IHL VIOLATION
        ↓
CREDIBLE WAR-CRIME QUESTION
        ↓
STATE RESPONSIBILITY EVIDENCE
        ↓
INDIVIDUAL CRIMINAL RESPONSIBILITY EVIDENCE
```

Not every incident will move all the way down that ladder.

The purpose is to show where the evidence currently sits.

---

## 🚫 What This Node Does Not Claim  

This node does not claim that:

- every cyber operation during war is governed identically;
- every intrusion is an attack for IHL purposes;
- every attack on state infrastructure is unlawful;
- every civilian effect establishes a war crime;
- every cyberattack against water is automatically a war crime;
- every Iran-linked actor is state-directed;
- every unlawful state operation produces individual criminal liability;
- or lack of public attribution means governments know nothing internally.

It argues for keeping the legal questions visible without pretending they are simpler than they are.

---

## 🧭 Working Rule  

The working rule is:

> Treat cyber operations during armed conflict as capable of engaging international humanitarian law, but preserve the distinction between technical incident, attack, unlawful attack, state responsibility, and individual war-crime responsibility.

For every serious case ask:

```text
WHAT HAPPENED?
WHAT WAS AFFECTED?
WHO WAS PROTECTED?
WHAT EFFECT FOLLOWED?
WHAT WAS FORESEEABLE?
WHO OPERATED IT?
WHO DIRECTED IT?
WHAT CAN BE PROVED PUBLICLY?
WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN?
```

Do not erase the legal issue because attribution is incomplete.

Do not turn the legal issue into a verdict because the incident is frightening.

Preserve the ladder.
