# 👾 Cyber War Crimes
**First created:** 2026-08-07 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-14  
*Cyber operations do not sit outside the laws of war merely because the weapon is code rather than explosives.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Cyber activity during an armed conflict can raise international humanitarian law questions.

But not every hostile cyber operation is a war crime.

Several separate questions have to be kept apart:

```text
CYBER INCIDENT
≠
CYBER OPERATION REGULATED BY IHL
≠
CYBERATTACK FOR IHL PURPOSES
≠
IHL VIOLATION
≠
PROSECUTABLE WAR CRIME
```

Two responsibility tracks then branch from the facts:

```text
STATE RESPONSIBILITY
≠
INDIVIDUAL CRIMINAL RESPONSIBILITY
```

There is another distinction that matters:

```text
LAWFUL / UNLAWFUL RESORT TO FORCE
≠
LAWFUL / UNLAWFUL CONDUCT DURING HOSTILITIES
```

The legality of resorting to force and the legality of particular conduct during an armed conflict are separate questions.

This node is principally concerned with the second.

It exists to stop these categories collapsing into one another.

The central questions are:

> What did the cyber operation actually do?

> What object or people did it affect?

> Was the operation connected to an armed conflict?

> Was the target civilian, military, or dual-use?

> What harm was intended, expected, or caused?

> What rules of international humanitarian law were engaged?

> Who can the conduct be attributed to?

> And is there enough evidence to establish individual criminal responsibility?

The answer to one question does not automatically answer the next.

---

## ⚔️ Cyber Does Not Create A Law-Free Zone

The fact that an operation is digital does not make international humanitarian law disappear.

Where the required armed-conflict nexus exists, familiar rules may become relevant, including:

- distinction;
- proportionality;
- precautions in attack;
- protection of civilians;
- protection of civilian objects;
- protection of medical services;
- and special rules concerning objects indispensable to civilian survival.

The difficult part is often not whether IHL matters.

It is how established legal rules apply to cyber operations whose effects may be:

- temporary;
- distributed;
- reversible;
- invisible;
- delayed;
- dependent on another system;
- or difficult to classify as physical damage.

Those questions remain legally contested in some areas.

The pack should therefore distinguish:

```text
SETTLED IHL PRINCIPLE
≠
SETTLED CYBER-SPECIFIC APPLICATION
```

Where states, courts, expert bodies, or humanitarian organisations take different positions on the application of an established rule to cyber operations, the disagreement should remain visible.

## 🧱 First Question: What Did The Operation Actually Do?

The phrase:

> cyberattack

can conceal many different kinds of conduct.

For example:

```text
credential theft
data theft
espionage
website defacement
service disruption
record manipulation
control-system access
command manipulation
physical-process change
physical damage
civilian injury
```

These do not necessarily have the same legal character.

The pack should first establish the technical event.

Only then should legal analysis begin.

## 🪜 Technical Depth Matters

For operational systems, use the existing machinery ladder:

```text
LEVEL 0 — EXTERNAL RECONNAISSANCE
        ↓
LEVEL 1 — IT / ADMINISTRATIVE ACCESS
        ↓
LEVEL 2 — OT NETWORK VISIBILITY
        ↓
LEVEL 3 — HMI / CONTROL INTERFACE ACCESS
        ↓
LEVEL 4 — CONTROLLER OR CONFIGURATION ACCESS
        ↓
LEVEL 5 — COMMAND / SETTING MANIPULATION
        ↓
LEVEL 6 — OBSERVED PHYSICAL-PROCESS CHANGE
        ↓
LEVEL 7 — SAFETY / SERVICE / PHYSICAL HARM
```

Do not infer the legal significance of Level 6 or 7 from evidence that only establishes Level 2 or 3.

Likewise:

```text
access
≠
attack

capability
≠
use

use
≠
civilian harm

civilian harm
≠
IHL violation

IHL violation
≠
war crime
```

The technical depth of an intrusion is evidence.

It is not, by itself, the legal conclusion.

## 🧍 Civilians Remain Civilians

The existence of an armed conflict does not remove civilian protection.

A civilian does not become lawfully targetable merely because:

- they live in a belligerent state;
- they use government services;
- their bank supports the national economy;
- their water comes from public infrastructure;
- their data is strategically useful;
- or they are politically aligned with one side.

Likewise:

```text
enemy-state citizen
≠
combatant
```

and:

```text
civilian status
≠
automatic lawful targetability
```

There is an important qualification.

Civilians are protected against attack **unless and for such time as they directly participate in hostilities**.

Cyber activity can, on the facts, amount to such participation.

That does not mean:

```text
civilian hacker
=
combatant
```

It means the legal analysis must distinguish civilian status from temporary loss of protection against attack arising from particular conduct.

Ordinary use of government services, employment in the civilian economy, political alignment, possession of strategically interesting data, or residence in an enemy state does not by itself remove civilian protection.

---

## 🏗️ State Infrastructure Is Not Automatically A Military Objective

This cluster uses **state infrastructure** functionally.

That is not an IHL target category.

A system may be:

- publicly owned;
- essential to government;
- economically important;
- strategically valuable;
- or relied upon by the armed forces

without automatically becoming a military objective.

The legal question is narrower and fact-specific.

So:

```text
essential to the state
≠
lawfully targetable
```

and:

```text
part of the battlespace
≠
military objective
```

The relevant inquiry concerns the actual function and use of the object and the applicable legal test for a military objective.

That distinction applies throughout this pack.

## 🚰 Water Requires Special Attention

Civilian drinking-water infrastructure deserves particular scrutiny.

Water may involve:

- treatment;
- pumping;
- pressure control;
- reservoirs;
- desalination;
- wastewater;
- electricity;
- telecommunications;
- and industrial control systems.

A cyber operation may therefore affect water without visibly destroying a plant.

Possible effects include:

- loss of trusted telemetry;
- forced manual operation;
- altered pressure;
- treatment disruption;
- pump manipulation;
- contamination risk;
- loss of service;
- or inability to maintain safe supply.

Drinking-water installations and supplies are expressly recognised in IHL as objects indispensable to the survival of the civilian population.

They therefore attract legal protections beyond the ordinary observation that they are civilian infrastructure.

The precise prohibition nevertheless depends on:

- the applicable treaty and customary rules;
- the function and use of the object;
- the purpose and expected effects of the operation;
- and any relevant exceptions or qualifications.

So:

```text
water system compromised
≠
special-protection rule necessarily violated
≠
war crime established
```

But serious interference with civilian drinking-water infrastructure during armed conflict plainly warrants specific IHL review rather than treatment as an ordinary infrastructure outage.

## 📈 Scale Matters, But Scale Does Not Supply The Missing Legal Elements

By late August, CISA had publicly quantified the July campaign at more than **100 internet-exposed water and wastewater systems**.

That is important.

It shows repeated interaction with a protected civilian sector at scale.

But scale does not solve every legal question.

The pack should still distinguish:

```text
100+ SYSTEMS TARGETED
≠
100+ SYSTEMS PHYSICALLY DISRUPTED

100+ SYSTEMS TARGETED
≠
100+ SYSTEMS ATTRIBUTED TO ONE OPERATOR

100+ CIVILIAN SYSTEMS IN THE CAMPAIGN ENVIRONMENT
≠
WAR CRIME PROVED
```

What scale can do is strengthen the need to ask:

- was civilian water infrastructure deliberately selected;
- what effects were intended or expected;
- what effects actually followed;
- whether the actor knew the civilian dependence;
- and whether the pattern formed part of conduct connected to the armed conflict.

Scale changes significance.

It does not remove the rest of the test.

## 🏥 Medical Systems Also Carry Particular Protection

Cyber operations affecting healthcare may raise especially serious questions.

Relevant systems include:

- hospitals;
- medical records;
- diagnostics;
- pharmacies;
- laboratories;
- blood and transplant systems;
- ambulance coordination;
- medical devices and systems used by protected medical services;
- and supply or support infrastructure whose disruption may foreseeably impair medical care.

A hospital does not need to collapse completely for harm to occur.

Cyber interference may produce:

- delayed treatment;
- cancelled care;
- ambulance diversion;
- unsafe workarounds;
- unavailable records;
- unreliable medical information;
- loss of access to equipment;
- or degradation of building-support systems.

Protected medical units, personnel and services receive particular protection during armed conflict.

Other suppliers or supporting infrastructure may remain civilian objects and may be critically important to proportionality and precautions without necessarily sharing the same special legal status.

That distinction should be preserved.

Confirmed cyber operations affecting protected medical services are especially important to record carefully for legal analysis.

protected-sector consequence

## 🧾 Data And Records Create Harder Questions

Cyber operations do not always destroy objects.

They may:

- copy data;
- delete data;
- alter records;
- corrupt provenance;
- make records unavailable;
- or create uncertainty over which version is authoritative.

The legal treatment of some purely data-focused operations remains more contested than operations producing clear physical damage or injury.

Two questions in particular remain contested:

```text
IS CIVILIAN DATA ITSELF AN "OBJECT"
FOR IHL PURPOSES?
```

and:

```text
CAN LOSS OF FUNCTIONALITY WITHOUT PHYSICAL DAMAGE
CONSTITUTE AN "ATTACK"?
```

States and expert bodies have not expressed identical positions on those questions.

Some states have expressly recognised that cyber operations producing effects equivalent or similar to those produced by kinetic attacks may qualify as attacks for IHL purposes.

The precise threshold for operations causing loss of functionality without physical destruction remains less uniformly settled.

That means the pack should avoid pretending that every serious data breach automatically fits neatly into the legal category of an **attack**.

It should also avoid presenting one cyber-specific interpretation as universally settled where material disagreement remains.

But the absence of physical destruction does not make the incident strategically or legally irrelevant.

It may affect:

- care;
- liberty;
- legal process;
- identity;
- safeguarding;
- public administration;
- or civilian protection.

The technical facts should be preserved even where the precise legal characterisation remains open.

## ⚖️ C-Track And Administrative Data Illustrate The Boundary

The C-Track compromise is legally useful precisely because court services remained available.

The public record supports:

- unauthorised access to court files;
- possible exposure of sensitive, sealed, or restricted material;
- continued service availability;
- and no established Iran or wartime nexus.

This should not be upgraded into:

```text
ATTACK ON JUSTICE SYSTEM
=
IHL ATTACK
```

without the missing elements.

The case belongs in the pack as an example of why:

```text
SERIOUS CIVILIAN DATA COMPROMISE
≠
SETTLED CYBER "ATTACK" CLASSIFICATION
```

and why technical, governance and human consequences can still be substantial.

## 🎯 Distinction Still Applies

Where an operation qualifies as an attack for IHL purposes, the attacker must distinguish between lawful military objectives and civilians or civilian objects.

That means the pack should ask:

```text
WHAT WAS THE TARGET?
WHAT WAS ITS ACTUAL FUNCTION?
WAS IT CIVILIAN?
WAS IT MILITARY?
WAS IT DUAL-USE?
WHAT MILITARY ADVANTAGE WAS EXPECTED?
```

The existence of some military use does not erase civilian consequence.

Shared infrastructure can support both.

Nor does describing something as:

> critical infrastructure

answer the legal targeting question.

That makes accurate description of the system, its function, and its use especially important.

## ⚡ The UK Generator Incident Raises A Cleaner Physical-Effect Question

The July cyberattack on a small British power generator, disclosed in August, is legally significant because the public record includes a real physical-service effect.

The generator was offline for four days.

Reporting described the attackers as Iran-linked.

A formal public NCSC attribution naming Iran, the IRGC or a specific operator was not identified in the reviewed record.

That produces:

```text
PHYSICAL OPERATIONAL EFFECT:
🟢 CONFIRMED

CIVILIAN / DUAL-USE STATUS OF THE SPECIFIC FACILITY:
NOT FULLY PUBLIC

IRAN-LINKED ASSESSMENT:
🟠 / 🟡 DEVELOPING

FORMAL PUBLIC STATE ATTRIBUTION:
⚪ NOT IDENTIFIED

INDIVIDUAL CRIMINAL RESPONSIBILITY:
⚪ NOT ESTABLISHED
```

This is a stronger candidate for separate IHL review than a pure data theft because a physical energy function was actually interrupted.

It is still not enough to write:

> Iran committed a cyber war crime against Britain.

The missing elements remain material.

## 📡 Telecommunications Can Produce Reverberating Civilian Effects

Telecommunications sit beneath:

- water;
- energy;
- hospitals;
- emergency services;
- banking;
- ports;
- and government.

A cyber operation against telecoms may therefore produce effects outside the first system compromised.

For example:

```text
TELECOMS DISRUPTION
→ WATER REMOTE CONTROL LOST
→ MANUAL OPERATION REQUIRED
```

or:

```text
TELECOMS DISRUPTION
→ HOSPITAL COMMUNICATION DEGRADED
→ CARE DELAYED
```

or:

```text
TELECOMS DISRUPTION
→ PAYMENT CONNECTIVITY LOST
→ CIVILIAN ACCESS TO MONEY DEGRADED
```

Where the legal framework requires expected civilian harm to be assessed, these dependencies matter.

The September reporting on increased Iran-linked attempts against telecommunications therefore raises the importance of preserving dependency evidence even where no major new outage has yet been attributed.

## ⚖️ Proportionality Still Matters

Even where a lawful military objective is involved, expected civilian harm matters.

Cyber operations can create indirect consequences.

For example:

```text
electricity disruption
→ water pumping affected
→ hospital backup systems stressed
→ civilian harm increases
```

Or:

```text
banking disruption
→ public payments delayed
→ essential suppliers affected
```

Those dependencies matter because the relevant harm may not occur inside the first compromised system.

The analysis should preserve both:

```text
DIRECT EFFECT
```

and:

```text
FORESEEABLE REVERBERATING EFFECT
```

where the applicable legal rule and available evidence permit those consequences to be assessed.

The further the claimed consequence moves down a dependency chain, the more carefully foreseeability and evidence should be recorded.

---

## 🛡️ Precautions Matter Too

A cyber operation may offer technical opportunities to reduce civilian harm.

That can include:

- limiting the systems affected;
- restricting duration;
- avoiding shared civilian infrastructure;
- choosing less harmful methods;
- stopping where unexpected civilian effects appear;
- or designing operations to affect only the intended function.

The existence of cyber precision does not guarantee lawful behaviour.

Nor does the theoretical existence of a more precise cyber technique automatically establish that it was feasible in the circumstances.

But where feasible precautions or less harmful means were available, that may matter to the legal analysis.

The UK generator incident is potentially relevant here.

A small facility being taken offline rather than a national grid can look like limited effect.

But:

```text
LIMITED EFFECT
≠
LAWFUL EFFECT
```

A deliberately limited disruption may reduce expected civilian harm.

It does not by itself resolve targetability, nexus, precautions, or attribution.

---

## 🧭 Demonstrative Disruption Is Still Legally Specific Conduct

Some cyber operations may appear designed to demonstrate access rather than maximise destruction.

That can include:

- short outages;
- small-facility disruption;
- temporary loss of pressure;
- reversible configuration changes;
- or public proof that machinery can be reached.

This pack uses **demonstrative disruption** as an analytical category.

It is not a legal defence.

The legal questions remain:

```text
WHAT WAS TARGETED?
WHAT EFFECT WAS INTENDED?
WHAT EFFECT WAS EXPECTED?
WHAT EFFECT OCCURRED?
WHAT CIVILIAN HARM WAS FORESEEABLE?
WHAT PRECAUTIONS WERE TAKEN?
```

Limited coercion can still be legally significant.

## 🕸️ Attribution Is A Separate Question

Even where the conduct appears legally serious, the next problem is:

> Who did it?

Technical attribution and legal attribution to a state are not the same inquiry.

A useful sequence is:

```text
TECHNICAL OPERATOR
        ↓
ORGANISATION / INTERMEDIARY
        ↓
POSSIBLE STATE CONNECTION
        ↓
APPLICABLE ATTRIBUTION ROUTE
```

Possible legal routes may include conduct by:

```text
STATE ORGAN
OR
ENTITY EXERCISING GOVERNMENTAL AUTHORITY
OR
ACTOR OPERATING ON STATE INSTRUCTIONS
OR
ACTOR UNDER RELEVANT STATE DIRECTION OR CONTROL
OR
CONDUCT ACKNOWLEDGED AND ADOPTED BY THE STATE
OR
OTHER APPLICABLE ATTRIBUTION RULE
```

The exact test depends upon the proposition being established and the applicable law.

The pack should not jump from:

> Iran-linked actor

to:

> Iran committed a war crime.

Those are different propositions.

Likewise:

```text
Iranian-affiliated operator
≠
conduct legally attributable to Iran
```

without the evidence required to establish an applicable route of attribution.

## 📣 Actor Claims Do Not Supply Legal Attribution

The September AT&T episode is useful here.

APT IRAN claimed a real AT&T outage.

AT&T attributed the outage to attempted physical cable theft and rejected the cyber explanation.

That gives a simple legal-evidentiary rule:

```text
ACTOR CLAIM
≠
CAUSATION

CAUSATION
≠
STATE ATTRIBUTION

STATE ATTRIBUTION
≠
WAR-CRIME RESPONSIBILITY
```

A dramatic actor claim should therefore never be allowed to jump several legal stages at once.

## 🧅 The Operator May Not Be The Customer

The cyber-war-crimes question becomes harder where tasking is layered.

A possible chain is:

```text
criminal operator
→ access broker
→ contractor
→ proxy
→ state-linked customer
→ wartime operational task
```

Different people may know different things.

One person may obtain access.

Another may buy it.

Another may select the civilian target.

Another may order the disruptive action.

Another may understand the expected consequences.

That means legal responsibility may sit at different layers.

The fact that the first hacker did not know the ultimate customer does not resolve responsibility higher in the chain.

The fact that a state benefited from the operation does not by itself prove the conduct is legally attributable to that state either.

The pack should therefore preserve:

```text
OPERATOR
≠
CUSTOMER
≠
BENEFICIARY
≠
LEGAL ATTRIBUTION
```

unless the evidence actually joins those layers.

## 🤖 AI-Assisted Access Does Not Change The Legal Elements

AI can change scale.

It can lower the cost of:

- discovering targets;
- generating scripts;
- sorting exposed systems;
- and repeating exploitation attempts.

That can increase:

- the number of civilian systems encountered;
- the number of operations requiring review;
- and the speed at which harmful effects can occur.

But AI does not collapse the legal test.

```text
AI-ASSISTED TARGETING
≠
AUTOMATIC STATE DIRECTION

AI-ASSISTED EXPLOITATION
≠
AUTOMATIC IHL ATTACK

AUTOMATION
≠
ABSENCE OF HUMAN RESPONSIBILITY
```

Where individual criminal responsibility is later considered, the relevant questions still concern:

- who designed;
- who deployed;
- who authorised;
- who selected;
- who knew;
- who intended;
- and which mode of liability applies.

Automation can complicate proof.

It does not remove the need for it.

## ⚓ Reconnaissance Is Not The Same As Attack

Anthropic's September disclosure concerning an Iran-nexus actor researching:

- US naval movements;
- personnel;
- maritime VSAT;
- communications equipment;
- and shipboard industrial-control products

is strategically important.

But no successful exploitation or operational effect was publicly established.

That places the public evidence at:

```text
RECONNAISSANCE / CAPABILITY DEVELOPMENT
```

not:

```text
ATTACK / PHYSICAL EFFECT
```

Espionage, reconnaissance and preparation can be relevant to armed-conflict analysis.

They should not be silently converted into completed attack conduct.

## 👤 War Crimes Concern Individual Criminal Responsibility

A war crime is not simply a label placed on a state.

War-crimes prosecution ultimately concerns criminal responsibility of individuals.

Relevant questions may include:

- who performed the conduct;
- who ordered it;
- who planned it;
- who facilitated or otherwise contributed to it;
- who knew the relevant facts;
- what intent or knowledge existed;
- what mode of liability applies;
- and what connection existed to the armed conflict.

That means:

```text
state responsibility
≠
individual criminal responsibility
```

It also means:

```text
IHL VIOLATION
≠
WAR CRIME
```

Not every violation of international humanitarian law is a war crime.

Individual criminal responsibility requires conduct falling within an applicable war-crime prohibition together with the required armed-conflict nexus, material elements, mental elements, and applicable mode of liability under the relevant criminal-law framework.

A state may bear international responsibility without investigators yet being able to establish a criminal case against a particular person.

An individual may also potentially incur criminal responsibility for conduct that is not legally attributable to a state.

The two tracks may overlap.

They do not depend upon one another in sequence.

The International Criminal Court's **Elements of Crimes** remains a useful discipline here: criminal responsibility requires the relevant jurisdictional, material and mental elements, not merely a serious incident or a persuasive geopolitical narrative.

That produces a longer evidentiary chain:

```text
ACCESS
→ OPERATION
→ ACTUAL OR EXPECTED EFFECT
→ ARMED-CONFLICT NEXUS
→ PROTECTED PERSON OR OBJECT
→ APPLICABLE RULE
→ BREACH
→ RESPONSIBLE ACTOR
→ REQUIRED MENTAL ELEMENT
→ MODE OF LIABILITY
→ JURISDICTION
→ PROSECUTABLE WAR-CRIME CASE
```

Every arrow needs evidence.

A failure at one stage does not make the underlying harm unreal.

It limits the legal conclusion that can presently be sustained.

## 🚨 What Would Materially Strengthen A War-Crime Assessment

The assessment would materially strengthen where credible evidence establishes:

- deliberate selection of protected civilian water or medical infrastructure;
- an armed-conflict nexus;
- intended or expected harmful effects on civilians;
- actual physical or service effects relevant to an applicable prohibition;
- lack of lawful military-objective status;
- disregard of feasible precautions;
- clearly excessive expected civilian harm relative to anticipated military advantage where proportionality applies;
- a special-protection rule being engaged and violated;
- a reliable technical operator attribution;
- a legally sufficient route of attribution to a state where state responsibility is alleged;
- identity of responsible individuals;
- evidence of order, planning, contribution, knowledge or intent;
- and jurisdiction under an applicable criminal-law framework.

The important point is that these elements accumulate.

No single dramatic fact substitutes for the others.

## 🚫 What This Node Does Not Claim

This node does not claim that:

- every hostile cyber operation in wartime is regulated identically;
- every cyber intrusion qualifies as an IHL attack;
- every civilian system affected during war was deliberately targeted;
- every water incident is a war crime;
- every medical disruption is a war crime;
- every loss of functionality without physical damage is universally accepted as an IHL attack;
- every strategically important civilian system is a military objective;
- every Iran-linked operator's conduct is legally attributable to Iran;
- every state-attributable IHL violation creates individual criminal liability;
- every actor claim proves causation;
- every AI-assisted operation is state-directed;
- every reconnaissance operation is an attack;
- or the present public record establishes a prosecutable cyber-war-crimes case across the Iran-linked infrastructure wave.

It argues something narrower and more useful:

> **Cyber operations can engage the laws of armed conflict, including rules protecting civilians, civilian objects, medical services, and objects indispensable to survival—but the legal conclusion has to be built proposition by proposition.**

---

## 🧭 Working Rule

The working rule is:

> Do not use “war crime” as a synonym for “serious wartime cyber incident.”

Start with the technical facts.

Then ask:

```text
IS IHL ENGAGED?
↓
WHAT WAS THE OBJECT?
↓
WHAT WAS ITS STATUS?
↓
WHAT DID THE OPERATION DO?
↓
WHAT HARM WAS EXPECTED OR CAUSED?
↓
WHAT RULE APPLIES?
↓
WAS IT BREACHED?
↓
WHO IS RESPONSIBLE?
↓
WHAT MENTAL ELEMENT EXISTS?
↓
WHAT MODE OF LIABILITY APPLIES?
↓
IS THERE A PROSECUTABLE CASE?
```

Keep:

```text
CIVILIAN INFRASTRUCTURE AFFECTED
≠
WAR CRIME CONFIRMED
```

and:

```text
IRAN-LINKED OPERATOR
≠
IRAN DIRECTED THIS OPERATION
```

and:

```text
STATE RESPONSIBILITY
≠
INDIVIDUAL CRIMINAL RESPONSIBILITY
```

visible all the way through.

That is how the node stays legally useful.
