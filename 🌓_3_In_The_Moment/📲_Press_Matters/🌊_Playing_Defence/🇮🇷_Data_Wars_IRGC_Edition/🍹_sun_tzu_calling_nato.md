# 🍹 Sun Tzu Calling NATO

**First created:** 2026-09-26 \| **Last updated:** 2026-09-26\
*On strategic time, conserved capacity, changing conditions,
cross-cultural depth on the bench, and the unfortunate discovery that
"DO SOMETHING" is not actually a strategy.*

## 🛰️ Orientation

This node began with a relatively simple question about prolonged war.

Then Sun Tzu arrived.

Then Robert Greene took us into the desert.

Then Mao turned up.

Then *wu wei* turned out to have specialists arguing about specialists.

Then the *I Ching* got in the car.

Then Napoleon invaded Russia.

Then Frederick the Great refused to die on schedule.

Then Bernard Montgomery had a problem with some bridges.

Then Franklin Roosevelt lent his neighbour a garden hose.

Then Dwight Eisenhower appeared to ask whether perhaps we could stop
spending the entire future on the present.

At which point it became apparent that the actual subject was not merely
**patience**.

It was **time**.

More specifically:

> **What happens when time itself becomes part of the strategic
> environment?**

This matters because contemporary Western security discussion can become
trapped inside a brutally thin binary:

``` text
THING HAPPENED
      ↓
DO SOMETHING
      ↓
VISIBLE ACTION = STRENGTH
```

The inverse then becomes:

``` text
NO IMMEDIATE VISIBLE ACTION = WEAKNESS
```

This is an extremely poor model of strategy.

A state, army, movement, company, civilian coalition or individual can
be doing a great deal while apparently doing very little. It can be
gathering information, conserving resources, increasing productive
capacity, rebuilding capability, allowing an opponent to overextend,
preserving optionality, waiting for political conditions to change,
refusing an unfavourable engagement, strengthening a coalition, or
simply remaining alive long enough for contingency to operate.

Conversely, a system can be moving very visibly while degrading its own
position.

It can gain territory while losing logistical viability.

It can answer every provocation while exhausting its incident-response
capacity.

It can demonstrate military capability while consuming the economic base
required to sustain that capability.

It can refuse negotiation while assuming that refusal somehow stops
time.

It can win the argument today while making itself less capable of
winning anything tomorrow.

So this is not a node arguing that **inaction is good**.

It is not a node arguing that **retreat is good**.

It is not a node arguing that **China has secret ancient wisdom**, that
**Iran is secretly responsible for every inconvenient cyber incident**,
or that **America has somehow misplaced every strategist it has ever
trained**.

It is a node about acquiring enough **depth on the bench** to ask a
better question:

> **What does this action --- or deliberate non-action --- do to our
> relative position over time?**

And, for the current transatlantic defence environment:

> **How expensive are we making it for an adversary to exhaust us?**

`☎️ NATO?`

`Sun Tzu is on line one.`

`Apparently he has been trying to reach you for approximately 2,500 years.`

## 1. 📖 Unfortunately, Sun Tzu Already Sent The Memo

Chapter II of *The Art of War*, usually translated as **Waging War**, is
not primarily a celebration of battlefield cleverness.

It is an account of expenditure.

The translation being read alongside this node gives the sequence
bluntly:

> "When you engage in actual fighting, if victory is long in coming,
> then men's weapons will grow dull and their armor will be damped."

Then:

> "If you lay siege to a town, you will exhaust your strength."

Then the system expands beyond the army:

> "Again, if the campaign is protracted, the resources of the state will
> not be equal to the strain."

And then comes the bit that matters enormously for contemporary
information and cyber environments:

> "Now, when your weapons are dulled, your armor damped, your strength
> exhausted, and your treasure spent, other chieftains will spring up to
> take advantage of your extremity."

Notice what this does **not** require.

The other chieftains do not need to have caused every component of the
exhaustion.

They need to recognise it.

That is a very different threat model.

Sun Tzu continues:

> "Thus, though we have heard of stupid haste in war, cleverness has
> never been associated with long delays."

And:

> "There is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged
> warfare."

And finally:

> "In war, then, let your great object be victory, not lengthy
> campaigns."

The useful thing here is not to turn an ancient text into a magic
instruction manual for NATO.

It is to notice the **system** Sun Tzu is modelling.

``` text
army
  ↓
weapons + armour + transport
  ↓
food + fodder + replacement
  ↓
distance
  ↓
expenditure
  ↓
prices
  ↓
taxation / exaction
  ↓
households
  ↓
state capacity
  ↓
future military capacity
```

Time runs through the whole thing.

A day is not merely a unit on a calendar.

A day consumes food.

A day wears equipment.

A day requires pay.

A day creates opportunities for illness, desertion, mechanical failure,
political disagreement and enemy adaptation.

A day can also create opportunities for learning, mobilisation,
reinforcement, diplomacy and recovery.

So the first principle of the node is:

> **Time is not neutral duration. Systems continue changing while time
> passes.**

This is why individual brilliance cannot rescue a sufficiently degraded
system indefinitely.

You can have the cleverest general alive.

If the horses are dead, the food is gone, the treasury is empty, the
soldiers are exhausted, the roads are unusable and somebody else has
noticed, the general has acquired a systems problem.

`Sun Tzu: “I did mention this.”`

`NATO: “Yes, but have you considered that our PowerPoint is extremely persuasive?”`

## 2. 🔋 Power You Haven't Spent Yet Is Still Power

One way this becomes culturally legible in familiar Western language is
through the language of **energy**.

There is a reason people reach for the physics metaphor.

Stored capacity does not cease to exist merely because it is not
currently being expressed as motion.

Strictly speaking, the useful physics analogy here is **potential
energy**, not "stored kinetic energy": kinetic energy belongs to motion;
potential energy belongs to position or configuration.

The strategic analogy is imperfect, but useful:

``` text
capacity ≠ expenditure
power ≠ visible activity
readiness ≠ constant deployment
restraint ≠ incapacity
stored potential ≠ failure to act
```

This also lets us separate three ideas that ordinary language tends to
collapse into **patience**.

### Patience as ethics

I should govern my impulses.

I should not lash out because I am angry.

I should tolerate frustration.

I should demonstrate self-command.

This is a value judgement about the actor.

### Conservation as capacity management

I possess finite resources.

I should not spend them merely because I can.

The relevant question becomes:

> **What return am I getting for this expenditure?**

### Time as strategy

Now the question changes again.

I may deliberately allow duration because duration changes the
environment.

My opponent may spend.

My coalition may strengthen.

New information may appear.

A political leader may fall.

A supply problem may worsen.

A new technology may arrive.

A third party may intervene.

Public interpretation may change.

My own productive capacity may grow.

An option that does not exist today may exist later.

This is not merely:

> **Be patient.**

It is:

> **Understand what time is doing.**

And sometimes:

> **Instrumentalise it.**

## 3. 🏜️ Robert Greene Has Entered The Desert

Robert Greene's Strategy 11 in *The 33 Strategies of War* is called
**Trade Space for Time: The Non-Engagement Strategy**.

The opening formulation is useful because it makes several mechanisms
explicit at once:

> "Retreating in the face of a strong enemy is a sign not of weakness
> but of strength."

Greene then tells the reader to resist the temptation to respond
immediately, to buy time to recover and think, to allow an opponent to
advance, and to recognise that **time can matter more than space**.

His argument is not simply:

``` text
RETREAT = GOOD
```

It is conditional.

The stronger enemy advances.

The weaker actor declines decisive engagement.

The stronger actor may interpret this as weakness.

Advance continues.

Distance and time increase costs.

Confidence may increase at precisely the moment constraints worsen.

The advancing system begins producing errors.

The relative positions change.

This gives us five distinct mechanisms:

1.  **Recovery** --- non-engagement preserves or restores your capacity.
2.  **Information** --- time gives you observations you did not possess
    at the start.
3.  **Logistics** --- movement can extend the opponent's dependencies.
4.  **Psychology** --- apparent weakness may encourage overconfidence.
5.  **Error production** --- a system operating for longer has more
    opportunities to reveal its assumptions and fail.

Greene's image for the strategy is the desert.

There is little to feed on.

Little to capture.

Little to convert into military advantage.

The opponent can enter.

But entering costs something.

That gives us an important distinction:

> **Useless space can have strategic value when occupying it imposes
> cost without providing compensating value.**

`🇺🇸: “DO SOMETHING.”`

`Greene: “Why?”`

`🇺🇸: “BECAUSE SOMETHING HAPPENED.”`

`Greene: “This does not answer my question.”`

`🇮🇷: 🍹😎`

## 4. 🧘 Self-Possession Is Not Paralysis

Greene attaches to this strategy a line attributed in his text to Sun
Tzu:

> "To remain disciplined and calm whilst waiting for disorder to appear
> amongst the enemy is the art of self-possession."

The word **waiting** can be misleading if we imagine somebody sitting in
a chair hoping history sorts itself out.

Strategic waiting can include:

-   observation;
-   preparation;
-   hardening;
-   rebuilding;
-   diplomacy;
-   training;
-   information collection;
-   coalition maintenance;
-   deception;
-   reserve creation;
-   contingency planning;
-   selective engagement;
-   refusal to reveal capabilities;
-   refusal to accept the opponent's preferred tempo.

The absence of a spectacular outward action does not establish the
absence of strategic activity.

This matters politically because visible action is easy to communicate.

It photographs well.

It produces headlines.

It creates an immediate sensation of agency.

But **agency and activity are not synonyms**.

Sometimes the most important act of agency is refusing to let somebody
else determine when, where and how you expend your capacity.

`🇮🇷: 🍹😎 *self-possesses harder*`

## 5. 🪷 Sometimes You Can Accomplish Most By Doing Nothing

At this point the discussion bumps into **wu wei** (無為).

And immediately requires us to stop being annoying.

*Wu wei* is not simply:

> **Chinese for strategic patience.**

It is an ancient philosophical concept with long and contested histories
of interpretation. It is commonly translated through phrases such as
**non-action**, **non-doing**, **effortless action**, **non-coercive
action** or **non-assertive action**.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes explicitly that *wu wei*
does **not** mean total inactivity. In discussion of the *Laozi*, it can
contrast with action driven by self-serving desire and is embedded in a
much larger philosophical account of naturalness, desire, government and
conduct.

That is already useful because it interrupts a crude binary:

``` text
action / inaction
```

and gives us room for:

``` text
forced action
unforced action
intervention
non-intervention
activity
non-engagement
efficacy
expenditure
```

The sinologist Jean François Billeter has been quoted describing *wu
wei* through the coexistence of situation and perceiver, efficacy, and a
**"perfect economy of energy."**

That phrase is extraordinarily useful for this node.

Not because NATO needs to become Daoist.

Not because Beijing is secretly running a *Daodejing* macro.

But because **economy of energy** gives us another angle on the same
object.

The question stops being:

> **How much can I do?**

and becomes:

> **How much do I need to do to produce the relevant effect?**

That is a very different theory of power.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Situation + perceiver

The other part of Billeter's formulation matters just as much as the
economy-of-energy line.

He describes a relationship between the **situation and the perceiver**.

That is immediately recognisable from Embodied Information Ecology.

The observer is not standing outside the system with perfect
information.

The observer is:

-   somewhere;
-   at a particular time;
-   carrying prior knowledge;
-   using a particular model;
-   seeing some signals and not others;
-   interpreting those signals through a cultural and institutional
    vocabulary.

So strategic efficacy is not merely:

``` text
choose correct action
```

It is also:

``` text
perceive situation
      ↓
construct model
      ↓
decide what counts as relevant information
      ↓
estimate effect
      ↓
choose expenditure
```

This gives us three useful pieces from the formulation:

**Situation + perceiver**\
→ the observer is situated inside the information environment.

**Efficacy**\
→ judge the action by the effect it produces, not by how dramatic the
action looks.

**Economy of energy**\
→ expenditure is itself part of the strategic calculation.

That is much closer to the thing this node is trying to teach than a
generic injunction to **be patient**.

A system with a bad model can expend enormous energy very efficiently on
the wrong problem.

## 6. ☯️ Unfortunately, The Specialists Have Specialists

Even *wu wei* refuses to behave nicely enough for a one-paragraph
strategy book.

Scholars distinguish different uses and traditions.

H. G. Creel famously distinguished, among other things, between two very
different uses of *wu wei*.

In the formulation we have been working from, one is:

> "An attitude of genuine non-action, motivated by a lack of desire to
> participate in human affairs"

and the other:

> "A technique by means of which the one who practices it may gain
> enhanced control of human affairs."

Same famous term.

Substantially different theory of what the actor is doing.

That is exactly the point.

`THERE ARE SPECIALISTS IN SPECIALISMS REGARDING THIS ONE CONCEPT.`

The important point for us is not to resolve several millennia of
scholarship before lunch.

It is the opposite.

> **There are specialists in specialisms regarding this one concept.**

This is evidence against flattening.

If one famous idea concerning action and non-action already requires
distinctions between texts, periods, purposes and schools, then:

``` text
ANCIENT CHINESE WISDOM SAYS—
```

is probably not going to cut it.

Nor is:

``` text
I Ching → Daoism → Sun Tzu → Mao → Xi Jinping
```

🚨 **NOPE NOPE NOPE** 🚨

Different texts.

Different periods.

Different political environments.

Different interpretative traditions.

Different institutions.

Different arguments.

Different purposes.

The value of cross-cultural study is not that it gives us one enormous
thing called **Chinese Thought**.

It is that it increases the number of distinctions available to us.

The *I Ching* is here.

He is assembling material about:

-   timing;
-   reversal;
-   changing conditions;
-   apparent weakness;
-   non-forcing;
-   preservation;
-   indirect efficacy;
-   the danger of assuming the present configuration is permanent.

One of Greene's most useful formulations in the chapter is:

> "our immersion in the present deprives us of true perspective."

That is almost an information-ecology problem.

The observer is **inside time**.

The observer cannot possess the later information yet.

A configuration that looks permanent now may be revealed later as
transitional.

An advance may later be legible as overextension.

A retreat may later be legible as preservation.

Greene is therefore doing something useful and something dangerous at

Useful:

> cross-shelf retrieval.

Dangerous:

> compressing very different traditions into one portable strategic

So we should reverse-engineer him.

``` text
claim
  ↓
historical example
  ↓
mechanism
  ↓
hidden assumptions
  ↓
failure conditions
  ↓
counterexample
  ↓
comparison with another tradition
```

That is a much better way to read strategy books than simply collecting
quotations that make us feel clever.

## 8. 📚 Where Did The West Put This Idea?

There is another interesting problem here.

Western traditions obviously contain restraint, strategic withdrawal,
indirect action, patience, deception, conservation and the use of time.

The issue is not **idea availability**.

It may sometimes be **idea legibility**.

Comparable knowledge can be stored across:

-   military history;
-   Stoicism;
-   scepticism;
-   Christian quietism;
-   diplomacy;
-   court politics;
-   political satire;
-   folklore;
-   resistance literature;
-   mysticism;
-   women's writing;
-   domestic knowledge;
-   novels.

Some of the most sophisticated knowledge about surviving asymmetrical
power is not necessarily filed under:

``` text
SERIOUS STRATEGIC THEORY
FOR SERIOUS MEN
```

Which brings us, inevitably, to Lady Catherine de Bourgh.

Elizabeth Bennet does not acquire greater formal power and smash through
Lady Catherine.

She refuses to provide the categorical information Lady Catherine wants.

Lady Catherine expends effort.

Lady Catherine reveals information.

Lady Catherine draws conclusions.

Lady Catherine transports Elizabeth's strategically ambiguous non-answer
to Darcy.

Darcy possesses different contextual information.

The same observation therefore has different informational value.

Lady Catherine accidentally helps alter the environment in favour of the
outcome she was trying to prevent.

This is **not wu wei**.

We are not retroactively Daoism-ing Jane Austen.

But another intellectual vocabulary can help us notice something Western
readers already recognise perfectly well when Elizabeth fucking does it.

``` text
Elizabeth:
does less
→ reveals less
→ preserves optionality
→ allows stronger actor to expend effort
→ lets stronger actor alter information environment
```

And Western classification says:

`📚 Romance. Ladies.`

😭

Cultures do not merely differ in whether they possess an idea.

They differ in:

-   where they store it;
-   what they call it;
-   who is permitted to articulate it;
-   what kind of authority it receives;
-   whether it is classified as philosophy, strategy, religion,
    literature, folklore, domestic knowledge or common sense.

That is not a side issue.

That is information architecture.

## 9. 🪑 Depth On The Bench

This gives us the concept we have been circling for months:

> **Depth on the bench.**

The objective of intellectual diversification is not:

``` text
WESTERN MODEL ❌
CHINESE MODEL ✅
```

It is:

> **What becomes visible when I rotate the object?**

The same restraint can be read as:

**Virtue**\
→ I govern my impulses.

**Energy management**\
→ I conserve finite capacity.

**Pragmatism**\
→ forcing the situation will not improve the result.

**Systems analysis**\
→ conditions are changing while I remain still.

**Information gathering**\
→ waiting produces observations unavailable to me now.

**Option preservation**\
→ acting now closes futures I may later need.

**Strategy**\
→ duration changes the relative position of the actors.

None of these necessarily cancels the others.

They give the analyst more positions from which to inspect the same
decision.

A thin conceptual bench gives policymakers:

``` text
DO SOMETHING
      or
DO NOTHING
```

A deeper bench gives:

``` text
intervene
signal
observe
delay
conserve
prepare
negotiate
withdraw
delegate
harden
gather information
create optionality
allow conditions to develop
establish a trigger for later action
```

That is why cultural and intellectual literacy matters even when you do
not agree with the tradition being studied.

> **You don't diversify intellectual traditions so that you can replace
> one set of answers with another. You diversify them so that when
> conditions change, you possess more than one way of constructing the
> question.**

## 10. 🪭 Ideas Travel. They Do Not Arrive Unchanged.

There is another reason not to turn *wu wei*, the *I Ching*, Sun Tzu or
anything else into a sealed box marked:

``` text
CHINESE CULTURE
```

Cultures are porous.

Ideas move through:

-   empire;
-   conquest;
-   diplomacy;
-   pilgrimage;
-   migration;
-   education;
-   religion;
-   trade;
-   translation;
-   bureaucracy;
-   marriage;
-   literature;
-   art;
-   war;
-   deliberate rejection;
-   local reinvention.

Historically, Chinese writing, political institutions, Confucian
traditions, Buddhist traditions and other intellectual and artistic
forms travelled extensively across East Asia.

But **transmission is not duplication**.

Korean polities did not become China because they received ideas from
China.

Japan did not become China.

Vietnam did not become China.

Ideas were translated, selected, contested, hybridised, rejected,
revived and fitted to local institutions and histories.

Korea is a particularly useful illustration because the traffic was not
one-way. Chinese and other traditions moved into the Korean peninsula;
Korean scholars, monks, diplomats, artisans and political institutions
developed their own forms; Korea in turn played an important role in
transmitting technologies and ideas to Japan.

That is what cultural exchange actually looks like.

``` text
idea
 ↓
movement
 ↓
translation
 ↓
local conditions
 ↓
reinterpretation
 ↓
new tradition
```

Not:

``` text
CHINA
  ↓
COPY
  ↓
REST OF ASIA
```

This matters strategically.

South Korea looks at China through South Korean history.

Japan looks at China through Japanese history.

Vietnam looks at China through Vietnamese history.

They also look at the United States, NATO, empire, occupation,
sovereignty, deterrence, trade and technological dependence through
their own historical environments.

Those perspectives overlap.

They are not interchangeable.

So cultural literacy about China is not useful only because:

> **we need to predict China.**

It is useful because the intellectual and historical environment around
China is part of the information ecology through which **allies,
partners, competitors and neighbouring states interpret one another**.

If your model contains only:

``` text
UNITED STATES
        ↕
      CHINA
```

you have already thrown away a large amount of the system.

Because the allied system is not merely watching China.

It is also watching **what America does about China**.

And China is watching what American allies do in response to America.

A better minimum model looks more like:

``` text
             🇨🇳 China
             ↕     ↖
             ↕       ↖
      🇺🇸 US / NATO ↔ 🇯🇵 🇰🇷 allies
```

Every actor observes the other two.

Every action therefore produces information for more than its intended
recipient.

An American semiconductor restriction is information to China.

It is also information to Japan.

South Korea.

Taiwan.

European governments and firms.

Global South governments.

Technology companies.

Investors.

Researchers.

Everybody updates their model.

This is Embodied Information Ecology at diplomatic scale:

> **the same action has different informational value to different
> observers because they receive it from different positions, with
> different histories, incentives, dependencies and prior information.**

So alliance management is not merely getting allies to support a policy.

It is understanding what the policy itself teaches them about you.

And one action can communicate different things to several audiences at
once.

A deterrent signal intended for an adversary might be received as:

``` text
adversary:
“THEY ARE SERIOUS.”
```

while one ally receives:

``` text
“GOOD. THEY ARE SERIOUS.”
```

and another receives:

``` text
“OH FUCK. THEY ARE ESCALATING.”
```

A domestic audience may receive:

``` text
“FINALLY SOMEONE DID SOMETHING.”
```

while a market receives:

``` text
“SUPPLY-CHAIN RISK JUST CHANGED.”
```

None of those observers has received a different physical act.

They have received different **information** from the same act because
they occupy different positions in the system.

This matters directly for AI policy.

Export controls, compute restrictions, semiconductor agreements,
model-access rules, safety standards and security partnerships are never
signals to only one recipient.

They are simultaneously observed by allies, competitors, companies,
researchers, investors and third states.

Strategic communication therefore cannot be modelled as:

``` text
sender → recipient
```

It is closer to:

``` text
                 → adversary
                /
sender → action → ally A
                \→ ally B
                 → domestic public
                 → firms / markets
                 → third states
```

Every observer updates a different model.

### 🗾 This is already an AI diplomacy problem

This is no longer hypothetical.

In July 2026, Japan's Ministry of Foreign Affairs literally titled its
policy page **AI Diplomacy**.

Japan frames international AI cooperation around a **safe, secure and
trustworthy AI** ecosystem, the Hiroshima AI Process, cooperation with
allies and like-minded states, and engagement with the Global South.

China's 2026 public AI-governance position uses a partly different
diplomatic vocabulary.

Chinese official statements emphasise:

-   multilateralism;
-   the role of the United Nations;
-   sovereign equality;
-   development alongside security;
-   capacity-building for the Global South;
-   openness and inclusion;
-   cultural and civilisational diversity;
-   international coordination on governance rules and technical
    standards.

Those are **official negotiating positions**.

They are not proof that every Chinese institution interprets every
principle identically.

They are not proof that stated principles and state behaviour will
always coincide.

And they should not be lazily reverse-engineered from Daoism,
Confucianism or the *I Ching*.

Nor does cultural literacy require accepting any state's preferred
description of itself as objective truth.

``` text
self-description
      ↓
information
```

Not:

``` text
self-description
      ↓
objective truth
```

That rule applies symmetrically.

American language about **openness** is information about American
self-framing.

European language about **rights-based governance** is information about
European self-framing.

Chinese language about **sovereignty**, **multilateralism** or
**civilisational diversity** is information about Chinese self-framing.

Then we compare:

``` text
stated principle
      ↕
institution
      ↕
incentive
      ↕
behaviour
```

Understanding the model somebody is presenting is necessary for
diplomacy.

Believing every claim inside that model is not.

But Western diplomats, defence institutions and technology companies
need to understand the conceptual and historical environments in which
words such as:

``` text
sovereignty
trust
security
openness
control
development
fairness
multilateralism
standards
civilisation
```

are being used.

Two delegations can use the same English noun while carrying different
models of what the noun requires.

And there is a still deeper problem:

> **Shared vocabulary does not guarantee shared ontology.**

Two delegations can agree that:

``` text
AI MUST BE SAFE
```

Wonderful.

Now ask:

-   What counts as an AI system?
-   What counts as harm?
-   Whose safety?
-   Safety from whom?
-   Individual safety or collective safety?
-   Physical, economic, informational or political harm?
-   Immediate harm or systemic harm?
-   Who has authority to determine that a threshold has been crossed?
-   What evidence is sufficient?
-   Who bears the cost of precaution?
-   Who bears the cost of non-intervention?

Now we are negotiating.

Translation may establish a shared word without establishing a shared
model of the thing the word refers to.

``` text
shared vocabulary
        ≠
shared ontology
```

That is an information problem before it becomes a treaty problem.

And AI makes the cost of getting it wrong considerably larger.

AI systems cross borders.

Models are trained on culturally heterogeneous information.

Compute and semiconductor supply chains cross jurisdictions.

Safety standards affect firms operating internationally.

Open-source ecosystems complicate territorial control.

AI agents can act across networks faster than diplomatic institutions
normally move.

Questions about data, copyright, privacy, labour, defence,
infrastructure, autonomy, censorship, human rights, national security
and economic development arrive in the same fucking meeting.

So **AI diplomacy needs depth on the bench**.

Not because every negotiator needs a doctorate in Chinese philosophy.

Because an international governance system that cannot recognise when
different actors are using different conceptual models will repeatedly
mistake:

``` text
translation
```

for:

``` text
agreement
```

or:

``` text
difference
```

for:

``` text
hostility
```

The technical problem and the diplomatic problem are already entangled.

## 11. 🇨🇳 If China Is Such A Fucking Big Deal, Read About China

This is where the argument needs precision.

The United States does **not** lack China expertise.

The Pentagon currently maintains dedicated policy machinery for China,

## 13. 🥾 Mao, The Encirclement, And The Annoying Persistence Of The Board

The more specific Mao example matters because it gives us something the
generic phrase **protracted war** does not.

The Chinese Communist movement itself changed strategy under conditions
of internal political struggle, military pressure and near-disaster.

During the early 1930s, Mao had lost much of his influence within the
Communist leadership while leaders associated with the Soviet-trained
group often called the **28 Bolsheviks** became influential.

At the same time, Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist government intensified
its encirclement campaigns against Communist base areas.

The Fifth Encirclement Campaign placed the Jiangxi Soviet under enormous
pressure.

The eventual Communist breakout in 1934 began the Long March.

This should not be turned into:

> **Mao cleverly decided to go for a very long walk and therefore won
> China.**

Absolutely fucking not.

The history contains:

-   catastrophic losses;
-   internal Communist political struggle;
-   changing military leadership;
-   Nationalist decisions;
-   Japanese invasion;
-   foreign assistance;
-   geography;
-   coercion;
-   mobilisation;
-   ideology;
-   logistics;
-   changing international conditions;
-   years of subsequent war.

But the mechanism we care about is visible.

The Nationalists succeeded in forcing the main Communist body out of
Jiangxi.

A contemporary US diplomatic report described the Nationalist occupation
of the Communist area as a significant success --- and then immediately
noted that the escape of the main Communist force in substantial
strength had **altered the location of the threat rather than eliminated
it**.

That is the strategic distinction.

``` text
lose base
   ≠
cease to exist
```

If the organisation survives, the board is still moving.

The Long March was devastating.

It was also part of the political environment through which Mao later
re-established dominant leadership within the Communist movement.

And the system into which the survivors emerged did not remain the
system they had left.

China's internal politics changed.

The war with Japan transformed the strategic environment.

Coalitions, legitimacy, military experience, territorial control and
external relationships changed.

Years later, the Communist movement was operating from a radically
different position.

So the useful lesson is not:

> **Retreat and you will eventually win.**

It is:

> **Destruction and displacement are not the same outcome.**

And:

> **If an actor preserves enough organisational capacity to remain in
> the system, time retains the possibility of changing its relative
> position.**

This is Frederick's principle in a completely different political and
military environment.

Remain on the board.

Not because the future is guaranteed to save you.

Because leaving the board guarantees that it cannot.

## 16. 👑 Frederick Would Like To Remain On The Board

Frederick the Great gives us a different use of time.

During the Seven Years' War, Prussia faced an extraordinarily dangerous

Greene's telling emphasises Frederick --- normally associated with

He did not have a calendar entry saying:

``` text

Peter III arrives
```

> **Remain an actor in the system long enough for the system to
> change.**

Normally we think of uncertainty as a threat:

Something unexpected can fuck up theirs.

Leadership changes.

Coalitions fracture.

Finances deteriorate.

Priorities move.

Public opinion changes.

An ally becomes an enemy.

An enemy becomes less hostile.

A new technology appears.

A third party intervenes.

A scandal erupts.

> **WAIT FOR TSARINA TO DIE.**

😂

> **You do not always need to know how circumstances will change in your

That is a different flavour of time from Russia 1812.

Russia uses time to make advance expensive.

Frederick uses time to remain exposed to favourable contingency.

## 18. 🇺🇸 America, You Literally Know How To Do This

Before anybody concludes that this node is asking the United States to
become spiritually Chinese, we should probably consult the American

It is not empty.

Two particularly useful twentieth-century examples give us two different
ways of using time.

### Franklin D. Roosevelt: time as mobilisation and positional improvement

Before formal American entry into the Second World War, the United
States was not simply doing nothing.

Roosevelt's administration used measures including Lend-Lease to support
states fighting the Axis while the United States was not yet formally a
belligerent.

The Roosevelt Library describes the crisis facing Britain at the end of
1940, Roosevelt's effort to persuade a sceptical Congress and public,
and the eventual passage of Lend-Lease in March 1941.

Roosevelt's famous garden-hose analogy is itself a tiny systems model.

If your neighbour's house is on fire, you lend the hose because stopping
the fire from spreading is part of protecting your own house.

This is activity beneath the threshold of the largest available action.

``` text
delay direct belligerency
        ≠
do nothing
```

Instead:

``` text
time
  ↓
industrial mobilisation
  ↓
aid
  ↓
preparation
  ↓
coalition survival
  ↓
greater future options
```

We should not turn this into a pristine master plan in which Roosevelt
knew exactly what would happen.

He did not know Pearl Harbor was going to produce precisely the later
decision environment.

That is the point.

Preparation improves the range of futures you can survive.

### Dwight Eisenhower: time as sustainable competition

Eisenhower gives us an even cleaner systems argument.

In his 1961 Farewell Address, he described a prolonged and complex
struggle in which the United States needed to remain on its course
**despite provocation**.

Then he warned against the recurring temptation to seek some spectacular
and costly action as the miraculous solution to a crisis.

His alternative was **balance**:

-   cost against hoped-for advantage;
-   immediate action against future national welfare;
-   military requirements against the wider structure of society.

That is astonishingly close to the problem in this node.

Eisenhower is not arguing against military capability.

The same speech insists that American arms must remain powerful and
ready.

He is asking something harder:

> **How do you sustain security without consuming the system whose
> security you are trying to preserve?**

This is Sun Tzu's systems problem wearing an American suit.

`Sun Tzu: “Protracted expenditure can exhaust the state.”`

`Eisenhower: “Yes. We have fucking spreadsheets.”`

And Eisenhower is particularly useful because he cannot easily be
dismissed as somebody who simply lacked the stomach for military action.

He had been Supreme Allied Commander.

The restraint matters **because he understood war**.

So the cross-cultural lesson is not:

> **America, learn to be China.**

It is:

> **America, you already have versions of this. Add more depth to the

## 19. ✊ Unfortunately, Civilians Can Use Time Too

At this point the defence-policy reader may be tempted to keep the whole
concept safely inside the military box.

No.

Civilians can do this.

Boycotts are one example.

A civilian boycott is a form of nonviolent political pressure in which
people deliberately withhold purchases, participation or cooperation in
order to alter the incentives facing another actor.

The political demand can be one you support.

It can be one you despise.

The mechanism does not care about your feelings.

At its simplest:

``` text
citizens withhold purchasing / cooperation
        ↓
target experiences economic / reputational / information effects
        ↓
movement attempts to sustain participation
        ↓
duration changes costs and expectations
        ↓
target chooses whether to concede / negotiate / substitute / counter-mobilise / wait
```

But there is another side:

``` text
participants also experience cost
        ↓
attention can decay
        ↓
substitutes may be inconvenient
        ↓
coalitions can fracture
        ↓
participation can collapse
```

So:

> **Time does not automatically favour the boycott.**

That would be an absurdly deterministic claim.

The useful question is:

> **Which side can sustain the relevant cost for longer, and what is
> happening to each side's position while they do?**

This is why refusal to negotiate is still a strategic choice.

A government can say:

> No.

A company can say:

> No.

A university can say:

> No.

A defence institution can say:

> No.

But the word **no** does not stop time.

The movement may collapse.

It may grow.

Costs may disappear.

They may accumulate.

Public opinion may change.

A substitute supplier may appear.

An election may intervene.

Third parties may change position.

The target may discover it can absorb the pressure indefinitely.

The movement may discover the opposite.

The strategic question is not merely:

> **Am I willing to concede today?**

It is:

> **What do I expect duration to do?**

This is particularly important when democratic governments confront
nonviolent campaigns over foreign or defence policy.

You can be deeply uncomfortable with the campaign.

You can reject its demands.

You can argue against it.

But if you refuse to model the mechanism because you dislike the
politics, you have voluntarily made your strategic bench thinner.

That is not ideological purity.

That is bad analysis.

## 20. 🗳️ Refusing Negotiation Does Not Freeze The Board

This deserves its own section because it is a recurring political
mistake.

There is a difference between:

> **We have considered negotiation and believe the available agreement
> would worsen our position.**

and:

> **We will not negotiate, therefore the situation is static until the
> other side gives up.**

The first is strategy.

The second may be wishful thinking.

A refusal can be entirely rational.

Sometimes negotiation rewards coercion.

Sometimes the other party cannot deliver.

Sometimes the proposed terms are unacceptable.

Sometimes delay favours you.

Sometimes your red line is real.

But refusing to negotiate does not remove the following from the system:

-   cost;
-   time;
-   public opinion;
-   attrition;
-   elections;
-   coalition maintenance;
-   third-party behaviour;
-   economic substitution;
-   organisational fatigue;
-   technological change;
-   information.

Therefore:

> **Refusing negotiation is not an absence of strategy. It is a strategy
> with an exposure to time.**

The policymaker still has to ask:

> **What happens if nothing changes in our formal position for six
> months?**

And:

> **What happens if nothing changes for two years?**

And:

> **What are we assuming will break first?**

That is the piece a thin action/inaction binary cannot see.

### 💸 Markets Do Not Have Passports

Each can create unintended effects.

The point is not:

> **REGULATE EVERYTHING.**

The point is:

> **"The market chose it" does not answer a national-security question
> unless market efficiency and national resilience have been shown to
> share the same objective function.**

This matters enormously for AI.

AI is not floating in a cloud made of ideas.

It depends on:

-   chips;
-   fabs;
-   minerals;
-   energy;
-   data centres;
-   networks;
-   cooling;
-   capital;
-   researchers;
-   skilled labour;
-   standards;
-   software;
-   intellectual property;
-   international supply chains.

So an AI diplomatic relationship is simultaneously a relationship about:

``` text
technology
economics
security
standards
infrastructure
trade
knowledge
sovereignty
dependency
```

If Western states enter that relationship imagining that commercial
incentives will automatically produce their preferred strategic
distribution of capacity, they have confused one system's objective
function with another's.

And if they enter it imagining that every strategically favourable
Chinese outcome must have required a secret Chinese intervention, they
have made the opposite mistake.

Sometimes:

`🇨🇳: *observes*`

is enough.

## 26. 🧮 Whose Protracted War Is It?

Before returning to the scoreboard, it is worth remembering why this
enormous intellectual safari is sitting inside the **Iran / IRGC Data
Wars** cluster.

Iran did not disappear from the node while we wandered through ancient
Chinese philosophy, Jane Austen, Mao, Napoleon, Montgomery, Frederick,
Roosevelt, Eisenhower, capitalism and AI.

The original Iran question exposed the larger analytical problem.

We were trying to understand an adversary's possible use of time without
enough models of what **strategic time** can be.

If Iran can benefit from Western expenditure it did not itself create,
then attribution alone is insufficient.

If NATO can preserve capability through restraint, then visible
non-response alone is insufficient.

If third actors can change the environment, then a bilateral model is
insufficient.

If later information can alter the meaning of an earlier event, then
instant interpretation is insufficient.

If allies receive different information from the same action, then
signalling to the adversary alone is insufficient.

That is why the detour was necessary.

It gave us a better scoreboard.

We can now return to Sun Tzu with it.

Not merely:

> How many attacks did we stop?

Not merely:

> How much territory did we gain?

Not merely:

> How many retaliations did we launch?

Ask:

-   Who is spending what?
-   What regenerates?
-   What does not?
-   Which losses accumulate?
-   Who controls tempo?
-   Who needs a quick resolution?
-   Who can tolerate protraction?
-   Who is learning?
-   Who is revealing information?
-   Who is preserving optionality?
-   Whose coalition is strengthening?
-   Whose coalition is weakening?
-   Whose legitimacy is changing?
-   Who can still act when the environment changes?
-   Which side is converting time into capacity?
-   Which side is converting capacity into exhaustion?

This also gives us a better way to approach the emotionally loaded
question:

> **Why are we fighting a war that we're losing?**

Do not assume the answer.

Test the premise.

What is the war?

What is the objective?

What counts as victory?

What counts as loss?

What is the relevant time horizon?

What is being expended?

What is being regenerated?

What does another year improve?

What does another year degrade?

What options become available?

Which disappear?

What does escalation purchase?

What does negotiation preserve?

What does non-engagement expose?

What happens if we simply continue?

This is not less urgent than shouting **WE ARE LOSING**.

It is more useful.

Because it tells you what would have to change.

## 27. 🪤 Stop Making Competent Adversary Strategy So Cheap

The defensive objective cannot realistically be:

> **NO DISRUPTION EVER OCCURS.**

That is not a serious standard for an open, networked, technologically
complex society.

A better objective is:

> **Reduce the return an adversary receives from making us expend
> resources.**

That means:

🔧 maintain capability;

🧠 preserve skilled people;

🕸️ build redundancy;

🤝 make alliances function;

📣 maintain democratic legitimacy;

🔐 keep sensitive shit secret;

⏳ understand whose time is being spent;

♻️ regenerate faster than disruption consumes;

🧯 restore quickly enough that failure does not become catastrophe.

The question is not merely:

> **Why haven't you stopped the attacks?**

It is:

> **Why does each attack cost us what it costs us?**

And:

> **Can we make ourselves cheaper to defend than we are to exhaust?**

This is where current US military thinking is actually encouraging.

Recent Army writing is explicitly revisiting endurance, reconstitution,
retreat and force preservation.

The intellectual material exists.

The challenge is routing it into a wider strategic culture that is
frequently rewarded for speed, visibility, certainty and dramatic
response.

## 28. 📣 Classified Is Not The Same As Inexplicable

There are excellent reasons not to tell the public everything.

Operational details can be classified.

Sources and methods can be protected.

Specific vulnerabilities can remain secret.

Attribution confidence may depend on intelligence that cannot safely be
published.

Planned responses may need secrecy.

**Trump blurting sensitive shit into the public information environment
is not the alternative we are requesting.**

But:

> **"We cannot disclose operational details" does not logically entail
> "we cannot explain the strategic environment."**

Democratic publics need enough information to understand:

-   what the objective is;
-   why restraint may be deliberate;
-   why escalation may be rejected;
-   why a capability is being preserved;
-   why negotiation may or may not be useful;
-   why resilience matters;
-   why attribution takes time;
-   what success actually means.

Otherwise visible non-action becomes politically legible only as:

``` text
WEAKNESS
```

And then the political system begins demanding expenditure merely to
demonstrate activity.

That is an extraordinarily exploitable feedback loop.

A competent adversary does not need to force you to waste resources if
your own political incentives will do it for them.

## 29. 🤖 AI Is Where The Bench Becomes Diplomatic Infrastructure

This is why the apparently wandering journey through Sun Tzu, *wu wei*,
Austen, Mao, Montgomery, Roosevelt, Eisenhower, boycotts and
manufacturing is not actually wandering.

AI is forcing states to negotiate about a system that does not sit
neatly inside any one existing diplomatic box.

It is simultaneously:

-   commercial;
-   military;
-   scientific;
-   cultural;
-   infrastructural;
-   informational;
-   regulatory;
-   labour-related;
-   environmental;
-   educational;
-   geopolitical.

And the actors arriving at the negotiating table do not share one
intellectual history of:

-   the state;
-   the market;
-   sovereignty;
-   individual rights;
-   collective welfare;
-   security;
-   openness;
-   censorship;
-   risk;
-   legitimate regulation;
-   technological development.

This does not mean cultural background mechanically determines policy.

It means **context changes information**.

A proposal that looks like:

``` text
reasonable safety regulation
```

to one actor may look like:

``` text
foreign control of domestic technological development
```

to another.

A proposal described as:

``` text
open innovation
```

may be modelled elsewhere as:

``` text
strategic dependency
```

A demand for:

``` text
sovereign control
```

may be read elsewhere as:

``` text
censorship
```

A demand for:

``` text
free information flows
```

may be read elsewhere as:

``` text
unaccountable foreign platform power
```

The answer is not to pretend those differences do not exist.

Nor is it to decide in advance that every disagreement is merely a
cultural misunderstanding.

Sometimes interests genuinely conflict.

Sometimes values genuinely conflict.

Sometimes states are bargaining hard.

Sometimes security concerns are real.

Sometimes **no** is the correct answer.

But diplomacy works better when everybody can distinguish:

``` text
different model
```

from:

``` text
bad faith
```

and:

``` text
different interest
```

from:

``` text
failure to understand
```

That requires specialists.

Technical specialists.

Regional specialists.

Diplomats.

Defence and security people.

Industry people.

Historians.

Lawyers.

People who understand standards.

People who understand how the other delegation's institutions actually
work.

But we can make the institutional requirement more concrete.

An AI diplomatic process needs, at minimum, three functions operating at
the same time.

### 🛠️ Technical / industry reality

What can the system actually do?

What cannot it do yet?

What standards are technically implementable?

What are the constraints created by:

-   compute;
-   chips;
-   fabs;
-   energy;
-   data centres;
-   networks;
-   model architecture;
-   software;
-   supply chains;
-   skilled labour?

A diplomatically beautiful agreement based on a technically false model
may be fucking useless.

### 🛡️ Strategic / defence-security reality

What does the arrangement do to:

-   national security;
-   critical infrastructure;
-   strategic dependency;
-   economic resilience;
-   military capability;
-   intelligence exposure;
-   productive capacity;
-   relative power;
-   alliance relationships?

A technically efficient system can still produce a strategically
unacceptable dependency.

### 🤝 Diplomatic / interpersonal reality

What can the other party actually agree to?

What language can survive their domestic politics?

What can survive ours?

Where are the real red lines?

Which differences are negotiable?

What does reciprocity mean to each side?

What relationship still has to exist the morning after the agreement is
signed?

A technically elegant agreement that cannot survive the political
relationship is not an agreement.

These do not have to be literally three people per country.

They are three functions that have to remain connected.

``` text
technical reality
       ↕
strategic reality
       ↕
diplomatic reality
```

If one becomes detached from the others, the system can optimise the
wrong problem extremely efficiently.

Not because everybody needs to agree.

Because **predictability is itself valuable**.

You do not need to like everybody.

You do not need to share a political system.

You do not need one universal philosophy.

You need enough mutual modelling that the system can distinguish:

-   red lines;
-   negotiable differences;
-   technical incompatibilities;
-   security concerns;
-   commercial interests;
-   rhetorical signalling;
-   actual escalation.

That is what depth on the bench looks like when it becomes international
infrastructure.

And if the United States, Europe and their allies are entering a
decades-long AI relationship with China while simultaneously treating
China as a major strategic competitor, then cultural and institutional
literacy is not an optional humanities flourish.

It is part of the fucking safety equipment.

### ⏳ And now Sun Tzu is calling the AI summit

There is one final reason this belongs in a node about strategic time.

AI systems are developing quickly.

Diplomatic institutions are comparatively slow.

Standards take time.

Treaties take time.

Infrastructure takes time.

Semiconductor fabs take time.

Grid expansion takes time.

Data centres take time.

Talent pipelines take time.

Safety research takes time.

Political administrations change.

Companies fail.

Models improve.

Dependencies harden.

And therefore AI diplomacy has a **time asymmetry** problem.

Some decisions become better if we wait for more information.

Some become harder to reverse the longer we wait.

Some technical standards may be dangerous to lock in prematurely.

Some infrastructure dependencies may become strategically entrenched if
nobody acts early.

Some risks may be speculative today and measurable later.

Some capabilities may diffuse faster than a treaty can be negotiated.

So once again the available choices are not:

``` text
ACT NOW = STRONG
WAIT = WEAK
```

Nor:

``` text
WAIT = WISE
ACT NOW = PANIC
```

The actual questions are:

> **Which things require agreement now?**

> **Which things become clearer if we wait?**

> **Which options disappear if we wait too long?**

> **Which premature actions would lock us into a bad model?**

> **What is regenerating while time passes?**

> **What is becoming harder to reverse?**

> **Whose capacity is increasing?**

> **Whose dependency is deepening?**

That is the whole node coming home.

AI diplomacy is not a choice between speed and delay.

It is another problem of understanding:

> **what time is doing to the system while everybody argues about what
> to do.**

`☎️ AI SUMMIT: “Hello?”`

`Sun Tzu: “Hi. Me again.”`

## 30. 🧠 The Actual Vulnerability Is A Thin Bench

This is the final teaching point.

The vulnerability is not merely lack of intelligence.

It is lack of **available conceptual models**.

If the only culturally legible options are:

``` text
ATTACK
or
SURRENDER
```

then most of strategy has disappeared before the meeting begins.

Cross-cultural study is therefore not ornamental.

It can be defensive capacity.

Not because another culture contains **The Correct Answer**.

Because another intellectual tradition may have spent centuries making
distinctions your own habitual vocabulary bundles together.

Then you bring those distinctions home.

Sun Tzu does not replace Eisenhower.

*Wu wei* does not replace Roosevelt.

Mao does not replace Montgomery.

The *I Ching* does not replace Jane Austen.

Frederick does not replace anybody.

He is extremely busy remaining on the board until somebody else's
dynasty does something surprising.

The point is the bench.

A deep bench gives you more ways to construct the problem before you
commit resources to solving it.

And if you are supposedly terrified of a strategic competitor --- if you
genuinely believe another major power may represent a profound challenge
to your security --- then refusing to study the intellectual, historical
and institutional environments from which that actor emerges is not
toughness.

It is voluntarily surrendering information.

Respecting an adversary as a peer does not require respecting their
politics.

It requires respecting the possibility that they know things you do not.

## 31. ☎️ Sun Tzu Calling NATO

We can now return to the beginning.

Sun Tzu's *Waging War* is not a cyber doctrine.

It is not a NATO instruction manual.

It does not tell a modern democracy which sanction to impose, which
server to isolate, which negotiation to enter, which missile to fire,
which boycott to tolerate, which alliance commitment to make or which
cyber incident to attribute.

What it gives us is older and more annoying.

A systems warning.

> **Conflict consumes the system conducting it.**

The strategic problem is therefore not merely how to exert power.

It is how to preserve the capacity from which power comes.

Sun Tzu gives us **prolonged expenditure**.

Greene gives us **non-engagement and overextension**.

*Wu wei* gives us another vocabulary for **efficacy, non-forcing and
economy of energy**.

The *I Ching* gives Greene a language of **changing configurations**.

Napoleon and Russia give us **irrecoverable time and expensive
advance**.

Montgomery gives us **visible progress concealing a deteriorating
timetable**.

Frederick gives us **survival as exposure to favourable contingency**.

Roosevelt gives us **time converted into preparation and productive
capacity**.

Eisenhower gives us **sustainable competition and the danger of
consuming the society being defended**.

Mao gives us **force preservation, mobility and deliberate protraction
under asymmetry**.

Civilian boycotts give us **withholding and duration as nonviolent
political pressure**.

Underdog stories give us **survival as information**.

Cyber conflict gives us **attention, expertise and restoration capacity
as contested resources**.

Iran gives us the reminder that **an adversary can benefit from costs it
did not personally create**.

China gives us the reminder that **studying another strategic tradition
can deepen our own bench**.

And America gives us perhaps the most important corrective of all:

> **You already know how to do some of this.**

The problem is not that the conceptual tools are foreign.

The problem is whether the system can retrieve the right tool before it
reaches automatically for the loudest one.

So:

`Sun Tzu: “I have some concerns regarding your expenditure.”`

`NATO: “How did you get this number?”`

`Sun Tzu: “I HAVE HAD THIS NUMBER FOR TWO AND A HALF THOUSAND YEARS.”`

`Monty: “Can we please not discuss the bridges.”`

`Frederick: *continues remaining alive out of spite*`

`🇨🇳: “Would anyone like some merch for this?”`

And the final question remains:

> **Are we spending more resources responding to disorder than an
> adversary needs to spend producing --- or merely exploiting --- it?**

If yes:

**recalculate the fucking exchange ratio.**

## 32. 📚 Sources And Reading Routes

The source list deliberately separates different epistemic functions.

``` text
📜 primary texts read alongside drafting
   → what the texts themselves say in the editions used here

📚 interpretative scholarship
   → how specialists analyse contested concepts and traditions

🏛️ historical / institutional sources
   → documentary and institutional evidence for historical mechanisms

🤖 contemporary policy sources
   → what governments and institutions currently say they are doing
```

These categories should not be treated as interchangeable.

A state's policy statement is evidence of its stated position.

It is not independent proof that the state behaves exactly as described.

A scholarly interpretation is evidence of an argument about a concept.

It is not the concept's only possible meaning.

A historical document records a situated observer's information.

It does not become omniscient merely because it is old.

making the decision.
