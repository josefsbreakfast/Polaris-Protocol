# 🍊 Why Is the Orange Being Weird?

**First created:** 2026-08-20 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-20  
*Why apparent reluctance to attribute attacks on American water infrastructure to Iran has several potentially serious explanations — and why the anomaly is more useful than prematurely choosing one.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Something about the public posture is weird.

That does not mean something secret and spectacular must therefore be happening.

During an escalating conflict with Iran, Donald Trump has been prepared to use extraordinarily aggressive rhetoric about Iran, victory, control of the Strait of Hormuz, and even American territorial claims over the strait.

Meanwhile, reporting around cyber incidents affecting American water infrastructure has produced a markedly different register.

Iranian involvement has been discussed as a serious possibility.

The activity sits inside a threat environment in which Iranian-affiliated actors are already known to have shown interest in exposed industrial-control systems.

Yet the President has not publicly seized upon the politically obvious formulation:

> **Iran is attacking American drinking water.**

Nor, at the time of writing, has he publicly transformed the incidents into:

> **terrorists are attacking American drinking water.**

That absence is interesting.

It is not evidence of any single explanation.

There are lots of potential reasons why the orange is being weird.

---

## 🍊 First: What Is Actually Weird?

The anomaly is not simply that attribution remains uncertain.

Cyber attribution frequently remains uncertain.

The interesting feature is the contrast between two rhetorical postures.

On one side:

**Iran → defeated → Hormuz → control → territory → maximalist rhetoric**

On the other:

**American civilian infrastructure → possible Iranian cyber activity → attribution caution**

Ordinarily, an administration seeking maximum domestic support for escalation could hardly ask for better political material than hostile interference with drinking-water infrastructure.

Water is:

- civilian;
- local;
- emotionally comprehensible;
- geographically distributed;
- essential to everyday life;
- and substantially easier to explain than the strategic architecture of the Persian Gulf.

If attribution were politically useful and sufficiently certain, the rhetorical opportunity is enormous.

It apparently has not been taken.

So why?

---

## 🧮 Hypothesis One: They Genuinely Don't Know

Start with the boring explanation.

It is frequently the correct one.

The incidents may resemble known Iranian methods without providing sufficient evidence for presidential-level attribution.

Similar techniques can be used by:

- Iranian state actors;
- Iranian-affiliated operators;
- hacktivists;
- criminal groups;
- access brokers;
- contractors;
- other states;
- and sufficiently motivated idiots with access to exposed systems.

Technical resemblance is not identity.

The intelligence community may therefore simply be saying:

> **We aren't confident enough yet.**

If so, presidential restraint would be entirely appropriate.

It would also be slightly remarkable restraint given everything else, but stranger things have happened.

---

## 🕸️ Hypothesis Two: They Know More Than They Can Prove Publicly

Intelligence confidence and publicly releasable evidence are different things.

Attribution may depend upon:

- classified collection;
- foreign intelligence partnerships;
- compromised adversary infrastructure;
- human sources;
- intercepted communications;
- sensitive telemetry;
- or access the United States does not wish an adversary to know it possesses.

The internal assessment could therefore be substantially stronger than the public assessment.

That creates a familiar problem:

**knowing something is not identical to being able to explain how you know it.**

Public ambiguity may consequently preserve intelligence capabilities.

---

## 🪜 Hypothesis Three: Attribution Creates An Obligation

Suppose the President publicly says:

> **Iran attacked American civilian water infrastructure.**

What happens next?

The statement does not merely describe an event.

It creates political expectations.

Congress asks what the administration is doing about it.

Governors ask whether their infrastructure is safe.

Journalists ask whether this constitutes an attack on the United States.

The public asks why Iran can interfere with American drinking water without consequence.

Allies ask what threshold has now been crossed.

Iran asks whether retaliation is coming.

Attribution therefore creates its own escalation pressure.

Sometimes ambiguity is useful precisely because certainty would require somebody to do something.

---

## 🌊 Hypothesis Four: Ambiguity Is A De-escalation Channel

Cyber conflict has an unusual property.

Both sides can sometimes understand what happened without publicly agreeing about what happened.

That permits something like:

```text
I know that was you.

You know I know that was you.

I know you know I know.

Nobody says it at the podium.
```

Ridiculous as this looks, it can be strategically useful.

A state can demonstrate capability without forcing its opponent publicly to acknowledge that a threshold has been crossed.

The recipient can strengthen defences, communicate privately, retaliate somewhere less visible, or simply absorb the signal.

Ambiguous attribution can therefore function as a **pressure-release valve**.

If that is occurring here, reluctance to say *Iran did it* may not represent uncertainty.

It may represent deliberate refusal to turn a private signal into a public casus belli.

Again:

**possible. Not established.**

---

## 📣 Hypothesis Five: Iran Wants To Be Noticed, Not Necessarily Blamed

An operation can communicate capability without requiring formal attribution.

If an Iranian or Iranian-aligned actor wanted Washington to understand:

> **we can reach civilian infrastructure inside the United States**

then completely concealing the operation would defeat part of the purpose.

But causing catastrophic harm could produce overwhelming retaliation.

That creates a middle territory:

**visible enough to be understood; limited enough to remain negotiable.**

The operational characteristics therefore matter.

Did the attacker maximise damage?

Did they merely demonstrate access?

Did they change settings that operators would notice?

Did they leave obvious artefacts?

Did they possess greater capability than they exercised?

Those questions help distinguish espionage, extortion, vandalism, preparation and signalling.

Sometimes the point of touching the machine is proving that you can touch the machine.

Notice me, senpai.

But geopolitically.

---

## 🎭 Hypothesis Six: Public Attribution Would Help Iran

This possibility runs in the opposite direction.

An administration may avoid loudly attributing relatively limited infrastructure interference because doing so would advertise Iranian capability.

Imagine the headline:

> **IRANIAN HACKERS PENETRATE AMERICAN WATER SYSTEMS ACROSS MULTIPLE STATES**

Even if the actual effects were modest and quickly contained, the propaganda value could be considerable.

Iran could present itself as capable of reaching directly into the American homeland.

Other actors could copy the techniques.

Public confidence in infrastructure security could fall.

The attacker might therefore receive more psychological value from American publicity than from the technical operation itself.

Sometimes the least rewarding response to *notice me* is:

> No.

---

## ⚖️ Hypothesis Seven: The Administration Does Not Want The Domestic Risk Question

Attribution creates another uncomfortable question:

> **Was this foreseeable?**

If American authorities had already warned that Iranian-affiliated actors were interested in exposed industrial-control systems, successful wartime interference naturally produces questions about preparedness.

Which systems remained exposed?

Were default credentials still in use?

Had utilities received actionable warnings?

Did governments know which vulnerable PLCs were deployed?

Were mitigations funded?

Were smaller utilities capable of implementing them?

And, critically:

> **Did decisions to escalate the conflict increase a known risk to civilian infrastructure without adequate domestic mitigation?**

That is politically less convenient than simply discussing victory abroad.

A government can simultaneously believe its military strategy is justified and prefer not to invite examination of the domestic risks attached to it.

---

## 🧿 Hypothesis Eight: Different Parts Of Government May Disagree

"The United States government" is not one brain.

Neither is an administration.

Possible participants include:

- the White House;
- FBI;
- CISA;
- NSA;
- Cyber Command;
- intelligence agencies;
- EPA;
- DHS;
- state governments;
- local utilities;
- military commands;
- diplomatic officials;
- and allied intelligence services.

They can possess different evidence.

They can apply different attribution thresholds.

They can have different institutional incentives.

One agency may think:

> **probably Iran.**

Another:

> **not enough evidence.**

Another:

> **yes, but don't say it.**

Another:

> **we care considerably more about getting these PLCs off the internet than winning the attribution argument.**

Public rhetoric can consequently look incoherent because the underlying system genuinely contains disagreement.

## 💰 Hypothesis Nine: The Operator And The Customer May Not Be The Same Thing

This is increasingly important.

An incident need not reduce neatly to:

```text
IRGC
 ↓
keyboard
 ↓
American water utility
```

The actual chain could involve:

```text
strategic requirement
        ↓
contractor
        ↓
intermediary
        ↓
access broker
        ↓
operator
        ↓
compromised infrastructure
```

Or some participants may be independently motivated.

An operator could be:

- criminal;
- ideological;
- commercially motivated;
- state affiliated;
- informally encouraged;
- explicitly commissioned;
- or serving several customers simultaneously.

That makes financial relationships unusually informative.

Who paid whom?

Who purchased access?

Who commissioned the objective?

Who selected the victim?

Who controlled the operator after access was obtained?

Who ultimately received the intelligence or strategic benefit?

Those questions may tell us more than whether a malware sample has an Iranian accent.

## 🧨 Hypothesis Ten: Somebody Commissioned Something And It Got Weird

This deserves its own category because modern outsourced capability markets repeatedly create the same governance problem.

Someone wants an outcome.

They commission somebody else to achieve it.

The instruction may effectively be:

> **Get it sorted.**

The commissioner thinks they have transferred the problem.

What they may actually have transferred is **control**.

An intermediary chooses methods.

Another actor supplies access.

Tools get reused.

Data gets copied.

Capabilities developed for one customer become available to another.

An intervention produces secondary consequences nobody priced properly.

Eventually somebody at the top discovers that:

> **this is not quite what I meant**

is not a rollback mechanism.

Cyber operations make this particularly dangerous because capability is reusable.

Funding an operator for one purpose can subsidise infrastructure subsequently used for:

- extortion;
- espionage;
- political interference;
- stalking;
- destructive operations;
- unrelated criminal activity;
- or another state's requirements.

Following the financials therefore helps reconstruct not merely culpability but **how the capability ecosystem reproduces itself**.

## 🔄 Risk Reduction Or Risk Displacement?

Aggressive interventions are frequently justified as reducing risk.

Sometimes they do.

Sometimes the graph actually looks like this:

```text
immediate visible problem ↓

but

operator autonomy ↑
criminal capability ↑
secondary markets ↑
retaliatory incentives ↑
information leakage ↑
uncontrolled consequences ↑
accountability distance ↑
```

The organisation experiences relief.

The system experiences additional risk.

Those are not the same thing.

"Move fast and break things" is relatively straightforward when the thing being broken is your own prototype.

It becomes substantially less charming when the objects being broken are:

- infrastructure;
- law;
- institutional norms;
- political opposition;
- civilian safety;
- or somebody else's rights.

---

## 🦠 Selective Impunity Has Downstream Effects

There is another feedback loop worth watching.

Suppose institutions make use of questionable intermediaries because those intermediaries are useful.

Later, those same actors commit straightforward criminal offences.

Enforcement now carries an institutional cost.

A serious investigation may expose:

- earlier commissions;
- embarrassing relationships;
- tolerated misconduct;
- financial transfers;
- intelligence dependencies;
- or politically inconvenient decisions.

The incentive to enforce ordinary rules can therefore weaken.

That creates:

```text
useful misconduct
        ↓
institution becomes implicated
        ↓
later criminality appears
        ↓
enforcement threatens earlier conduct
        ↓
under-enforcement / compartmentalisation
        ↓
greater practical impunity
        ↓
more misconduct
```

Nobody needs to decide consciously that crime is now permitted.

The incentive structure can accomplish that by itself.

This is why selective impunity is dangerous.

It changes the expected cost of misconduct for everyone observing the system.

## 🍉 Hypothesis Eleven: Target Selection May Be Audience Selection

If Iranian-linked operators are involved anywhere in the wider campaign, target selection may have an information dimension.

Iran does not need Western publics to become enthusiastic supporters of the Iranian government.

A substantially easier objective is:

> **reduce Western public sympathy for the target.**

Companies associated with:

- fossil fuels;
- defence;
- surveillance;
- concentrated wealth;
- unpopular healthcare economics;
- Israeli state relationships;
- environmental damage;
- extractive data practices;
- or other existing political grievances

may begin an information conflict with substantially less public legitimacy than a children's hospital or neighbourhood water utility.

That does not legitimise attacking them.

It changes the **political environment in which the attack is interpreted**.

An adversary capable of audience research would be foolish not to notice.

## 📉 Denial Of Solidarity

The desired public reaction need not be:

> **Iran is right.**

It may be sufficient to produce:

> **Obviously cyberattacks are bad, but…**

That *but* matters.

Consensus has fractured.

The attacker has potentially reduced the political cost of the operation without persuading anyone to endorse the attacker.

This is better understood as **denial of solidarity** than persuasion.

And it creates another reason to distinguish victim selection from access acquisition.

An actor may compromise fifty organisations opportunistically and then selectively:

- exploit some;
- publicise others;
- retain quiet access to strategically valuable systems;
- embarrass politically unpopular companies;
- or avoid operations likely to produce immediate civilian sympathy.

Therefore the relevant dataset is not merely:

**Who got hacked?**

It is also:

**Who got used? Who got hurt? Who got publicised? Who got left alone?**

## 🏛️ America Is At War — Except This Is Legally More Complicated Than That

There is another reason to be careful about interpreting presidential reluctance to attribute attacks on American water infrastructure simply as embarrassment.

The United States is plainly participating in an armed conflict with Iran.

Under international humanitarian law, an international armed conflict does not depend upon a formal declaration of war.

American domestic constitutional architecture presents a different question.

Article I of the U.S. Constitution gives Congress the power to declare war.

Congress has not formally declared war against Iran.

That distinction does not make the missiles imaginary.

It does mean that the political and constitutional architecture surrounding an already very real armed conflict remains contested.

And that becomes relevant when the executive branch requires Congress to continue funding it.

### 💰 Congress Hasn't Declared War, But Congress Is Being Asked To Pay For It

The relevant political geometry looks something like:

```text
executive conducts sustained hostilities
        ↓
Congress has not formally declared war
        ↓
continued operations require resources
        ↓
Congress debates authority + expenditure
        ↓
domestic political legitimacy matters
```

Now add another proposition:

> **Iran is attacking civilian infrastructure inside the United States.**

That does not merely make the existing conflict sound more serious.

It potentially changes its domestic political character.

## 🌊 If Iran Did It, The War Has Come Home

This remains conditional.

Public attribution of the water incidents to Iran has not been established.

But suppose sufficiently strong evidence eventually supports it.

The political statement would no longer simply be:

> **American forces are fighting Iran overseas.**

It becomes:

> **During that conflict, Iranian or Iranian-directed actors interfered with essential civilian infrastructure on American territory.**

For Americans, that distinction is likely to matter.

It would matter in almost any country.

Attacks experienced on one's own territory are psychologically different from military operations experienced primarily through news reports, casualty figures, fuel prices and maps of somewhere else.

Water makes that difference particularly intimate.

This isn't:

> **something happened to an American military installation thousands of miles away.**

It is potentially:

> **somebody reached into the infrastructure supplying my town.**

That is an extremely powerful political fact if established.

Which makes presidential reluctance to claim it more interesting, not less.

## 🧠 America Still Has A 9/11-Shaped Scar

There is a historical sensitivity here that should be discussed carefully rather than weaponised.

The United States experienced a spectacular mass-casualty attack on its own territory on 11 September 2001.

That trauma was real.

The grief was real.

The fear was real.

And its political consequences were enormous.

The decades afterwards also demonstrated something many other societies have learned in their own ways:

> **fear can produce security responses whose downstream harms considerably exceed what frightened people believed they were authorising at the time.**

That observation is not anti-American.

Quite the opposite.

Other societies can recognise the danger precisely because versions of it have happened to them too.

Britain has experience of terrorism and emergency security legislation.

France has experience of terrorism and prolonged emergency powers.

Germany, Spain and Italy have histories of political violence and counterterrorism.

Japan has experienced spectacular domestic terrorism.

Russia has repeatedly responded to terrorism and insurgency through extensive securitisation.

Israel and Palestine contain catastrophic and continuing examples of civilian fear, political violence, militarisation and security doctrine becoming mutually reinforcing.

Different histories should not be collapsed into equivalence.

The recurring systems problem is nevertheless recognisable:

```text
real attack
    ↓
real public fear
    ↓
demand that government DO SOMETHING
    ↓
exceptional powers become politically cheap
    ↓
oversight becomes politically expensive
    ↓
temporary measures acquire infrastructure
    ↓
infrastructure develops constituencies
    ↓
exception becomes ordinary
```

People elsewhere saying:

> **Guys, be careful here.**

need not be minimising what happened to Americans.

Sometimes it means:

> **We believe you about the wound. We are warning you because we recognise what frightened states can subsequently do to themselves and others.**

That can come from a place of solidarity.

---

## 🚨 Which Makes "Terrorism" A Particularly Consequential Word

This is why the absence of the terrorism framing around the water incidents is worth observing without overinterpreting it.

If a President publicly establishes:

**Iran → attacks civilian American infrastructure → terrorism**

several emotionally powerful categories collapse into one another.

The conflict is no longer psychologically contained overseas.

The attacker is no longer merely another belligerent state.

American civilians become direct victims.

Domestic infrastructure becomes part of the battlefield.

And the political demand becomes:

> **What are you going to do to protect us?**

That can expand political permission for retaliation and domestic security measures extremely quickly.

It can also make restraint much harder.

## 🍊 So "He's Embarrassed" Isn't Enough

Embarrassment remains a possible explanation.

Perhaps the administration simply does not want to acknowledge that an adversary penetrated American infrastructure.

But there are larger institutional incentives to consider.

Public attribution could affect:

- congressional war-powers arguments;
- additional funding requests;
- demands for retaliation;
- public perceptions of whether the war is making Americans safer;
- scrutiny of domestic infrastructure preparedness;
- questions about whether retaliation was foreseeable;
- pressure for new surveillance or cybersecurity authorities;
- negotiations with Iran;
- and the administration's ability to control the narrative around how and why the conflict continues.

None establishes why Trump is behaving as he is.

Together they make:

> **he's embarrassed**

an inadequate default explanation.

The more useful question is:

> **What changes legally, politically, financially and psychologically the moment the President says that Iran attacked essential civilian infrastructure on American soil?**

Quite a lot, potentially.

Which is precisely why it is worth noticing that he hasn't.

## 🏺 Everyone Learns War Locally

There is a temptation, when looking at unfamiliar military behaviour, to treat it as either completely novel or somehow culturally mysterious.

Neither is especially useful.

Everyone learns war locally.

States inherit:

- geography;
- neighbours;
- previous invasions;
- military victories;
- catastrophic defeats;
- technologies;
- institutions;
- alliances;
- political constraints;
- and stories about what their predecessors discovered the hard way.

Strategic culture is not destiny.

But neither is military doctrine invented from scratch every generation.

A state inherits a library of remembered problems, remembered humiliations, remembered victories and remembered tricks that seemed to work.

Modern doctrine then updates that library for new technology and new enemies.

Iran is no exception.

## 🏹 The Useful Question Is Not Whether Cyber Is Ancient Persia With Wi-Fi

The useful question is not whether modern Iranian cyber strategy is **ancient Persian warfare in digital form**.

It obviously isn't.

The Achaemenids did not have programmable logic controllers.

The Parthians were not credential stuffing.

Shah Tahmasp was not running phishing infrastructure.

The interesting question is whether contemporary Iranian planners are working inside a much longer strategic inheritance in which materially powerful opponents repeatedly had to be managed through some combination of:

- indirection;
- mobility;
- strategic depth;
- patience;
- deception;
- diplomacy;
- manipulation of expectations;
- cost imposition;
- selective engagement;
- and making an adversary fight under conditions it did not choose.

That does not prove authorship of any modern cyber operation.

It tells us something about why particular strategic objectives may make sense to Iranian planners.

## 👑 Achaemenid Persia: War Was Larger Than The Battlefield

The Achaemenid Empire is useful precisely because reducing Persian power to individual battles misses much of the imperial machinery.

The empire governed enormous distances and highly diverse populations.

Military power existed alongside:

- diplomacy;
- provincial administration;
- local elites;
- taxation and tribute;
- communications networks;
- intelligence and reporting;
- roads;
- logistics;
- and political accommodation.

The teaching point is not:

> **Achaemenids invented hybrid warfare.**

They did not.

It is:

> **Iranian history contains very old examples of power being exercised through political, informational, logistical and military systems simultaneously.**

## 🐎 Parthia: Don't Fight Rome The Way Rome Wants You To

At Carrhae in 53 BCE, a much smaller Parthian force under Surena catastrophically defeated the Roman army of Marcus Licinius Crassus.

The important thing is not merely that Rome lost.

It is **how the problem was solved**.

Surena relied heavily upon mobile horse archers alongside heavily armoured cavalry.

The Romans expected the missile attack eventually to exhaust itself.

Surena had brought a large mobile ammunition supply carried by camels.

The arrows kept coming.

The famous Parthian ability to fire while withdrawing became almost a visual metaphor for the wider problem:

> **retreating does not necessarily mean losing.**

The Parthian achievement was an alignment of:

**terrain + mobility + logistics + weapons + adversary expectations.**

### 📚 Start Here

- **[Encyclopaedia Iranica — Carrhae](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/carrhae-harran-town-in-mesopotamia-where-in-may-53-b/)** — scholarly account of Surena's campaign, cavalry, ammunition logistics and Roman defeat.
- **[Encyclopaedia Iranica — Orodes II](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/orodes-ii/)** — additional discussion of Parthian warfare and Surena.
- **Plutarch, *Life of Crassus*** — indispensable ancient narrative source, but not neutral CCTV footage.

Then ask:

> **Which side successfully imposed its preferred form of warfare on the other?**

## 🏜️ Safavid Iran: Sometimes The Country Is Part Of The Weapon

The Safavids learned painfully at Chaldiran in 1514 what could happen when cavalry confronted Ottoman firearms and artillery under conditions favourable to the Ottoman army.

Subsequent Safavid strategy changed.

Under Shah Tahmasp I, avoiding the opponent's preferred decisive engagement could itself become strategy.

Delay, scorched earth, attacks upon logistics and strategic depth made repeated Ottoman incursions difficult to sustain.

Time can become a weapon.

Distance can become a weapon.

Logistics can become a weapon.

### 📚 Start Here

- **[Encyclopaedia Iranica — Chaldiran](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/calderan-battle/)** — the 1514 Safavid defeat and Ottoman military advantages.
- **[Encyclopaedia Iranica — Peace of Amasya](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/amasya-peace/)** — Safavid delay and scorched-earth tactics, Ottoman supply problems and the eventual settlement.

Then ask:

> **What happens when denying somebody victory is easier than defeating their army?**

## ⚖️ Qajar Iran: What Do You Learn From Losing Territory?

Qajar Iran confronted expanding Russian power in the Caucasus and persistent British involvement in Iranian affairs.

The Russo-Persian wars ended with major territorial losses.

Military weakness did not eliminate strategy.

It changed what strategy had to accomplish.

Diplomatic balancing, concessions, resistance, external alignment, internal reform and attempts to preserve autonomy all became parts of the survival problem.

Strategic cultures remember humiliation as well as victory.

## 🌍 Pahlavi Iran: Neutrality Does Not Necessarily Protect You

Iran declared neutrality during the Second World War.

Britain and the Soviet Union invaded anyway in August 1941.

The episode supplies another historical problem:

> **What does a state learn when declaring itself outside somebody else's war does not stop stronger powers deciding that its territory and infrastructure are strategically necessary?**

> **Iranian perceptions of foreign interference appeared from nowhere.**

## 🩸 The Iran–Iraq War: The Modern Furnace

For contemporary Iranian military doctrine, the Iran–Iraq War is vastly more important than Carrhae.

Iran experienced invasion, enormous casualties, attacks on cities, chemical warfare, economic pressure, international isolation, and the perception that much of the outside world was willing to tolerate Iraqi aggression when strategically convenient.

Modern Iranian doctrine was profoundly shaped by that experience.

The strategic problem became:

> **How does Iran deter or survive an opponent that may be richer, technologically superior and conventionally stronger?**

### 📚 Start Here

- **[Michael Connell — *Iran's Military Doctrine*, The Iran Primer](https://iranprimer.usip.org/sites/default/files/Iran_s%20Military%20Doctrine.pdf)** — the Iran–Iraq War as formative experience and Iranian adaptation to technologically superior adversaries.
- **[RAND — *The Future of Warfare*, Volume 3](https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/research_reports/RR2800/RR2849z3/RAND_RR2849z3.pdf)** — broader treatment of asymmetric strategies used to offset stronger conventional capabilities.

Spend disproportionate time here if the objective is understanding modern Iranian military behaviour.

Ancient history provides comparison.

The Iran–Iraq War provides institutional memory within living organisations.

## 🕸️ Modern Iran: Make Superiority Expensive

Modern Iranian doctrine has invested heavily in asymmetric approaches involving combinations of:

- missiles;
- drones;
- maritime disruption;
- partners and proxies;
- dispersed forces;
- strategic depth;
- cyber operations;
- intelligence;
- information operations;
- economic pressure;
- and the ability to threaten costs across several domains simultaneously.

The objective does not necessarily have to be:

> **defeat the United States in a conventional war.**

A more achievable objective can be:

> **make the cost of continuing exceed the political value of continuing.**

That makes the adversary's **political willingness to continue** part of the battlespace.

## 🧠 The Adversary Is Also A System To Be Learned

If an adversary is materially stronger than you, information about **how that adversary makes decisions** becomes extremely valuable.

You want to know:

- what frightens it;
- what embarrasses it;
- what makes its population angry;
- what makes its population indifferent;
- what produces congressional resistance;
- what produces demands for retaliation;
- what causes markets to move;
- what forces infrastructure spending;
- what divides allies;
- what creates media attention;
- and what can happen without crossing the threshold for a much larger response.

That is not merely propaganda.

It is strategic reconnaissance of a political system.

A small cyber operation can generate:

**technical resilience + institutional coordination + political thresholds + media behaviour + public psychology + escalation tolerance**

The attacker learns from the machine.

It can also learn from the press conference afterwards.

## 🌊 Which Is Why Small Infrastructure Incidents May Matter

Suppose an actor interferes with a water system but causes limited damage.

The material effect may be small.

The informational return may not be.

The operation can reveal how quickly operators notice, whether manual fallback works, when federal agencies become involved, whether incidents are correlated nationally, whether Congress asks questions, whether the President comments, whether the public becomes frightened, and whether the incident changes military decision-making.

If the actor repeats the process, it receives another observation.

Then another.

Then another.

> **Each repetition can teach the attacker more precisely where the defender's technical, political and psychological thresholds actually lie.**

That does not require premature attribution.

It requires recognising that **uncertainty itself is part of the battlespace**.

---

## 🎯 You Do Not Have To Destroy The Stronger Power

Carrhae is useful here without pretending Carrhae and cyber operations are the same thing.

Surena did not need to become better Roman infantry than Rome.

He needed to understand what Roman infantry required in order to exercise its advantages — and then deny it those conditions.

That is the transferable strategic principle.

A modern weaker power does not necessarily need to outspend, out-fly, out-compute or outgun the United States.

It can ask:

> **What happens if we make the systems upon which American superiority depends politically, economically or psychologically expensive to use?**

The weapon is not necessarily the thing that explodes.

Sometimes the weapon is **the decision you force the opponent to make**.

---

## 🪞 And Everybody Else Does This Too

None of this is uniquely Persian.

Britain learns war partly through an island, maritime and imperial strategic history.

Russia inherits strategic memories involving enormous land borders, invasion, depth and catastrophic continental warfare.

The United States inherits continental security, industrial scale, expeditionary warfare, technological superiority and the political memory of Pearl Harbor and 9/11.

Israel, China, France, Japan, Ukraine and others have their own strategic archives.

Everyone learns war locally.

Those histories do not mechanically determine present behaviour.

They influence which problems institutions notice first, which solutions feel familiar, which analogies officers reach for, and which failures they are particularly determined not to repeat.

The mistake is not looking at strategic culture.

The mistake is treating strategic culture as fate.

---

## 🧭 History Gives Us Priors, Not Attribution

If an operation involves deception, strategic patience, asymmetric cost, psychological pressure, ambiguous attribution, exploitation of superior-power vulnerabilities, or manipulation of expectations, we cannot say:

> **Aha. Very Persian. Therefore Iran.**

Absolutely not.

Other states and criminal organisations use these techniques too.

Historical comparison instead asks:

> **Would this objective make strategic sense inside the military and political tradition of the actor we are considering?**

That adjusts plausibility.

It does not establish identity.

History gives us **priors**.

Forensics, intelligence, financial evidence, infrastructure, human sources and operational mistakes give us **attribution**.

Do not swap one for the other.

## 🎥 If You Prefer Watching Or Listening

Good historical orientation does not have to begin with an academic monograph.

Prefer:

- university lectures;
- museum lectures;
- scholarly interviews;
- historians discussing their own research;
- archival documentaries that identify their sources;
- and talks where disagreement between historians is acknowledged.

Useful search topics include:

- **Achaemenid Persia beyond the Greek sources**
- **Battle of Carrhae / Surena / Parthian warfare**
- **Safavid–Ottoman warfare / Shah Tahmasp**

Be cautious with videos promising to explain:

> **THE PERSIAN WAY OF WAR**

in twelve minutes over dramatic music.

We are trying to understand strategic history.

We are not selecting a civilisation bonus in *Age of Empires*.

## 📣 Questions Journalists Could Ask

The competing hypotheses become considerably more useful if they generate questions capable of distinguishing between them.

The useful question is not simply:

> **Was it Iran, yes or no?**

Better questions identify the boundary between:

**what is known, what is assessed, what is releasable, what is being mitigated, and what somebody has decided not to say.**

Journalists should not demand operational details that would expose defensive weaknesses or intelligence sources.

The objective is accountability.

Not publishing an attacker's troubleshooting guide.

### 🍊 White House

Ask about presidential knowledge, policy thresholds and escalation consequences:

- Has the President been briefed on an assessment that Iranian or Iranian-affiliated actors may be responsible for any recent water-system incidents?
- Is the President's public reluctance to attribute these incidents consistent with the current intelligence assessment, or does the administration believe attribution remains unresolved?
- Has the administration established a threshold at which cyber interference with civilian water infrastructure would require a national-security response?
- Would public attribution alter the administration's military or diplomatic posture?
- Has concern about escalation influenced what the White House is prepared to say publicly?
- Were increased cyber risks to domestic critical infrastructure included in planning for hostilities with Iran?
- What additional protection was provided to vulnerable civilian infrastructure as the conflict escalated?

### 🕸️ FBI

Ask about investigative attribution and evidentiary confidence:

- Is the Bureau investigating affected water incidents as potentially related events or primarily as separate compromises?
- Has the FBI identified technical or behavioural commonalities across incidents in different states?
- Has the Bureau assessed whether a common operator, access broker, contractor or sponsor may be involved?
- What confidence threshold would be required before publicly attributing these incidents to a state or state-affiliated actor?
- Is the present obstacle lack of evidence, conflicting evidence, or evidence that cannot safely be released?
- Has the Bureau identified financial transactions, purchased access, intermediary tasking or criminal-service providers?
- Are investigators distinguishing between whoever obtained access, whoever operated inside the system, and whoever may ultimately have commissioned or benefited from the activity?

The person at the keyboard is not necessarily the customer.

### 🧰 CISA / DHS

Ask about campaign structure, exposure and defensive preparation:

- Do affected organisations share technologies, vendors, remote-access arrangements or configurations that could explain clustering without common sponsorship?
- How many potentially exposed PLCs or equivalent industrial-control devices have been identified nationally?
- Does the federal government know how many remain internet-accessible?
- Were affected operators warned before the incidents that their specific equipment or configurations were vulnerable?
- Has CISA observed overlapping reconnaissance against energy, chemical, food, manufacturing or other sectors?
- Does current activity look primarily like opportunistic scanning, deliberate victim selection, pre-positioning, signalling, or attempted disruption?
- Are there cases in which attackers appear capable of producing greater effects than they actually caused?

### 🚰 EPA

Ask about water-sector preparedness and regulatory reality:

- How many affected or potentially exposed utilities had previously been informed that their industrial-control systems were vulnerable?
- Does EPA maintain, or have access to, an adequate national inventory of vulnerable control equipment?
- How many utilities cannot implement recommended mitigations because of cost, staffing or technical constraints?
- Have utilities requested federal assistance that could not be provided before an incident occurred?
- Were increased cyber risks incorporated into emergency planning after the conflict escalated?
- What proportion of affected systems continued through manual fallback?
- How long could those fallbacks realistically have been sustained?

### ⚡ Department Of Energy

Ask whether the same activity is visible beyond water:

- Has DOE observed reconnaissance or attempted access against energy-sector industrial-control systems using techniques similar to recent water incidents?
- Are operators being warned about a specific campaign, a general increase in opportunistic activity, or both?
- Has DOE identified actors obtaining access and retaining it without immediately producing operational effects?
- Does DOE assess that current activity represents preparation for possible future disruption?
- Are energy-sector incidents being correlated with activity reported across other sectors?

### 🛰️ NSA / U.S. Cyber Command

Ask about assessment and doctrine, not sources and methods:

- Does U.S. policy distinguish between destructive cyberattack, operational disruption, reconnaissance and demonstrative access to civilian infrastructure?
- Can cyber activity deliberately remain publicly unattributed as part of escalation management?
- Can limited interference with industrial systems constitute coercive signalling even where physical damage is minimal?
- Can an actor's decision not to exercise its full available capability inform assessments of intent?
- How does the government assess activity where technical operators may be criminals or contractors but ultimate tasking remains uncertain?

### ⚖️ Department Of Justice

Ask about intermediaries, contractors and access markets:

- Does DOJ have evidence that access to American critical infrastructure is being sold or transferred between criminal and state-linked actors?
- How does DOJ distinguish financially motivated intrusion from state tasking where the same operator serves several customers?
- Can an initially criminal intrusion later become state-directed through purchase, tasking or transfer of access?
- Are investigators following financial relationships alongside malware and infrastructure indicators?
- Where contractors perform both commissioned and criminal work, how does DOJ assess whether apparently legitimate payments materially sustain the wider capability?

### 🏛️ Congress

Ask questions connecting attribution, authorisation, appropriations and domestic consequences:

- Have relevant committees received a classified attribution assessment concerning the water incidents?
- Would confirmed Iranian interference with civilian infrastructure on U.S. territory alter members' positions on war powers or authorisation?
- Would it alter members' positions on additional appropriations?
- Has Congress been asked to fund domestic infrastructure hardening as part of the financial response to the conflict?
- What assessment has Congress received of foreseeable cyber retaliation against the American homeland?
- Has the executive requested cybersecurity, surveillance or emergency powers as a consequence?
- What limits would Congress place on those authorities?

### 🏛️ State Governments

Ask what governments actually knew about local exposure:

- Before the incidents, did the state know which water systems used potentially vulnerable PLCs or remote-access configurations?
- When did the state receive federal warnings?
- When were those warnings passed to operators?
- Did every affected utility receive them?
- Were remediation resources available to small or rural utilities?
- Has the state identified additional exposed systems since the incidents began?
- Are apparently separate incidents being treated as potentially related?

### 🌊 Water Utilities

Do not pressure operators to disclose configurations that would make another compromise easier.

Ask about impact, warning and resilience:

- When did you first become aware of the compromise?
- Which operational functions were affected, in broad terms?
- Did the attacker appear capable of causing greater disruption than actually occurred?
- Did you receive warnings before the incident?
- Were recommended mitigations technically and financially achievable?
- How long could manual operation have been maintained safely?
- Did the incident affect pressure, alarms, treatment, pumping, monitoring or other physical processes?
- Has the utility received additional funding or technical assistance since the incident?

The public deserves to understand the effect.

It does not need the password.

### 🌍 State Department

Ask about signalling and diplomacy:

- Has suspected cyber interference with civilian infrastructure been raised directly with Iran or through intermediaries?
- Has Washington privately communicated that particular categories of cyber activity would cross an escalation threshold?
- Are cyber operations part of current diplomatic or de-escalation discussions?
- Could privately communicated attribution serve a deterrent purpose without public attribution?
- Has Iran communicated, directly or indirectly, that particular cyber activity was intended as warning or demonstration rather than maximum damage?

### 💣 Department Of Defense

Ask about the relationship between foreign military escalation and homeland infrastructure risk:

- Did planning for operations against Iran include an assessment of likely cyber retaliation against American civilian infrastructure?
- Were civilian agencies given specific warning about increased risks before escalation?
- Has the character of Iranian or Iran-linked cyber activity changed following particular military operations?
- Does DoD assess that attacks on civilian critical infrastructure could form part of Iranian deterrence or coercion?
- Has cyber-retaliation risk affected military decision-making?

## 🔬 Ask The Same Question Twice

Equivalent questions should be asked at different institutions.

**White House:** Is attribution unresolved?

**FBI:** Is the evidentiary threshold unmet?

**CISA:** Are the incidents technically related?

**EPA:** Were utilities warned because of a specific threat?

**State:** Has the issue been raised diplomatically?

**DoD:** Is Iranian cyber retaliation incorporated into current threat planning?

**Congress:** Has a classified assessment been provided?

Individually, each answer may be cautious.

Together, they can reveal where uncertainty actually sits.

That is considerably more informative than spending three press conferences trying to make somebody say the magic word **IRGC**.

## 🧿 Listen To The Verbs

There is a significant difference between:

> **We have no evidence linking these incidents to Iran.**

and:

> **We are not prepared to attribute these incidents publicly.**

and:

> **The investigation remains ongoing.**

and:

> **We refer you to another agency on attribution.**

Those statements are not interchangeable.

Neither are:

> **We don't know.**

and:

> **We aren't telling you.**

The entire orange problem may ultimately live somewhere inside that distinction.

## 🧠 Hypothesis Twelve: Trump May Simply Be Trump

This remains available.

Not every rhetorical inconsistency represents sophisticated signalling, intelligence constraints, contractor architecture or secret diplomacy.

Political leaders contradict themselves, improvise, misunderstand briefings, respond differently depending upon who asked the question, become attached to particular narratives, and say strange things.

Any analysis that requires every presidential utterance to be the visible tip of an exquisitely coordinated strategy will eventually disappear up its own arse.

Human inconsistency remains in the model.

## 🔬 How Do We Tell?

Do not choose a hypothesis because it makes the best story.

Watch what happens next.

Evidence favouring **uncertain attribution** would include continuing cautious federal language and eventual attribution elsewhere.

Evidence favouring **deliberate ambiguity** would include stronger private, allied or subsequently disclosed attribution without equivalent presidential rhetoric.

Evidence favouring **escalation management** would include quiet defensive measures, private warnings or negotiated restraint while public attribution remains muted.

Evidence favouring **contractor/proxy complexity** would include financial links, access-market evidence, tasking records, overlapping customers or operators whose apparent identities do not explain victim selection.

Evidence favouring **audience-sensitive targeting** would require a pattern across the denominator: politically convenient targets should be disproportionately exploited or publicised relative to the wider pool of available compromises.

Evidence favouring **strategic learning** would include repeated probes whose technical effects remain limited while target selection, timing or methods evolve in ways suggesting adaptation to previous responses.

And evidence favouring **Trump just being weird** will presumably continue arriving at industrial scale.

## 🌊 The Useful Rule

We should resist turning anomalous behaviour immediately into hidden certainty.

The anomaly tells us where to look.

It does not tell us what we will find.

For now:

> **The President's public posture toward suspected cyber interference with American water infrastructure appears unusually restrained relative to his wider rhetoric about Iran.**

That is an observation.

There are several plausible explanations.

Some are mundane.

Some are strategic.

Some are institutional.

Some concern attribution.

Some concern escalation.

Some concern domestic constitutional politics.

Some concern markets of intermediaries and commissioned capability.

Some concern propaganda and audience psychology.

Some concern the strategic history through which Iran understands stronger adversaries.

Several could be true simultaneously.

And if Iranian-linked actors are conducting coercive probes, uncertainty does not necessarily deprive those operations of value.

The attacker may still learn:

**what frightened the defender;**

**what the defender fixed;**

**what the defender admitted;**

**what produced retaliation;**

and

**what the defender was frightened to say out loud.**

That is why the orange being weird is useful.

Not because weirdness proves the theory.

Because weirdness tells us **which assumptions deserve another look**.
