# 🍊 Why Is the Orange Being Weird?
**First created:** 2026-08-20 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-14  
*Why the presidential posture around Iran-linked attacks on American infrastructure is increasingly interesting as a governance problem — and why the contradiction matters more than guessing the motive.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Something about the public posture is weird.

That still does **not** mean something secret and spectacular must therefore be happening.

The anomaly is now more specific than it was in August.

The administration formally says it wants to:

- harden American critical infrastructure;
- deny adversaries initial access;
- improve resilience across water, energy, telecommunications and hospitals;
- coordinate government and industry;
- increase offensive and defensive cyber capability;
- and impose consequences on adversaries.

At the same time, the administration also says it wants to:

- streamline cyber regulation;
- reduce compliance burden;
- preserve private-sector agility;
- and avoid treating cybersecurity as a costly regulatory checklist.

Those positions are not automatically contradictory.

They become more difficult to reconcile when:

- more than 100 American water and wastewater systems are disclosed as targeted;
- some attacks reach operational technology;
- some produce physical-process effects;
- Iranian government-linked activity reportedly expands into electricity and telecommunications;
- AI reduces the cost of scanning and exploitation;
- and the President publicly rejects Iran as the explanation for the Minnesota water attacks without publicly identifying an alternative explanation.

That gives us a sharper question:

> **Why is the President willing to preserve domestic regulatory freedom when the measurable externality of that freedom may be additional attack surface for states the United States is actively fighting?**

This node does not answer that question by guessing what is in Donald Trump's head.

It treats presidential behaviour as one variable inside a wider system.

The useful object is the governance contradiction.

## 🍊 First: What Is Actually Weird Now?

The anomaly is no longer simply:

```text
Iran possible
+
Trump quiet
```

The current structure is:

```text
FORMAL CYBER STRATEGY:
harden critical infrastructure

FORMAL REGULATORY STRATEGY:
reduce cyber compliance burden

WAR ENVIRONMENT:
Iran-linked / suspected-Iran attacks on US infrastructure

OPERATIONAL RECORD:
100+ water systems targeted
+
some physical OT effects
+
energy / telecom targeting expansion

PRESIDENTIAL INCIDENT RESPONSE:
public rejection of Iran attribution in Minnesota
without publicly supplying an alternative actor
```

That is more interesting.

The contradiction is not:

> Trump does not care about cyber.

The administration plainly has cyber policy.

The contradiction is:

> **What kind of cyber defence does this administration think can succeed while preserving a strong presumption against mandatory domestic constraint?**

That is the puzzle.

## 🧭 The Administration's Own Strategy Creates The Test

President Trump's March 2026 Cyber Strategy says the United States should remain unrivalled in cyberspace.

It calls for:

- offensive and defensive cyber capability;
- coordination across government and industry;
- stronger critical-infrastructure resilience;
- secure technology supply chains;
- denial of adversary initial access;
- and national-level responsibility rather than simply devolving risk to states and localities.

It expressly names:

- energy;
- finance;
- telecommunications;
- data centres;
- water utilities;
- hospitals;
- and operational-technology supply chains.

The same strategy also contains a pillar titled:

> **Promote Common Sense Regulation**

and says the administration will streamline regulations to reduce compliance burdens and give the private sector greater agility.

Those commitments can coexist where regulation genuinely creates duplication without improving security.

They become harder to reconcile where an exploited weakness persists because nobody is required to remove it.

The administration therefore has to answer its own design problem:

```text
HOW DO YOU DENY ADVERSARIES INITIAL ACCESS
WHILE PRESERVING OPERATOR DISCRETION
OVER WHETHER TO REMOVE THE CONDITIONS
THAT PROVIDE INITIAL ACCESS?
```

That question belongs to the architecture.

Not to personality analysis.

## 🧮 Streamlining Is Not The Same As Deregulating Everything

There is a legitimate case for rationalising cyber regulation.

GAO reported in July that:

- 37 federal agencies had issued 117 cybersecurity regulations affecting private entities across nine critical-infrastructure sectors;
- 80 of those regulations contained reporting requirements duplicative in type of at least one other regulation;
- and the administration intended to streamline the system.

That is a real governance problem.

Duplicate reporting can consume:

- compliance staff;
- legal capacity;
- technical time;
- incident-response bandwidth;
- and money

without necessarily reducing exploitability.

So:

```text
DUPLICATIVE REPORTING
≠
GOOD SECURITY
```

But another proposition is equally important:

```text
BAD REGULATION EXISTS
≠
MANDATORY SECURITY REQUIREMENTS ARE USELESS
```

The live question is whether the administration is distinguishing:

```text
REPORTING BURDEN
```

from:

```text
SECURITY OBLIGATION
```

and:

```text
ADMINISTRATIVE DUPLICATION
```

from:

```text
REQUIREMENT TO REMOVE A KNOWN EXPLOITABLE CONDITION
```

Those are not the same thing.

## 🚰 The Water Wave Makes The Trade-Off Concrete

This is where the abstract regulatory argument becomes physical.

By late August, CISA had disclosed that more than **100 internet-exposed water and wastewater systems** were targeted during July.

The campaign included:

- operational-technology access;
- controller interaction;
- configuration changes;
- loss of monitoring or control;
- movement to manual operation;
- pressure effects;
- and some flooding.

The exact depth varied by system.

Formal attribution varied by incident.

But the vulnerability pattern was no longer hypothetical.

A system exposed directly to the internet can turn a local configuration choice into a national-security externality.

That creates a governance problem:

```text
LOCAL OPERATOR SAVES COST / PRESERVES FLEXIBILITY
→ VULNERABLE CONFIGURATION REMAINS
→ HOSTILE ACTOR GAINS ACCESS CHEAPLY
→ LOCAL STAFF ABSORB INCIDENT
→ FEDERAL GOVERNMENT INVESTIGATES / ATTRIBUTES / RESPONDS
→ PUBLIC BEARS THE SECURITY COST
```

The operator and the nation do not necessarily experience the same cost-benefit calculation.

That is exactly the sort of problem regulation exists to solve.

## 🍊 The Minnesota Response Is Still Odd

On 31 July, Trump publicly rejected Iran as the explanation for the Minnesota water attacks.

He instead blamed Minnesota's government and Governor Tim Walz.

The White House did not publicly identify an alternative actor.

At the time:

- the FBI was investigating similar incidents across several states;
- technical experts were identifying similarities with prior Iran-linked activity;
- and the public federal attribution remained unresolved.

Presidential caution about attribution would be entirely defensible.

But:

```text
WE DO NOT YET HAVE ENOUGH EVIDENCE TO ATTRIBUTE
```

is different from:

```text
IT WAS NOT IRAN
```

The second is itself an attribution judgment.

It closes one branch of the tree.

If that conclusion was based on intelligence unavailable publicly, that is one explanation.

If it was political instinct, that is another.

If it was aimed at avoiding escalation pressure, that is another.

If it reflected a genuine inter-agency disagreement, that is another.

The important thing is that the presidential statement did **not** merely preserve uncertainty.

It narrowed it.

That makes the basis of the narrowing analytically important.

## 🧩 Hypothesis One: They Genuinely Know It Wasn't Iran

The simplest explanation remains available.

The President may have received intelligence that materially weakens the Iran attribution.

That intelligence might involve:

- technical telemetry;
- foreign-partner intelligence;
- infrastructure analysis;
- operator identity;
- intercepted communications;
- or evidence pointing elsewhere.

If so, the public record is incomplete.

That would explain the presidential certainty.

It would not yet explain why the alternative has not been supplied publicly.

There may be good reasons for that too.

This hypothesis remains open.

## 🕸️ Hypothesis Two: The White House And Technical Agencies Are Applying Different Standards

The United States government is not one brain.

Possible actors include:

- White House;
- FBI;
- CISA;
- NSA;
- Cyber Command;
- intelligence agencies;
- EPA;
- DHS;
- state governments;
- utilities;
- military commands;
- and allied intelligence services.

They may be answering different questions.

For example:

```text
CISA:
what vulnerability is being exploited?

FBI:
who conducted these intrusions?

INTELLIGENCE COMMUNITY:
which state or customer sits behind them?

WHITE HOUSE:
what attribution is politically useful or supportable now?

STATE GOVERNOR:
what should the public know about local risk?
```

Those questions can produce different language without anybody being dishonest.

The problem appears where the differences are not reconciled.

A state can tolerate classified disagreement.

Operators still need one usable threat model.

## 🪜 Hypothesis Three: Attribution Creates An Obligation

Suppose the President says:

> **Iran attacked American civilian water infrastructure during the war.**

That statement creates consequences.

Congress asks what threshold has been crossed.

Governors ask what the federal government failed to harden.

Utilities ask who will fund remediation.

Allies ask whether this is now a formally recognised retaliatory campaign against coalition civilian infrastructure.

Iran asks what response is coming.

The public asks:

> **How are they able to reach our drinking water while we are bombing them?**

Attribution therefore creates two obligations:

```text
EXTERNAL:
what are you going to do to Iran?

INTERNAL:
why was the infrastructure reachable?
```

Avoiding attribution can postpone both questions.

That does not prove that this is why the administration is cautious.

It explains why public certainty is politically expensive.

---

## 🌊 Hypothesis Four: Ambiguity Is A De-Escalation Channel

Cyber conflict permits a strange kind of mutual signalling.

A state can believe it knows who conducted an operation while declining to say so publicly.

That allows:

- private warning;
- covert retaliation;
- defensive hardening;
- diplomatic communication;
- or deliberate non-escalation.

The structure can be:

```text
I know
you know I know
you know I could say it
I choose not to
```

This is not absurd in strategic terms.

It can provide room to contain an incident.

If this explains the presidential posture, public restraint may be deliberate.

But that hypothesis sits awkwardly beside Trump's otherwise highly escalatory rhetoric toward Iran.

That does not disprove it.

It is why the contrast remains interesting.

---

## 📣 Hypothesis Five: Public Attribution Would Advertise Iranian Reach

Publicly saying:

> **Iran reached American water infrastructure across multiple states**

could itself create propaganda value for Iran.

The technical effects were often limited.

The headline would not be.

The administration may therefore prefer:

```text
FIX THE SYSTEMS
+
DENY THE PUBLICITY
```

rather than:

```text
MAXIMISE ATTENTION
```

That is a coherent possibility.

The September APT IRAN / AT&T episode strengthens the logic.

A real telecom outage was claimed by APT IRAN.

AT&T said the cause was attempted physical cable theft and rejected the cyber explanation.

That shows that actors can gain narrative value by attaching themselves to real American infrastructure problems.

Publicity can therefore be part of the battlefield.

## ⚡ Hypothesis Six: The Administration Prefers Offensive Cyber To Mandatory Resilience

The administration's cyber strategy emphasises both:

- hardening infrastructure;
- and imposing costs on adversaries.

The broader posture may therefore favour:

```text
OFFENSIVE DISRUPTION
+
PUBLIC-PRIVATE COORDINATION
+
MARKET / TECHNOLOGY ADAPTATION
```

over:

```text
BROAD MANDATORY SECURITY REGULATION
```

That would be a coherent doctrine.

It would also carry a specific risk.

Offensive cyber can punish or disrupt attackers.

It does not automatically remove:

- internet-exposed PLCs;
- weak vendor credentials;
- insecure remote access;
- missing segmentation;
- incomplete asset inventories;
- or poorly resourced small utilities.

Those are domestic conditions.

They remain available to the next attacker.

The question is therefore:

> **Is the administration substituting adversary punishment for domestic attack-surface reduction where both are required?**

That is currently a hypothesis.

It is testable.

## 🪙 The Externality Problem

The central economics are simple.

A private or local operator may rationally prefer not to spend money replacing or isolating vulnerable equipment.

The cost is immediate.

The attack may never happen.

But if it does:

- federal investigators respond;
- CISA issues guidance;
- intelligence agencies assess attribution;
- neighbouring systems increase readiness;
- public communications activate;
- insurers react;
- state governments spend money;
- and national political risk rises.

The economic loop becomes:

```text
AVOID COMPLIANCE / MODERNISATION COST
→ PRESERVE EXPLOITABLE CONFIGURATION
→ ADVERSARY GAINS ACCESS CHEAPLY
→ INCIDENT RESPONSE SOCIALISES THE COST
```

This is a classic security externality.

The administration does not need to love regulation to recognise it.

It does need some mechanism for internalising the cost.

The mechanism could be:

- regulation;
- insurance;
- procurement;
- liability;
- grants;
- standards;
- vendor requirements;
- market access;
- or some combination.

If none of those reliably remove the exposure, “agility” becomes another word for leaving the door open.

## 🧰 The Administration Is Not Doing Nothing

This matters.

The governance contradiction should not be exaggerated into:

> **Trump abolished cyber defence.**

That is unsupported.

The administration has taken several actions consistent with serious cyber concern.

Examples include:

- the March Cyber Strategy;
- the July **Gold Eagle Initiative** coordinating vulnerability information across government, critical infrastructure and open-source software;
- national-security-system cybersecurity policy;
- CISA and partner advisories;
- and continued federal involvement in critical-infrastructure defence.

Gold Eagle in particular is aimed at speeding detection and patching through coordinated government-industry processes.

That supports a real administration theory:

```text
MOVE FASTER
+
COORDINATE BETTER
+
REDUCE ADMINISTRATIVE DRAG
```

The live question is whether that model can reliably reach:

- small utilities;
- legacy OT;
- under-resourced municipalities;
- privately owned infrastructure;
- third-party integrators;
- and vendors whose insecure configuration creates national consequences.

The programme exists.

The outcome remains empirical.

## 📡 The Telecom Case Sharpens The Deregulation Question

The telecommunications layer is especially interesting.

Telecoms underpin:

- banking;
- water remote access;
- energy operations;
- emergency communications;
- hospitals;
- government;
- and military logistics.

GAO reported in July that an FCC reconsideration had rescinded an earlier interpretation requiring telecommunications carriers to implement specified cybersecurity measures and replaced it with a revised interpretation of the FCC's role.

That legal/administrative dispute should not be overstated.

But it belongs in the same structural question:

```text
CRITICAL DEPENDENCY
+
KNOWN HOSTILE INTEREST
+
LESS PRESCRIPTIVE FEDERAL CYBER OBLIGATION
=
WHAT MECHANISM NOW ENSURES THE SECURITY OUTCOME?
```

If the answer is:

> industry will adapt faster voluntarily

then the administration needs evidence that this works at the scale and speed of the threat.

## 🤖 AI Makes Voluntary Delay More Expensive

The September energy reporting adds another complication.

Attackers can use AI to:

- discover weak systems faster;
- generate or adapt scripts;
- automate reconnaissance;
- interact with OT protocols;
- and concentrate human effort on systems that respond.

This changes the timing.

A regulatory or market model that assumes operators have months to respond may face adversaries operating at machine-assisted speed.

The asymmetry becomes:

```text
ATTACKER AUTOMATES SEARCH
+
DEFENDER NEGOTIATES UPGRADE / BUDGET / VENDOR CHANGE
=
ATTACKER GETS MORE ATTEMPTS BEFORE REMEDIATION
```

That makes delay itself a security cost.

## 🧬 One War, Many Threat Ecosystems Makes Presidential Simplification Harder

The new dataset also explains why the White House may dislike a clean public story.

The war environment contains:

- Iran-linked OT pressure;
- probable criminal ransomware;
- China-linked hacker-for-hire activity;
- mass exploitation of shared software;
- supplier breaches;
- unattributed incidents;
- narrative ride-alongs;
- and genuine technical failures.

A President saying:

> **Iran is attacking American infrastructure**

may be directionally useful at campaign level while being wrong about particular incidents.

A President saying:

> **This is all local incompetence**

may be incidentally correct for some failures while obscuring a real Iran-linked campaign.

The correct national-security posture therefore requires tolerating an annoying sentence:

> **Some of this appears Iran-linked; some of it clearly is not; some remains unresolved; and all of it is consuming the same defence capacity.**

That is accurate.

It is not especially slogan-friendly.

## 🍊 Hypothesis Seven: The Political System Rewards Simpler Blame

The Minnesota response turned quickly into a partisan conflict.

Trump blamed state-level incompetence.

Governor Walz blamed weakened federal cyber capacity and pointed to the multi-state pattern.

That creates two domestic stories:

```text
STATE FAILURE
```

and:

```text
FOREIGN ATTACK + FEDERAL UNDER-PREPARATION
```

Both have political consequences.

The first keeps responsibility local.

The second nationalises it.

The President has an obvious political incentive to prefer the first where the facts permit it.

That does not establish that his attribution judgment was politically motivated.

It explains why the direction of the judgment matters.

## ⚖️ Hypothesis Eight: Attribution Raises The Domestic War-Authority Question

If Iran is formally blamed for attacks on civilian infrastructure inside the United States during an ongoing armed conflict, the political character of the war changes.

The public story becomes:

```text
US FORCES FIGHTING IRAN ABROAD
+
IRAN-LINKED EFFECTS ON US CIVILIAN INFRASTRUCTURE AT HOME
```

That invites questions about:

- war aims;
- domestic preparedness;
- congressional authority;
- spending;
- retaliation thresholds;
- civilian protection;
- and how long the conflict is expected to continue.

The cyber incidents do not answer those questions.

Attribution makes avoiding them harder.

## 🌍 The Alliance Layer Makes The Weirdness More Expensive

The upstream nodes now add another dimension.

America's power is partly stored in allied expectation.

Allies make plans based on assumptions about:

- US basing;
- intelligence;
- sanctions;
- military support;
- cyber attribution;
- logistics;
- and common political positions.

If the President repeatedly acts against previously coordinated allied interests, allies begin to hedge.

That can mean:

- duplicated capability;
- slower commitments;
- more cautious intelligence sharing;
- extra national contingency planning;
- and reluctance to expose civilian infrastructure or political capital on the assumption that Washington will hold its line.

This matters to the cyber problem because attribution and response are often coalition products.

A British generator goes offline.

US water systems are attacked.

Telecoms are probed.

Naval systems are mapped.

The useful response is not only national.

It is allied.

If Washington becomes the variable allies must plan around, the coalition pays a transaction cost.

## 🧱 Predictability Is Stored Power

A powerful state does not have to renegotiate every commitment from zero.

Partners act partly because they believe:

> **If Washington said this yesterday, it will probably still mean it tomorrow.**

That predictability is stored power.

It reduces:

- negotiation;
- hedging;
- duplicated planning;
- reassurance;
- and monitoring of the ally itself.

When predictability falls:

```text
SAME MILITARY CAPABILITY
+
LESS RELIABLE EXPECTATION
=
MORE EXPENSIVE LEADERSHIP
```

The United States can remain extraordinarily powerful while making its power more costly to use.

That cost eventually returns to Americans through:

- more US capability required;
- more US logistics;
- more US intelligence burden;
- more diplomatic work;
- and more money spent reproducing effects allies previously supplied with less friction.

This is why presidential unpredictability is not merely a style issue.

It can be a defence externality.

## 🌊 Iran Does Not Need To Cause The Alliance Seam

This distinction must stay explicit.

There is no need to claim:

> Iran created allied mistrust.

The useful question is:

> Can Iran exploit allied mistrust once it exists?

Yes.

So can Russia.

So can China.

So can criminal actors watching defenders become overloaded.

The sequence is:

```text
US / ALLIED POLICY DIVERGENCE
→ ALLIED HEDGING
→ SLOWER COMMON RESPONSE
→ WIDER OPPORTUNITY WINDOW

WIDER OPPORTUNITY WINDOW
≠
ADVERSARY CAUSED POLICY DIVERGENCE
```

That is enough to make the seam strategically relevant.

## 🍊 Hypothesis Nine: Trump May Be Optimising For Freedom Of Action

A common thread may connect several apparently inconsistent positions.

The administration appears to value:

- executive freedom of action;
- private-sector flexibility;
- reduced regulatory constraint;
- fast technology deployment;
- offensive capability;
- and the ability to alter policy quickly.

Those preferences can all be rational within a particular governing model.

The cost is that resilience often depends on the opposite qualities:

- standardisation;
- mandatory baselines;
- predictable coordination;
- institutional memory;
- stable alliance commitments;
- and constraints that stop one actor externalising risk onto everyone else.

The underlying tension may therefore be:

```text
FREEDOM OF ACTION
vs
SYSTEM RELIABILITY
```

Not because freedom is always bad.

Because resilience is partly produced by commitments that actors cannot casually abandon.

## 🪙 Optionality Has A Cost

From the perspective of one decision-maker, keeping options open can look powerful.

From the perspective of everyone who has to plan around that decision-maker, optionality can become uncertainty.

The loop is:

```text
LEADER PRESERVES MAXIMUM DISCRETION
→ PARTNERS CANNOT RELY ON STABLE COMMITMENT
→ PARTNERS HEDGE
→ COORDINATION BECOMES MORE EXPENSIVE
→ LEADER MUST SPEND MORE TO ACHIEVE SAME RESULT
```

This applies domestically too.

If private infrastructure operators preserve maximum discretion over security investment:

```text
OPERATOR PRESERVES FLEXIBILITY
→ NATIONAL BASELINE BECOMES UNEVEN
→ ADVERSARY SEARCHES FOR WEAKEST NODE
→ FEDERAL SYSTEM ABSORBS INCIDENT COST
```

The same governance tension appears at two scales.

That is interesting.

## 🧠 The Common Structure May Be Externalised Risk

The domestic and alliance problems now have a common shape.

### Domestic

```text
local / private operator
keeps flexibility
→ national government absorbs security externality
```

### Alliance

```text
US president
keeps policy flexibility
→ allies absorb planning externality
```

In both cases:

```text
one actor's optionality
=
another actor's uncertainty
```

And:

```text
another actor's uncertainty
=
system cost
```

This may be the strongest current explanation for why the node feels coherent.

It does not require a secret motive.

It describes an observable governance preference and its externalities.

## 🧮 Hypothesis Ten: The Administration Believes The Market Can Price The Risk Better Than Regulation

Another possible model is that:

- insurers;
- customers;
- investors;
- vendors;
- procurement;
- and commercial reputation

will discipline poor cyber practice more efficiently than federal mandates.

That is a coherent policy position.

Critical infrastructure creates problems for it.

The public cannot easily choose another:

- water utility;
- electricity grid;
- emergency telecom network;
- local hospital;
- or municipal control system.

Nor can an adversary's strategic benefit be fully priced by the operator being attacked.

The market may price:

```text
expected private loss
```

while the state cares about:

```text
national-security externality
```

Those numbers need not match.

That is where a pure market solution becomes difficult.

## 🧰 What Would Falsify The Governance-Contradiction Thesis?

The thesis should be testable.

It would weaken if the administration demonstrates that its preferred model reliably produces:

- rapid removal of internet-exposed OT;
- complete enough asset inventories;
- strong supplier controls;
- effective IT/OT segmentation;
- fast remediation at small utilities;
- enforceable vendor-security standards;
- low recurrence after warning;
- and a stable allied attribution and response framework.

In that world:

```text
less regulation
+
better outcomes
=
not a contradiction
```

It would also weaken if evidence shows that the Minnesota attacks were not Iran-linked and that the President's rejection rested on accurate intelligence.

The anomaly would then partly resolve.

---

## 🚨 What Would Strengthen The Governance-Contradiction Thesis?

It would strengthen if we see:

- repeated exploitation of the same known exposures after warnings;
- continued internet-facing OT after federal guidance;
- operators unable to afford or implement remediation;
- cyber requirements weakened without effective substitute mechanisms;
- widening attacks across water, energy and telecoms;
- federal agencies spending increasing resources compensating for preventable local exposure;
- political dismissal of technically supported attribution;
- allied cyber assessments diverging from White House messaging;
- allies hedging around US policy instability;
- or evidence that offensive action is being prioritised while domestic resilience remains structurally underfunded.

The strongest version would be:

```text
KNOWN EXPLOITABILITY
+
KNOWN ADVERSARY INTEREST
+
REPEATED INCIDENTS
+
POLICY CHOICE TO PRESERVE THE EXPOSURE
```

That would no longer be merely weird.

It would be a policy trade-off with a measurable national-security cost.

## 🚫 What This Node Does Not Claim

This node does not claim that:

- Trump secretly supports Iranian cyber activity;
- the administration has no cyber strategy;
- every deregulation measure weakens security;
- every federal cyber regulation is useful;
- every water incident was Iranian;
- Trump's Minnesota statement was knowingly false;
- classified intelligence does not explain the public posture;
- offensive cyber activity is a substitute for defence;
- market mechanisms cannot improve cybersecurity;
- every allied disagreement is caused by Trump;
- NATO has collapsed;
- Iran created alliance divergence;
- presidential unpredictability alone explains the threat environment;
- or one motive explains every apparently inconsistent action.

It argues something narrower:

> **The administration's observable preference for discretion, deregulation and policy optionality now sits in measurable tension with a threat environment in which resilience depends upon common baselines, predictable coordination, and reducing externalised risk.**

That contradiction is worth tracking.
