# 🗺️ Who Iran Sees As Inside The War  
**First created:** 2026-08-01 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-01  
*The operational coalition map is wider than the formal list of belligerents.*  

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

Western governments often describe involvement in narrow legal and political categories:

- direct belligerent;
- defensive support;
- collective self-defence;
- basing permission;
- logistics;
- air defence;
- maritime protection;
- sanctions;
- intelligence;
- or support to an ally.

Iran does not have to accept those categories.

Its practical question is simpler:

> Did this state attack Iran, enable attacks on Iran, protect the forces carrying them out, constrain Iran's ability to retaliate, or support the wider coalition sustaining the war?

That produces a wider map.

A state can insist that it is not formally at war while still appearing inside Iran's operational picture.

This distinction matters for cyber analysis because essential infrastructure may be targeted according to the adversary's map, not the target government's preferred description of its role.

---

## 🎯 The Core Belligerents  

### 🇮🇱 Israel  

Israel is a central direct belligerent.

It is not merely a supporting state, host state, or defensive participant.

For Iranian strategic analysis, Israel sits at the centre of the war alongside the United States.

Its military, intelligence, government, energy, communications, transport, health, and financial systems therefore sit inside the highest-risk category for Iranian and Iran-linked cyber activity.

### 🇺🇸 United States  

The United States is the other central direct belligerent.

US strikes, forces, bases, regional partnerships, intelligence architecture, logistics, and economic power place it at the centre of Iran's war map.

Iranian and Iran-linked cyber operations against the United States may therefore serve several functions at once:

- retaliation;
- disruption;
- reconnaissance;
- signalling;
- pre-positioning;
- coercion;
- data theft;
- and pressure on civilian confidence.

The relevant US attack surface includes federal systems, defence, energy, water, transport, health, banking, telecommunications, and thousands of locally operated public utilities.

---

## 🇬🇧 Britain Is Not Outside The Map  

The United Kingdom presents the clearest gap between domestic description and adversary perception.

The British government has repeatedly described its role as limited and defensive.

It has said that:

- British aircraft were conducting coordinated defensive operations;
- British forces were intercepting Iranian drones and missiles;
- the UK was supporting collective self-defence;
- US forces were permitted to use British bases for specific operations against Iranian missile capabilities;
- RAF Fairford and Diego Garcia were included in that support;
- British forces were operating across the Eastern Mediterranean and parts of the Gulf region;
- and British industry was supporting Gulf partners under Iranian attack.

The government has also insisted that this did not amount to joining the wider offensive war.

That distinction may matter in Parliament and in legal argument.

It does not bind Iran.

The IRGC has publicly warned that Britain would become a legitimate target if it supported US military operations against Iran. It has also referred to the use of British airfields and bases as evidence of operational cooperation.

Iran has already struck close to British personnel at RAF Akrotiri in Cyprus.

The practical Iranian reading is therefore likely to be:

```text
British aircraft intercept Iranian weapons
+
British bases support US missions
+
British forces protect regional allies
+
British industry reinforces those allies
=
Britain is inside the hostile coalition
```

Britain can call that defensive support.

Iran can still treat it as participation.

For this pack, Britain belongs firmly inside the tracking perimeter.

## 🏝️ Cyprus And The Sovereign Base Problem  

Cyprus occupies two overlapping positions.

The Republic of Cyprus can argue that British sovereign base areas are not under Cypriot operational control.

Iran may understand that distinction.

It may also regard the island as a single operational space containing foreign military infrastructure used by states supporting the war.

Iranian officials have explicitly raised concerns about foreign bases in Cyprus, including RAF Akrotiri.

This creates a layered exposure:

- the British sovereign bases;
- the Republic of Cyprus;
- civilian infrastructure near military sites;
- ports, airports, energy, communications, and logistics;
- and systems supporting foreign forces.

The constitutional map and the target map are not necessarily the same map.

---

## 🌊 Gulf And Regional States  

Iran has attacked or threatened states across the Gulf and wider region during the war.

The relevant group includes:

- Bahrain;
- Kuwait;
- Qatar;
- United Arab Emirates;
- Saudi Arabia;
- Oman;
- Jordan;
- Iraq;
- and other states hosting US, British, or coalition forces.

Their status is not identical.

Some host foreign bases.

Some intercept Iranian weapons.

Some protect regional infrastructure.

Some provide logistics or intelligence.

Some have participated more directly in joint operations.

Iran may sometimes claim that it is targeting only foreign military facilities located inside those states.

That distinction becomes weaker where the host state actively supports interception, targeting, basing, or coalition operations.

From a cyber perspective, the likely target set extends beyond military networks to:

- energy production;
- oil and gas facilities;
- desalination;
- ports and airports;
- banking and payments;
- telecommunications;
- government administration;
- transport;
- and systems supporting foreign bases.

The Gulf is therefore not a secondary cyber theatre.

It is part of the central operational environment.

---

## 🇫🇷 France  

France sits inside the wider hostile strategic camp but below the United States, Israel, and Britain in the public Iranian targeting hierarchy.

France has participated in:

- regional air defence;
- protection of French forces and citizens;
- support to Cyprus;
- diplomatic coordination with Britain and Germany;
- and planning for maritime security and freedom of navigation.

French support may be described as defensive and limited.

Iran may still see it as helping to contain Iranian retaliation and preserve the coalition's operational freedom.

France therefore belongs inside the monitoring perimeter even where no public evidence places it in the same category as a direct attacking state.

The relevant distinction is:

> involved enough to monitor closely; not automatically equivalent to a first-order belligerent.

---

## 🇩🇪 Germany  

Germany is also part of the wider strategic coalition.

Its role has included:

- political alignment through the E3 and NATO;
- military support to allied air defence;
- protection of deployed forces;
- and support for regional defensive operations.

Germany has publicly maintained that it is not a direct party to offensive action against Iran.

Iran may nevertheless view German air defence, military support, sanctions, intelligence cooperation, and NATO alignment as part of the architecture constraining it.

Germany therefore belongs inside the cyber tracking perimeter, particularly for:

- government systems;
- defence;
- energy;
- transport;
- telecommunications;
- banking;
- and deployed military infrastructure.

Again, inclusion does not mean equivalence.

It means the state may appear inside Iran's wider war map.

---

## 🇮🇹 Italy, 🇧🇬 Bulgaria, And European Basing States  

Italy and Bulgaria illustrate how basing can move a state into the operational picture without a formal declaration of war.

Iran has raised concerns where European territory, airfields, tankers, personnel, or logistics support US military operations.

Bulgaria was directly pressed over the temporary deployment of US tanker aircraft and personnel at Bezmer air base.

Italy has faced scrutiny over the role of US bases and support flights.

The governments involved may insist that:

- the missions are technical;
- the support is defensive;
- offensive strikes are not authorised;
- or national forces are not participating.

Iran may instead ask whether those arrangements make US operations possible.

For this pack, European basing states should be tracked where there is credible evidence that their territory, airspace, logistics, or infrastructure support the campaign.

---

## 🇬🇷 Greece, 🇳🇱 Netherlands, 🇪🇸 Spain, 🇹🇷 Turkey And Others  

A wider group may enter the map through:

- NATO air defence;
- maritime protection;
- naval deployments;
- protection of Cyprus;
- shipping security;
- intelligence sharing;
- logistics;
- or support for freedom of navigation.

This may include Greece, the Netherlands, Spain, Turkey, Canada, Japan, and other coalition or partner states.

These countries should not be flattened into one category.

Their inclusion should depend on what they are actually doing.

The pack should therefore record:

- military role;
- basing role;
- defensive role;
- maritime role;
- sanctions role;
- intelligence role;
- and the date on which that role was assessed.

A country can move up or down the map as the war changes.

## 🧭 A Practical Tiering System  

For tracking purposes, the coalition map can be divided into four broad tiers.

### Tier One — Direct Belligerents  

- Israel  
- United States  

These states are central direct enemies in Iranian war framing.

### Tier Two — Operational Enablers And Explicitly Threatened States  

- United Kingdom  
- British sovereign bases in Cyprus  
- selected Gulf states hosting or supporting US and allied forces  
- states whose territory is directly used for operations against Iran  

These states may be treated as active participants even where they describe their role as defensive.

### Tier Three — Defensive And Strategic Support States  

- France  
- Germany  
- Italy  
- Bulgaria  
- Greece  
- Netherlands  
- Spain  
- Turkey  
- other NATO or European partners providing defence, logistics, maritime support, or basing  

These states are inside the wider hostile architecture but are not necessarily treated as equal belligerents.

### Tier Four — Diplomatic, Economic, Maritime, Or Supporting Coalition States  

- Canada  
- Japan  
- additional G7, NATO, maritime, sanctions, or logistics partners  

These states may matter more for cyber espionage, influence, coercion, or signalling than for immediate disruptive attack.

This tiering is analytical.

It is not an official IRGC list.

## ⚠️ There Is No Single Published IRGC List  

The IRGC does not need to publish a stable master list of enemy countries.

Strategic ambiguity gives it flexibility.

A state may be:

- threatened publicly;
- targeted indirectly;
- treated as a host rather than an enemy;
- distinguished from foreign forces on its territory;
- or moved into a higher category after one operational decision.

The map is therefore dynamic.

The pack should avoid language such as:

> Iran considers all NATO states direct belligerents.

That is broader than the public evidence supports.

It should instead say:

> Iran's operational target map appears to widen where states provide basing, interception, logistics, intelligence, maritime protection, or other support that materially enables the opposing campaign.

---

## 💻 Why This Matters For Cyber Tracking  

Cyber operations do not require the same threshold as kinetic strikes.

Iran may avoid firing missiles at a European capital while still authorising, encouraging, tolerating, or benefiting from:

- espionage;
- credential theft;
- data exfiltration;
- disruption of weak public systems;
- attacks on local utilities;
- pressure on banks;
- targeting of dissidents;
- proxy harassment;
- or pre-positioning inside critical infrastructure.

That means the cyber perimeter should be wider than the kinetic target list.

A state may be too politically costly to strike openly but still attractive for deniable pressure.

The relevant question is not only:

> Would Iran bomb this country?

It is also:

> Would Iran benefit from making this country's systems less reliable, its citizens less secure, or its government more politically constrained?

Each country should be assessed using the same frame:

```text
COUNTRY:
IRANIAN PERCEPTION OF INVOLVEMENT:
PUBLIC MILITARY ROLE:
BASING OR LOGISTICS ROLE:
DEFENSIVE OR INTERCEPTION ROLE:
MARITIME OR ECONOMIC ROLE:
KNOWN CYBER INCIDENTS SINCE 28 FEBRUARY 2026:
ATTRIBUTION STATUS:
ESSENTIAL INFRASTRUCTURE EXPOSURE:
PUBLIC WARNINGS OR THREATS:
RIVAL INTERPRETATIONS:
LAST REVIEWED:
```

This prevents the pack from confusing:

- political alignment;
- military participation;
- target exposure;
- and proven cyber activity.

They overlap.

They are not the same thing.
