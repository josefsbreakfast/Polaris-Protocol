# 🧭 What This Pack Is Tracking  
**First created:** 2026-08-01 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-01  
*Defining the perimeter of a live wartime cyber timeline without pretending that attribution is cleaner than it is.*  

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

This pack tracks cyber incidents affecting the systems through which states continue to function during the Iran war.

That means more than defence networks.

It includes water, energy, health, education, government administration, policing and justice systems, transport, telecommunications, banking, payments, and the contracted infrastructure that keeps those systems operating.

The central question is not:

> Has Iran switched off an entire Western country?

The question is:

> Are essential systems across states Iran regards as participating in the war showing a meaningful pattern of intrusion, disruption, data compromise, coercion, or operational degradation?

A modern cyber campaign may not arrive as one spectacular national outage.

It may arrive as a hundred smaller failures that are easier to dismiss one at a time.

---

## 🎯 The Tracking Perimeter  

An incident belongs in this pack where it affects, or credibly threatens to affect, one or more of the following:

- water and wastewater;
- energy supply and industrial control;
- healthcare and medical supply;
- education systems and public records;
- banking, payments, and financial confidence;
- transport and logistics;
- telecommunications and state connectivity;
- defence and security systems;
- government administration;
- policing and justice infrastructure;
- or a private contractor performing an essential public function.

The test is functional.

A company does not become irrelevant merely because it is privately owned. If it operates part of the machinery through which ordinary life or state capacity continues, it belongs inside the perimeter.

The issue is not branding.

The issue is what stops working when the system fails.

---

## 🌍 Which Countries Are In Scope  

The United States and Israel are the central direct belligerents.

The wider tracking perimeter includes states Iran may regard as participating where they:

- host forces used in operations against Iran;
- provide basing, logistics, refuelling, intelligence, interception, or air defence;
- protect regional infrastructure supporting the opposing coalition;
- join military, maritime, sanctions, or support arrangements;
- or constrain Iran's ability to retaliate.

A government may describe its role as defensive support.

Iran may still read that role as participation.

This pack therefore follows the wider coalition and support architecture rather than limiting itself to countries that publicly call themselves parties to the war.

That does not mean every country in scope is equally involved.

It means the cyber timeline should reflect the operational map Iran may be using, not only the legal or political description preferred by Western governments.

## 🧩 What Counts As A Relevant Incident  

The pack includes more than successful destructive attacks.

Relevant incidents may include:

- confirmed operational disruption;
- manipulation of industrial or control systems;
- forced movement to manual operation;
- theft of state or public-service data;
- loss of access to essential records;
- compromise of administrative or justice systems;
- interference with payment or banking functions;
- attacks on supply chains essential to public services;
- repeated reconnaissance or intrusion against critical systems;
- extortion where public functions are materially affected;
- and breaches that reveal an exploitable institutional seam.

A public website being briefly unavailable may not be enough.

A breach that removes sensitive public records from state custody may be.

A hospital remaining open does not mean the attack was strategically irrelevant.

A water system continuing under manual control does not mean no harm occurred.

The pack records operational effect, not only total failure.

---

## 🕸️ Attribution Is Graded  

This pack does not divide incidents into only two categories:

```text
Iran did it
Iran did not do it
```

The record should distinguish between:

- confirmed Iranian state attribution;
- probable or suspected attribution;
- actor claims;
- technical resemblance to known Iranian methods;
- proxy or criminal overlap;
- unattributed incidents;
- competing explanations;
- and what remains unknown.

Some incidents may be included because they affect essential state infrastructure during the same wartime period, even where no public Iran attribution exists.

That inclusion is not an accusation.

It is a decision to preserve the timeline until more is known.

No public attribution does not mean no strategic relevance.

Strategic relevance does not mean proven Iranian direction.

Both propositions can be true at once.

## 📉 Why Small Incidents Matter  

Waiting for one dramatic collapse is an outdated model of cyber defence.

A campaign can have strategic effect through accumulation.

That effect may come from:

- repeated local disruption;
- attacks spread across several sectors;
- weakly protected control systems;
- stolen public records;
- uncertainty about whether data can still be trusted;
- repeated institutional recovery costs;
- pressure on staff already operating in crisis conditions;
- or the public discovery that hostile actors can obtain state-held data more easily than affected citizens can obtain an accountable explanation.

The state does not need to stop functioning completely for confidence in the state to be damaged.

The relevant pattern may be:

```text
small breach
→ operational friction
→ institutional uncertainty
→ public distrust
→ strategic pressure
```

That is still a campaign effect even where no single incident looks like a missile strike.

## 🧅 Why The Operator May Not Know The Customer  

Cyber operations can be assembled through layers.

A person may begin with a small paid task, credential theft, account access, malware deployment, or data handling without knowing who ultimately commissioned the work.

A plausible chain may look like:

```text
recruit
→ small task
→ criminal intermediary
→ access broker
→ contractor or proxy
→ state customer
```

This matters because public attribution often assumes a clean line between operator and sponsor.

The real structure may be deliberately muddy.

A criminal actor may be opportunistic.

A proxy may be partially directed.

A nominal hacktivist group may exaggerate its role.

A contractor may serve several customers.

A recruit may understand only one step of the operation.

This is not unique to Iran.

It is part of the wider ecology through which states, intelligence-linked actors, criminal organisations, and political networks can obtain deniable labour.

## 🤐 Silence Can Advertise The Vulnerability  

A blanket posture of:

> We can neither confirm nor deny.  
> We are not discussing the incident.  
> We do not comment on operational matters.

may protect some investigations.

Used too broadly, it can also advertise that the state has no coherent way to protect the person whose data has been taken, copied, circulated, or weaponised.

That matters because the old ransomware model was built around organisations:

```text
do not pay
contain the breach
restore from backups
notify regulators
resume operations
```

That model does not transfer cleanly to an individual whose personal information may expose them to further harm.

A company can restore a server.

A person cannot restore a previous identity, undo the circulation of intimate data, reverse reputational damage, or make hostile citizens, criminal actors, political networks, or foreign intelligence services forget what they have obtained.

The question is therefore not only whether the state pays, attributes, or comments publicly.

It is whether the state can:

- tell the affected person what category of incident they are facing;
- assess the risk of further exploitation;
- coordinate across police, regulators, intelligence, health, and public bodies;
- identify which protections are available;
- preserve evidence;
- and provide a written, accountable route through the aftermath.

Where institutions refuse to acknowledge anything at all, the refusal may become part of the vulnerability.

It tells an adversary that:

- the victim may be left to manage the consequences alone;
- responsibility is fragmented;
- public bodies may hide behind each other's thresholds;
- the state may protect the secrecy of the system more effectively than the safety of the person;
- and further exploitation may occur before any institution accepts ownership.

This is especially serious where the attacker does not need the state itself to cause all later harm.

Once data is released or circulated, citizens of the target country, employers, journalists, political organisations, abusive relatives, stalkers, organised crime, or commercial intermediaries may continue the damage.

The original intruder may only need to open the door.

So silence is not always neutral.

Sometimes it preserves operational security.

Sometimes it signals that the state has identified a weakness and still has no citizen-protection pathway for it.

That distinction belongs inside cyber defence analysis.

## 🔎 What The Pack Is Looking For  

The timeline should not merely collect dramatic headlines.

It should look for:

- clustering around military escalation;
- repeated techniques across countries or sectors;
- movement from reconnaissance into disruption;
- repeated targeting of weakly protected essential systems;
- recurrence of the same infrastructure, malware, aliases, or access routes;
- cross-sector timing;
- evidence of proxy or criminal reuse;
- and changes in operational effect.

The point is not to force every incident into one theory.

The point is to notice when several incidents begin to form a pattern that cannot be seen from one headline alone.

---

## 🚫 What This Pack Does Not Claim  

This pack does not claim that:

- every unattributed attack is Iranian;
- every actor using Iranian rhetoric is state-directed;
- every wartime breach is part of one campaign;
- similarity of method proves common sponsorship;
- timing proves causation;
- public silence proves hidden attribution;
- or absence of public attribution means intelligence agencies know nothing.

It also does not assume that official attribution is always immediate, complete, or politically neutral.

The purpose is to preserve evidence, uncertainty, and pattern without flattening them into certainty.

## 📰 Reporting Standard  

Each incident should be recorded with:

```text
DATE:
COUNTRY:
SECTOR:
AFFECTED BODY:
WHAT HAPPENED:
OPERATIONAL EFFECT:
CLAIMED ACTOR:
OFFICIAL ATTRIBUTION:
OTHER ATTRIBUTION:
CONFIDENCE:
IRAN RELEVANCE:
RIVAL EXPLANATIONS:
SOURCES:
LAST REVIEWED:
CORRECTION STATUS:
```

The reporting rule is:

> Do not attribute beyond the evidence. Do not erase a developing strategic pattern merely because public attribution is incomplete.

Repetition is not corroboration.

Ten articles may still trace back to one advisory, one vendor report, or one unverified actor claim.

The source chain matters.

## 🧭 How To Read The Timeline  

The timeline is provisional.

Rows may move between confidence categories as investigations develop.

Some incidents will later be excluded.

Some will acquire stronger attribution.

Some may remain unresolved indefinitely.

The timeline should therefore be read as:

- a live evidentiary record;
- a map of operational effects;
- a prompt for further investigation;
- and a way to compare state responses across the widening war.

It is not a final list of who did what.

It is a record of what happened, what was claimed, what was established, and what remains open.
