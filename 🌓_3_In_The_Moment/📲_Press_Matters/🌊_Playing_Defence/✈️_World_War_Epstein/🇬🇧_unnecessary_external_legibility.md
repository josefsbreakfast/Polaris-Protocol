# 🇬🇧 Unnecessary External Legibility

**First created:** 2026-08-25 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-25  
*When secrecy reduces domestic scrutiny without meaningfully reducing what an external adversary can infer, opacity can become a security vulnerability rather than a protection.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Defence requires secrecy.

That is not the argument here.

The problem begins when a state assumes that making itself less transparent to its own population necessarily makes itself less legible to an adversary.

It does not.

Foreign states do not depend upon British FOI responses, parliamentary candour or investigative journalism to understand what Britain is doing. They have their own intelligence capabilities. They observe behaviour. They analyse deployments, procurement, industrial activity, diplomatic sequencing, exercises, budgets, partnerships, sanctions, personnel movements, technical development and reactions to external events.

The British public occupies a very different information environment.

If governmental transparency declines, Parliament receives less information, journalists encounter greater barriers, records become harder to obtain, and decisions increasingly disappear behind national-security language, the people most effectively blinded may therefore be British.

The state’s behaviour can remain perfectly visible from outside.

That produces an uncomfortable possibility:

> **Britain may become less legible to its own population without becoming meaningfully less legible to its adversaries.**

That is not simply a democratic problem.

It is a defence vulnerability.

---

## 👀 Secrecy And Legibility Are Not The Same Thing

There is a straightforward security logic to secrecy:

```text
sensitive information
        ↓
restricted
        ↓
adversary has less information
        ↓
reduced ability to predict, disrupt or exploit
```

Where that relationship genuinely exists, secrecy may be entirely justified.

But restricting domestic information does not automatically produce the final two steps.

Sometimes the actual system looks more like:

```text
government information becomes harder to access
        ↓
public knows less
        ↓
Parliament knows less
        ↓
journalists and researchers know less
        ↓
external behaviour remains observable
        ↓
capable adversaries continue analysing it
```

The important question is therefore not merely:

> **Is this information secret?**

It is:

> **Secret from whom?**

And after that:

> **Does restricting it actually reduce hostile understanding?**

---

## 🕸️ Adversaries Read Behaviour

A foreign intelligence service does not need Britain to explain itself honestly.

It can observe what Britain does.

Military posture creates information.

Procurement creates information.

Diplomacy creates information.

Budget allocation creates information.

Industrial investment creates information.

Repeated responses to particular threats create information.

Changes in alliance behaviour create information.

The cancellation, acceleration or modification of programmes creates information.

Even silence can become informative when compared against previous behaviour.

An adversary can therefore construct models from **revealed behaviour** even where declared policy is incomplete, misleading or unavailable.

That is one of the fundamental asymmetries.

The British citizen may reasonably require access to documents in order to establish what their government decided.

A capable foreign state may have several other ways of answering the question.

---

## 🧩 The Asymmetric Legibility Problem

The resulting information architecture can become badly distorted:

```text
                         UK STATE
                            │
             ┌──────────────┴──────────────┐
             │                             │
      domestic visibility          external observability
             │                             │
         declining                       remains
             │                             │
   Parliament / public             foreign governments
   journalists / NGOs              intelligence services
   academics / specialists         military analysts
   democratic institutions         hostile networks
             │                             │
             ↓                             ↓
       KNOW LESS                    KEEP INFERRING
```

This is not successful secrecy.

It is **asymmetric legibility**.

The state has reduced the visibility available to actors capable of scrutinising it without necessarily imposing equivalent informational costs upon actors capable of threatening it.

---

## ⚖️ Democratic Scrutiny Is Also A Security Function

Transparency is usually discussed as though it sits opposite security.

That is too simple.

Domestic scrutiny provides feedback.

Parliamentarians can identify contradictions between policy and implementation.

Journalists can expose failures that have become normalised internally.

Academics and independent specialists can challenge assumptions.

Industry can identify unrealistic procurement expectations.

Civil society can identify consequences invisible from inside a defence bureaucracy.

Courts and regulators can identify unlawful or poorly governed activity.

Opposition politicians can force ministers to explain decisions they would otherwise prefer not to explain.

None of these mechanisms is perfect.

Collectively, however, they enlarge the state’s ability to see itself.

Remove too much of that visibility and the state does not merely become more secret.

It risks becoming less self-aware.

---

## 🪞 Friendly Eyes And Hostile Eyes Are Not Equivalent

This produces one of the stranger assumptions within excessive secrecy.

Information accessible to:

- Parliament;
- British journalists;
- researchers;
- watchdogs;
- civil society;
- courts;
- and the British public

is treated as though its principal security significance lies in the possibility that an adversary might also see it.

Sometimes that is obviously true.

Sometimes it is not.

The calculation has to include what happens when **friendly scrutiny disappears**.

If an adversary can independently infer the relevant behaviour while domestic observers cannot scrutinise the decision-making behind it, secrecy has imposed its greatest informational cost on the wrong population.

---

## 📉 The Feedback Deficit

Consider the two information loops.

### Open Enough To Scrutinise

```text
government acts
      ↓
behaviour becomes observable
      ↓
domestic scrutiny
      ↓
errors / contradictions / vulnerabilities identified
      ↓
political and institutional feedback
      ↓
government adapts
```

### Internally Opaque But Externally Readable

```text
government acts
      ↓
behaviour becomes observable
      ↓
              ┌───────────────────────┐
              ↓                       ↓
      domestic observers       external adversaries
      lack information         analyse behaviour
              ↓                       ↓
      weak feedback            hostile adaptation
              │                       │
              └──────────┬────────────┘
                         ↓
                 strategic asymmetry
```

The second system has managed something quite impressive.

It has weakened its **corrective loop** while preserving somebody else’s **observational loop**.

---

## 🌊 Defence Is Particularly Vulnerable To This

Defence cannot be hidden simply by classifying documents because defence is expressed materially.

Ships move.

Aircraft fly.

Personnel deploy.

Factories produce things.

Contracts are awarded.

Infrastructure is constructed.

Allies coordinate.

Exercises occur.

Weapons are transferred.

Companies recruit.

Supply chains move.

Budgets change.

Diplomats travel.

Military doctrine affects behaviour.

A sophisticated observer therefore does not need perfect documentary transparency to understand significant parts of British strategy.

The more that defence becomes integrated across:

- industry;
- technology;
- infrastructure;
- energy;
- cyber;
- space;
- universities;
- private capital;
- international partnerships;
- and civilian resilience,

the larger this observable surface becomes.

The state can classify the paperwork.

**It cannot classify reality.**

---

## 🔬 Classification Can Conceal The Explanation Without Concealing The Pattern

This distinction is important.

Suppose an external observer can establish:

```text
Britain does A
when B occurs

Britain increases C
after D

Britain consistently protects E
while accepting risk around F

Britain repeatedly coordinates with G
before doing H
```

They may not know why.

They may not possess the relevant internal assessment.

They may not know precisely which minister authorised what.

But repeated behaviour permits inference.

Eventually:

```text
observable behaviour
        +
repetition
        +
external intelligence
        +
allied behaviour
        +
commercial information
        +
technical observation
        =
strategic model
```

Meanwhile the domestic public may still be waiting for an FOI response explaining what happened three years ago.

That is not an especially comforting information advantage.

---

## 🧠 The Adversary Does Not Need Certainty

Another mistake is assuming that secrecy succeeds whenever an adversary cannot know something definitively.

Military and intelligence analysis rarely requires perfect certainty.

An opponent may only need:

- a probable dependency;
- a likely response;
- an estimated threshold;
- a recurring institutional preference;
- an apparent capability gap;
- or a sufficiently reliable model of decision-making.

If British behaviour repeatedly validates the model, confidence increases.

The relevant security question therefore cannot be:

> **Have we prevented outsiders from obtaining definitive documentary proof?**

It must include:

> **Have we materially reduced their ability to predict us?**

Those are different tests.

---

## 🧮 The Cost-Benefit Calculation Is Wrong If It Counts Only Disclosure

Secrecy assessments naturally consider the potential harm caused by disclosure.

They also need to consider the potential harm caused by **non-disclosure**.

That includes:

- weaker democratic challenge;
- reduced institutional accountability;
- poorer external expert scrutiny;
- slower identification of policy failure;
- increased groupthink;
- weaker public understanding of commitments undertaken in its name;
- reduced ability to identify contradictions between stated and actual policy;
- and diminished trust when observable behaviour diverges from official explanation.

If hostile inference remains possible throughout, these costs become particularly difficult to justify.

The relevant calculation is not:

```text
transparency = risk
secrecy = safety
```

It is:

```text
security value gained through secrecy
        versus
friendly feedback lost through secrecy
        versus
information still available through external observation
```

That is a considerably harder calculation.

It is also a more honest one.

---

## 🛡️ Necessary Secrecy Still Exists

None of this requires operational transparency.

There are obvious reasons to protect:

- operational plans;
- intelligence sources and methods;
- technical vulnerabilities;
- targeting information;
- force protection;
- sensitive capabilities;
- live operations;
- cryptographic material;
- and information whose disclosure would genuinely improve hostile capability.

The distinction is therefore not:

> **secrecy versus openness.**

It is:

> **useful secrecy versus reflexive opacity.**

Useful secrecy imposes an informational cost upon the adversary.

Reflexive opacity may merely impose one upon the citizen.

---

## 🧿 The Test

For every restriction justified on defence or national-security grounds, several questions follow:

1. **What specific hostile inference does withholding this information prevent?**
2. **Could a capable external actor obtain substantially the same understanding through observable behaviour or other sources?**
3. **Who actually loses information because of the restriction?**
4. **What domestic scrutiny disappears with it?**
5. **Does that lost scrutiny perform a corrective or security function?**
6. **Are we protecting an operational secret, or protecting the state from explanation?**
7. **Has secrecy made British behaviour less predictable externally, or merely less accountable internally?**

The final question is particularly important.

Because if the answer is the latter, the policy may have created the inverse of its stated objective.

---

## ⚠️ The Vulnerability

The dangerous configuration is:

> **externally readable + internally opaque**

An adversary retains enough information to model British behaviour.

The British public loses enough information to struggle to challenge it.

The state consequently receives less corrective feedback.

Policy becomes easier to repeat without scrutiny.

External observers continue adapting to what Britain actually does.

The resulting vulnerability does not arise because Britain told everyone too much.

It arises because Britain **mistook domestic opacity for external invisibility**.

---

## 🌊 Playing Defence

A competent defence posture has to understand that information moves differently through different audiences.

The objective cannot simply be to reduce information everywhere outside government.

It should be to preserve:

- secrecy where secrecy materially frustrates hostile capability;
- transparency where transparency strengthens democratic and institutional feedback;
- oversight capable of operating where public transparency genuinely cannot;
- and enough external challenge to prevent secrecy becoming an environment in which bad assumptions reproduce unchecked.

Otherwise the country risks constructing an information regime in which the people least able to understand British defence policy are the people on whose behalf it is supposedly being conducted.

Meanwhile everyone Britain is worried about is watching what Britain actually does.
