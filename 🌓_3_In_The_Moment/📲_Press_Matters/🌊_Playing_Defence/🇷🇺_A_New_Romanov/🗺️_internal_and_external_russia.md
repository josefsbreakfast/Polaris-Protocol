# 🗺️ Internal and External Russia
**First created:** 2026-08-29 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-29  
*A working node for separating the audiences, political functions and possible interpretations carried by the same Russian communication.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The previous node established a basic proposition:

> **Communication can itself be political action.**

That immediately creates another problem.

Who is the action for?

It is easy to write:

> **Russia said X.**

But that formulation collapses several different questions:

- Who in Russia said it?
- Through which institution?
- Who was explicitly addressed?
- Who else was expected to hear it?
- What did it mean abroad?
- What did it mean at home?
- Was the same interpretation intended for everyone?

There is no single audience called **Russia**.

There is no single foreign audience called **the West**.

And the same act can potentially:

> **deter abroad, reassure at home, warn an ally, satisfy a constituency and preserve a negotiating position at the same time.**

That possibility is the subject of this node.

---

## 🌀 From Communication to Audience

If influential communication is:

> **deliberate, sustained, audience-aware communication intended to change how an audience understands a situation and ultimately influence how that audience behaves,**

then identifying the audience is not optional.

See:

- [🌀 Influential Communication](./🌀_influential_communication.md).

The analytical move is therefore from:

> **What did Russia say?**

to:

> **Which Russian actor communicated what, through which channel, in front of which audiences, and toward what plausible political effect?**

This does not require us to know the answer in advance.

It gives us a better question.

---

## 🎙️ First: Who Is Speaking?

Before identifying the audience, identify the speaker.

Possible Russian communicators include:

- the President;
- the Kremlin spokesperson;
- the Foreign Ministry;
- the Defence Ministry;
- the Security Council;
- intelligence or security figures;
- regional political actors;
- parliamentary figures;
- state media;
- other institutionally significant voices.

Different speakers may have:

- different formal authority;
- different institutional responsibilities;
- different audiences;
- different incentives;
- different communication styles.

A Defence Ministry statement should not automatically be treated as identical in function to:

- a presidential speech;
- a Foreign Ministry statement;
- a state-media segment.

This also guards against assuming perfect institutional coordination.

A state is a system.

It is not one mouth.

---

## 🇷🇺 Internal Russia Is Plural

“Russian domestic opinion” is too coarse to do all the analytical work.

Potential internal audiences include:

- the general Russian public;
- military personnel;
- the security establishment;
- political elites;
- business elites;
- nationalist constituencies;
- Orthodox constituencies;
- regional governments;
- families affected by war;
- economically exposed groups;
- people whose principal political preference is stability.

These categories may overlap.

They may also respond differently to the same event.

A message that demonstrates resolve to one audience may communicate economic danger to another.

A message that reassures the security establishment may not reassure military families.

A message that satisfies nationalist constituencies may increase anxiety elsewhere.

Do not decide those responses in advance.

Track them.

---

## 🌍 External Russia Is Plural Too

“The West” is similarly lossy.

Potential external audiences include:

- Ukraine;
- Washington;
- London;
- Berlin;
- Paris;
- Warsaw;
- Baltic governments;
- NATO;
- the European Union;
- China;
- states in the Global South;
- international business and capital;
- other governments watching how Russia is treated.

These actors do not occupy identical positions.

They have different:

- military capabilities;
- alliance roles;
- histories with Russia;
- economic relationships;
- geographic exposures;
- political constraints;
- diplomatic channels;
- abilities to alter Russian outcomes.

There is therefore no obvious reason to assume that Russian communication toward:

- Washington;
- London;
- Berlin;
- Warsaw

should be identical.

That becomes a live hypothesis later.

See:

- [🇺🇸 Washington Is Not London](./🇺🇸_washington_is_not_london.md).

## 👂 The Overhearing Audience

Public communication usually travels farther than its grammatical recipient.

A statement may have:

- an explicit recipient;
- a principal intended audience;
- secondary audiences;
- anticipated overhearers.

For example, a warning apparently directed at Britain might also be received by:

- Ukraine;
- other NATO governments;
- states considering supplying similar capabilities;
- Russian domestic audiences;
- military institutions;
- financial markets.

That does not prove all of those groups were intended targets.

It tells us where to look.

Ask:

> **Who is being spoken to?**

Then:

> **Who is expected to overhear?**

And finally:

> **Who may be expected to understand the same communication differently?**

---

## 🧩 One Action, Several Functions

Consider a hypothetical Russian warning linked to foreign military assistance.

The same communication could plausibly perform several functions.

### Externally

> **Stop enabling these strikes.**

### Internally

> **Russia will not tolerate attacks without responding.**

### Across the alliance

> **States assisting Ukraine may incur costs.**

### Diplomatically

Potentially:

> **Change the specified behaviour and this particular escalation pathway can stop.**

The final interpretation requires evidence.

A threat does not automatically contain an off-ramp.

But when a communication is conditional, the condition itself matters.

That gives us something more precise to analyse than:

> **Russia threatened Britain.**

We can ask:

- What behaviour was identified?
- What consequence was attached?
- Was the consequence conditional?
- Was any route away from escalation left visible?

---

## ⚠️ Threat Is Not One Category

Threatening communication can perform different political functions.

Distinguish where possible between:

- unconditional hostility;
- conditional threat;
- deterrent warning;
- coercive demand;
- bargaining position;
- domestic performance.

These categories can overlap.

But they imply different futures.

An unconditional threat says approximately:

> **This consequence is coming.**

A deterrent warning says:

> **Do not do this, or this consequence may follow.**

A coercive demand says:

> **Change existing behaviour, or this consequence may follow.**

A bargaining position may say:

> **Change this behaviour and this particular source of pressure can be removed.**

Those are not interchangeable messages.

---

## 🚪 Threat and Off-Ramp Can Coexist

Threat and reassurance are not necessarily opposites when multiple audiences are involved.

One communication environment can contain:

- threat toward one audience;
- reassurance toward another;
- demonstration of resolve domestically;
- preservation of an off-ramp privately.

Likewise, a state can:

- increase military pressure;
- preserve diplomatic contact;
- issue explicit demands;
- maintain intelligence channels.

That combination may be incoherent.

Or it may represent several instruments operating simultaneously.

We should not decide which before looking at the sequence.

## 🧬 Different Histories for Different Audiences

The historical block gives us another dimension to test.

Different repertoires may perform different political functions.

### 🏛️ Romanov / imperial language

Potential functions:

- prestige;
- continuity;
- great-power status;
- historical standing;
- European recognition.

### 👻 Soviet language

Potential functions:

- endurance;
- hostile encirclement;
- sacrifice;
- mobilisation;
- victory;
- superpower memory.

### 🕳️ Post-Soviet language

Potential functions:

- restoration after humiliation;
- stability after disorder;
- recovered state capacity;
- regained international consequence.

### 🛡️ Putin-era language

Potential functions:

- sovereignty;
- security;
- order;
- multipolarity;
- restored state authority.

Different combinations may be useful to different audiences.

The question is empirical:

> **Do they actually vary with audience in recurring ways?**

See:

- [🧬 Which Russia Is Speaking?](./🧬_which_russia_is_speaking.md).

## 🏠 Domestic Narrative Is a Strategic Constraint

Foreign-policy analysis can become so focused on relations between states that it forgets governments have to survive inside states.

Any eventual settlement must be:

- signed;
- explained;
- implemented;
- maintained.

That creates domestic political constraints.

Potentially destabilising Russian narratives might include:

- humiliation;
- emasculation;
- unconditional defeat;
- surrender;
- collapse of great-power status.

Potentially more survivable narratives might include:

- negotiated settlement;
- recognised Russian status;
- negotiation among consequential states;
- defence of defined interests;
- successful prevention of some claimed threat;
- return to diplomacy;
- return to commerce.

These are hypotheses about possible political narratives.

They are not recommendations that Russia should be rewarded for aggression.

---

## 🤝 Political Survivability Is Not a Russian Exception

This point should not be exoticised.

Every government faces domestic constraints.

Ukraine must be able to explain and sustain an agreement.

Britain must.

The United States must.

European governments must.

Russia must.

So:

> **This is not about giving Putin a propaganda victory.**

It is about recognising that:

> **an agreement requires a political mechanism by which each government can continue implementing tomorrow what it signed today.**

A technically elegant agreement that immediately creates overwhelming incentives for repudiation may not be a durable settlement.

---

## 🪞 The Identical-Outcome Test

A useful thought experiment is:

> **Could the same material diplomatic outcome be politically survivable or politically unsurvivable depending on how it is narrated?**

Suppose the actual terms remain identical.

The:

- border;
- withdrawal;
- monitoring mechanism;
- security provision;
- trade arrangement

does not change.

But one government describes the outcome as:

> **submission imposed by enemies.**

Another possible narrative describes the same outcome as:

> **a negotiated settlement between consequential states that secured defined national interests.**

The material agreement has not changed.

Its domestic political environment has.

This does **not** mean vocabulary can solve substantive disagreement.

It means narrative can affect whether an agreed substantive outcome survives.

## ♂️ Masculinity, Status and Humiliation

The wider cluster is interested in:

- status;
- prestige;
- humiliation;
- strength;
- weakness;
- endurance;
- masculinity.

Handle this carefully.

Do not reduce Russian politics to:

> **Russian men are macho.**

Instead ask whether specific political communication actually gender-codes concepts such as:

- strength;
- weakness;
- submission;
- protection;
- humiliation;
- endurance;
- domination.

Then source the claim.

Ideas about ideal masculinity vary across cultures.

So do political uses of masculinity.

A trope legible in Britain or the United States may not carry exactly the same meaning in Russia.

And a trope deliberately made legible to Western audiences may itself be worth noticing.

But cultural interpretation needs evidence, not vibes.

## 🌐 Translation Is Part of the Evidence

Where possible, preserve and compare:

- the original Russian;
- an official English translation;
- an independent translation where wording matters;
- Western press paraphrase;
- domestic Russian presentation.

Potentially important differences may appear in:

- emphasis;
- historical vocabulary;
- status language;
- conditionality;
- threat;
- audience.

But translation differences are not automatically strategic.

Before treating a difference as deliberate:

- verify the language;
- inspect context;
- check whether the difference is routine translation practice;
- avoid building an argument from headline wording alone.

---

## 🗞️ Domestic and External Presentation

Watch for differences between:

- domestic speeches;
- foreign-policy speeches;
- military statements;
- diplomatic statements;
- state-media presentation;
- official translations;
- statements intended principally for foreign audiences.

Questions:

- Is the grievance framed differently?
- Is the threat stronger domestically?
- Is the off-ramp clearer externally?
- Is historical language concentrated in one version?
- Is status language added or removed?
- Is the same event presented as deterrence abroad and victory at home?

Again:

> **difference is evidence to examine, not proof of deliberate segmentation.**

## 🧪 Working Method

For an important communication, record:

### SPEAKER

Who actually communicated?

### INSTITUTION

What authority or institutional role does the speaker possess?

### EXPLICIT RECIPIENT

Who is grammatically or formally addressed?

### DOMESTIC AUDIENCE(S)

Who inside Russia may plausibly receive the message?

### EXTERNAL AUDIENCE(S)

Which foreign actors may plausibly receive it?

### LIKELY OVERHEARERS

Who else is likely to notice?

### DOMESTIC FUNCTION

What political effect might the communication perform at home?

### EXTERNAL FUNCTION

What political effect might it perform abroad?

### CONDITION / DEMAND

What behaviour, if any, is being linked to the consequence?

### OFF-RAMP, IF ANY

Does the communication identify a route by which this particular escalation can stop?

### ALTERNATIVE READING

What simpler explanation fits?

Then watch behaviour.

---

## ⚠️ Do Not Overread

Do not assume:

- every audience was deliberately targeted;
- every wording difference is meaningful;
- every Russian institution is coordinated;
- every domestic constituency reacts identically;
- every conditional threat is a sincere negotiating offer;
- every preserved channel indicates willingness to compromise;
- every historical reference is audience-specific;
- every apparent off-ramp is acceptable or compatible with Ukrainian sovereignty.

The framework exists to produce better questions.

Not prettier certainty.

## 🧿 Working Proposition

The central proposition is simple:

> **The same Russian action can have one function abroad and another at home.**

But the more useful version is:

> **There is no single audience called “Russia”, and there is no single foreign audience called “the West”. The same act can deter abroad, reassure at home, warn an ally, satisfy a constituency and preserve a negotiating position at the same time.**

Our job is not to assume that all of those functions exist.

It is to separate them sufficiently that we can test whether they do.
