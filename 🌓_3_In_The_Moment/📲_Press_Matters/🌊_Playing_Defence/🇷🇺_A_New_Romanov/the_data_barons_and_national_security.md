# 🦠 The Data Barons and National Security
**First created:** 2026-08-30 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-30  
*Explaining the physical machine beneath the cloud, the private concentration around it, and the national-security and democratic questions created when states become dependent on commercial compute.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Modern states increasingly depend on privately owned infrastructure for:

- data storage;
- cloud computing;
- AI;
- communications;
- cybersecurity;
- logistics;
- health systems;
- finance;
- government administration;
- defence.

That creates a structural problem before it creates a conspiracy.

> **Privatisation changes ownership. It does not necessarily change strategic function.**

A capability can remain essential to national power after ownership moves outside the state.

This node starts one level earlier than the ownership question.

Before asking who the data barons are, ask:

> **What the fuck is the infrastructure we are talking about?**

Because:

- data centre;
- cloud;
- compute;
- data fusion;
- sovereign compute;
- AI infrastructure

are often discussed as though they are interchangeable.

They are not.

---

## 🧱 What Is a Data Centre?

A data centre is physical infrastructure.

It contains some combination of:

- servers;
- storage;
- networking equipment;
- cooling;
- backup power;
- physical security;
- connections to telecommunications networks.

It provides physical capacity for things such as:

- websites;
- databases;
- cloud services;
- enterprise systems;
- financial systems;
- health systems;
- government services;
- scientific computing;
- AI training;
- AI inference;
- defence workloads.

The cloud is not an ethereal layer floating above the economy.

> **The cloud is still somebody else's physical computer somewhere.**

---

## 🔬 What Is a Data Fusion Centre?

A data fusion centre is a different object.

It is primarily an organisational and analytical capability.

It brings information from multiple sources together in order to create:

- shared situational awareness;
- pattern detection;
- links between datasets;
- operational intelligence;
- decision support;
- a common operating picture.

A fusion centre may rely heavily on:

- data centres;
- cloud infrastructure;
- databases;
- analytical software;
- AI.

But:

> **data centre ≠ data fusion centre.**

One supplies physical compute and storage.

The other attempts to turn multiple information streams into usable knowledge.

That distinction matters particularly in:

- policing;
- intelligence;
- defence;
- cyber;
- emergency response.

---

## 🏗️ Why Are There Suddenly So Many?

Several different demand stories are currently being bundled together.

Growth can come from:

- ordinary cloud adoption;
- digitisation of business;
- digitisation of government;
- increasing data storage;
- streaming and consumer services;
- financial services;
- scientific computing;
- AI training;
- AI inference;
- cybersecurity;
- defence and intelligence;
- resilience and redundancy;
- sovereign or onshore compute;
- commercial demand from overseas customers.

Current UK policy expects substantial growth in AI-capable data-centre capacity.

That creates several different questions.

- How much demand already exists?
- How much is forecast?
- How much is speculative?
- How much capacity is being built because customers already require it?
- How much future demand is expected to appear because the capacity has been built?
- How much is intended to attract companies that might otherwise locate elsewhere?

Keep separate:

> **Britain needs more compute.**

from:

> **Every proposed data centre is necessary.**

The first proposition can be true without establishing the second.

---

## ⚙️ What Are We Actually Computing?

Potential workloads include:

- ordinary cloud applications;
- databases;
- government services;
- financial transactions;
- health systems;
- scientific modelling;
- weather and climate modelling;
- AI model training;
- AI inference;
- cybersecurity;
- defence;
- commercial analytics;
- advertising;
- entertainment;
- consumer AI.

This produces a deceptively simple question:

> **What proportion of new British compute capacity is actually expected to perform each function?**

There is no simple public national workload ledger.

That can reflect legitimate reasons:

- commercial confidentiality;
- cybersecurity;
- national security;
- constantly changing cloud allocation;
- decentralised ownership;
- customers purchasing capacity dynamically.

But it creates a transparency problem.

> **"AI infrastructure" can describe a strategic category without telling the public what a particular machine will spend Tuesday afternoon doing.**

---

## 🏨 Is Compute Basically Being Rented Out?

Often, yes.

The layers can include:

- landowner;
- data-centre owner;
- data-centre operator;
- colocation provider;
- hardware owner;
- cloud provider;
- software provider;
- customer;
- end user.

These can all be different organisations.

Capacity can be:

- commercially leased;
- colocated;
- sold through cloud services;
- reserved for a particular customer;
- publicly funded;
- allocated to research;
- allocated to government;
- dedicated to national-security workloads.

A data centre can therefore function partly like:

> **an extremely secure hotel for computers.**

The building may belong to one company.

The servers may belong to another.

The cloud platform may belong to another.

The workload may belong to a customer who never sees the physical machine.

So when Britain says it has gained more compute capacity, ask:

> **Whose capacity does Britain actually have?**

---

## 🧱 The Compute Stack

The physical and political stack can be mapped roughly as:

```text
land
  ↓
planning permission
  ↓
electricity
  ↓
water / cooling
  ↓
building
  ↓
network connection
  ↓
servers
  ↓
chips
  ↓
cloud platform
  ↓
software
  ↓
data
  ↓
models
  ↓
operators
  ↓
legal jurisdiction
```

Different actors can control different layers.

Therefore:

> **Physical location is not the same thing as operational control.**

A facility can sit physically inside Britain while depending upon:

- foreign chips;
- foreign cloud platforms;
- foreign software;
- foreign technical expertise;
- foreign corporate decision-making.

> **A data centre can be physically sovereign without the capability running inside it being sovereign.**

---

## 🇬🇧 What Does "British Compute" Mean?

Separate:

- located in Britain;
- British-owned;
- operated by a British company;
- governed by British law;
- available to the British government;
- reserved for British users;
- dependent on foreign hardware;
- dependent on foreign software;
- controlled by a foreign cloud provider.

Do not use:

> **sovereign compute**

as though sovereignty were a binary property.

Sovereignty may exist differently at different layers of the stack.

A more useful question is:

> **Which parts of this capability can Britain continue to operate if an external supplier, jurisdiction or company becomes unavailable?**

---

## 🛡️ Why National Security Suddenly Cares

Private infrastructure increasingly supports:

- communications;
- government administration;
- health;
- finance;
- logistics;
- cyber defence;
- intelligence;
- military systems;
- AI;
- emergency response.

That makes the private/public distinction operationally complicated.

A privately owned capability may still be essential to:

- state continuity;
- defence;
- crisis response;
- economic security.

The key change is not simply that private companies have become large.

It is that:

> **the state may become unable to perform important functions without them.**

---

## 👑 Enter the Data Barons

"Data baron" is analytical shorthand.

It does not mean:

- every technology billionaire shares one political project;
- ownership proves conspiracy;
- companies and governments are interchangeable;
- private infrastructure is inherently illegitimate.

It points toward concentrated private control over capabilities that states increasingly require.

Relevant forms of power can include:

- ownership;
- compute;
- cloud;
- data;
- models;
- network infrastructure;
- capital;
- technical expertise;
- government contracts;
- interoperability;
- switching costs.

The national-security question is:

> **What happens when losing access to a private provider becomes a national-security problem?**

---

## 🧬 Dependency Is More Than Ownership

Map:

- who owns the infrastructure;
- who operates it;
- who maintains it;
- who supplies the chips;
- who controls the software;
- who holds the contracts;
- who possesses the expertise;
- who can replace the provider.

Then ask:

- What is the switching cost?
- How long would replacement take?
- Can government operate the capability itself?
- Does a credible alternative exist?
- Is the alternative genuinely independent or dependent on the same upstream supplier?

Concentration becomes strategically important when:

> **exit becomes difficult.**

---

## 🧠 Information Concentration Is Power

Data infrastructure does not merely hold information.

It can enable:

- aggregation;
- search;
- modelling;
- prediction;
- pattern detection;
- automated decision support;
- institutional memory.

A provider may therefore possess several different forms of power:

- technical power;
- informational power;
- infrastructural power;
- bargaining power.

These overlap.

They are not identical.

---

## 🔐 Public Infrastructure, Private Visibility

Government may publicly say:

- data centres are critical infrastructure;
- more compute is strategically necessary;
- AI capacity is economically important;
- resilience requires domestic capacity.

The public may still have limited visibility into:

- exact workloads;
- utilisation;
- customer mix;
- public-sector dependency;
- defence dependency;
- redundancy;
- commercial allocation.

Some opacity is legitimate.

But opacity changes the democratic question.

If the public is being asked to support major resource allocation to an infrastructure class, it is reasonable to ask what public function that infrastructure is expected to serve.

---

## 🗳️ Strategic Necessity Does Not Abolish the Demos

Do not reduce public attitudes to:

- pro-AI;
- anti-AI;
- pro-growth;
- NIMBY.

People may have more nuanced preferences.

Attitudes may depend upon:

- local jobs;
- electricity demand;
- grid priority;
- water consumption;
- land use;
- environmental effects;
- ownership;
- public benefit;
- national-security need;
- transparency;
- whether the workload is public or commercial.

Public opposition may therefore represent:

- opposition;
- insufficient information;
- conditional support;
- distrust;
- disagreement over cost;
- disagreement over who receives the benefit.

The democratic question is not merely:

> **Do people like data centres?**

It is:

> **What do people think they are being asked to trade, for what benefit, and how much control do they believe they have over the decision?**

---

## ❓ What Exactly Is the Democratic Proposition?

If government asks society to provide:

- land;
- grid capacity;
- water;
- planning priority;
- public incentives;
- infrastructure protection;
- emergency powers;

then the demos can reasonably ask:

- Why this much compute?
- Why here?
- For what?
- Who owns it?
- Who rents it?
- Who uses it?
- Who profits?
- What public benefit follows?
- What happens if projected demand is wrong?
- What strategic capability does Britain actually gain?

> **If compute is becoming sovereign infrastructure, the democratic question is not merely whether Britain has enough of it. It is who decided how much was enough, what it would be used for, and which costs the public agreed to carry.**

---

## ⚖️ National Necessity Versus Local Consent

Representative government can legitimately make strategic infrastructure decisions that are locally unpopular.

National benefits and local costs can genuinely diverge.

That does not remove the governance questions.

Ask:

- Who decides?
- What consultation occurs?
- What evidence is disclosed?
- What compensation or local benefit exists?
- When can national strategic need override local objection?
- What review exists?

Avoid:

> **Local objection automatically defeats national infrastructure.**

Also avoid:

> **National security automatically defeats democratic scrutiny.**

---

## 🚨 Emergency Prioritisation

Strategic infrastructure becomes especially interesting when capacity is scarce.

In ordinary conditions, access may be allocated through:

- contracts;
- price;
- commercial demand;
- normal public procurement.

In an emergency, another allocation logic may appear:

> **Who gets priority?**

Possible claimants include:

- defence;
- intelligence;
- emergency services;
- NHS;
- central government;
- critical infrastructure operators;
- financial systems;
- communications;
- commercial customers;
- research;
- ordinary public services.

This changes the public/private problem.

A privately owned system may continue to be commercially operated while government acquires or seeks:

- priority access;
- direction powers;
- emergency allocation rights;
- resilience requirements;
- restrictions on ordinary commercial use.

Do not assume that any particular power exists without checking its legal basis.

The analytical question comes first:

> **What would the state need to be able to prioritise during a serious emergency?**

---

## ⚡ Scarcity Makes Sovereignty Visible

Ask what happens if there is not enough:

- grid capacity;
- compute;
- cloud capacity;
- network bandwidth;
- cooling;
- fuel;
- replacement hardware;
- technical labour.

The important question becomes:

> **Who is allowed to lose service first?**

That is not merely a technical question.

It is a political allocation decision.

For example:

- Does an AI company lose compute before an NHS system?
- Does commercial cloud capacity get redirected toward defence?
- Does defence receive power priority over ordinary industry?
- Do critical financial systems outrank consumer services?
- Who decides?
- What legal authority permits the decision?
- Who reviews it?

These are questions to research.

Do not fill the answers in because the scenario makes intuitive sense.

---

## 🧮 Capacity Is Not Neutral

Building more infrastructure does not eliminate allocation politics.

It may instead create categories such as:

- protected workloads;
- priority users;
- interruptible users;
- strategic reserves.

Therefore ask:

> **How much of Britain's compute is genuinely available to the market, and how much could become reserved or redirected during crisis?**

The answer may be:

- contractually defined;
- regulated;
- legally directed;
- technically possible but undocumented;
- unknown.

Unknown is a valid answer.

---

## 🗳️ The Second Democratic Question

Emergency prioritisation creates a second democratic problem.

The first is:

> **Did the public agree to the infrastructure build-out?**

The second is:

> **Did the public agree to the hierarchy of needs that governs it during crisis?**

If scarce infrastructure receives:

- public planning priority;
- grid investment;
- regulatory privilege;
- national-security protection;

then citizens can reasonably ask:

- Who receives priority when capacity is scarce?
- Who can be disconnected?
- Which private contracts can be overridden?
- Who compensates displaced users?
- What oversight applies?
- How long can emergency priority persist?

> **The real sovereignty test is not only who owns the infrastructure in normal times. It is who decides what it does when there is not enough to go around.**

---

## 🧩 Private Power and State Power Can Increase Together

Emergency integration can produce two apparently opposite effects.

### Private provider gains power

Because:

- government depends on its infrastructure;
- replacement is difficult;
- expertise is concentrated;
- the provider becomes strategically important.

### State gains power over provider

Potentially through:

- emergency direction;
- priority allocation;
- mandatory resilience;
- restrictions on service;
- compelled cooperation.

The exact powers require jurisdiction-specific legal analysis.

The structural point is:

> **Emergency integration can strengthen both corporate importance and executive authority at the same time.**

This is central to [⚖️ Emergency Power and Governance](./⚖️_emergency_power_and_governance.md).

## 🌍 What Does an Adversary See?

A competent foreign state may map:

- who owns British compute;
- where major data centres sit;
- which providers serve government;
- where cloud dependency exists;
- which cables connect them;
- which firms are difficult to replace;
- which commercial capabilities contribute to state function.

That does not require:

- conspiracy;
- secret coordination;
- direct relationships with providers.

It is ordinary strategic analysis.

The reversal is simple:

Western state:

> **This private infrastructure is essential to national security.**

Potential adversary:

> **Then this infrastructure is relevant to national power.**

---

## ⚠️ Strategic Relevance Is Not Targeting Law

Keep separate:

- strategic importance;
- domestic critical-infrastructure designation;
- military targeting law.

Strategic relevance does not automatically make civilian infrastructure:

- a military object;
- a lawful target.

Legal targeting questions require their own analysis.

This distinction carries forward into [🌐 When Private Infrastructure Becomes State Power](./🌐_when_private_infrastructure_becomes_state_power.md).

---

## 🧪 No Conspiracy Required

The structural argument does not require:

- secret coordination;
- malicious billionaires;
- manufactured emergencies.

A plausible sequence is simply:

```text
digital state
    ↓
increasing compute requirement
    ↓
private sector already owns scalable capability
    ↓
government buys capability
    ↓
dependency grows
    ↓
provider becomes strategically important
    ↓
government protects provider
    ↓
provider becomes more deeply integrated
    ↓
switching becomes harder
```

That is enough to produce a governance problem.

---

## 🦠 Why "Barons"?

The metaphor should do limited work.

It points toward:

- concentrated ownership;
- control of strategically important assets;
- asymmetric bargaining power;
- dependence between sovereign authority and private capability.

It should not imply:

- feudal equivalence;
- unified political allegiance;
- absence of state power;
- a single coordinated elite project.

In fact, later nodes should show that dependency can increase:

- private power over the state;

and:

- state power over private infrastructure.

The relationship can become mutually binding.
