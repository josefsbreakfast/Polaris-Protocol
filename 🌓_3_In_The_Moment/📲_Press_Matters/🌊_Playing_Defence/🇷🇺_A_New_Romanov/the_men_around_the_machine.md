# 🕸️ The Men Around the Machine
**First created:** 2026-08-30 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-30  
*Using people as probes into different surfaces of political, technological, financial and security power without mistaking proximity for coordination.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The previous nodes establish two related problems.

[🦠 The Data Barons and National Security](./🦠_the_data_barons_and_national_security.md) asks what happens when strategically important capability becomes concentrated in private hands.

[💾 The Data State](./💾_the_data_state.md) asks what happens when the British state increasingly depends upon infrastructure it may procure, regulate and protect without necessarily owning.

This node adds people.

Not because:

> **important men know other important men, therefore conspiracy.**

But because people occupy positions inside systems.

Those positions can expose different parts of the machine.

The useful question is:

> **What becomes visible from where this person sits?**

---

## 🧭 People as Analytical Probes

This node uses particular people as probes into different surfaces of power.

Candidate figures already identified include:

- Ehud Barak;
- Steve Bannon;
- Larry Ellison;
- Tony Blair.

They are deliberately not equivalent.

They represent different combinations of:

- government;
- security;
- military experience;
- political strategy;
- technology;
- capital;
- diplomacy;
- elite networks;
- institutional influence.

The point is not to establish one group.

The point is to ask whether looking from several very different positions helps us understand the structure around the machine.

---

## ⚠️ The Guardrail Comes First

Do not infer:

- coordination from acquaintance;
- agreement from contact;
- culpability from proximity;
- shared ideology from shared rooms;
- common geopolitical objectives from overlapping networks;
- one unified elite project from repeated names;
- Russian alignment from criticism of Western policy;
- Western alignment from participation in Western institutions.

Keep separate:

```text
same document
≠
same room
≠
same social network
≠
same institution
≠
same commercial interest
≠
same political objective
≠
coordination
≠
culpability
```

The evidentiary burden rises at every step.

## 🕸️ Network Position Is Not Network Membership

A person can be useful analytically because of where they sit without belonging to any meaningful common network with another person in the node.

For each figure ask:

- Which institutions have they occupied?
- Which industries do they understand?
- Which decision-makers can they plausibly access?
- Which forms of information are routine in their world?
- Which incentives are visible from their position?
- Which dependencies are likely to be obvious to them?
- Which parts of the system might they systematically misunderstand?

This gives us:

> **network position → epistemic position.**

Where you sit affects what you can see.

It does not make what you see automatically correct.

## 🔭 Different Windows Onto the Same System

The same strategic environment may look very different from:

- a military command;
- an intelligence service;
- a cloud provider;
- a venture-capital portfolio;
- a political campaign;
- a diplomatic office;
- a think tank;
- a government department;
- a media organisation.

That matters because arguments about:

- Russia;
- AI;
- defence;
- cloud;
- sovereignty;
- emergency power;
- industrial strategy

can appear contradictory when participants are actually looking at different parts of the system.

The analytical task is therefore not merely:

> **Who is right?**

It is also:

> **What does each position make unusually easy to notice?**

## 🪖 Ehud Barak

Use Barak principally as a probe into:

- military command;
- national security;
- intelligence-adjacent statecraft;
- political leadership;
- the interaction between technology and security;
- elite international networks.

Questions:

- What does a former senior military and political actor notice about technological dependence?
- How does someone formed inside a security state conceptualise civilian technology?
- Where do military, intelligence, commercial and political networks naturally overlap without requiring conspiracy?
- How might national-security actors understand capabilities that civilian institutions still categorise as ordinary commercial infrastructure?

Do not make Barak stand in for:

- Israel;
- intelligence services generally;
- technology generally;
- one unified security worldview.

He is a probe, not a representative sample.

## 🧨 Steve Bannon

Use Bannon principally as a probe into:

- political communication;
- populist mobilisation;
- media ecosystems;
- ideological entrepreneurship;
- elite counter-elite politics;
- narrative power.

Questions:

- How does political strategy operate around infrastructure that most citizens cannot directly see?
- Who translates technical systems into emotionally legible political stories?
- How can anti-establishment rhetoric coexist with access to elite networks?
- How does narrative power alter the political meaning of technology, sovereignty and national decline?
- What becomes visible when the machine is viewed through persuasion rather than engineering?

Bannon is especially useful for separating:

> **control of infrastructure**

from:

> **control of the story told about infrastructure.**

Those are different forms of power.

## 🧩 These Men Are Not the Same Thing

Do not flatten the case studies.

A technology founder has one kind of leverage.

A former prime minister has another.

A political strategist has another.

A former military and political leader has another.

The differences are analytically useful.

They allow us to separate:

- infrastructure power;
- information power;
- political power;
- narrative power;
- capital;
- institutional access;
- security expertise.

The node works if the reader finishes with a more differentiated map.

It fails if the reader finishes with:

> **all powerful men are secretly one guy.**

## 🧠 Epistemic Position

Every institutional position produces both:

- privileged visibility;
- characteristic blind spots.

A technology executive may understand:

- scalability;
- infrastructure;
- technical dependence;
- procurement.

They may understand less about:

- democratic legitimacy;
- local public consent;
- military doctrine.

A military or intelligence actor may understand:

- threat;
- resilience;
- capability;
- adversarial behaviour.

They may underweight:

- commercial incentives;
- civil liberties;
- ordinary public experience.

A political strategist may understand:

- audiences;
- emotion;
- narrative;
- mobilisation.

They may underweight:

- engineering constraints;
- administrative complexity.

A political leader may understand:

- institutional authority;
- coalition-building;
- international bargaining.

They may depend heavily upon technical intermediaries to understand the machinery itself.

Therefore:

> **expertise is positional.**

## 🔄 The Translator Class

Modern technological government creates demand for people who can translate between domains.

For example:

```text
engineer
    ↕
technology executive
    ↕
consultant / policy entrepreneur
    ↕
civil servant
    ↕
minister
    ↕
security institution
    ↕
public
```

Translation is necessary.

It also creates power.

The translator can influence:

- which problem is recognised;
- which solution appears feasible;
- which supplier appears credible;
- which risks are emphasised;
- which alternatives disappear from view.

This does not require dishonesty.

Framing is unavoidable.

The governance question is whether alternative expertise remains available.

## 💰 Capital Sees the Machine Differently

Investors and owners may see:

- future demand;
- bottlenecks;
- scarcity;
- infrastructure opportunity;
- government procurement;
- regulatory protection;
- strategic premiums.

Government may see:

- resilience;
- capability;
- urgency;
- national security.

The public may see:

- electricity use;
- water;
- planning;
- jobs;
- surveillance;
- public spending.

An adversary may see:

- dependency;
- concentration;
- chokepoints;
- strategic capability.

These views can all describe the same asset.

That is why:

> **asset classification depends partly upon the observer.**

## 🛡️ Security Sees Function Before Ownership

A national-security institution may ask:

- What does this capability enable?
- What happens if it fails?
- Who can disrupt it?
- How quickly can it be replaced?

That can make the formal public/private distinction less important operationally.

A company remains a company.

But its infrastructure can still become part of the state's capability map.

This connects directly to:

- [🌐 When Private Infrastructure Becomes State Power](./🌐_when_private_infrastructure_becomes_state_power.md).

## 🗳️ Democracy Sees Authority

The demos have another legitimate question:

> **Who authorised these people to have this much influence over systems the state now describes as essential?**

The answer may be perfectly ordinary:

- ownership;
- entrepreneurship;
- contracts;
- elections;
- appointments;
- expertise;
- philanthropy;
- advisory work;
- market success.

But ordinary routes to influence can still produce extraordinary concentrations of power.

So ask:

- What authority is formal?
- What authority is commercial?
- What authority is reputational?
- What authority comes from expertise?
- What authority comes from access?
- What authority is democratically accountable?

Do not collapse these categories.

---

## 🚪 Access Is a Resource

Elite access can matter without proving improper conduct.

Access can provide:

- information;
- introductions;
- credibility;
- opportunities to advocate;
- early awareness of policy;
- understanding of institutional priorities.

But:

> **access is not command.**

Someone being able to speak to a decision-maker does not establish:

- control;
- agreement;
- successful influence;
- coordination.

Record the access.

Then look for evidence of effect.

---

## 📡 Influence Requires an Object

Avoid vague claims that somebody is "influential."

Ask:

- influential over whom?
- regarding what?
- through which mechanism?
- with what observable result?

Possible mechanisms include:

- formal office;
- ownership;
- procurement;
- advice;
- lobbying;
- funding;
- media;
- personal relationships;
- technical dependency;
- institutional reputation.

Then ask:

> **Can the claimed effect actually be observed?**

---

## 🧾 Contact Is Evidence of Contact

This rule matters enough to repeat.

A:

- photograph;
- diary entry;
- email;
- meeting;
- flight record;
- address-book entry;
- invitation;
- introduction

can establish a particular kind of contact or proximity.

It does not automatically establish:

- friendship;
- agreement;
- coordination;
- knowledge of misconduct;
- participation in misconduct;
- shared political purpose.

This becomes essential in:

- [🧾 Epstein as Accidental Topology](./🧾_epstein_as_accidental_topology.md).

That node can use messy documentary material to illuminate elite social structure without converting topology into guilt.

```text
SECURITY POSITION:
POLITICAL POSITION:
WHAT THIS POSITION MAKES VISIBLE:
LIKELY BLIND SPOTS:
ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATION:

```

Allow:

> **unknown.**

Do not fill boxes because they exist.

## 🔬 Evidence Ladder

Use an explicit ladder.

### Level 1 — presence

- same event;
- same document;
- same database;
- same photograph.

### Level 2 — contact

- direct communication;
- meeting;
- introduction;
- correspondence.

### Level 3 — relationship

- repeated contact;
- documented professional or personal relationship.

### Level 4 — shared activity

- contract;
- project;
- organisation;
- campaign;
- investment;
- formal collaboration.

### Level 5 — coordination

- evidence that actors deliberately aligned behaviour toward a shared objective.

### Level 6 — culpability

- evidence relevant to specific wrongdoing.

Do not jump levels.

> **Topology is not culpability.**

## 🧪 No Conspiracy Required

A system can produce concentrated elite overlap because:

- important industries are concentrated;
- government needs specialist expertise;
- capital seeks political information;
- political actors seek money and technology;
- security institutions seek capability;
- wealthy people attend the same institutions and events;
- global elites are a relatively small social population.

Repeated overlap can therefore be:

- structurally interesting;
- politically important;
- evidentially useful

without proving secret coordination.

That is the more interesting problem.

## 🪞 The Machine Produces Its Own Social Geography

As infrastructure becomes more strategically important, the people around it become more important too.

Government increasingly needs:

- engineers;
- cloud providers;
- AI companies;
- investors;
- cybersecurity specialists;
- policy translators.

Those actors increasingly need:

- planning;
- electricity;
- procurement;
- regulation;
- state protection;
- political legitimacy.

This can produce recurring contact between:

- capital;
- technology;
- government;
- defence;
- intelligence;
- media;
- policy institutions.

No central planner is required.

> **Strategic dependency generates social proximity.**

---

## 🚨 Emergency Compresses the Network

Crisis can make these relationships more operational.

Ordinary procurement may become:

- urgent procurement.

Ordinary advice may become:

- emergency consultation.

Commercial infrastructure may become:

- protected national capability.

A provider may move rapidly from:

> **vendor**

to:

> **indispensable partner.**

This creates the bridge to:

- [💸 Emergency Is a Market Condition](./💸_emergency_is_a_market_condition.md);
- [🧪 Asset Accelerationism and Defence](./🧪_asset_accelerationism_and_defence.md).

The question is not necessarily:

> **Who engineered the emergency?**

It is:

> **Who is positioned to become more powerful when emergency changes the allocation of money, attention, access and authority?**

## ⚠️ Boundaries

Do not:

- use social proximity as guilt by association;
- infer political alignment from personal contact;
- assume everyone in an elite network knows everyone else;
- infer coordination from shared incentives;
- treat wealth as evidence of wrongdoing;
- treat criticism of a state as allegiance to its adversary;
- collapse technology, finance, politics and intelligence into one system;
- assume documentary appearance establishes meaningful relationship.

Keep separate:

- position;
- access;
- relationship;
- activity;
- coordination;
- culpability.

## 🧿 Research Questions

- What formal roles has each case-study figure held?
- Which relevant companies, governments or institutions have they actually worked with?
- Which relationships are documented?
- Which are merely alleged or inferred?
- What forms of power does each person possess?
- Which infrastructure or policy layers can each affect?
- What evidence exists of actual effect?
- Which relationships recur over time?
- Which overlaps are ordinary for the relevant elite environment?
- Which are unusual?
- Which networks are commercial?
- Which are political?
- Which are social?
- Which are security-related?
- Where do those categories genuinely intersect?
- What becomes visible from each person's institutional position?
- What are the characteristic blind spots of that position?
- Does mapping several positions improve understanding of the machine?
- Does it improve prediction?
- Or does it merely create an aesthetically satisfying network diagram?
