# ⚓_deterrence_without_a_destination.md

- Purpose:
  - separate deterrence as a tool from deterrence as a substitute for strategy;
  - force the cluster to articulate what political outcome British and Western security policy is actually trying to produce.

- Core proposition:
  > Deterrence is an instrument.
  > It is not automatically an end state.

- Central questions:
  - what behaviour are we trying to stop?
  - what behaviour are we trying to encourage?
  - what does success look like?
  - what does partial success look like?
  - what permits de-escalation?
  - what would count as a credible off-ramp?
  - what is the stopping condition?

- Current problem:
  - public Western discussion often treats:
    - more deterrence;
    - more defence spending;
    - more resilience;
    - more capability
    as self-evidently positive outputs.
  - but capability only matters strategically if it serves an articulated political objective.

- Distinguish:
  - deterrence of territorial aggression;
  - deterrence of attacks on NATO;
  - deterrence of cyber / infrastructure attacks;
  - deterrence of escalation;
  - deterrence by punishment;
  - deterrence by denial.

- Ask:
  - are these all trying to produce the same end state?
  - or are different policies being bundled under one word?

- Strategic failure mode:
  - every Russian signal of threat produces:
    - more spending;
    - more infrastructure;
    - more emergency powers;
    - more forward capability;
  - but no one specifies when those measures would become less necessary.

- Feedback-loop question:
  > If every escalation generates another layer of deterrent machinery,
  > how do we know whether deterrence is succeeding or the confrontation is simply becoming self-reinforcing?

- Important:
  - this does NOT mean:
    - deterrence is unnecessary;
    - Russia should be trusted;
    - capability should be reduced unilaterally.

- It means:
  - capability must remain subordinate to strategy.

- Link to Russia-signalling thesis:
  - if Moscow is signalling:
    - red lines;
    - conditions;
    - status demands;
    - possible openings,
  - then deterrence policy should be able to distinguish:
    - threats to resist;
    - signals to test;
    - opportunities to exploit.

- Strong working line:
  > A deterrence policy that cannot describe the conditions under which deterrence has worked risks becoming a permanent mobilisation policy instead.
