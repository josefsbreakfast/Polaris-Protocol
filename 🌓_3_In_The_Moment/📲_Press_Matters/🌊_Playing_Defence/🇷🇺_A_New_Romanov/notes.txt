
# Working Notes — Russian Signalling, Western Interpretation, and the Defence Ecosystem

- PURPOSE:
  - Build a defence-notes series for watching Russian strategic signalling in real time.
  - This is an observational / hypothesis-testing project, not a settled theory of Russian intentions.
  - The purpose is to record:
    - what Russia actually says and does;
    - who appears to be addressed;
    - what historical / political / cultural language accompanies it;
    - how Western governments, defence institutions, political networks and press interpret it;
    - what happens next.
  - Preserve competing explanations.
  - Update or abandon the hypothesis when subsequent evidence does not fit.

---

## Baseline normative position

- Nothing in this analysis requires:
  - supporting the Russian invasion;
  - accepting Russian territorial claims;
  - wanting Russia to prevail militarily;
  - accepting the Kremlin's description of events;
  - treating Russian threats as legitimate.

- Baseline:
  - Ukraine is sovereign territory.
  - Russia does not acquire a legitimate entitlement to Ukrainian territory because Moscow regards it as historically or strategically important.
  - In extremely abbreviated terms:
    - Ukraine's territory.
    - Fuck off.

- But moral opposition to Russian conduct does not remove the analytical requirement to understand Russia accurately.

- In particular:
  - taking an adversary seriously is not endorsement;
  - understanding its historical self-conception is not accepting that self-conception;
  - identifying an attempted diplomatic signal does not require agreeing to its terms;
  - recognising an escalation concern does not establish that Russia's account of responsibility for escalation is correct.

---

## Core concern

- The common PUBLIC Western interpretation of Russian behaviour may be self-limiting.

- Important scope limitation:
  - this is principally an observation about:
    - press coverage;
    - public political messaging;
    - publicly available defence analysis.
  - We do NOT know what:
    - intelligence agencies;
    - diplomats;
    - military planners;
    - ministers;
    - private advisers
    are saying behind closed doors.

- Therefore do not infer:
  - "Western governments have failed to notice X"
  merely because X is poorly represented in public discourse.

- Instead test:
  - "Is the publicly visible Western interpretive environment systematically underweighting X?"

---

## The possible missing variable: signalling

- Russian behaviour should not be read solely through:
  - military capability;
  - readiness;
  - doctrine;
  - escalation ladders;
  - battlefield utility;
  - force posture.

- Also examine:
  - intended audience;
  - historical memory;
  - prestige;
  - status;
  - political culture;
  - domestic narrative survivability;
  - diplomacy;
  - commercial relationships;
  - cultural signalling;
  - recognition;
  - Russia's conception of its place in Europe;
  - Russia's conception of its place in the international order.

- Working proposition:
  - coercion and diplomacy are not necessarily opposite signals.

- A state can simultaneously:
  - threaten;
  - impose costs;
  - demonstrate capability;
  - establish a boundary;
  - demand recognition;
  - preserve communications channels;
  - indicate conditions under which negotiation remains possible.

## Current case study: CIA contact + British bases warning

- Recent sequence worth recording as a single signalling episode:
  - high-level US intelligence contact with Moscow;
  - continued US–Russian intelligence communication;
  - Russian rejection of claims that it intends to attack NATO;
  - simultaneously sharper Russian rhetoric toward Britain;
  - explicit warning that British military facilities could enter the retaliatory target set in relation to British-enabled strikes.

- Do not flatten this into:
  - "Russia threatens NATO."

- Test whether Russia is differentiating between:
  - Washington;
  - London;
  - NATO collectively;
  - individual European governments;
  - Ukraine.

- Possible interpretation to TEST:
  - Washington is being treated as a great-power / escalation-management interlocutor.
  - Britain is being told that particular capabilities / policies are approaching or crossing a Russian operational boundary.
  - NATO is being warned while direct Russia–NATO confrontation remains something Moscow may still wish to avoid.

- Alternative explanations must remain open.

## Historical signalling

- Contemporary Russian foreign policy should also be examined through Russian historical self-conception.

- Peter I / Peter Alekseyevich is a particularly important test case.

- Western defence readings commonly emphasise:
  - military modernisation;
  - territorial expansion;
  - state-building;
  - readiness;
  - Land Power.

- Those are real components.

- But Peter's project also involved:
  - diplomacy;
  - commerce;
  - maritime access;
  - technical modernisation;
  - cultural interaction;
  - reputation;
  - state transformation;
  - European engagement;
  - making Russia a power other European states had to recognise.

- Avoid reducing this to simple "Westernisation".
  - Better:
    - modernising Russian state capacity;
    - expanding Russia's ability to participate in and shape the European state system.

- Therefore contemporary Kremlin references to Peter MAY carry more than:
  - conquest;
  - territorial revision;
  - military strength.

- They may also communicate:
  - recognition;
  - status;
  - access;
  - engagement;
  - prestige;
  - consequential-power status.

## Existing support for taking the Peter frame seriously

- Previous research identified:
  - General Sir Patrick Sanders / RUSI Land Warfare Conference:
    - Putin described as using the campaigns of Peter the Great as a reference point.
  - Michael Hikari Cecire / RUSI:
    - useful imperial / strategic-culture interpretation.
  - Daniel Shultz / NATO Defense College:
    - Russian historical narrative as active contemporary statecraft / cognitive warfare.
  - Clint Reach / NDU / PRISM:
    - historical and structural roots of Russian strategic conduct.

- These sources do NOT prove the broader Polaris interpretation.
- They establish that:
  - Russian historical reference-making is strategically relevant;
  - Peter is not an invented Polaris connection;
  - historical narratives can perform contemporary political functions.

- Possible compressed Russian message:

  - status:

## Domestic narrative survivability

- Any prospective settlement has both:
  - external strategic requirements;
  - internal political requirements.

- Ask what settlement the Russian state could actually narrate domestically.

- Potentially destabilising narratives:
  - humiliation;
  - emasculation;
  - unconditional defeat;
  - submission;
  - destruction of great-power status.

- Potentially more survivable narratives:
  - negotiated settlement;
  - engagement between consequential powers;
  - recognition of Russian status;
  - restoration of diplomacy / commerce;
  - successful defence of some stated Russian interest.

- This does NOT mean Western governments must provide Putin with propaganda victories.

- It means:
  - settlement design should account for the domestic political system that has to implement and survive the settlement.

## Britain and Germany

- Britain and Germany may be relevant as more than interchangeable NATO members.

- Both occupy historically specific positions in Russian political memory.

- Test whether contemporary Russian signalling treats them differently because of:
  - historical relationships;
  - prestige;

  - diplomatic symbolism.

- Possible implication:
  - historically meaningful European states may have particular usefulness within eventual negotiating architecture.

  - either is neutral;
  - Russia necessarily wants either as broker;
  - historical importance automatically produces diplomatic utility.

## Core epistemic warning

- Intelligent people can be locally correct and globally incomplete.

- Therefore:

  > Capability without culture tells us what Russia can do,
  > but not necessarily what Russia thinks it is doing.

  > Signalling without audience analysis tells us what was said,
  > but not necessarily who was supposed to hear it.

  > Deterrence without a defined desirable end state tells us how
  > confrontation is maintained, but not how security is achieved.

## Critical infrastructure / private power

- Contemporary defence increasingly depends on:
  - cloud;
  - data centres;
  - AI;
  - communications;
  - ports;
  - rail;
  - energy;
  - satellites;
  - subsea cables;
  - logistics;
  - private defence contractors;
  - software.

- Therefore Russia may conceptualise nominally private infrastructure as part of Western state capability.

- Simultaneously, Western governments increasingly conceptualise the same private infrastructure as:
  - critical national infrastructure;
  - strategic capability;
  - national-security dependency.

- Important convergence:
  - adversary and defending state can collapse the public/private distinction from opposite directions.

## Governance question

- A genuine Russian threat does NOT make every domestic security response automatically desirable.

- Ask:

  > When a genuine external threat intersects with a pre-existing governmental
  > desire for stronger control over strategic private infrastructure,
  > how do we distinguish necessary resilience from opportunistic or
  > path-dependent expansion of executive power?

- Do NOT assume bad faith.

- The more interesting failure mode may be structural:

  real threat
      ↓
  infrastructure vulnerability
      ↓
  private capability becomes essential state capability
      ↓
  stronger state–corporate security integration
      ↓
  dependency increases
      ↓
  protecting particular private infrastructure becomes synonymous
  with protecting the state

- Then ask:
  - who gains durable authority?
  - who gains procurement advantage?
  - who gains market position?
  - who gains policy access?
  - which emergency arrangements become permanent?

## Trident / Dreadnought and strategic path dependence

- Britain has made enormous long-term commitments to continuous at-sea nuclear deterrence.

- The £8.4bn Dreadnought announcement discussed previously was:
  - not an entirely new programme;
  - part of an existing long-term commitment.

- But its political presentation demonstrates how deterrence becomes simultaneously:
  - defence policy;
  - industrial policy;
  - jobs policy;
  - regional policy;
  - skills policy;
  - prestige policy.

- This creates path dependence.

- Once the state has organised:
  - shipyards;
  - specialist workers;
  - communities;
  - supply chains;
  - doctrine;
  - bases;
  - budgets
  around one strategic architecture, reopening the foundational choice becomes increasingly difficult.

- Important question:
  - does Britain periodically ask whether it needs deterrence?
  - or does it genuinely re-test whether THIS inherited deterrence architecture remains optimal?

- Technological environment has changed:
  - drones;
  - robotics;
  - autonomous systems;
  - AI;
  - cyber;
  - precision conventional strike;
  - surveillance;
  - missile defence;
  - alliance integration.

- Defence-notes series should distinguish:
  - "Britain needs credible deterrence"
  from
  - "therefore the inherited deterrence architecture is necessarily optimal."

## The outcome problem

- Deterrence is an instrument.
- It is not automatically an end state.

- Ask repeatedly:

  - What outcome are we trying to produce?
  - What behaviour are we trying to deter?
  - What behaviour are we trying to encourage?
  - What would successful de-escalation look like?
  - What Russian signal would indicate that it was working?
  - What would make negotiation possible?
  - What is the stopping condition?

- Warning sign:
  - if every adversarial signal produces another investment in the machinery required to sustain confrontation,
  - while no observable condition exists for reducing that machinery,
  - the system may become self-reinforcing.

- This does NOT prove anyone intentionally wants permanent confrontation.

- Path dependence and institutional incentives can produce similar outcomes without conspiracy.

## Material security / Black Sea / grain

- Foreign policy eventually reaches ordinary households.

- Ukraine is a major global grain producer / exporter.

- Relevant system includes:
  - farmland;
  - Black Sea ports;
  - storage;
  - rail;
  - shipping;
  - insurance;
  - logistics.

- War can affect:
  - expected global supply;
  - shipping risk;
  - freight;
  - insurance;
  - futures;
  - alternative-exporter prices.

- Therefore de-escalation can potentially produce material benefits through:
  - safer production;
  - safer shipping;
  - lower war-risk premiums;
  - more predictable exports;
  - greater commodity stability.

- European governments therefore possess a material domestic interest in successful de-escalation.

## The Weetabix test

- Deliberately mundane systems diagnostic:

  > I would like cheaper Weetabix.

- Meaning:
  - what does "security" ultimately return to ordinary people?

- Possible successful chain:

  credible defence
      ↓
  successful diplomacy
      ↓
  reduced escalation
      ↓
  safer Black Sea commerce
      ↓
  reduced freight / insurance pressure
      ↓
  more stable commodity markets
      ↓
  reduced household economic pressure

- Security outcomes should eventually include:
  - fewer deaths;
  - lower war probability;
  - functioning trade;
  - resilient infrastructure;
  - manageable food / energy costs;
  - fiscal room for civilian services.

- If policy produces indefinitely:
  - greater weapons expenditure;
  - greater emergency authority;
  - greater infrastructure securitisation;
  - greater confrontation
  without an articulated route to a preferable end state:

  ask what the system is actually optimising for.

## Falsification / competing hypotheses

- The Russian-signalling hypothesis gets WEAKER if:

  - supposed historical signals do not recur;
  - Russia behaves inconsistently with the claimed distinction between audiences;
  - diplomatic channels close despite claimed openness;
  - Moscow systematically rejects plausible status-preserving off-ramps;
  - military behaviour better explains events without requiring the additional cultural interpretation;
  - claimed signalling never predicts subsequent Russian positioning;
  - evidence shows the relevant historical references serve primarily domestic propaganda functions.

- It gets STRONGER if:

  - the same historical/status vocabulary recurs across otherwise different episodes;
  - Russia repeatedly differentiates particular Western interlocutors;
  - coercive statements repeatedly coexist with preserved negotiating channels;
  - later Russian diplomatic proposals correspond with earlier rhetorical signalling;
  - Russian internal messaging frames negotiation through recognition / great-power status;
  - cultural/historical interpretation predicts developments that capability-only analysis misses.

## Central proposition for the series

- Not:

  > Russia is right.

- Not:

  > Western defence analysis is wrong.

- Not:

  > Putin is secretly communicating with named Western businessmen.

- Not:

  > Polaris has discovered the Kremlin's real plan.

- Instead:

  > Russia appears to communicate across military, political, historical,
  > cultural, diplomatic and status registers simultaneously.

  > Public Western interpretation often appears substantially more comfortable
  > analysing the military register than integrating the others.

  > This series will therefore record those signals prospectively and test
  > whether treating them as parts of one communicative system improves
  > our ability to understand or predict subsequent Russian behaviour.

- The advantage of doing this NOW:
  - we cannot retrofit every interpretation after knowing what happened.

- Timestamp the hypothesis.
- Preserve the predictions.
- Watch what happens.
- Let the evidence embarrass us if we're wrong.
