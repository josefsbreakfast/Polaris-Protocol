# 🌀 Influential Communication
**First created:** 2026-08-29 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-29  
*A working model for analysing communication as political action: who is being addressed, what effect is being sought, and what subsequent behaviour tells us about the signal.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The historical-memory block leaves us with a possibility.

Contemporary Russian political communication may have several historical vocabularies available to it:

- Petrine;
- Romanov / imperial;
- Soviet;
- late-Soviet;
- post-Soviet;
- Putin-era;
- contemporary nationalist / civilisational.

But identifying a vocabulary does not establish that somebody is using it deliberately.

History gives us possible meanings.

It does not prove intent.

So the next analytical move is from:

> **What does this signal mean?**

toward:

> **What might somebody reasonably expect this signal to do?**

A historical reference can be:

- culturally habitual;
- politically convenient;
- institutionally conventional;
- consciously strategic;
- improvised;
- or several of those things at once.

We therefore need a communications model that does not require:

- mind-reading;
- secret codes;
- perfect coordination;
- extraordinary sophistication;
- or the assumption that Vladimir Putin personally scripts every communicative act of the Russian state.

What we can observe is communication.

What we can test is whether patterns of communication appear designed to alter the information environment and, ultimately, behaviour.

---

## 🌀 Working Definition

> **Influential communication is deliberate, sustained, audience-aware communication intended to change how an audience understands a situation and ultimately influence how that audience behaves.**

The endpoint matters.

Communication is not analytically interesting merely because somebody communicated.

The useful question is:

> **What political or behavioural result would make saying or doing this useful?**

Possible effects include:

- persuasion;
- deterrence;
- reassurance;
- coercion;
- recruitment;
- signalling;
- perception management;
- narrative framing;
- prestige;
- status;
- selective disclosure;
- repetition;
- symbolic action.

Communication can therefore be an instrument of state power.

It sits alongside:

- military action;
- diplomacy;
- intelligence;
- economic pressure;
- law;
- trade;
- sanctions;
- alliance management.

It is not merely commentary accompanying the real thing.

Sometimes communication is part of the real thing.

---

## 🎯 From Statement to Intended Effect

A basic press-reading question is:

> **What did Putin say?**

Keep asking it.

But add:

> **What political or behavioural result would make saying this useful?**

For every candidate communication, ask:

- What should the recipient believe?
- What should they fear?
- What should they stop doing?
- What should they begin doing?
- What outcome is being offered?
- What consequence is being threatened?
- What interpretation should third parties take away?
- What should domestic audiences understand?
- How long has the message been repeated?
- Through which channels?
- Has the demand changed?
- Has the threat changed?
- Has an apparently contradictory channel remained open?

This does not tell us automatically what the communicator intended.

It gives us propositions that subsequent behaviour can test.

---

## 📡 Communication Is Not Only Speech

States communicate through more than words.

Potential communicative acts include:

- speeches;
- press conferences;
- diplomatic notes;
- diplomatic activity;
- military movement;
- intelligence contacts;
- choice of interlocutor;
- deliberate publicity around meetings;
- conspicuous lack of publicity around meetings;
- sanctions;
- economic measures;
- military exercises;
- threats;
- preserved communication channels;
- symbolic visits;
- historical references;
- choice of venue;
- timing;
- silence.

An action can communicate without communication being its only purpose.

A military exercise can simultaneously:

- train personnel;
- test readiness;
- demonstrate capability;
- reassure allies;
- frighten an adversary;
- create bargaining pressure.

Those functions do not cancel one another out.

The same is true of diplomacy, intelligence contact and economic action.

## ⏱️ Sequence, Not Screenshot

One statement is often ambiguous.

A sequence is harder to dismiss.

Do not treat each statement as an isolated object.

Track:

- repetition;
- escalation;
- de-escalation;
- modification;
- audience;
- channel;
- accompanying behaviour;
- preserved channels;
- withdrawn offers;
- repeated demands.

A message may become legible only as a sequence.

Something unclear at `T+0` may look substantially different at:

- `T+48 hours`;
- `T+1 week`;
- `T+1 month`.

That creates an obvious methodological danger:

> **hindsight.**

So preserve what we thought at the time.

The later timeline node exists partly to prevent us quietly rearranging the evidence once we know what happened.

See:

- [⏱️ UK–Russia Signal Timeline](./⏱️_uk_russia_signal_timeline.md).

## 🧬 When History Becomes Functional

The preceding historical nodes give us possible political vocabularies.

A reference to:

- Peter Alekseyevich;
- Romanov Russia;
- Soviet victory;
- hostile encirclement;
- post-Soviet humiliation;
- restored sovereignty

may do more than decorate a speech.

Historical language can potentially:

- establish status;
- identify an audience;
- define a grievance;
- legitimise action domestically;
- establish continuity;
- identify an interlocutor;
- define an enemy;
- make a demand culturally legible;
- establish an acceptable future outcome;
- make compromise narratable without making it look like capitulation.

But the null hypothesis remains available:

> **Sometimes Russians reference Russian history because they are Russian.**

Cultural familiarity is not evidence of an operation.

The test is whether the reference:

- recurs;
- varies with audience;
- accompanies particular demands;
- predicts subsequent behaviour;
- or otherwise appears to perform a consistent political function.

See:

- [🧬 Which Russia Is Speaking?](./🧬_which_russia_is_speaking.md).

## 🕵️ Putin and Professional Formation

There is a temptation to compress this argument into:

> **Putin was basically a PR guy.**

No.

There is, however, a serious point underneath the joke.

Putin was professionally formed inside an intelligence environment in which questions of:

- information;
- networks;
- cultivation;
- intermediaries;
- audiences;
- persuasion;
- influence;
- perception;
- behavioural effects

could be operational concerns.

Foreign intelligence is not simply:

- stealing documents;
- recruiting spies.

It can also require understanding:

- who matters;
- who knows whom;
- what somebody wants;
- what somebody fears;
- who can reach them;
- which intermediary carries credibility;
- what information changes their judgement;
- what behaviour you are trying to produce.

Hence the deliberately stupid shorthand:

> **KGB = PR.**

It is shorthand.

The KGB was not a public-relations agency.

The serious proposition is narrower:

> **Influential communication as an instrument of political action should not be treated as an exotic possibility when analysing a political leader professionally formed within intelligence work.**

That does not establish the intention behind any particular message.

It tells us that audience, information and behavioural effect belong inside the analytical frame.

## 🎭 Not Every Signal Requires Putin Personally

A state is not one man's group chat.

Communication may emerge from:

- presidential leadership;
- foreign ministry practice;
- defence institutions;
- intelligence institutions;
- established doctrine;
- bureaucratic habit;
- domestic political incentives;
- media systems;
- cultural convention;
- improvisation.

Intent therefore has degrees.

A useful ladder might be:

1. centrally designed strategic communication;
2. coordinated institutional communication;
3. established institutional repertoire;
4. politically useful rhetoric;
5. culturally habitual rhetoric;
6. improvised communication;
7. accidental signalling.

Real cases may sit between categories.

This matters because otherwise analysis collapses into:

> **Putin planned everything.**

That is neither necessary nor useful.

A communication environment can become influential without every component having one author.

## 🪞 A Useful Foil: Simplification and Layering

Different political communicators can use very different architectures.

Donald Trump's public communication has often derived force from simplification:

- complicated situation;
- memorable reduction;
- identifiable problem;
- identifiable antagonist;
- apparently simple solution.

That can be extremely effective.

The Russian model being tested here may sometimes operate differently:

- layered historical references;
- multiple audiences;
- ambiguity;
- different meanings available simultaneously;
- explicit threat combined with preserved communication;
- domestic and external readings of the same act.

Neither architecture is automatically:

- cleverer;
- more sophisticated;
- more truthful;
- more effective.

They are different possible ways of producing political effects.

That distinction becomes useful later when comparing authoritarian signalling styles without pretending that the states themselves are equivalent.

See:

- [🫎 Nuclear Moose](./🫎_nuclear_moose.md).

## 🗺️ Intended Audience Is Not the Whole Audience

A public communication rarely has only one recipient.

There may be:

- the person explicitly addressed;
- the government behind them;
- allied governments;
- adversaries;
- domestic audiences;
- military audiences;
- financial markets;
- journalists;
- political intermediaries;
- people expected to retransmit the message.

So ask not only:

> **Who is being spoken to?**

but:

> **Who is expected to overhear?**

And then:

> **Who is expected to understand it differently?**

This is why internal and external Russian communication needs to be separated before it can be recombined.

See:

- [🗺️ Internal and External Russia](./🗺️_internal_and_external_russia.md).

## 🛰️ Audience Architecture

The practical framework developed later in this block asks:

1. Who is explicitly addressed?
2. Who is expected to overhear?
3. Who may be intended to interpret the message differently?
4. What behaviour should stop?
5. What behaviour should begin?
6. What consequence accompanies non-compliance?
7. What benefit accompanies compliance?
8. Why this communication channel?
9. Why this speaker?
10. Why this timing?
11. Which channels remain deliberately open?
12. What does the message mean domestically?

## 🌍 Same Language, Different Language

Miscommunication does not require anybody to be stupid.

Two people can technically speak the same language while attaching different meanings to the same expression.

British English and American English provide endless low-stakes examples.

The words are recognisable.

The cultural assumptions around them are not always identical.

Strategic communication has the same problem at much higher stakes.

States may share concepts such as:

- deterrence;
- sovereignty;
- escalation;
- prestige;
- defence;
- humiliation;
- negotiation;
- compromise;
- victory.

That does not guarantee that the concepts carry identical cultural or political weight.

Non-verbal signalling has the same problem.

A gesture that looks routine to one political culture may look:

- threatening;
- humiliating;
- conciliatory;
- weak;
- prestigious;
- insulting

to another.

That makes specialist cultural knowledge part of security analysis rather than decorative context.

It is one reason Ukrainian expertise on Russia cannot simply be substituted with generic Western foreign-policy expertise.

The point is not that Russia is uniquely unknowable.

It is almost the opposite:

> **Russia is a bespoke political culture, like every other political culture, and understanding an adversary is part of dealing competently with it.**

## 📥 Intended Message ≠ Received Message

Even deliberate communication can fail.

Track separately:

### INTENDED

What effect might the communicator plausibly have wanted?

### RECEIVED

How did the recipient publicly or behaviourally appear to interpret it?

### RESPONSE

What did the recipient subsequently do?

### RESULT

Did the apparent communication objective occur?

This distinction matters because:

- a threat can provoke rather than deter;
- reassurance can be disbelieved;
- ambiguity can produce escalation;
- historical language can be misread;
- domestic messaging can accidentally alarm foreign audiences;
- foreign signalling can create unwanted domestic expectations.

Miscommunication is not noise outside strategy.

It can become part of the strategic environment.

## 📰 The Public Western Read

Western defence and security analysis is often extremely good at questions such as:

- capability;
- force posture;
- readiness;
- escalation;
- battlefield utility;
- weapons range;
- alliance commitments.

Those questions are indispensable.

The question for this cluster is whether publicly visible interpretation gives equivalent weight to:

- audience;
- status;
- recognition;
- historical grammar;
- prestige;
- humiliation;
- domestic narrative;
- preserved channels;
- communicative purpose.

Be precise about the claim.

We cannot see:

- classified intelligence;
- diplomatic cables;
- private military advice;
- intelligence liaison;
- private ministerial discussion.

So the claim is not:

> **The West does not understand Russia.**

It is:

> **Publicly visible Western interpretation may underweight some dimensions of Russian communication, and we can test whether adding them improves the analysis.**

- [📰 What the Press Thinks Is Happening](./📰_what_the_press_thinks_is_happening.md);

## ⚠️ Failure Modes

This framework has several obvious ways to become bullshit.

### Pareidolia

Finding meaningful patterns because we expected to find them.

### Assuming sophistication

Ambiguity is not proof that somebody constructed a brilliant multilayered message.

Sometimes communication is simply unclear.

### Assuming institutional unity

Russian institutions can disagree.

Messages can conflict.

Bureaucracies can be messy.

### Confusing effect with intent

If a statement frightened Britain, that does not prove frightening Britain was its intended purpose.

### Confusing repetition with coordination

Several actors repeating a culturally common idea does not automatically establish central direction.

### Strategicising ordinary culture

Not every historical reference is a signal.

Not every symbol is an operation.

### Secret-success logic

If a communication apparently fails, we cannot rescue the theory by announcing that failure was secretly the real objective.

### Retrospective message discovery

If the intended meaning becomes visible only after the outcome, we need to ask whether we actually predicted anything.

## 🧪 Operational Method

For each candidate signal, record:

### OBSERVED

What was actually said or done?

### AUDIENCE

Who was explicitly addressed?

Who else was likely to receive it?

### CHANNEL

Speech?

Diplomatic contact?

Military action?

Intelligence channel?

Economic measure?

Symbolic act?

Silence?

### REPERTOIRE

If relevant, which historical or political grammar appears?

### PLAUSIBLE INTENDED EFFECT

What change in belief or behaviour would make the communication useful?

### ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATION

What simpler explanation fits?

### EXPECTED BEHAVIOURAL RESPONSE

If our interpretation is correct, what might happen next?

### OBSERVED RESPONSE

What actually happened?

Then leave the record alone.

Do not quietly rewrite the prediction.

## 📈 What Would Make This Model Useful?

The model earns its place if it helps us:

- identify differentiated audiences;
- distinguish threats from bargaining positions;
- recognise preserved diplomatic channels;
- interpret historical references prospectively;
- identify behavioural demands;
- notice changes in signalling;
- anticipate responses;
- understand why apparently contradictory actions coexist;
- design better questions about diplomatic off-ramps.

If capability-only analysis performs just as well:

> **use capability-only analysis.**

If this framework explains everything after the fact and predicts nothing beforehand:

> **bin it.**

## 🧿 Working Proposition

The historical block asked:

> **Which Russian past is being activated for this audience, in this dispute, for this purpose?**

This node adds a second question:

> **What would activating that past be expected to make the audience think or do?**

That is the transition.

History gives us possible vocabularies.

Communication analysis asks whether those vocabularies are being used to produce effects.

Audience analysis then asks:

> **effects on whom?**

So from here the cluster moves into:

- domestic versus external political function;
- audience architecture;
- differentiated treatment of Western states;
- prospective timeline analysis.

The rule throughout remains simple:

> **Start with the observable communication, identify plausible intended effects, and then watch behaviour.**
