# ☢️_trident_and_path_dependence.md

- Purpose:
  - use Dreadnought / Trident as a case study in strategic path dependence;
  - examine how a defence architecture can become embedded in industry, employment, regional policy, prestige, and doctrine.

- Core distinction:
  > Britain may require credible deterrence.
  >
  > That does not automatically establish that every inherited component of the current deterrence architecture remains optimal indefinitely.

- Existing context:
  - Dreadnought is a long-running programme;
  - recent £8.4bn political presentation did not create the programme from scratch;
  - it demonstrated how nuclear deterrence is now narrated simultaneously as:
    - defence;
    - industrial policy;
    - jobs;
    - apprenticeships;
    - regional investment;
    - national prestige.

- Path-dependence sequence:

  strategic choice
      ↓
  infrastructure
      ↓
  shipyards
      ↓
  specialist skills
      ↓
  supply chains
      ↓
  communities / jobs
      ↓
  doctrine
      ↓
  budgets
      ↓
  political constituency for continuation

- At that point:
  - the industrial ecosystem may begin preserving the strategic architecture,
    rather than merely implementing a newly chosen strategy.

- Questions:
  - when was the foundational choice last genuinely reopened?
  - what alternatives were compared?
  - were alternatives treated as serious options or merely supporting capabilities?
  - what assumptions were held constant?

- Contemporary technological changes to consider:
  - drones;
  - autonomous systems;
  - robotics;
  - AI;
  - cyber;
  - precision conventional strike;
  - advanced surveillance;
  - air / missile defence;
  - alliance integration;
  - distributed systems.

- Possible alternatives / complements to compare:
  - submarine-launched nuclear deterrence;
  - other nuclear architectures;
  - precision conventional deterrence;
  - autonomous systems;
  - cyber;
  - missile defence;
  - alliance structures;
  - mixed deterrence models.

- Important:
  - this node is not "abolish Trident".
  - it is:
    > periodically re-test the strategic assumption rather than only re-fund the inherited answer.

- Political-economy question:
  - how much of the programme's durability now comes from:
    - strategic necessity;
    - sunk cost;
    - industrial capacity;
    - employment;
    - prestige;
    - institutional inertia?

- Strong working line:
  > A strategic system can become difficult to question precisely because too many non-strategic things have been built around it.
