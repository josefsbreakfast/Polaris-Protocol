# 💾 The Data State
**First created:** 2026-08-30 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-30  
*What happens when the British state increasingly acts through data, cloud, compute and infrastructure that it may regulate, procure and protect without necessarily owning?*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

[🦠 The Data Barons and National Security](./🦠_the_data_barons_and_national_security.md) establishes the broad problem:

> **Privatisation changes ownership. It does not necessarily change strategic function.**

This node turns that question toward Britain.

The British state increasingly depends upon digital systems for:

- administration;
- health;
- defence;
- intelligence;
- policing;
- communications;
- finance;
- logistics;
- emergency response;
- scientific research;
- AI.

But "the state" does not necessarily own every layer through which those functions operate.

It may instead:

- own;
- procure;
- rent;
- license;
- regulate;
- certify;
- protect;
- subsidise;
- prioritise;
- depend upon

different pieces of the same capability.

The central question is:

> **At what point does "government using private infrastructure" become "private infrastructure functioning as part of the state"?**

---

## 🧱 The State Is Not One Computer

Avoid imagining British government data as one giant database.

The actual object is closer to an ecosystem of:

- departmental systems;
- legacy infrastructure;
- commercial cloud;
- private data centres;
- government-owned infrastructure;
- specialist databases;
- health infrastructure;
- defence systems;
- intelligence systems;
- communications networks;
- outsourced software;
- shared services;
- public-sector compute;
- research compute;
- AI infrastructure.

These systems do not necessarily:

- use the same architecture;
- have the same security classification;
- share data;
- interoperate;
- sit under the same legal regime;
- have the same commercial supplier.

The data state is therefore not necessarily one integrated machine.

It is better understood as:

> **a state whose ability to act increasingly depends upon interconnected digital machinery.**

---

## 🇬🇧 What Does Britain Actually Control?

For each important capability, separate:

- physical location;
- legal jurisdiction;
- ownership;
- operation;
- maintenance;
- hardware;
- cloud platform;
- software;
- data;
- models;
- technical expertise;
- contractual control;
- emergency authority.

These can produce very different answers.

A system can be:

- located in Britain;
- governed by British law;

while still being:

- foreign-owned;
- dependent on foreign hardware;
- dependent on proprietary software;
- difficult for government to operate independently.

Likewise, government can have substantial contractual or regulatory control over infrastructure it does not own.

So:

> **ownership is one component of sovereignty, not the whole test.**

---

## ☁️ Sovereign Cloud Is Not a Magic Phrase

"Sovereign cloud" can refer to different properties.

Ask which sovereignty is actually being claimed.

### Data sovereignty

- Where is the data stored?
- Which law governs it?
- Who can legally access it?

### Operational sovereignty

- Can Britain continue operating the service independently?
- Who possesses the expertise?
- Who controls updates and maintenance?

### Technological sovereignty

- Who owns the hardware?
- Who designs the chips?
- Who controls the platform and software?

### Commercial sovereignty

- Can government switch supplier?
- How long would migration take?
- What would it cost?
- Is there a credible substitute?

### Strategic sovereignty

- Can Britain preserve the capability during:
  - geopolitical disruption;
  - supplier failure;
  - sanctions;
  - cyber attack;
  - war;
  - severe infrastructure failure?

A service being hosted on British soil answers only part of that problem.

> **Compute located in Britain is not necessarily compute controlled by Britain.**

---

## 🏢 Data Centres as Critical National Infrastructure

Britain designated data centres as Critical National Infrastructure in 2024.

That is conceptually important.

It places privately operated physical compute infrastructure alongside a wider class of systems whose disruption can have serious consequences for:

- essential services;
- economic security;
- public administration;
- national security.

This does not turn every data centre into a government facility.

It does establish that:

> **private ownership and national strategic importance can coexist.**

The designation also tells us something about British state perception.

If government decides an infrastructure class requires national-security protection, it has identified that infrastructure as part of the country's strategic dependency map.

Cross-reference:

- [🇬🇧 What Is Britain Signalling?](./🇬🇧_what_is_britain_signalling.md).

---

## 🛡️ Protecting Something Reveals That It Matters

Critical-infrastructure policy is defensive.

It is also informational.

When Britain publicly protects:

- data centres;
- subsea cables;
- communications;
- cloud;
- energy infrastructure;

it reveals something about what Britain considers important to continued state and economic function.

That does not mean the infrastructure becomes a lawful military target.

It means:

> **defensive policy can reveal strategic dependency.**

A capable adversary can read the same policy documents.

---

## 🪖 Defence Cloud

The defence problem makes the category especially visible.

Modern military effectiveness increasingly depends upon:

- data;
- communications;
- cloud;
- software;
- AI;
- logistics;
- sensor integration;
- analytical systems.

Commercial technology can therefore contribute directly to military capability without the commercial provider becoming the military.

The Oracle / MOD relationship is useful as a case study because it brings into one public story:

- commercial cloud;
- defence data;
- AI;
- digital transformation;
- operational effectiveness.

Do not infer from a commercial defence contract that:

- all government systems are integrated;
- civilian data is available to defence;
- the supplier controls military decisions;
- every product offered by the supplier is used by MOD.

The narrower point is sufficient:

> **state military capability can depend upon commercially supplied digital infrastructure.**

---

## 🏥 The Civilian State

The same problem exists outside defence.

Relevant systems include:

- NHS infrastructure;
- benefits administration;
- tax;
- identity;
- local government;
- transport;
- emergency services;
- communications;
- financial infrastructure.

Civilian systems can become national-security relevant because state continuity depends upon them.

But keep the categories separate.

Publicly stated separation between civilian and military systems does not by itself answer questions about:

- emergency interoperability;
- contingency arrangements;
- technical compatibility;
- emergency legal authority.

Equally:

> **theoretical interoperability is not evidence that integration occurs.**

---

## 🔗 Interoperability Is a Capability, Not Proof of Use

This distinction needs to remain hard.

Ask separately:

### What is legally possible?

- Which data-sharing powers exist?
- Which emergency powers exist?
- What requires ministerial authority?
- What requires another legal gateway?

### What is technically possible?

- Can systems communicate?
- Are formats compatible?
- Can capacity be transferred?
- Can workloads migrate?

### What has actually happened?

- Has interoperability been exercised?
- Has data been shared?
- Has capacity been redirected?
- Has an emergency power been used?

### What is publicly documented?

- Which arrangements are confirmed?
- Which remain unknown?

Two rules:

> **Absence of a public prohibition is not evidence that integration occurs.**

and:

> **Absence of public detail is not evidence that integration cannot occur.**

Unknown remains a valid category.

---

## 🧠 Government Compute

Not all compute available to the British state is the same thing.

Separate:

- commercial cloud purchased by government;
- dedicated government infrastructure;
- research supercomputing;
- AI compute;
- defence compute;
- intelligence infrastructure;
- commercially hosted public services;
- capacity physically located in Britain but serving private customers.

For each ask:

- Who paid for it?
- Who owns it?
- Who allocates it?
- Who can use it?
- What workload is it intended for?
- Can capacity be reassigned?
- Is it reserved?
- Is it shared?
- Is it commercially available?

This prevents:

> **Britain has X amount of compute**

from becoming a misleading shorthand.

---

## 🧮 Capacity Versus Capability

Raw compute capacity is not automatically state capability.

A useful distinction is:

```text
capacity
    ↓
access
    ↓
software
    ↓
data
    ↓
expertise
    ↓
operational integration
    ↓
usable state capability
```

A government can live beside enormous commercial compute capacity without having an automatic right or technical ability to use it.

Conversely, relatively small dedicated capacity may be strategically valuable if government has:

- guaranteed access;
- appropriate software;
- relevant data;
- trained operators;
- resilient connectivity.

So ask:

> **How much compute exists?**

and separately:

> **How much compute can the British state actually command for a defined purpose?**

---

## 🚨 The Emergency Test

Normal conditions can hide dependency.

Emergency conditions expose it.

Ask what happens during:

- major cyber attack;
- infrastructure failure;
- severe energy shortage;
- communications disruption;
- hostile-state activity;
- war;
- another national emergency.

If capacity becomes scarce:

> **Who gets priority?**

Potential claimants include:

- defence;
- intelligence;
- NHS;
- emergency services;
- central government;
- critical infrastructure;
- finance;
- communications;
- research;
- commercial customers;
- ordinary public services.

This is where questions of:

- ownership;
- contract;
- regulation;
- emergency law;
- technical control

meet.

---

## ⚡ Who Is Allowed to Lose Service?

Scarcity makes sovereignty visible.

Imagine insufficient:

- electricity;
- network bandwidth;
- compute;
- cloud capacity;
- cooling;
- fuel;
- replacement hardware;
- technical labour.

Then ask:

- Which workloads are protected?
- Which are interruptible?
- Can commercial capacity be redirected?
- Can government require priority?
- Who makes that decision?
- Who compensates displaced users?
- What oversight applies?
- How long does priority last?

Do not assume the answers.

This node should map what is:

- publicly established;
- legally possible;
- technically possible;
- exercised;
- unknown.

> **The real sovereignty test is not only who owns the infrastructure in normal times. It is who decides what it does when there is not enough to go around.**

## ⚖️ Direction Powers

The Cyber Security and Resilience framework makes the governance question particularly important.

If ministers acquire or exercise stronger powers to direct strategically important infrastructure during an imminent or live national-security threat, the public/private distinction changes again.

The state may not own the infrastructure.

But it may be able to tell the owner:

> **You must do this.**

That raises questions about:

- thresholds;
- necessity;
- proportionality;
- duration;
- review;
- compensation;
- judicial oversight;
- parliamentary oversight;
- transparency after the emergency.

This belongs in detail in:

- [⚖️ Emergency Power and Governance](./⚖️_emergency_power_and_governance.md).

Here, the important structural point is:

> **private ownership can coexist with substantial state direction.**

---

## 🧩 Dependency Runs Both Ways

The relationship between government and provider is not simply:

> corporation powerful, state weak.

Nor is it simply:

> state regulates, corporation obeys.

Increasing integration can make both actors more powerful in different ways.

### Provider power can increase because:

- government depends on the service;
- migration is difficult;
- expertise is concentrated;
- alternatives are limited;
- switching carries operational risk.

### State power can increase because:

- infrastructure is designated strategic;
- resilience requirements increase;
- security regulation expands;
- emergency direction becomes possible;
- commercial operation becomes subject to national-security requirements.

Therefore:

> **state dependency and state authority can expand at the same time.**

---

## 🔒 Lock-In Is a National-Security Variable

Vendor lock-in is normally discussed as:

- procurement;
- competition;
- value for money.

Once the service becomes strategically essential, it is also a resilience question.

Ask:

- How quickly can government migrate?
- Can data be exported in usable formats?
- Are applications portable?
- Does another supplier have sufficient capacity?
- Do staff know how to operate the alternative?
- Would migration itself create security risk?
- Are several nominal alternatives dependent on the same upstream infrastructure?

A provider does not need malicious intent for lock-in to become strategically important.

> **Dependency can be dangerous even when everybody behaves properly.**

---

## 🌍 Foreign Ownership and Allied Dependency

Foreign ownership is not automatically hostile ownership.

Britain routinely relies upon companies headquartered in allied states.

That can provide:

- capability;
- scale;
- innovation;
- interoperability;
- investment.

But allied dependency remains dependency.

Ask:

- Which jurisdiction governs the parent company?
- What legal demands could another state make?
- What happens during political disagreement?
- What happens during export restrictions?
- What happens if supply chains fail?
- What happens if the ally needs the same scarce capacity?

The correct question is not:

> **foreign = unsafe.**

It is:

> **What dependencies exist, and how resilient are they under conditions less friendly than today?**

---

## 🌐 The Data State Has Geography

Digital infrastructure is physical.

It depends upon:

- land;
- electricity;
- water;
- cables;
- network routes;
- ports;
- supply chains;
- repair capacity;
- skilled labour.

This connects the data state directly to:

- energy policy;
- planning;
- industrial policy;
- maritime security;
- subsea-cable protection;
- civil resilience.

A "digital" state can therefore be disrupted by extremely analogue problems.

> **There is no cloud without power, cables and somewhere to put the computers.**

## 🗳️ The Democratic Problem

The public debate around digital government often focuses on:

- privacy;
- efficiency;
- innovation;
- cost.

National-security integration adds another layer.

Citizens can reasonably ask:

- Which state functions depend upon private infrastructure?
- Which companies have become difficult to replace?
- What public money built or supports the capability?
- What guarantees of access does government possess?
- What happens in emergency?
- Which services receive priority?
- What powers can ministers exercise over providers?
- What oversight follows?

This is not an argument that every operational detail should be public.

Security sometimes requires secrecy.

The governance question is whether enough is visible for democratic institutions to assess:

- dependency;
- concentration;
- resilience;
- authority;
- accountability.

> **Secrecy about operations does not require vagueness about governance.**

---

## 🪞 What Britain Signals by Building the Data State

The infrastructure has an external audience whether Britain intends one or not.

Investment in:

- sovereign compute;
- defence cloud;
- cyber resilience;
- data-centre protection;
- subsea infrastructure;
- AI capacity

can communicate:

- expectation of prolonged digital dependency;
- preparation for infrastructure disruption;
- commitment to AI-enabled state capability;
- identification of strategic vulnerabilities;
- willingness to integrate commercial technology into national power.

That does not establish that every programme is Russia-directed.

The threat environment is broader.

But Russia can still map the capability.

> **If Britain identifies something as essential to national security, Britain has also told the outside world that it matters.**

---

## 🇷🇺 The Adversary Does Not Care About the Org Chart

A foreign strategic analyst may care less about whether a capability is formally:

- public;
- private;
- outsourced;
- commercially operated;

than whether it helps Britain:

- communicate;
- decide;
- mobilise;
- defend;
- sustain government;
- support allies;
- recover from attack.

This is where the legal and strategic maps can diverge.

A private company may be legally outside the state while its infrastructure contributes materially to state capability.

That does not erase the distinction between company and government.

It means:

> **the distinction may matter differently to lawyers, citizens, suppliers and adversaries.**

---

## ⚠️ Boundaries

Do not infer:

- one integrated British database;
- automatic civilian-military data sharing;
- secret defence access to NHS data;
- government access to all privately owned compute;
- emergency requisition powers without legal evidence;
- Russia-specific purpose from general resilience measures;
- hostile intent from foreign ownership;
- state control merely because infrastructure is designated CNI.

Keep separate:

- ownership;
- access;
- technical possibility;
- legal authority;
- exercised power;
- strategic relevance.

- Which British state functions depend materially on commercial cloud?
- Which providers hold major central-government contracts?
- Which providers support MOD?
- What infrastructure remains government-owned?
- What does current UK policy mean by "sovereign cloud" and "sovereign compute"?
- Where is relevant infrastructure physically located?
- Which foreign jurisdictions sit elsewhere in the stack?
- What exit plans exist for major providers?
- What are realistic migration times?
- Which systems are interoperable?
- Which interoperability arrangements have actually been exercised?
- What emergency direction powers exist now?
- What additional powers are proposed?
- What triggers those powers?
- Who receives priority during electricity, network, cloud or compute scarcity?
- Are priority categories publicly defined?
- Can commercial workloads be displaced?
- What compensation applies?
- What parliamentary, judicial or regulatory oversight exists?
- Which dependencies are publicly acknowledged?
- Which remain unknown?
- How much state-accessible compute exists compared with total compute physically located in Britain?
