# 👻 The Soviet Ghost in the Room
**First created:** 2026-08-29 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-29  
*The Romanovs are not the only Russian past in the room.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

There is an obvious danger in asking whether contemporary Russia is making selected Romanov political meanings usable again:

> **the Romanovs can start eating the rest of Russian history.**

They should not.

Modern Russia passed through more than seven decades of Soviet government between the execution of the imperial family and the creation of the Russian Federation.

That period was not an intermission.

It transformed:

- the state;
- the military;
- the security services;
- the economy;
- borders;
- institutions;
- education;
- political language;
- public memory;
- Russia's relationship with Europe;
- Russia's relationship with the United States;
- ideas about war;
- ideas about sacrifice;
- ideas about security;
- ideas about Russia's place in the world.

So if [🏛️ The Romanov Memory](./🏛️_the_romanov_memory.md) asks what imperial history has become politically usable again, this node asks the necessary second question:

> **Where is the Soviet inheritance still doing the actual work?**

---

## 🧬 More Than One Past Can Be Alive

Romanov memory and Soviet memory are not mutually exclusive.

Contemporary Russian political culture does not have to choose neatly between:

> **Empire**

and:

> **USSR.**

A state can draw from several historical repertoires at once.

A message might contain:

### Romanov / imperial language

- prestige;
- continuity;
- territorial history;
- great-power recognition;
- Orthodoxy;
- European consequence;
- restoration.

### Soviet language

- hostile encirclement;
- endurance;
- mobilisation;
- sacrifice;
- industrial capacity;
- anti-fascism;
- security-state competence;
- superpower status;
- suspicion of Western intention.

And both can appear in the same political story.

That mixture is not necessarily incoherent.

It may be one of the defining characteristics of contemporary Russian historical politics.

---

## 👻 The Ghost Is Not One Thing

"Soviet" is itself too broad if we use it carelessly.

The USSR existed from 1922 to 1991, after a revolutionary period that had already radically transformed the former empire.

Those decades contained substantially different political environments.

So this node should distinguish at least:

- revolutionary / early Soviet;
- Stalinist;
- post-Stalin;
- Brezhnev-era;
- late-Soviet;
- intelligence-service institutional culture.

A reference that sounds Soviet may belong much more specifically to one of those repertoires.

> **Do not solve an overbroad Romanov analogy by replacing it with an overbroad Soviet analogy.**

---

## 🔥 Revolutionary / Early Soviet

The revolutionary and early Soviet period brings its own political grammar.

Potentially relevant themes include:

- rupture with the old order;
- revolutionary legitimacy;
- internal and external enemies;
- civil war;
- ideological mobilisation;
- international revolutionary politics;
- construction of a new state;
- political violence;
- survival under extreme pressure.

This repertoire is particularly different from Romanov continuity.

Where imperial memory can say:

> **We have been here for centuries.**

revolutionary memory can say:

> **We survived the destruction of the old world and built another one.**

Those are not the same claim.

---

## 🏭 Stalinist Repertoires

Stalinist history introduces another cluster of meanings:

- forced industrialisation;
- centralised state capacity;
- extreme mobilisation;
- repression;
- sacrifice;
- rapid military-industrial development;
- state survival;
- victory at catastrophic human cost.

It also sits underneath one of the most politically powerful Soviet memories:

> **the Great Patriotic War.**

That memory cannot be treated as a decorative historical reference.

It can carry:

- enormous family memory;
- national sacrifice;
- military legitimacy;
- survival;
- victory;
- anti-fascism;
- existential threat;
- the idea that extraordinary suffering can be endured and ultimately overcome.

When contemporary Russian rhetoric uses the language of fascism, existential danger, sacrifice or historical survival, the relevant grammar may be far more Soviet than Romanov.

---

## 🎖️ The Great Patriotic War

For Western readers, "Second World War" and "Great Patriotic War" should not be treated as emotionally interchangeable labels.

They overlap historically.

They do not necessarily perform the same cultural work.

Within Russian and wider former-Soviet memory, the Great Patriotic War can operate as:

- family history;
- state history;
- military history;
- trauma;
- victory;
- sacrifice;
- proof of endurance;
- proof of Russian / Soviet consequence;
- a foundational political myth.

That makes it an unusually powerful political vocabulary.

It can help frame:

- who counts as an aggressor;
- who counts as a fascist;
- what resistance means;
- what sacrifice is justified;
- what victory requires;
- what historical role Russia imagines itself occupying.

This does not mean every contemporary use of anti-fascist language is analytically valid simply because the Soviet Union fought Nazi Germany.

It means:

> **the language arrives carrying an enormous pre-existing emotional and political charge.**

That charge matters when analysing influence.

---

## 🧱 Encirclement

One of the most important Soviet repertoires to track is the idea of hostile external encirclement.

Possible components include:

- adversarial blocs;
- hostile Western intention;
- military containment;
- ideological hostility;
- external attempts to weaken the state;
- strategic depth;
- buffer space;
- suspicion of neighbouring military infrastructure.

This can intersect strongly with contemporary Russian discussion of:

- NATO;
- missile systems;
- bases;
- Ukraine;
- sanctions;
- Western intelligence;
- regime change;
- political interference.

The important question is not:

> **Is every Russian claim about encirclement objectively correct?**

The analytical question is:

> **How deeply does an encirclement model structure the way Russian institutions interpret Western behaviour?**

A state can act on a threat perception even when another state thinks that perception is exaggerated, self-serving or wrong.

---

## 🗺️ Strategic Depth

Strategic depth also has more than one Russian historical route.

Russia's geography long predates the Soviet Union.

So do invasions of Russian territory.

But the Soviet experience gives strategic depth a particularly powerful twentieth-century military memory.

That matters when analysing concepts such as:

- buffers;
- border security;
- neighbouring alliances;
- military infrastructure;
- forward deployment;
- invasion routes;
- territorial defence.

Again:

> **explaining why a security concept is culturally or institutionally powerful does not validate territorial aggression undertaken in its name.**

Ukraine remains sovereign territory.

Historical security memory does not create a contemporary entitlement to someone else's country.

---

## 🧱 Endurance

Romanov prestige and Soviet endurance are not the same political story.

Soviet memory contains repeated narratives of:

- surviving invasion;
- surviving deprivation;
- absorbing enormous losses;
- industrial mobilisation;
- strategic patience;
- continuing under pressure;
- eventually outlasting an opponent.

That can create a very different communication problem for Western policymakers.

If one side thinks:

> **pressure will make the opponent conclude the cost is intolerable**

while the opponent's political culture is simultaneously mobilising:

> **we have endured worse and we will outlast you**

then both sides may believe time is working for them.

That does not tell us which side is correct.

It tells us to pay attention to what each side believes endurance means.

---

## 🏭 Industrial Capacity as Political Memory

The Soviet Union was not only a political system.

It was also an enormous industrial and military-industrial project.

Industrial capacity therefore carries historical meanings around:

- sovereignty;
- military strength;
- technological competence;
- mobilisation;
- independence;
- strategic resilience;
- great-power status.

Contemporary Russian policy around:

- defence production;
- domestic manufacturing;
- technological substitution;
- sanctions adaptation;
- energy;
- military-industrial capacity

may therefore operate in a landscape where industrial capability already has unusually dense political meaning.

Sometimes this will simply be ordinary wartime economics.

Sometimes the historical resonance may matter.

The job is to distinguish them.

---

## 🕵️ The Security State

This is one of the clearest places where a Romanov analogy can become actively misleading.

Modern Russian security institutions inherit much more directly from Soviet institutions than from the imperial court.

That includes professional traditions around:

- intelligence;
- counterintelligence;
- political security;
- secrecy;
- cultivation;
- networks;
- influence;
- intermediaries;
- information;
- perception;
- behavioural effect.

Putin's own professional formation belongs inside this history.

That does not mean:

> **Putin does everything because KGB.**

It means that when analysing a political system in which former intelligence and security personnel have been consequential, intelligence-service professional culture belongs inside the explanatory set.

See:

- [🌀 Influential Communication](./🌀_influential_communication.md).

---

## 🌀 "KGB = PR"

This is shorthand.

It is deliberately ridiculous shorthand.

It is not a literal institutional description.

The serious proposition underneath it is:

> **Influential communication can be an operational tool.**

Intelligence work is not limited to:

- stealing documents;
- recruiting spies;
- breaking codes.

Relevant professional concerns can include:

- audience analysis;
- cultivation;
- persuasion;
- influence;
- recruitment;
- intermediaries;
- network analysis;
- information operations;
- perception management;
- behavioural effect.

So when contemporary Russian communication appears:

- sustained;
- audience-aware;
- repetitive;
- segmented;
- channel-specific;
- designed to change behaviour rather than merely state a position,

it is reasonable to ask whether security-service professional inheritance belongs somewhere in the analysis.

Not because every speech is a secret operation.

Because:

> **people and institutions tend to use the tools they know how to use.**

---

## 📡 Propaganda Is Too Small a Word

"Propaganda" can sometimes become a way of avoiding analysis.

It tells us:

> **this communication is trying to persuade us.**

Fine.

Then what?

The more useful questions are:

- persuade whom?
- of what?
- toward what behaviour?
- through which channel?
- over what period?
- with what emotional register?
- using which historical memory?
- with which intermediaries?
- accompanied by which material actions?
- what happens if the audience refuses?

That is why this node feeds directly into:

- [🛰️ Audience Architecture](./🛰️_audience_architecture.md).

The question is not merely whether something is propaganda.

It is what the communication is trying to **do**.

---

## 🌍 Superpower Memory

The Soviet Union was not merely a large state.

It was one pole of a global superpower system.

That history matters when contemporary Russia talks about:

- multipolarity;
- strategic parity;
- Washington;
- nuclear status;
- spheres of influence;
- great-power negotiation;
- international order;
- recognition.

Here the Romanov and Soviet repertoires may overlap.

Romanov memory can supply:

> **Russia is an old great power and should be treated as one.**

Soviet memory can supply:

> **Russia belongs at the table where the major powers decide the strategic order.**

Those propositions can reinforce each other while coming from different histories.

---

## 🇺🇸 Washington Has a Soviet Ghost Too

The United States occupies a particularly important place in Soviet strategic memory.

The Cold War created decades of:

- direct strategic competition;
- intelligence conflict;
- nuclear deterrence;
- proxy conflict;
- ideological competition;
- arms-control negotiation;
- crisis management;
- bilateral superpower diplomacy.

That means contemporary Moscow–Washington communication may carry institutional habits that are not best explained through Peter Alekseyevich or the Romanovs at all.

A preserved intelligence channel, nuclear dialogue or direct strategic negotiation may be legible partly through a Cold War inheritance in which:

> **adversaries could threaten one another profoundly while continuing to communicate because the consequences of not communicating were worse.**

This matters to:

- [🇺🇸 Washington Is Not London](./🇺🇸_washington_is_not_london.md).

---

## 🧠 Suspicion of Western Intention

A recurrent Soviet repertoire concerns not merely Western capability but Western **intention**.

The underlying question becomes:

> **What are they really trying to do to us?**

That can generate interpretations centred on:

- containment;
- subversion;
- intelligence penetration;
- political interference;
- economic pressure;
- regime change;
- military encirclement.

Some of those threats can be real.

Some can be exaggerated.

Some can be politically useful.

Those possibilities can coexist.

The analytical mistake is assuming that because a narrative is politically useful, nobody inside the system believes it.

The opposite mistake is assuming that because actors believe it, their interpretation must be objectively correct.

## 🗣️ Internal and External Functions

Soviet-derived language can also do different work for different audiences.

### Internally

It can support:

- endurance;
- mobilisation;
- legitimacy;
- sacrifice;
- unity;
- historical continuity;
- threat perception.

### Externally

It can communicate:

- resolve;
- willingness to absorb costs;
- escalation risk;
- suspicion;
- deterrence;
- demands for recognition.

One message may perform both functions.

See:

- [🗺️ Internal and External Russia](./🗺️_internal_and_external_russia.md).

## 👑 Where Romanov and Soviet Languages Meet

The most interesting cases may be the ones that refuse to fit neatly into either box.

Consider:

### Great-power status

Romanov:
- old European imperial power;
- recognised sovereign consequence;
- prestige.

Soviet:
- superpower;
- nuclear parity;
- global strategic consequence.

### Territorial security

Romanov:
- imperial expansion;
- frontier;
- strategic access.

Soviet:
- buffers;
- strategic depth;
- hostile blocs;
- invasion memory.

### State strength

Romanov:
- ruler;
- dynasty;
- administrative modernisation;
- imperial continuity.

Soviet:
- industrial mobilisation;
- security state;
- central capacity;
- collective endurance.

### Historical legitimacy

Romanov:
- centuries of state continuity.

Soviet:
- revolution;
- victory;
- sacrifice;
- superpower achievement.

Contemporary Russia may be capable of saying:

> **We are the old Russia.**
>
> **We are the victorious Soviet state.**
>
> **We are the post-Soviet state that survived collapse.**
>
> **We are the modern Russian Federation.**

Those identities do not have to reconcile perfectly to remain politically useful.

## 🧬 Which Russia Is Speaking?

This is the diagnostic hand-off.

For a significant contemporary Russian message, ask:

### Romanov?

Look for:

- dynasty;
- imperial prestige;
- Orthodox continuity;
- European great-power recognition;
- territorial restoration;
- Petrine modernisation.

### Soviet?

Look for:

- encirclement;
- sacrifice;
- mobilisation;
- anti-fascism;
- Great Patriotic War;
- hostile blocs;
- superpower status;
- security-state legitimacy.

### Specifically late-Soviet?

Look for:

- mature superpower management;
- strategic parity;
- nuclear risk;
- intelligence channels;
- controlled confrontation;
- institutional suspicion;
- prolonged competition.

### Post-Soviet?

Look for:

- humiliation;
- collapse;
- disorder;
- oligarchy;
- economic insecurity;
- loss of status;
- restoration of state capacity.

### Putin-era?

Look for:

- sovereignty;
- multipolarity;
- civilisational language;
- security-state continuity;
- personalised executive power;
- contemporary nationalism.

Then ask:

> **Are several of these speaking at once?**

That synthesis belongs in:

- [🧬 Which Russia Is Speaking?](./🧬_which_russia_is_speaking.md).

## 🪩 Guardrails

Do not use Soviet inheritance as a universal explanation either.

Avoid:

- "Russia has always been paranoid";
- treating every security concern as Soviet nostalgia;
- assuming every intelligence technique is uniquely Soviet;
- flattening seven decades of Soviet history into Stalin;
- treating Russian people as passive recipients of state memory;
- assuming Soviet references mean communist restoration;
- confusing cultural memory with policy causation;
- treating anti-Western rhetoric as proof that diplomacy is impossible;
- using history to excuse contemporary aggression.

And especially:

> **Do not replace "Putin = Tsar" with "Putin = KGB" and congratulate ourselves on having become sophisticated.**

The point is to improve explanatory resolution.

---

## 🧪 Prospective Tests

This repertoire becomes more useful if it helps us identify things **before** the outcome is known.

Track whether:

- encirclement language intensifies around particular NATO actions;
- Great Patriotic War / anti-fascist language clusters around particular audiences or objectives;
- endurance rhetoric accompanies attempts to resist economic pressure;
- industrial mobilisation is framed through recognisably Soviet historical memory;
- Washington receives communication resembling superpower-management habits;
- intelligence channels remain open during periods of sharp public confrontation;
- Soviet and Romanov language appear together in consistent combinations;
- later Russian behaviour corresponds with the audience effects apparently sought.

Also record when:

- the historical explanation adds nothing;
- ordinary military logic explains the event better;
- contemporary political incentives explain it better;
- the supposed pattern disappears.

The ghost has to earn its place in the room too.

---

## 🧿 Working Proposition

> **If the Romanovs supply prestige and continuity, the Soviet inheritance may supply endurance, encirclement, security-state competence and the grammar of confrontation.**

But that is a starting hypothesis, not a sorting algorithm.

The larger possibility is more interesting:

> **Contemporary Russia may treat imperial, Soviet and post-Soviet history not as mutually exclusive regimes that analysts must choose between, but as one long reservoir of usable Russian pasts.**

If so, the analytical task is not to decide whether Russia is Romanov **or** Soviet.

It is to identify:

> **which Russia is speaking, to whom, and what it wants them to do.**
