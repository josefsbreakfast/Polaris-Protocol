# ⚪️ Why Is A White Lady Writing This?

**First created:** 2026-08-24 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-24  
*Why this research exists, what it is for, and why it should not be mistaken for an attempt to take ownership of a conversation that belongs first to Black communities and specialists.*

---

> **This is complex in its literature and associated knowledge gaps; please use expertise wherever possible and treat specialist labour with the respect it deserves.**

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

There is a reasonably obvious question here.

**Why is a white woman writing about this?**

The answer is not:

> because Black people needed somebody else to notice racism for them.

They very obviously did not.

Black creators, families, advocates, journalists and researchers have already been expressing substantial concern about these deaths, including fear that some could represent racial violence, concern about the speed of suicide determinations, and distress at repeatedly seeing Black people reported deceased in circumstances that carry an unmistakable history in the United States.

That concern exists before Polaris arrives.

Polaris is not here to invent it.

Polaris is not here to authorise it.

Polaris is not here to redirect it.

We are here because there appears to be a **data and institutional-reporting gap sitting beside an already visible qualitative conversation**.

---

## 🫂 The Distress Already Exists

Black creators have been discussing these deaths publicly.

Some are frightened.

Some are angry.

Some distrust official findings.

Some believe particular deaths deserve further investigation.

Some are asking why apparently similar deaths keep appearing.

Some are asking why major institutions do not seem to be asking the same question.

It would be inappropriate to treat those reactions as if they only became intellectually serious once somebody constructed a spreadsheet.

The spreadsheet is not what makes the concern legitimate.

The concern is already there.

The purpose of quantitative work is to help establish **what the evidence can and cannot say about the pattern producing that concern**.

---

## 📚 Other People Have Already Done Serious Work

This is also not untouched territory.

There is already relevant work from:

- Black academics;
- suicide researchers;
- clinicians;
- historians;
- racial-trauma scholars;
- forensic specialists;
- civil-rights organisations;
- NGOs;
- journalists;
- families;
- and community organisations.

Some of that work addresses Black suicide directly.

Some addresses racial trauma.

Some addresses historical lynching and contemporary racial violence.

Some examines suicide method and location.

Some examines individual deaths.

Some NGOs and advocacy organisations have already undertaken case collection or investigation around suspicious or contested deaths.

That labour should be cited rather than absorbed.

Where specialist expertise exists, use it.

Where somebody has already done the work, credit them.

Where a Black scholar or organisation is better placed to explain an issue, **read them rather than translating them unnecessarily through Polaris**.

---

## 🔎 So Why Is Polaris Here?

Because Polaris often performs a specific kind of work:

> **taking a qualitative concern visible in media or public discussion and asking what happens when we put the quantitative evidence underneath it.**

Sometimes that strengthens the concern.

Sometimes it narrows it.

Sometimes it reveals a different problem.

Sometimes it demonstrates that an apparent pattern was created by ascertainment, duplication or bad denominators.

Sometimes it turns a frightening story into a boring one.

Sometimes it turns a supposedly boring story into a much more serious public-health question.

That is the useful contribution here.

---

## 📊 The Missing Data Problem

At the time this folder was created, there did not appear to be an easily accessible national dataset answering the specific question being raised publicly:

> How often are Black Americans being found deceased following suspension in public or outdoor environments, particularly where a tree is the suspension point?

Federal mortality data can answer related questions.

They can tell us about:

- suicide mortality;
- race;
- method;
- age;
- sex;
- geography;
- and some circumstances.

But the scene-specific question being discussed publicly does not appear to map neatly onto an existing national category.

That leaves people trying to understand the apparent pattern through:

- individual news stories;
- social-media posts;
- local reporting;
- incomplete lists;
- family accounts;
- official statements;
- and memory.

That is not a good information environment.

---

## 📰 The Institutional Reporting Gap

There is also an apparent reporting problem.

Individual cases receive coverage.

Some receive excellent coverage.

Some journalists follow them carefully.

Some local reporters do the difficult work of obtaining records and staying with families after national attention disappears.

But there does not appear to have been sufficient institutional uptake of the **aggregate question**.

That question is not:

> Are these definitely lynchings?

It is:

> **What exactly is happening here?**

That should include:

- how many cases there actually are;
- whether their frequency has changed;
- how they compare with historical baselines;
- what final investigations found;
- what proportion were classified as suicide, homicide, undetermined or otherwise;
- whether investigations were independently reviewed;
- whether apparent clusters are partly products of increased reporting;
- what Black suicide trends look like;
- whether prevention systems are working;
- and why these deaths continue to generate substantial public fear.

That is ordinary investigative and public-health journalism.

---

## ⚪️ Not Replacing The Conversation

This project should not become:

> Here is what a white researcher thinks Black Americans should be concerned about.

Absolutely not.

The qualitative conversation belongs to the people having it.

Black communities do not require Polaris to tell them what lynching imagery means.

They do not require Polaris to explain why distrust exists.

They do not require Polaris to decide which emotions are reasonable.

The purpose here is narrower.

**Provide press-facing evidentiary infrastructure that may help institutional journalists investigate the questions people are already asking.**

---

## 🧮 Doing The Boring Bit

There is an unfair labour problem buried in this too.

When communities become frightened by something, institutional responses can effectively become:

> Show us the dataset.

That often leaves the people experiencing the harm responsible for:

- discovering the cases;
- collecting the links;
- checking the dates;
- verifying identities;
- finding autopsy findings;
- correcting duplicated cases;
- tracking investigations;
- locating historical comparators;
- and constructing the denominator required before institutions will take the underlying concern seriously.

That is backwards.

People experiencing distress should not have to become unpaid epidemiologists before journalists decide something deserves examination.

So part of the function of this folder is very simple:

> **Here is the data bit.**

Not because data replaces testimony.

Because people giving the testimony should not also have to do every piece of technical labour required to make institutions listen.

## 🫂 The Intended Audience

This material is primarily intended to help:

- institutional journalists;
- editors;
- researchers;
- public-health reporters;
- data journalists;
- clinicians;
- civil-rights reporters;
- and other professionals who may be in a position to investigate the subject properly.

It is not intended as instructions to Black communities about how they should interpret their own experiences.

It is intended to make it easier for journalists with institutional resources to ask better questions.

## ⚖️ What Polaris Can Contribute

Polaris can reasonably contribute:

- case reconstruction;
- source comparison;
- date verification;
- denominator checking;
- quantitative baselines;
- methodological transparency;
- identification of missing data;
- separation of established facts from hypotheses;
- literature routing;
- tracking cases to disposition;
- and identifying questions that deserve specialist investigation.

Polaris cannot replace:

- forensic pathology;
- epidemiology;
- psychiatry;
- Black studies;
- racial-trauma scholarship;
- civil-rights investigation;
- local knowledge;
- family testimony;
- or lived experience.

Use the appropriate expertise.

---

## 🧠 Expertise Matters Here

This subject crosses several disciplines simultaneously.

A serious investigation may require expertise in:

- epidemiology;
- suicide prevention;
- forensic pathology;
- psychiatry;
- psychology;
- sociology;
- Black history;
- lynching history;
- racial trauma;
- policing;
- civil-rights law;
- media studies;
- statistics;
- and public-health communication.

No single person is likely to be expert in all of them.

That is not a reason not to investigate.

It is a reason to **stop pretending interdisciplinary work means everybody's specialist labour is interchangeable**.

Use specialists.

Cite specialists.

Pay specialists.

Listen when specialists identify limitations.

---

## 🕯️ Distress Does Not Need Permission From The Dataset

The current project may ultimately find that the apparent increase is smaller than it initially appears.

It may find that ascertainment explains a substantial part of it.

It may find that most or all individual deaths have well-supported suicide determinations.

None of those findings would make the original public distress foolish.

People were responding to real reports of Black people being found deceased in circumstances visually associated with racial terror.

The responsibility of institutions is to answer reasonable fear with evidence.

Not to demand that frightened people prove the epidemiology first.

---

## 📰 What We Want Journalists To Do With This

Ideally:

Take the register.

Check it.

Improve it.

Find cases we missed.

Correct cases we got wrong.

Talk to Black suicide researchers.

Talk to forensic specialists.

Talk to Black historians.

Talk to families.

Talk to local reporters who already did the work.

Request the records.

Build the proper denominator.

Ask the CDC what it can and cannot currently measure.

Ask whether the relevant surveillance systems are adequate.

Ask what prevention infrastructure exists.

Ask whether investigations produced sufficient public reassurance.

Then publish something better than:

> People online are worried.

or:

> Police say suicide.

The subject deserves considerably more than either sentence.

## 🚧 This Is Still Mid-Research

The warning elsewhere in this folder applies here too.

**The reading is not finished.**

**The dataset is not finished.**

**The historical reconstruction is not finished.**

**The literature review is not finished.**

The project should therefore defer confidently to specialist literature where it exists and remain explicit about knowledge gaps where it does not.

This note explains **why Polaris entered the conversation**.

It does not grant Polaris authority over the conversation.

## 🧭 The Basic Position

The position is ultimately quite modest.

There is visible distress.

There is relevant existing scholarship and advocacy.

There appears to be an incomplete institutional response.

There is an identifiable quantitative question that can help clarify what is happening.

Polaris knows how to do that sort of boring evidence work.

So:

> **Here is the data bit, so that the people experiencing the distress do not have to collect it for you.**

Now please use it responsibly.
