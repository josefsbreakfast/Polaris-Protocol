## 🛰️ Orientation

This investigation began with a simple and disturbing observation:

Reports continue to appear of Black Americans being found deceased following suspension in public or outdoor places, including from trees.

Those scenes inevitably evoke the history of lynching in the United States.

That historical association makes the deaths unusually difficult to report responsibly.

There are several obvious risks.

One is **underreaction**:

> Authorities classify an individual death as suicide, reporting stops, and nobody asks whether the aggregate pattern itself deserves examination.

Another is **overreaction**:

> A frightening scene is treated as evidence of contemporary lynching before evidence establishes homicide.

Neither is acceptable.

This folder exists because there is another option:

**count carefully, investigate carefully, read the literature, preserve uncertainty, follow individual cases to disposition, establish an appropriate historical baseline, and find out what the evidence actually supports.**

---

## 🕯️ Why These Deaths Require Care

A Black person found suspended from a tree in the United States presents an unusually difficult evidential and social problem.

The visual resemblance to historical racial terror is immediate.

That resemblance is **not evidence that an individual death was a lynching**.

It is nevertheless relevant to understanding why such deaths can cause profound public alarm.

These two propositions can coexist.

Responsible investigation should therefore neither presume racial homicide nor behave as though concern about racial homicide is irrational.

The historical context creates a foreseeable need for particularly credible:

- investigation;
- evidence preservation;
- medicolegal examination;
- communication;
- follow-up;
- transparency;
- and public reassurance.

---

## ⚠️ What The Current Case Register Is

The developing Polaris register is a **case-discovery dataset**.

It records cases identified through searches of:

- local reporting;
- national reporting;
- police statements;
- medical examiner and coroner material;
- federal investigative material;
- retrospective reporting;
- and other sufficiently attributable sources.

Where possible, cases are followed beyond the original headline to later investigative findings.

The register currently distinguishes between cases meeting a strict scene definition and adjacent cases useful for comparison.

It also retains screened exclusions where doing so helps demonstrate why apparently relevant cases were not counted.

---

## ❌ What The Current Case Register Is Not

It is **not**:

- a complete enumeration of deaths in the United States;
- a CDC mortality dataset;
- a substitute for medical examiner records;
- a denominator-adjusted incidence series;
- evidence that every qualifying case received equivalent media attention;
- evidence that every qualifying historical case has been discovered;
- evidence that every death was suicide;
- evidence that every death was homicide;
- evidence of a coordinated phenomenon;
- evidence of contemporary lynching;
- or proof that the apparent increase survives proper statistical analysis.

The current counts are best described as:

> **cases located under a defined search methodology.**

Nothing stronger should presently be inferred from them.

## 🧠 Hypotheses Are Not Findings

Several hypotheses naturally arise from the cases.

They should remain hypotheses until tested.

Possible explanations for an apparent pattern include:

- genuine change in incidence;
- changing Black suicide mortality;
- changing suicide-method distribution;
- changing location distribution;
- increased media ascertainment;
- increased public attention following George Floyd's murder and the 2020 Black Lives Matter protests;
- differences in healthcare access;
- differences in crisis intervention;
- changing social and economic conditions;
- changes in institutional trust;
- failures of detection or rescue;
- individual homicide;
- racial violence;
- classification error;
- incomplete historical discovery;
- or combinations of several mechanisms.

The existence of a plausible explanation does not establish that explanation.

## 🧠 Racial Trauma And Method Selection

This is one area where particular restraint is necessary.

There is substantial literature connecting racism, racial trauma and structural conditions with psychological distress and suicide risk among Black Americans.

There is also literature examining the continuing psychological effects of historical and contemporary racial violence.

However, during the research completed **so far**, we have not established an empirical basis for claiming that Black Americans who die by suicide through tree suspension are symbolically reenacting lynching.

That possibility should not be attributed to individual deceased people without evidence.

It is especially important not to convert the historical meaning experienced by an audience into an assumed psychological motivation of the deceased.

These are different questions.

The literature review on trauma, suicide and method selection remains incomplete.

## 📈 The Bigger Suicide Question

One possibility increasingly important to this investigation is less sensational and potentially much larger.

**What is happening to Black suicide mortality?**

If suicide among Black Americans is increasing, then a public-health investigation should ask not only why the rate is changing but whether the **composition and circumstances of fatal cases are changing with it**.

A changing population distribution of serious suicidal distress could potentially change:

- who reaches lethal crisis;
- prior contact with psychiatric services;
- previous attempt history;
- age;
- social circumstances;
- healthcare access;
- method;
- location;
- and opportunity for intervention.

This is a research question.

It is not presently offered as an explanation for the cases in this folder.

---

## 📍 Why Public Space Matters

"Outdoor" is not a sufficient category.

There is an important difference between:

- remote woodland;
- secluded rural land;
- an isolated part of a park;
- an ordinary neighbourhood;
- a heavily used urban park;
- civic space;
- university grounds;
- church grounds;
- commercial areas;
- and other routinely occupied public environments.

Location affects both interpretation and prevention.

A person deliberately selecting an isolated location where interruption is unlikely presents a different systems question from someone reaching lethal crisis in a routinely observed environment without successful intervention.

---

## 📹 Visibility Is Not Detection

Modern public space can contain:

- CCTV;
- traffic cameras;
- police patrols;
- private security;
- park staff;
- transport workers;
- commercial premises;
- pedestrians;
- emergency services;
- and extensive communications infrastructure.

The existence of those systems does not prove that meaningful observation occurred.

A camera may record without being monitored.

A patrol may exist without passing the relevant location.

A crowded location at noon may be deserted at night.

The appropriate question is therefore not:

> Was this place surveilled?

It is:

> **What realistic opportunities existed for somebody or some system to identify acute danger early enough for intervention?**

Where evidence permits, the dataset should eventually examine:

- time of day;
- public accessibility;
- expected footfall;
- nearby staffed premises;
- patrol presence;
- security presence;
- CCTV availability;
- whether CCTV was actively monitored;
- who discovered the person;
- estimated discovery interval;
- emergency response;
- prior missing-person reports;
- and preceding welfare concerns.

This is about prevention.

It should not be used to imply that earlier intervention would necessarily have changed an individual outcome.

---

## 🩺 Healthcare Is Part Of The Environment

The investigation should also consider what happened **before** the final crisis.

Potentially relevant contextual variables include:

- health-insurance coverage;
- recent loss or change of coverage;
- access to mental-health services;
- continuity of treatment;
- crisis-service availability;
- previous service contact;
- local clinician density;
- availability of Black clinicians;
- culturally appropriate care;
- hospital or service closures;
- 988 infrastructure;
- economic disruption;
- housing instability;
- and community support.

These are potential population and systems variables.

They should not be retrospectively imposed as explanations for an individual death without supporting evidence.

---

## 🔎 If It Is Homicide

Then it should be investigated as homicide.

Where there are credible inconsistencies, forensic concerns, evidence of third-party involvement, family concerns, civil-rights concerns or other reasons for additional scrutiny, those should be documented accurately.

No commitment to suicide prevention requires dismissing evidence of homicide.

No concern about sensationalism requires treating official findings as infallible.

---

## 🩺 If It Is Suicide

Then the story changes.

It does not disappear.

A well-supported suicide determination creates different questions:

- Why is Black suicide mortality changing?
- Are particular demographic groups changing?
- Are methods changing?
- Are locations changing?
- Are people reaching services?
- Are existing risk models recognising the people now dying?
- Are healthcare barriers relevant?
- Are people being discovered quickly enough?
- Are opportunities for intervention being missed?
- Are social conditions producing additional serious distress?
- Are several individually explicable deaths collectively telling us something?

**"Suicide" is a manner-of-death finding.**

It is not a public-health explanation.

---

## 📊 The Aggregate Question

Individual investigations answer individual questions.

They do not automatically answer population questions.

If several similar deaths occur, somebody still needs to ask:

> **Is this happening more often than it used to?**

That requires:

- historical reconstruction;
- consistent inclusion criteria;
- denominator data;
- demographic comparison;
- appropriate population mortality statistics;
- assessment of ascertainment;
- careful treatment of 2020;
- and ideally comparison with other methods and locations.

This work is currently underway.

It is **not finished**.

---

## 🗓️ Why 2020 Needs Separate Treatment

2020 presents an obvious methodological problem.

It combined:

- the COVID-19 pandemic;
- major disruption to ordinary life;
- substantial mental-health stress;
- George Floyd's murder;
- enormous Black Lives Matter mobilisation;
- intense national attention to racist violence;
- and unusually high journalistic interest in reports of Black people found suspended.

That creates at least two competing possibilities:

1. something about incidence changed;
2. something about discovery and reporting changed.

Both may have happened.

Neither should be assumed.

For that reason, 2020 should not casually become either the "normal" comparison year or proof of an exceptional phenomenon.

---

## 🧾 Follow Every Case To Disposition

Where possible, the project should distinguish:

**initial report → preliminary assessment → autopsy → investigation → final manner → subsequent review**

This matters because early reporting frequently survives longer in public memory than later evidence.

It also matters in the opposite direction.

An early official statement should not be treated as the final word where later investigation changed or complicated it.

Cases should therefore be dated by the **event**, not by the publication date of later coverage.

Duplicate reporting and retrospective reporting must be controlled for.

---

## 🫂 Reasonable Fear Deserves A Serious Answer

There is a further institutional question independent of the eventual statistical result.

Black Americans have substantial historical reasons to understand public tree suspension through the history of lynching.

If contemporary deaths repeatedly recreate that visual scene, institutions should expect fear.

The appropriate response is neither:

> Every one of these deaths is a lynching.

nor:

> Authorities said suicide, so stop worrying.

A functioning system should be capable of saying:

> **We understand why this is frightening. Here is how the death was investigated. Here is what the evidence establishes. Here is what remains uncertain. Here is whether similar deaths are becoming more common. And here is what we are doing about whichever problem the evidence identifies.**

Reassurance should be built from evidence.

---

## 📰 Why This Sits Under Responsible Reporting

This investigation belongs here because journalism faces two symmetrical dangers.

### Sensationalism

A frightening scene can be turned into unsupported claims of racial homicide.

That can:

- terrify communities;
- spread misinformation;
- prejudice investigations;
- exploit deceased people;
- and produce irresponsible suicide reporting.

### Premature closure

An official suicide determination can be reported as though every relevant question has therefore been answered.

That can obscure:

- prevention failures;
- mortality trends;
- investigative weaknesses;
- community distrust;
- healthcare failures;
- and aggregate patterns.

Responsible reporting has to survive between those poles.

---

## 🕯️ The Question Is Not "Should We Panic?"

No.

The question is:

> **Is there something here that responsible institutions should understand better?**

At this stage, the answer is clearly yes.

That is a much smaller claim than establishing an epidemic of racial homicide.

It is also enough to justify doing the work properly.

## 🧭 Working Standard

For every significant claim, ask:

- What exactly are we claiming?
- What evidence supports it?
- Is that evidence primary or secondary?
- Is the finding preliminary or final?
- Are we describing an individual case or a population?
- Is the denominator appropriate?
- Could ascertainment explain the apparent pattern?
- Could historical search incompleteness explain it?
- Have we followed the case to disposition?
- Are we distinguishing scene from cause?
- Are we distinguishing historical meaning from individual intent?
- Are we distinguishing reasonable concern from evidence of homicide?
- Are we distinguishing a suicide determination from an explanation of suicide?
- Has the relevant literature actually been reviewed yet?

If the answer to the last question is no:

**keep reading.**
