# 🧭 What Good Reporting Would Ask

**First created:** 2026-08-24 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-24  
*Questions for journalists approaching reports of Black Americans found deceased following public or outdoor suspension: investigate the death, investigate the pattern, and investigate what happens if the official finding is correct.*

---

> **This is complex in its literature and associated knowledge gaps; please use expertise wherever possible and treat specialist labour with the respect it deserves.**
>
> **This folder remains mid-research. The literature review, historical reconstruction and case register are not complete. These questions are an investigative framework, not conclusions about the deaths being studied.**

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Good reporting does not need to begin by deciding whether these deaths are lynchings.

It also should not begin by deciding that an official suicide classification makes the wider story disappear.

There is a much simpler starting point:

> **What would we need to know to understand this properly?**

That requires several different kinds of reporting.

There is the **individual death**.

There is the **quality of its investigation**.

There is the **aggregate pattern**.

There is the **public-health context**.

There is the **historical meaning of the scene**.

There is the **institutional response to reasonable public fear**.

And there is the question that remains whichever way the evidence goes:

> **What should happen next?**

---

## 🕯️ Start With The Person

Before the death becomes a pattern, statistic, symbol or controversy, somebody died.

Ask:

- Who was this person?
- What information about them has been confirmed?
- What information comes from family or friends?
- What information comes from police?
- What information comes from the medical examiner or coroner?
- What information is merely being repeated between news organisations?
- Has race or ethnicity actually been established?
- Has the family requested privacy around particular information?
- Are photographs or graphic descriptions genuinely necessary?
- Is reporting preserving the dignity of the deceased?
- Has the person become little more than an illustration for somebody else's argument?

Do not allow the unusual circumstances of death to erase the person who died.

---

## 📍 Establish The Scene Precisely

"Found hanging" is not enough information for serious analysis.

Where reporting and records permit, establish:

- Was the person indoors or outdoors?
- Was the location public or private?
- Was the suspension point a tree or another structure?
- Was the location rural, suburban or urban?
- Was it isolated or ordinarily frequented?
- Was it readily visible from paths, roads, buildings or public facilities?
- What time was the person last known to be alive?
- What time were they discovered?
- Who discovered them?
- Was the location normally staffed?
- Was security present?
- Were routine police patrols expected?
- Was CCTV present?
- Was that CCTV actively monitored or merely recording?
- Was footage subsequently recovered?
- Had anybody already reported the person missing or raised a welfare concern?

These are not details for voyeurism.

They help establish **what opportunities for detection, intervention and subsequent investigation actually existed**.

---

## 🔎 Ask What Was Investigated

An official conclusion should be reported alongside the process that produced it.

Ask:

- Which agency initially investigated the death?
- Who had responsibility for the scene?
- Was the scene treated as potentially suspicious until evidence indicated otherwise?
- Was a forensic examination performed?
- Was an autopsy performed?
- Was toxicology undertaken?
- Was relevant surveillance footage sought?
- Were witnesses identified and interviewed?
- Was digital evidence considered where appropriate?
- Was evidence of third-party involvement specifically assessed?
- Was the possibility of racial violence considered?
- What evidence supported the eventual manner-of-death determination?
- What evidence weighed against competing explanations?
- Was the determination preliminary or final?
- Did it subsequently change?
- Was another agency asked to review the case?
- Was there FBI or civil-rights involvement?
- Was an independent examination requested?
- Were family concerns investigated?
- Were the findings communicated to the family?

Not every answer belongs in the eventual article.

But somebody reporting confidently on the conclusion should know how that conclusion was reached.

---

## ⚖️ Do Not Treat Suspicion As Proof

A scene can reasonably produce fear without establishing homicide.

Ask:

- What evidence actually indicates third-party involvement?
- What evidence indicates self-inflicted death?
- Are reported inconsistencies genuinely inconsistent?
- Has information circulating online been independently verified?
- Are photographs, witness accounts or timelines being interpreted by people with appropriate expertise?
- Are rumours being repeated because they are evidentially important or merely because they are frightening?
- Has historical resemblance silently become causal inference?

A tree is historically significant.

It is not, by itself, forensic evidence of lynching.

---

## 🧾 Follow The Case To Disposition

The first headline is rarely enough.

Return to the case.

Ask:

- Was the autopsy completed?
- Was toxicology completed?
- What was the final cause of death?
- What was the final manner of death?
- Did the investigation remain open?
- Was it subsequently closed?
- Why?
- Did another agency review the evidence?
- Were family concerns resolved?
- Was an independent autopsy obtained?
- Were previously reported claims corrected?
- Did later evidence materially alter the initial story?
- Did the newsroom publish the eventual result with anything approaching the prominence of the original report?

A preliminary statement should not become the permanent public record simply because everybody moved on.

---

## 📊 Then Ask The Aggregate Question

Once several similar reports exist, individual reporting is no longer enough.

Ask:

> **How often is this actually happening?**

Then make the question precise.

- What exactly counts as a case?
- What years are being examined?
- What population is being examined?
- Are public and private locations being distinguished?
- Are trees being distinguished from other suspension points?
- Are suicide, homicide and undetermined deaths all being captured before manner is used analytically?
- Are cases being dated by the death rather than the publication date?
- Have duplicates been removed?
- Have racial identifications been verified?
- Have widely circulated false identifications been removed?
- Are retrospective reports accidentally being counted in the wrong year?
- How complete is historical news coverage?
- Are older cases harder to discover than recent ones?

Do not announce a trend until you understand how the cases entered the dataset.

---

## 📈 Ask For The Denominator

Seven cases can mean very different things in different populations and periods.

Ask:

- What is the relevant Black population?
- How has that population changed?
- What is the overall Black suicide rate?
- What is the Black suicide rate by age and sex?
- How has that changed over time?
- What proportion of Black suicide deaths involve hanging, strangulation or suffocation?
- Has that proportion changed?
- Are comparable changes occurring among other racial groups?
- Are particular age groups driving the increase?
- Are particular states or regions driving it?
- Is there an appropriate population-adjusted rate?
- What can CDC data actually tell us?
- What can it **not** tell us?

Do not force a scene-specific question into a mortality category that cannot actually answer it.

---

## 🗓️ Treat 2020 Carefully

2020 should not casually become anybody's baseline.

Ask what changed simultaneously:

- COVID-19;
- social isolation;
- healthcare disruption;
- economic disruption;
- George Floyd's murder;
- Black Lives Matter mobilisation;
- national attention to racist violence;
- newsroom attention to Black deaths;
- public willingness to report apparently suspicious scenes;
- and institutional scrutiny of policing.

A 2020 spike could reflect:

- increased incidence;
- increased detection;
- increased reporting;
- changing suicide mortality;
- changing circumstances of death;
- or several of these at once.

Ask which explanation the available evidence can distinguish.

---

## 🔭 Look Before 2020

If the question is whether something changed, go backwards.

Do not compare:

> now versus the year everybody remembers.

Build a historical baseline.

Ask:

- Were similar deaths reported in 2019?
- 2018?
- 2017?
- Before Trump's first presidency?
- During the Obama administration?
- How reliably can older cases be recovered?
- Did national newspapers cover them?
- Did they remain local stories?
- Were they described using different terminology?
- Are modern search engines systematically better at recovering recent cases?

Sometimes the most important investigative work is discovering what nobody noticed at the time.

---

## 🩺 If The Finding Is Suicide, Keep Reporting

This is one of the most important questions in this folder.

If an investigation credibly establishes suicide, ask:

> **Why are Black Americans dying by suicide?**

Then:

- Is Black suicide mortality rising?
- Which demographic groups are driving that change?
- Has the profile of people dying changed?
- Are younger people increasingly represented?
- Are people dying without previous psychiatric contact?
- Are there changes in method?
- Are there changes in location?
- Are people reaching crisis services?
- Are they insured?
- Are they able to obtain mental-health care?
- Is treatment continuous?
- Are culturally appropriate services available?
- Are Black clinicians available?
- Are people disengaging because services are inaccessible or mistrusted?
- What do Black suicide researchers think is changing?
- What do Black clinicians see in practice?
- What does the literature actually establish?

A suicide determination answers an important forensic question.

It does not answer the public-health question.

---

## 🏥 Ask What Happened Before The Crisis

Do not begin the prevention analysis at the moment of death.

Where ethical and possible, ask about systems rather than retrospectively diagnosing the deceased.

Relevant questions may include:

- What mental-health services existed locally?
- How easy were they to access?
- What did they cost?
- What insurance coverage was available?
- Had local provision recently changed?
- Were crisis services available?
- Were services culturally competent?
- Was continuity of care realistic?
- What were local waiting times?
- Had healthcare facilities closed?
- Were there known workforce shortages?
- Was there adequate availability of Black clinicians?
- What community support infrastructure existed?

The appropriate unit of analysis is often not:

> What was wrong with this person?

It may be:

> **What happened around people who became dangerously distressed?**

---

## 📹 Ask Whether Observation Produced Protection

Modern America observes an enormous amount of public space.

That creates an uncomfortable but legitimate question:

> **Does surveillance actually help people in acute danger?**

Ask:

- Was the location covered by cameras?
- Were those cameras monitored?
- Were recordings reviewed only after discovery?
- Were police routinely present nearby?
- Were security personnel nearby?
- Was the location ordinarily busy?
- Did anybody encounter the person before the death?
- Had unusual behaviour been reported?
- Was a missing-person report active?
- Had somebody requested a welfare check?
- Was there a realistic opportunity for earlier detection?

The existence of surveillance should not be confused with the existence of a functioning intervention system.

---

## 🫂 Ask Why People Are Frightened

Do not report Black public concern as an unexplained emotional reaction.

Ask:

- What is the history of lynching in this region?
- Have previous racist killings occurred locally?
- How have local authorities historically handled racial violence?
- How much institutional trust exists?
- Have previous deaths been mishandled?
- What contemporary racist threats exist?
- What are Black residents actually saying?
- What are Black historians and scholars saying?
- What information would people need in order to evaluate the official conclusion themselves?

Historical context does not determine manner of death.

It helps explain why **evidentiary reassurance has to be credible**.

---

## 📰 Ask What The Newsroom Is Doing

Journalists should investigate their own information system too.

Ask:

- Why did this case receive coverage?
- Why did another similar case not?
- Are we discovering more cases because incidence increased or because journalists started looking?
- Did our reporting amplify an incorrect racial identification?
- Did we correct it?
- Did we accidentally count an older case as contemporary?
- Are we relying on another newsroom's reporting without checking its source?
- Did we publish the frightening preliminary story but not the final investigation?
- Are graphic descriptions necessary?
- Are headlines overstating uncertainty?
- Are headlines understating legitimate uncertainty?
- Are we treating official statements as findings before they become findings?
- Are we avoiding the subject because it is politically uncomfortable?
- Are we treating race-conscious analysis as advocacy rather than ordinary demographic reporting?

Newsrooms are part of the ascertainment mechanism.

That belongs in the analysis.

---

## 🧑🏿‍🏫 Ask The People Who Actually Know

This is not a subject for one journalist and a search engine.

Talk to specialists.

Depending on the question, that may include:

- Black suicide researchers;
- Black psychiatrists and psychologists;
- suicide epidemiologists;
- forensic pathologists;
- medical examiners;
- racial-trauma researchers;
- historians of lynching;
- Black studies scholars;
- civil-rights lawyers;
- suicide-prevention specialists;
- statisticians;
- public-health researchers;
- local journalists;
- community organisations;
- and families.

Different questions require different expertise.

Do not ask a historian to determine forensic causation.

Do not ask a forensic pathologist to explain Black historical memory.

Do not ask a psychiatrist to manufacture an epidemiological trend from seven news stories.

And do not expect Black academics to provide unlimited unpaid labour because a newsroom suddenly discovered the subject.

**Use expertise wherever possible and treat specialist labour with the respect it deserves.**

---

## ⚪️ Ask Who Is Doing The Work

There is another reporting question hiding underneath all of this:

> **Who has been expected to notice, document and prove the problem?**

If Black creators are already:

- finding cases;
- saving articles;
- comparing circumstances;
- checking official statements;
- contacting families;
- documenting concerns;
- and asking whether something larger is happening,

then institutional journalism should not respond by demanding that those communities also construct the national dataset before the question becomes worthy of investigation.

Listen to the qualitative signal.

Then use institutional resources to do the quantitative work.

---

## 🧠 Ask What You Actually Know

At every stage, separate:

### Established

Supported by sufficiently reliable evidence.

### Probable

Supported strongly but not completely established.

### Plausible

A reasonable hypothesis requiring additional evidence.

### Unknown

The evidence currently cannot answer it.

### Unsupported

Repeated or suggested, but not presently supported by adequate evidence.

These categories are much more useful than forcing every question into **true/false** before the investigation is ready.

---

## 🚨 Ask What Would Change Your Mind

Before publishing a strong interpretation, write down:

- What evidence would support it?
- What evidence would weaken it?
- What evidence would falsify it?
- What evidence has not yet been obtained?

If no conceivable evidence could change the conclusion, you are probably no longer investigating.

---

## 🧭 And Finally: What Happens Next?

Every serious piece of reporting should eventually reach this question.

If the deaths represent homicide:

> What investigative and protective response follows?

If they represent suicide:

> What prevention response follows?

If they represent a changing mortality pattern:

> What public-health response follows?

If the apparent pattern is ascertainment:

> What does that tell us about previous invisibility and contemporary reporting?

If investigations were robust:

> How can that evidence be communicated sufficiently to reassure frightened communities?

If investigations were inadequate:

> Who is responsible for improving them?

If the data are insufficient:

> **Who has the resources and responsibility to collect better data?**

There does not need to be a scandal hiding behind every answer.

But there should be an answer.

---

## 🕯️ The Minimum Standard

A responsible story should leave its reader understanding:

- what happened;
- what is established;
- what remains uncertain;
- how the official conclusion was reached;
- why alternative explanations were considered or rejected;
- whether similar cases exist;
- whether their frequency is unusual;
- what the relevant population data show;
- why the circumstances carry particular historical meaning;
- what specialists say;
- what prevention questions remain;
- and what institutions are doing next.

That is considerably more useful than:

> **People online fear lynchings.**

And considerably more useful than:

> **Police say suicide.**

Neither sentence does enough work.
