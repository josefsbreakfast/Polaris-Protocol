# ☘️ North Of Or Northern Ireland
**First created:** 2026-10-05 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-05  
*Responsible reporting around jurisdiction, identity, constitutional aspiration, authorial bias, peace, and the words used for a place whose people do not all understand it in the same way.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Polaris uses geographical and constitutional language deliberately.

**Northern Ireland**, capitalised, is the formal name of the jurisdiction which currently forms part of the **United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland**.

That is useful language when Polaris is talking specifically about law, government, administration, constitutional arrangements, official statistics, or the jurisdiction as a legal object.

It is not, however, politically or culturally neutral shorthand for how everybody living on that land understands where they are from.

A significant number of people understand themselves as Irish and may describe the place as **the North of Ireland**, **the North**, or simply as part of **Ireland**. For some people, the existence and terminology of Northern Ireland are inseparable from partition, British sovereignty, and an unresolved question of national self-determination.

Other people understand **Northern Ireland** as their country or home, identify as Northern Irish and/or British, support its continuing place within the United Kingdom, or simply use the formal name without intending every use of it as a constitutional declaration.

These descriptions cannot responsibly be collapsed into synonyms.

The purpose of this node is not to decide which community has the Correct Noun.

It is to stop Polaris accidentally deciding a constitutional question through house style.

---

## ☘️ The drafting convention

When Polaris means the **formal jurisdiction**, use **Northern Ireland**.

When reporting somebody's identity, political position, community vocabulary, constitutional understanding, or own account of where they come from, preserve their terminology wherever reasonably possible.

In practical terms:

- **Northern Ireland** does not automatically mean **the North of Ireland**.
- **the North of Ireland** does not automatically mean **Northern Ireland**.
- **the North** should not be silently “corrected” merely because Northern Ireland is the jurisdiction's formal name.
- **Northern Ireland** should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that a speaker endorses unionism or British sovereignty.
- A person who understands their home as Northern Ireland should not have that understanding silently rewritten as the North of Ireland merely because the author is more sympathetic to Irish nationalism or republicanism.
- Administrative geography should not be used to overwrite self-description.
- Self-description does not confer factual correctness or immunity from criticism. It does confer a claim to being **reported accurately**.

**Precision about the jurisdiction; fidelity to the person.**

## 🪺 Existing Polaris material may not yet meet this standard

Polaris is a large, living archive maintained under real constraints of time, health, capacity, processing, and attention.

Some existing nodes may therefore **not yet consistently reflect this convention**.

Earlier material may:

- use *Northern Ireland* where more precise contextual language would have been appropriate;
- fail to preserve *the North*, *the North of Ireland*, or another speaker's own terminology;
- muddle jurisdictional terminology with geographical, national, political, or identity terminology;
- inconsistently capitalise or distinguish these terms;
- or otherwise fail the standard described in this node.

Those inconsistencies should not automatically be interpreted as deliberate constitutional positioning by Polaris.

They are recognised editorial debt.

They will be corrected through later review and integrity passes, but affected material may remain live while that work is incomplete.

Where an older node's incidental terminology conflicts with this guidance, **this node represents the current Polaris drafting convention**.

The archive will be corrected.

The archive does not need to pretend it was always already correct.

## 🪞 Where I am standing

There is no useful purpose in pretending that the person writing this arrived without a history of her own.

I was raised in Britain.

I am therefore extremely accustomed to **Northern Ireland** as the ordinary name for this place, because that is overwhelmingly the terminology through which Britain taught me to understand it.

That creates an **automatic bias**.

I also have another bias pulling in a different direction.

I have considerable sympathy with the historical experiences and perspectives of Irish Catholics in the jurisdiction / North, and parts of my own politics overlap with parts of the much broader Irish republican political tradition — including socialist, anti-imperial and working-class traditions.

For the avoidance of an apparently necessary British misunderstanding:

**political republicanism is not a synonym for bombing things.**

Irish republicanism contains multiple political traditions, histories, organisations, strategies and arguments. Political overlap with some of those traditions does not imply support for political violence.

Those sympathies nevertheless matter because they create an **emotional and political bias**. Left entirely to instinct, I may sometimes prefer **the North of Ireland**.

That is precisely why I need a rule.

I am not one of the people whose constitutional future is being decided.

I do not live there. I do not live beside the border. A change in the constitutional status of Northern Ireland would not alter the sovereignty governing my own home in the way that it would alter the constitutional circumstances of people living there.

I can have opinions from Britain.

I can study the history. I can criticise British policy. I can sympathise with Irish nationalism or republican political traditions. I can criticise nationalism, republicanism, unionism, loyalism, the British state, the Irish state, political parties, institutions, organisations, and individual political actions where the evidence warrants it.

What I cannot do is turn my sympathy into somebody else's consent.

And if I have a preference?

Well.

**It isn't my fucking border.**

## 🧭 Bias recognised does not have to become bias reproduced

This is a useful example of what Polaris means when it talks about recognising the position of the observer.

Three different forces are visible here.

### Automatic bias

British socialisation means **Northern Ireland** is the terminology most likely to arrive automatically in my language.

I did not independently reason my way towards that phrase every time I use it. I inherited it.

### Emotional and political bias

My sympathies and areas of political affinity can pull me towards **the North of Ireland**.

That preference is also situated rather than magically objective.

### Legal and constitutional constraints

I am also writing from within the United Kingdom, whose law implements the Belfast / Good Friday Agreement and the principle of consent governing Northern Ireland's constitutional status.

The Northern Ireland Act 1998 states that Northern Ireland remains part of the United Kingdom unless a majority voting in a poll chooses otherwise, and requires proposals to give effect to a majority wish to form part of a united Ireland.

Recognising these three influences does not magically make me neutral.

It gives me a better chance of noticing when one of them has grabbed the steering wheel.

## 🕊️ This does not challenge the Belfast / Good Friday Agreement

Nothing in this terminology convention is intended to pre-empt, circumvent, or challenge the constitutional settlement established through the **Belfast / Good Friday Agreement**.

Quite the opposite.

The Agreement is built around the principle that people in Northern Ireland hold different constitutional aspirations and identities which cannot responsibly be wished out of existence.

It recognises the principle of consent governing Northern Ireland's constitutional status and the birthright of people there to identify and be accepted as **British or Irish, or both**.

That means Polaris can simultaneously recognise that:

- some people understand themselves as Irish and describe their home as **the North of Ireland**, **the North**, or **Ireland**;
- some people understand themselves as British and/or Northern Irish and describe their home as **Northern Ireland**, including as part of the United Kingdom;
- other people hold identities and constitutional positions that do not fit neatly into either category;
- and none of those individual descriptions, including Polaris's terminology when accurately reporting them, determines the constitutional status of the jurisdiction.

The constitutional future of the place belongs to the people whose constitutional future it actually is, through the democratic mechanisms established by the settlement.

Polaris does not need to make everybody use the same language in order to respect that settlement.

**Respecting people's different relationships to the place is part of taking the settlement seriously.**

---

## 🍊 This rule applies to people I disagree with too

An ethical reporting rule which only protects the self-description of people the author likes is not much of an ethical reporting rule.

That means Polaris should accurately represent unionist and loyalist understandings of place as well as nationalist and republican ones.

If an Orange Order member, unionist, loyalist, or anybody else understands their home as **Northern Ireland**, understands themselves as British and/or Northern Irish, and wants Northern Ireland to remain within the United Kingdom, Polaris should report that position in terms which the person concerned would recognise before analysing or criticising it.

Likewise, somebody who understands themselves as Irish and describes their home as **the North of Ireland** should not have that terminology administratively translated back into the British constitutional default.

This is not an instruction to agree with everybody.

It is an instruction to know what somebody actually said, believed, or meant before arguing with them.

**Self-description does not confer immunity from criticism. It does confer a claim to being reported accurately.**

## ❤️ Why this matters

There is a technical explanation for this convention involving jurisdiction, constitutional status, identity, partition, sovereignty, peace, and contested geographical language.

There is also a much simpler one:

> **Polaris gives more of a fuck about people than lines on maps.**

Administrative boundaries matter.

Laws matter.

Jurisdictions matter.

Borders have material consequences, and Polaris needs language precise enough to describe them.

But a line on a map does not get to overwrite the people living around, across, or underneath it.

Where people's relationships to place, identity, nationality, and belonging are contested, Polaris should distinguish the administrative object from the human beings being described. It should preserve people's own language wherever possible rather than quietly forcing them through whichever terminology happens to appear on the official map.

That does not require Polaris to settle the constitutional question.

It requires Polaris to remember what the map is for.

**The map describes people's world. People do not exist to make the map tidy.**

## 🪺 What Polaris is actually trying to do

Something considerably less grand than solving Ireland.

**I am trying my best not to make anything fucking worse.**

Northern Ireland / the North of Ireland is a place where questions of territory, sovereignty, religion, national identity, citizenship, political power, and belonging have previously been associated with prolonged and devastating violence.

Those questions did not cease to exist because an agreement was signed.

The peace process instead created political and constitutional structures through which profound disagreement could continue without requiring that disagreement to be settled through continued political violence.

The constitutional future of the place therefore remains legitimately contestable through democratic means.

People can want Northern Ireland to remain within the United Kingdom.

People can want a united Ireland.

People can identify as British.

People can identify as Irish.

People can identify as Northern Irish.

People can identify as more than one of those things.

People can also have considerably more complicated relationships with all of them.

Polaris does not need to manufacture agreement between those people.

It does need to avoid casually turning their disagreement into something more dangerous.

That means trying to report people's positions accurately; preserving self-description where possible; distinguishing legal jurisdiction from personal identity; acknowledging the author's own biases; refusing to treat constitutional disagreement as permission for sectarian hostility; and remembering that the people who bear the consequences of this argument are principally **the people who actually live there**.

This is not an exercise in finding the one perfectly neutral name for a contested place.

There probably isn't one.

It is an exercise in behaving responsibly when there isn't one.

**Their peace matters more than my preferred terminology.**

And if responsible reporting sometimes means holding several descriptions of the same place in my head at once because the people who live there understand that place differently?

Fine.

That seems a remarkably small inconvenience compared with what they have already had to live through.

## 🕯️ Sectarianism, tolerance, and Voltaire

Responsible terminology is also part of responsible reporting about a society in which language, religion, nationality, constitutional aspiration, and communal identity have sometimes become attached to real human danger.

The Belfast / Good Friday Agreement belongs to a peace architecture committed to democratic politics, different constitutional aspirations being pursued peacefully, equality, and respect for different identities.

Polaris should not inflate disagreement into dehumanisation.

For the longer historical problem of what happens when religious difference becomes permission to persecute, punish, or kill one another, Polaris routes elsewhere in the archive to **Voltaire's _Treatise on Tolerance_**.

Different century.

Different political structures.

Different conflict.

But a useful recurring question:

**what happens when disagreement about belief or belonging becomes permission to stop recognising the other person as fully human?**

That is the historical and ethical connection.

It is not a claim that Voltaire was writing about the Troubles, nor that eighteenth-century religious conflict and modern Northern Irish history are interchangeable.

## 🕊️ Peace agreement or ceasefire agreement?

Polaris will not enforce one magical Correct Noun™ for the **Belfast / Good Friday Agreement**.

It is ordinarily and formally described as a **peace agreement**, **peace settlement**, or **political and constitutional settlement**.

But the language of **ceasefire** is not analytically meaningless, and Polaris should not automatically correct it out of somebody's framing.

There is an important chronological distinction.

The Agreement signed in 1998 did not create the first republican and loyalist ceasefires. Those preceded it and formed part of the peace process which made the eventual negotiations possible.

So, narrowly:

**the ceasefires ≠ the Good Friday Agreement.**

But that does not exhaust what somebody may be doing when they describe the settlement through the language of ceasefire.

Calling it a **peace agreement** foregrounds what the political architecture was trying to build.

Talking about it through the history and function of **ceasefire** foregrounds what the peace process was trying to stop.

Both can carry useful information when their meaning is explained.

The reporting problem begins when terminology quietly assigns the role of **belligerent** to only one side of a conflict involving republican paramilitaries, loyalist paramilitaries, British state forces, policing institutions, governments, political organisations, and communities with profoundly different experiences of all of them.

Polaris should therefore pay attention not merely to whether somebody says **peace** or **ceasefire**, but to **who disappears from the story when they say it**.

The shooting had to stop.

Something capable of surviving after the shooting stopped also had to be built.

Those propositions are not enemies.

## 🔎 The language is information too

Apparently minor changes in terminology can also be analytically useful.

**The language a state chooses to describe its own historically violent or constitutionally sensitive political questions is observable information.**

That does not mean every change of noun reveals a secret national-security assessment.

It means language can be treated as data.

An outside observer can compare:

- which constitutional principles are repeatedly emphasised;
- which historical events receive renewed attention;
- whether official language foregrounds **peace**, **security**, **stability**, **terrorism**, **paramilitarism**, **consent**, **reconciliation**, or **constitutional settlement**;
- which actors are described as threats;
- which forms of violence are foregrounded or backgrounded;
- and whether those patterns change over time.

None of those observations alone establishes what “the British state feels”.

They can nevertheless become signals which an analyst compares with other information.

## 🔐 Closing loops where they exist

None of this assumes that a foreign intelligence service is attempting to understand Britain from newspaper vocabulary alone.

That would be silly.

Foreign intelligence organisations may possess substantially more information than is publicly available. Polaris cannot know the full collection picture available to another state.

That is precisely why a small public signal may sometimes matter as a **feeder or corroborating proxy** rather than as a complete intelligence product.

Something weak in isolation may help another analyst:

- corroborate an existing assessment;
- test a hypothesis;
- establish timing;
- identify a change in institutional emphasis;
- distinguish an isolated event from a wider pattern;
- or close an informational loop whose other components are invisible to the public.

The useful question is therefore not:

> **Has this noun revealed Britain's secret feelings?**

It is:

> **If somebody already possesses substantially more information than we do, what might this observable signal help them confirm?**

This does not require democratic institutions to become opaque.

Britain cannot make itself unreadable, and democratic government should not attempt to make itself unreadable.

The practical response is considerably more boring.

If inconsistent terminology is inherited institutional habit, clean it up.

If different terminology describes genuinely different concepts, explain the distinction.

If the language changed because policy changed, communicate the policy clearly.

If inherited framing is unintentionally broadcasting assumptions the institution itself no longer notices, examine the assumptions.

**Close unnecessary loops where they exist.**

---

## 📣 Or, less dramatically: good comms discipline

None of this needs to become **🚨 OPSEC BREACH 🚨** every time somebody changes a noun.

The hierarchy is much simpler:

**responsible reporting → accurate terminology → consistent institutional language → good communications discipline → incidental security benefit.**

The information-security observation is worth retaining because inconsistent public signals can become useful when combined with other information.

But institutions should already want consistent terminology for the ordinary reason that **clear institutions should know what their words mean**.

Transparency about reality is not the same thing as accidental informational leakage through inconsistency.

Not everything needs to be classified.

Sometimes somebody just needs to proofread the fucking comms strategy.

## 🧰 A future integrity pass

This node is the human-facing explanation.

A later House Style / integrity SOP pass can turn it into a short mechanical check for affected Polaris material:

1. Are we referring to the **formal jurisdiction**, or to somebody's relationship with the place?
2. Whose terminology is being used?
3. If the language comes from a person, community, organisation, or source, have we preserved their terminology accurately?
4. Have we accidentally treated administrative geography as personal identity?
5. Have we silently translated **the North / the North of Ireland** into **Northern Ireland**, or vice versa?
6. Have we represented unionist, nationalist, loyalist, republican, British, Irish, Northern Irish, and mixed identities by the same reporting standard?
7. If **peace agreement**, **ceasefire**, or another conflict term is being used, is it clear what the term refers to and who is included in the framing?
8. Does the wording respect the principle that constitutional change belongs to democratic consent rather than authorial preference?
9. Could inconsistent terminology create unnecessary ambiguity for readers or downstream machine processing?
10. If an older node fails this check, flag it for correction without pretending the historical version never existed.

That SOP does not need to solve the constitutional question either.

It just needs to catch the nouns.
