# 🗺️ What Macdonald Was Asked to Test

**First created:** 2026-09-06 \| **Last updated:** 2026-09-06\
*Before asking what the unpublished review concluded, establish what Lord Macdonald was actually commissioned to examine.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The Independent Review of Public Order and Hate Crime is now carrying a surprising amount of weight inside the Government's response to e-petition 746640.

That makes one distinction essential:

> **the Macdonald review was not commissioned as a misogyny review.**

Its published remit is broader.

The useful question is therefore not:

> `What did Macdonald recommend about misogyny?`

We do not yet know.

It is:

> **Which parts of Petition 746640 genuinely fall within the review's remit, which parts only overlap indirectly, and which parts cannot fairly be parked there at all?**

That matters because Government itself invoked the review in its formal response to the petition.

Once Government does that, Parliament is entitled to ask what problem the review was actually designed to solve.

**Question answered:** What was Lord Macdonald asked to test, and how much of the petition's problem can reasonably be treated as part of that review?

---

## 🧾 The Basic Chronology

The review was commissioned by the Home Secretary in **November 2025**, following the October 2025 terrorist attack at Heaton Park Synagogue in Manchester.

Lord Macdonald of River Glaven, a former Director of Public Prosecutions, was appointed to conduct the Independent Review of Public Order and Hate Crime.

The broad purpose of the review, as described in the parliamentary material used in this cluster, was to test:

-   whether public-order and hate-crime law remains **fit for purpose**;
-   whether relevant legal **thresholds and safeguards** are right and effective;
-   whether the framework produces appropriate and consistent outcomes.

The final report was submitted to Government in **May 2026**.

In June 2026, Home Office minister Dan Jarvis told Parliament that Government was reviewing the report and would publish it, together with a formal response, **"shortly"**.

By the time of the Commons Library briefing published on 4 September 2026, publication remained outstanding.

See [🕯️ Where Is Macdonald?](./🕯️_where_is_macdonald.md) for the publication timeline.

This node is about the remit rather than the delay.

## 🗺️ What the Review Was Asked to Look At

The source notes for this cluster identify the review's remit as covering the operation of public-order and hate-crime law, including questions around:

-   whether the framework remains fit for purpose;
-   legal thresholds;
-   safeguards;
-   incitement and stirring-up issues;
-   harassment, alarm and distress;
-   police guidance and training;
-   CPS guidance;
-   resourcing;
-   oversight;
-   consistency.

That is a broad remit.

But it is still a legal and operational remit.

It does not automatically become a general review of:

-   misogyny;
-   VAWG;
-   online radicalisation;
-   platform recommendation;
-   survivor support;
-   education policy.

Those issues may intersect with the review.

They are not the same thing.

## ⚖️ Fit for Purpose

"Fit for purpose" is a deceptively useful phrase.

It asks more than:

> `Does a statute exist?`

A legal framework can exist and still fail because:

-   the threshold is unclear;
-   the offence is too narrow;
-   the offence is too broad;
-   safeguards are inadequate;
-   guidance is inconsistent;
-   police and prosecutors interpret the same provision differently;
-   the law is rarely used because nobody understands it;
-   the law catches conduct Parliament did not intend;
-   the law misses conduct Parliament did intend;
-   enforcement becomes politically inconsistent.

So a fit-for-purpose review can legitimately examine the **relationship between law on the books and law in operation**.

That is one reason the review is relevant to this petition.

Petition 746640 is not only asking:

> `Can Parliament write a new rule?`

It is also asking whether institutions can:

-   recognise misogynistic hostility;
-   record it;
-   prosecute it appropriately;
-   distinguish online abuse from lawful ugliness;
-   intervene before escalation;
-   support survivors.

Macdonald's remit covers some of those functions.

Not all of them.

---

## 🚪 Thresholds Matter Because Law Needs Doors

Public-order and hate-crime law depends heavily on thresholds.

The threshold determines when conduct moves from:

``` text
lawful
```

to:

``` text
criminal
```

or from:

``` text
basic offence
```

to:

``` text
aggravated offence
```

Threshold questions can include:

-   what hostility means;
-   what mental element must be proved;
-   when expression becomes threatening;
-   what counts as stirring up hatred;
-   how context affects meaning;
-   whether actual or likely audience matters;
-   what level of intent or recklessness is required.

These are not abstract lawyer problems.

They determine who can be arrested, charged, convicted and sentenced.

See [📣 Men With Microphones](./📣_men_with_microphones.md) for the practical importance of case law, reach and generally applicable legal standards.

---

## 🧯 Safeguards Are Part of the Law, Not an Obstacle to It

The review was also asked to examine safeguards.

That matters especially when speech, protest and public-order powers are involved.

Safeguards can concern:

-   freedom of expression;
-   political speech;
-   protest;
-   evidential sufficiency;
-   prosecutorial discretion;
-   consistency;
-   proportionality;
-   protection against overbroad interpretation.

A robust law needs both:

``` text
capacity to intervene
        +
limits on intervention
```

This matters for misogyny too.

If Parliament eventually considers expanding stirring-up hatred law to sex or gender, the same legal system will have to distinguish:

-   misogynistic mobilisation;
-   offensive speech;
-   feminist political argument;
-   hostile sex/gender debate;
-   journalism;
-   academic work;
-   survivor testimony;
-   criminal incitement or stirring-up conduct.

So the review's safeguard work may be highly relevant even though misogyny is not named in the remit.

## 📣 Incitement and Stirring Up

The review's public-order remit overlaps with one of the most important unresolved questions in the petition cluster.

The Public Order Act contains stirring-up offences covering some protected characteristics.

There is no equivalent sex/gender stirring-up offence identified in the Commons Library briefing prepared for this debate.

The Law Commission's Recommendation 23 proposed extending stirring-up hatred legislation to sex or gender and expressly discussed extremist misogynist/incel ideology.

That creates an obvious set of questions:

-   Did Macdonald revisit the Law Commission's Recommendation 23?
-   Did he consider whether sex/gender should be included in stirring-up legislation?
-   Did he consider the thresholds proposed by the Law Commission?
-   Did he consider whether existing public-order offences already capture the relevant conduct?
-   Did he identify safeguard problems?
-   Did he recommend a different route?

We do not know.

The remit makes those questions plausible.

It does not prove the report answers them.

That distinction matters.

---

## 🕳️ Did the Review Look at the Section 66 Asymmetry?

Section 145 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026 extends specified aggravated offences to hostility based on sex.

Section 66 of the Sentencing Code was not extended in the same way.

See [🕳️ The Section 66 Gap](./🕳️_the_section_66_gap.md).

Because Macdonald was asked to examine whether hate-crime law remains fit for purpose, it is reasonable to ask whether the review considered:

-   the relationship between the aggravated-offence framework and section 66;
-   the uneven distribution of protected characteristics across hate-crime mechanisms;
-   whether the 2026 reform creates a deliberate or accidental asymmetry;
-   whether another mechanism should address sex-based hostility outside the specified aggravated offences.

Again:

> **reasonable to ask is not the same as established to be in the report.**

The unpublished report is precisely what would resolve that uncertainty.

---

## 🚨 Harassment, Alarm and Distress

Public-order law around harassment, alarm and distress is directly relevant to the review's remit.

That matters for the petition because many forms of misogynistic harm are experienced through:

-   harassment;
-   public intimidation;
-   repeated hostile conduct;
-   targeted abuse;
-   threatening or degrading behaviour.

But this is another place where categories can slide.

``` text
misogynistic speech
        ≠
harassment
        ≠
public-order offence
        ≠
stirring up hatred
```

A fit-for-purpose review should care about whether these boundaries are:

-   clear;
-   usable;
-   consistent;
-   evidence-sensitive.

That is relevant to Petition 746640 without turning the whole review into a misogyny inquiry.

---

## 👮 Police Guidance and Training

The review's remit includes police guidance and training.

That overlaps directly with one of the petition's explicit demands.

Petition 746640 asks Government to fund training for police and prosecutors.

The review therefore may be able to tell Parliament something about:

-   whether existing guidance is coherent;
-   whether officers understand relevant legal thresholds;
-   whether different forces apply the law consistently;
-   whether training is sufficient;
-   whether ambiguity in the law creates operational confusion.

This is a stronger overlap than some of the others.

But even here, be precise.

The review may examine **public-order and hate-crime training generally**.

That does not automatically mean it was asked to design **misogyny-specific police training**.

The overlap is real.

The scope is not identical.

---

## 🧑‍⚖️ CPS Guidance

The same is true for prosecutors.

The review's remit includes CPS guidance.

That potentially engages:

-   charging consistency;
-   interpretation of hostility;
-   public-order thresholds;
-   evidential sufficiency;
-   the relationship between offensive expression and criminal speech;
-   use of aggravated-offence provisions.

That intersects with the petition's prosecutor-training demand.

But:

> **reviewing CPS guidance is not the same thing as funding prosecutor training.**

See [🧮 How Government Translated the Petition](./🧮_how_government_translated_the_petition.md).

This is exactly the kind of translation gap that becomes visible when remit and petition are placed side by side.

## 💷 Resourcing

A legal framework can be perfectly drafted and still perform badly if the institutions using it lack:

-   staff;
-   specialist knowledge;
-   training time;
-   analytical capacity;
-   digital evidence capability;
-   prosecution resources.

The review's resourcing strand is therefore relevant to implementation.

It may help answer:

> **is inconsistency a problem of law, guidance, training, capacity, or some combination?**

That question matters because the wrong diagnosis produces the wrong reform.

If the problem is under-resourcing, changing statutory wording may do little.

If the problem is unclear law, more training may only train people into the ambiguity.

If the problem is inconsistent guidance, neither new law nor new funding alone solves it.

---

## 🧭 Oversight and Consistency

This is another direct overlap with the Petition GC's wider systems analysis.

The review's interest in oversight and consistency speaks to questions such as:

-   Are similar cases treated similarly?
-   Do different police forces use the same thresholds?
-   Do prosecutors apply guidance consistently?
-   Is there a reliable way to identify systemic variation?
-   Who notices when one institution is behaving unusually?

This connects with the control-case logic in [🪩 Solidarity Is Multifaceted](./🪩_solidarity_is_multifaceted.md).

A system becomes more governable when somebody can distinguish:

``` text
normal variation
        from
systemic inconsistency
        from
exceptional handling
```

That matters for hate-crime law generally.

It may also matter for how misogynistic hostility is recorded and prosecuted.

## 🧮 Put the Review Next to the Petition

The useful comparison is not:

> `Macdonald review = answer to misogyny petition`

It is this:

  -----------
  Petition concern                         Macdonald remit overlap            What the review may plausibly tell us                                  What it cannot automatically answer
  ---------------------------------------- ---------------------------------- ---------------------------------------------------------------------- -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  misogynistic hostility in criminal law   **Indirect / structural**          whether hate-crime architecture and thresholds are fit for purpose     whether misogyny-specific recognition should exist as a policy matter

  section 66 gap                           **Potentially relevant**           whether uneven hate-crime mechanisms create incoherence                whether the report actually considered sex specifically

  stirring up misogynistic hatred          **Strong legal overlap**           public-order / incitement / stirring-up architecture and safeguards    whether Government will implement Recommendation 23

  anti-feminist radicalisation             **Partial**                        criminal/public-order thresholds around mobilisation                   wider prevention, education and platform ecosystem

  police training                          **Strong overlap**                 quality/consistency of public-order and hate-crime guidance/training   dedicated misogyny training funding

  prosecutor training                      **Strong overlap**                 CPS guidance and operational consistency                               funding and delivery of training

  recording misogynistic motivation        **Possible operational overlap**   consistency/oversight questions                                        a full national misogyny data architecture unless explicitly examined

  online abuse                             **Partial**                        public-order/criminal threshold questions                              platform regulation under the Online Safety Act generally

  survivor support in education            **Minimal / none**                 little obvious direct remit                                            funding/support systems in schools, colleges and universities

  safer online environments                **Partial**                        criminal-law boundary                                                  recommendation systems, age-appropriate design and Ofcom regulation
  -----------

This table is the core of the node.

Government may legitimately rely on Macdonald for some questions.

It cannot use one broad review as a bucket into which every unanswered part of the petition disappears.

## 🚫 What Macdonald Was Not Obviously Asked to Do

The review was not, on the material available here, commissioned to produce a complete answer to:

-   VAWG strategy;
-   survivor support in education;
-   university safeguarding;
-   platform recommendation;
-   Ofcom enforcement;
-   online misogyny generally;
-   male loneliness;
-   relationship education;
-   broader prevention work.

That matters because Petition 746640 spans all of those systems.

So:

> **"Macdonald is looking at hate crime" cannot be the answer to every gap.**

The remit itself tells us where another institution has to own the problem.

## 🧠 The Review May Matter Most at the Joins

The Petition GC has repeatedly found that the hardest problems are not always inside one statute.

They sit between systems.

Macdonald's review may be especially useful if it addresses joins such as:

``` text
law
↕
guidance
↕
training
↕
charging
↕
oversight
↕
consistency
```

That is different from the wider joins identified in [🧮 How Government Translated the Petition](./🧮_how_government_translated_the_petition.md):

``` text
criminal justice
↕
education
↕
safeguarding
↕
platform regulation
↕
VAWG policy
```

The first set looks squarely like review territory.

The second extends beyond it.

That boundary is useful.

## ⚖️ Current Case Law Makes the Review More Important

The discussion in [📣 Men With Microphones](./📣_men_with_microphones.md) adds another reason Parliament should care about thresholds and safeguards.

Current politically contentious speech cases are generating generally applicable legal principles around:

-   statutory construction;
-   direction and audience;
-   mental elements;
-   risk;
-   evidential sufficiency;
-   freedom of expression.

Those principles do not stay attached to Palestine-related cases.

Case law travels.

So if Macdonald considered:

-   public-order thresholds;
-   proscription-related speech;
-   incitement;
-   stirring up;
-   prosecutorial consistency;

his conclusions may have implications for later cases that look politically unrelated.

That includes any future attempt to legislate around misogynistic mobilisation.

> **A review of public-order law is partly a review of the rules future Governments will inherit.**

That is why safeguards matter even when today's defendant is unsympathetic.

-   **Which parts of e-petition 746640 does Government consider to fall within Lord Macdonald's remit?**
-   **Did the Review consider the relationship between section 145 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026 and section 66 of the Sentencing Code?**
-   **Did it examine the Law Commission's Recommendation 23 on stirring up hatred on grounds of sex or gender?**
-   **Did it consider extremist misogynistic or incel mobilisation specifically, or only public-order law at a general level?**
-   **What did the Review conclude about the legal thresholds governing stirring up hatred, incitement and harassment/alarm/distress offences?**
-   **What safeguards did it recommend for political expression, journalism, protest and other lawful speech?**
-   **Did it examine how actual reach, intended audience and online distribution should be treated in public-order or hate-crime cases?**
-   **What did it conclude about police guidance and training?**
-   **What did it conclude about CPS guidance and charging consistency?**
-   **Did it identify resourcing problems that cannot be fixed by changing legislation alone?**
-   **Did it identify inconsistent practice between police forces or prosecution areas?**
-   **Did it make recommendations about recording or data that would help Government observe misogynistic or sex-based hostile offending more consistently?**
-   **Which parts of the petition does Government accept were outside the Review's remit and therefore still require a separate answer?**

These questions do not assume the report contains a particular recommendation.

They ask Government to tell Parliament what work the review actually did.

## 🗺️ Core Finding

> **Macdonald was commissioned to review the public-order and hate-crime machinery, not to conduct a general inquiry into misogyny.**

That distinction protects the analysis in both directions.

It prevents us from expecting the report to answer questions it was never asked.

It also prevents Government from using the existence of the review as a general-purpose answer to every unresolved part of Petition 746640.

The review appears directly relevant to:

-   legal thresholds;
-   safeguards;
-   stirring-up and incitement questions;
-   harassment/alarm/distress;
-   police and CPS guidance;
-   training;
-   resourcing;
-   oversight;
-   consistency.

It may also illuminate:

-   the section 66 asymmetry;
-   Recommendation 23;
-   the treatment of online reach and mobilisation.

But the unpublished report is needed before those possibilities can become findings.

> **First establish the map. Then ask where the report is.**
