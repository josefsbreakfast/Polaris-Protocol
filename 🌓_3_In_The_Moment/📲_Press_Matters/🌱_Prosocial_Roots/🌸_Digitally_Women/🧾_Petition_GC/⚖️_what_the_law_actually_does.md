# ⚖️ What the Law Actually Does

**First created:** 2026-09-06 \| **Last updated:** 2026-09-06\
*Reading aid to the Commons Library briefing: separate the different
hate-crime mechanisms before shorthand turns them into one imaginary
law.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

There is **no single offence of "hate crime"** in England and Wales.

That is the first thing to keep hold of.

The legal architecture has developed in pieces. Different mechanisms
cover different offences, different protected characteristics and
different legal consequences. The House of Commons Library briefing
prepared for the 7 September 2026 Westminster Hall debate is the primary
parliamentary research baseline for this node.

### 📚 Read alongside

-   [House of Commons Library: "e-petition relating to crimes motivated
    by misogyny and hate crime law" --- Research Briefing
    CBP-11073](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-11073/)

This node does not replace that briefing or the underlying legislation.
Its narrower job is to put the mechanisms side-by-side so that phrases
such as *"misogyny is now a hate crime"* do not conceal what Parliament
has --- and has not --- actually legislated.

**Question answered:** When somebody says the law now treats misogyny as
hate crime, what has actually changed, what has not, and which legal
mechanism are they talking about?

---

## 🧱 Start With the Weird Architecture

"Hate crime law" is an umbrella description.

Underneath it sit several different mechanisms:

``` text
                    HATE-CRIME ARCHITECTURE
                              │
          ┌───────────────────┼───────────────────┐
          ↓                   ↓                   ↓
Crime and Disorder       Sentencing Code      Public Order Act
Act aggravated           s66 aggravation      stirring-up
offences                                       offences
          │                   │                   │
aggravated version       aggravating factor   separate offences
of specified basic       at sentencing        concerning the
offence; potentially     within ordinary      stirring up of
higher maximum           maximum              specified hatred
```

The protected characteristics are **not distributed identically across
those branches**.

That is why a sentence such as:

> `sex has been added to hate-crime law`

can be simultaneously directionally true and legally inadequate.

See [📚 Definitions and
Registers](../📖_Previously_On_The_Internet/📚_definitions_and_registers.md)
for the difference between social, legal and operational vocabulary.

## 🧱 First: There Is Still an Underlying Offence

The Crime and Disorder Act framework does not create a free-standing
offence of:

> `being misogynistic`

Nor does the 2026 reform create a free-standing offence called:

> `misogyny`

Instead, the framework creates aggravated versions of specified **basic
criminal offences** where the statutory hostility requirements are
satisfied.

The Commons Library briefing identifies:

-   various forms of assault;
-   criminal damage;
-   specified public-order offences involving harassment, alarm or
    distress;
-   harassment;
-   stalking.

So the basic structure is:

``` text
specified criminal offence
          +
statutory hostility element
          ↓
aggravated form of that offence
```

That distinction matters.

A person's beliefs, unpleasantness or sexism do not replace proof of the
underlying criminal offence.

---

## ⚖️ Layer One: Crime and Disorder Act Aggravated Offences

Sections 28--32 of the Crime and Disorder Act 1998 contain the
aggravated-offence architecture.

Before the 2026 reform discussed here, the specified aggravated offences
were framed around racial or religious aggravation.

Section 145 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026 legislates to extend the
framework so that the specified offences can also be aggravated by
hostility based on:

-   disability;
-   sexual orientation;
-   transgender identity;
-   **sex**.

The government's petition response describes this as covering **sex or
presumed sex**.

This is a substantive legal change.

For offence types already central to many VAWG patterns --- particularly
**assault, harassment and stalking** --- the ability to charge an
aggravated form where the statutory hostility test is proved can
materially alter the available criminal-law response.

---

## ⏳ But Section 145 Is Not Yet in Force

This is easy to lose in political shorthand.

The House of Commons Library briefing published on **4 September 2026**
states that section 145 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026 is:

> **not yet in force**

So, for the 7 September debate, distinguish:

``` text
law enacted by Parliament
          ≠
law currently commenced and operating
```

A clean description is:

> **Parliament has legislated to extend the specified aggravated
> offences to sex-based hostility, but the provision had not yet come
> into force when the Commons Library published its briefing for the
> debate.**

If commencement changes, check the current legislation and commencement
provisions before relying on this node as a statement of current law.

---

## 📈 What an Aggravated Offence Changes

The aggravated offence is legally distinct from its basic equivalent and
can carry a **higher statutory maximum sentence**.

The Commons Library gives criminal damage as a simple illustration:

``` text
basic criminal damage
maximum: 10 years

racially / religiously aggravated criminal damage
maximum: 14 years
```

The point is the legal ceiling.

It does **not** mean that every aggravated case receives the maximum
sentence.

Sentencing remains case-specific.

But this is why it would also be wrong to say:

> `nothing has changed because misogyny is not a standalone offence`

Extending an aggravated-offence framework is a real criminal-law
intervention.

---

## 🧠 Hostility Is Doing Legal Work Here

The legal category is not simply:

> `the offender is a misogynist`

It is also not:

> `the victim is a woman`

The legislation works through statutory tests concerning **hostility
based on a protected characteristic**.

Keep the registers separate:

``` text
misogyny
    ≠
sex
    ≠
hostility based on sex
    ≠
evidence satisfying the statutory hostility test
```

That is particularly important here because the petition speaks in the
social-language category **misogyny**, while the 2026 reform legislates
through the protected characteristic **sex**.

See [🔖 What the Petition Actually Asked
For](./🔖_what_the_petition_actually_asked_for.md) for that translation.

## 🕳️ Layer Two: Section 66 of the Sentencing Code

The second mechanism is different.

Under section 66 of the Sentencing Code, hostility can operate as an
**aggravating factor at sentencing** for offences within the provision's
scope.

As the Commons Library briefing explains:

-   where the statutory conditions are satisfied, the court must treat
    the hostility as an aggravating factor;
-   the judge must state in open court that the offence was aggravated
    on that basis;
-   the sentence must remain within the statutory maximum for the
    **basic offence**;
-   section 66 itself does **not** create the higher maximum available
    through the Crime and Disorder Act aggravated offences.

So:

``` text
Crime and Disorder Act route
        ↓
aggravated offence
        ↓
potentially higher statutory maximum


Sentencing Code s66 route
        ↓
aggravating factor at sentencing
        ↓
ordinary offence's statutory maximum remains
```

These mechanisms should not be casually described as the same thing.

---

## 🕳️ Sex Is Not Added to Section 66

This is the conspicuous boundary.

The Commons Library briefing states that section 66 does not refer to
hostility on grounds of sex or gender, and that the Crime and Policing
Act 2026 did not extend it in that way.

Therefore the architecture described by the Library looks like this:

``` text
specified Crime and Disorder Act offence
+ proven sex-based hostility
        ↓
new aggravated-offence framework can apply
once section 145 is commenced


offence outside that specified framework
+ sex-based / misogynistic hostility
        ↓
no equivalent sex-based s66 hostility aggravation
```

This is why:

> **"sex was added to hate-crime law" does not mean "sex was inserted
> everywhere the existing hate-crime framework recognises hostility."**

The detailed consequences belong in [🕳️ The Section 66
Gap](./🕳️_the_section_66_gap.md).

This node's job is simply to make the boundary impossible to miss.

## 🧭 A Historical Twist: This Was Not the Law Commission's Recommended Route

There is an important wrinkle in the policy history.

In its 2021 hate-crime report, the Law Commission recommended
**against** adding sex or gender to the protected characteristics for
aggravated offences and enhanced sentencing.

Its concern was not that misogynistic harm was unimportant.

The Commission argued that this route could be ineffective or
counterproductive for women and girls, particularly in contexts such as
rape and domestic abuse, while exclusions for those contexts could
create another uneven hierarchy of protection.

Instead, its recommendations included:

-   extending **stirring up hatred** offences to sex or gender;
-   a government review of the need for a specific public
    sexual-harassment offence.

The Law Commission records that government accepted its recommendation
against adding sex or gender to aggravated offences and enhanced
sentencing in 2023.

The Crime and Policing Act 2026 therefore represents a later policy
choice to take a different route for the specified aggravated offences.

That does not settle whether the 2026 choice is good or bad.

It does mean the legal history is more interesting than:

``` text
Law Commission recommended parity
        ↓
government implemented it
```

That is **not** what happened on sex/gender.

---

## 📣 Layer Three: Stirring Up Hatred

Stirring-up offences are another separate branch again.

The Public Order Act 1986 contains:

-   offences concerning stirring up racial hatred;
-   offences concerning stirring up hatred on grounds of religion;
-   offences concerning stirring up hatred on grounds of sexual
    orientation.

The Commons Library briefing states that there is **no equivalent
offence involving stirring up hatred on the basis of misogyny**.

This is especially relevant because the petition does not only describe
completed offences.

It also asks government to tackle:

> anti-feminist hate groups that target and radicalise young people
> online.

---

## 📣 Recommendation 23

The Law Commission recommended extending stirring-up hatred legislation
to **sex or gender**.

Its published reasoning expressly connected that recommendation to
extremist misogynist "incel" ideology and its potential to lead to
serious criminal offending.

That makes the recommendation unusually relevant to the upstream part of
the petition.

But it is still important not to overstate what such an offence would
do.

``` text
online misogyny
        ≠
radicalisation
        ≠
stirring up hatred
        ≠
incitement / facilitation of violence
```

Different conduct can occupy different legal and safeguarding
categories.

According to the Commons Library briefing of 4 September 2026,
government had not published a formal response to Recommendation 23 or
otherwise indicated that it intended to legislate to extend the
stirring-up offences to sex or gender.

See [📣 Men With Microphones](./📣_men_with_microphones.md) for the
harder boundary between speech, reach, misogynistic mobilisation and
criminal thresholds.

## 📲 "Online" Is Not Another Hate-Crime Offence

The petition also asks for tougher laws for online abuse.

But:

> **"online misogyny" is a problem-space, not one criminal offence.**

Conduct occurring through:

-   social media;
-   messaging;
-   forums;
-   video platforms;
-   coordinated online activity;

still has to be mapped onto the legal framework actually applicable to
the conduct.

Depending on the facts, relevant systems can include:

-   harassment and stalking law;
-   public-order offences;
-   other substantive criminal offences;
-   safeguarding;
-   institutional disciplinary processes;
-   platform duties under the Online Safety Act 2023.

The Online Safety Act is principally a **service-regulation framework**.
It imposes systems-and-processes duties on in-scope services and gives
Ofcom enforcement functions. It should not be silently converted into a
new criminal offence called "online misogyny".

See [📲 Online Safety and
Platforms](../🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/📲_online_safety_and_platforms.md).

## 🚫 Five Things Not to Say

### ❌ "Misogyny has been made a crime."

No.

The reform does not create a free-standing offence of misogyny.

### ❌ "Misogyny is now a hate crime."

Too broad.

Parliament has legislated to extend **specified aggravated offences** to
hostility based on sex. That is not the whole hate-crime architecture.

### ❌ "Sex has simply been added everywhere the other protected characteristics appear."

No.

The frameworks already treated protected characteristics differently,
and the 2026 Act does not add sex to section 66 or create a sex/gender
stirring-up offence.

### ❌ "Nothing changed because misogyny is not a standalone offence."

Also wrong.

Aggravated offences are substantive offences and can carry higher
statutory maxima.

### ❌ "The reform is already operating."

Not on the basis of the 4 September Commons Library briefing.

Section 145 was enacted but **not yet in force** at that point.

## 🗞️ The Press-Safe Paragraph

> **Parliament has legislated to extend specified aggravated offences to
> hostility based on sex. The reform does not create a standalone
> offence of misogyny, does not extend sex-based hostility across
> section 66 of the Sentencing Code, and does not create a stirring-up
> hatred offence based on sex or gender. The Commons Library briefing
> published on 4 September 2026 states that the new aggravated-offence
> provision, section 145 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026, was not yet
> in force.**

That is less exciting than `MISOGYNY BECOMES HATE CRIME`.

It also has the advantage of describing the law.

## 🔖 Now Reconnect It to the Petition

The petition asked government to:

> amend the Sentencing Act 2020 to record crimes motivated by misogyny
> as hate crimes.

Government instead enacted a substantial part of its legislative answer
through the **Crime and Disorder Act aggravated-offence architecture**,
using the Crime and Policing Act 2026.

That is not automatically a defect.

A public petition is not a draft Bill, and government does not have to
use the petitioner's proposed statutory route.

But the difference matters because different routes produce different
effects.

If the intended effect was:

``` text
specified assault / harassment / stalking etc.
        +
proven sex-based hostility
        ↓
aggravated offence with higher statutory ceiling
```

section 145 answers something important.

If the intended effect also included:

``` text
other criminal offence
        +
proven sex-based hostility
        ↓
mandatory hostility aggravation at sentencing
```

the section 66 gap matters.

If the concern is:

``` text
upstream misogynistic mobilisation
        ↓
stirring-up law
```

Recommendation 23 is the unresolved branch.

And if the concern is:

``` text
online ecosystem / recommendation / platform exposure
```

we have moved beyond hate-crime architecture and into a different
regulatory and safeguarding problem.

That is why [🔖 What the Petition Actually Asked
For](./🔖_what_the_petition_actually_asked_for.md) had to come first.

## 🧩 The Legal Map Is Not the Harm Map

The law asks questions such as:

-   What offence occurred?
-   What evidence proves it?
-   Was statutory hostility demonstrated or motivational?
-   Which aggravation mechanism applies?
-   What sentencing range is legally available?

The wider VAWG / radicalisation problem asks different questions:

-   What happened before the offence?
-   What environment normalised the behaviour?
-   Was a young person being recruited into a grievance community?
-   Were harassment signals visible across platforms or institutions?
-   Could education, safeguarding or platform intervention have
    interrupted the pathway?

Those questions overlap.

They are not substitutes for one another.

See [🕸️ From Online Grievance to Offline
Harm](../🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/🕸️_from_online_grievance_to_offline_harm.md)
for the behavioural pathway that criminal law only encounters at
particular points.

and whether its thresholds and safeguards are right and effective.

That makes this fragmented architecture directly relevant to the review.

But its published terms of reference, as summarised by the Commons
Library, do not specifically mention misogyny.

## 🧮 Then Ask What Government Translated

Once the legal mechanisms are separated, the government response becomes
easier to analyse.

The question is no longer vaguely:

> `Did government make misogyny a hate crime?`

It becomes:

-   Which offences did government choose to aggravate?
-   Which sentencing mechanism did it leave unchanged?
-   What happened to recording?
-   What happened to stirring up hatred?
-   What happened to the online-abuse ask?
-   Which parts were routed into VAWG policy rather than criminal law?
-   Which parts depend on the unpublished Macdonald review?

That belongs in [🧮 How Government Translated the
Petition](./🧮_how_government_translated_the_petition.md).
