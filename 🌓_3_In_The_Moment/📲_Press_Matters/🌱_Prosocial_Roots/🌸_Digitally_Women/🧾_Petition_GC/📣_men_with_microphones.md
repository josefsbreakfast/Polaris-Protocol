# 📣 Men With Microphones

**First created:** 2026-09-06 \| **Last updated:** 2026-09-06  
*Speech, reach, recommendation and the point at which one man with a
microphone becomes a systems question.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The working joke for this node is beautifully simple:

> **[Stop giving men microphones.](https://youtu.be/713dLh2kNsc?is=DQ_PpHA7_jv4a1_R)**

The policy proposal requires some refinement.

Annoying men are allowed microphones. Men are allowed podcasts. Men are
allowed to tell other men that women are dreadful, dating is warfare,
their ex was definitely the problem and an insufficiently decorative
girlfriend represents civilisational decline.

A functioning democracy has to tolerate an impressive quantity of
absolute bullshit.

The interesting question begins when we stop looking only at the speaker
and start looking at the system around the microphone.

``` text
speaker
   ↓
microphone
   ↓
platform
   ↓
recommendation
   ↓
audience formation
   ↓
community reinforcement
   ↓
monetisation / status
   ↓
possible mobilisation
```

The microphone is not really the microphone.

> **It is reach.**

**Question answered:** When does "some guy saying horrible things into a
microphone" become a legitimate problem for platforms, safeguarding,
public policy or criminal law --- and when does it not?

---

## 🎙️ Meet Tequila Nate

For analytical purposes, Polaris now has a synthetic case study.

His name is **Tequila Nate**.

Tequila Nate records *Women Sucks with a Z* from his basement. He
dispenses terrible relationship advice. He believes men are the prize.
His mother is upstairs attempting to establish whether he wants meatloaf
or chicken nuggets.

Tequila Nate is imaginary.

This is useful.

Nobody needs to be accused of anything while Nate bravely tests the
outer boundaries of liberal democracy.

The **Tequila Nate Test** is therefore:

> **What exactly has changed between one idiot saying something
> misogynistic and a networked system producing measurable risk or
> harm?**

At each stage, the relevant actor, intervention and threshold may
change.

## 📡 A Microphone Is a Distribution Technology

Historically, access to mass distribution was relatively scarce.

Large audiences often required access to some combination of:

-   a publisher;
-   a newspaper;
-   a broadcaster;
-   a radio or television network;
-   a venue;
-   substantial capital;
-   institutional gatekeepers.

Networked media dramatically lowered those barriers.

That is not inherently bad.

The same change enabled feminist, queer, disabled, racialised, survivor
and other marginalised communities to speak to one another without first
obtaining permission from traditional media institutions.

See [💾 Women Have Always Been
Online](../📖_Previously_On_The_Internet/💾_women_have_always_been_online.md)
and [🌸 Guide to Online
Feminisms](../📖_Previously_On_The_Internet/🌸_guide_to_online_feminisms.md).

But democratising publication also democratises misogynistic
broadcasting.

And contemporary recommendation systems add another distinction:

``` text
everyone can speak
        ≠
everyone receives equal distribution
```

The important question is therefore not only **who spoke?**

It is also:

> **Who distributed it, to whom, how often, under what incentives, and
> with what information about the recipient?**

---

## 🗣️ Speech Is Not Harm Merely Because We Hate It

This distinction has to survive the whole node.

Do not silently turn:

``` text
sexist
  ↓
misogynist
  ↓
harmful
  ↓
extremist
  ↓
illegal
```

into an automatic escalator.

Those are different descriptions operating at different registers.

A more useful spectrum might contain:

``` text
obnoxious opinion

sexist generalisation

misogynistic ideology

targeted degradation / harassment

group-directed hostility

mobilising / threatening conduct

criminal conduct
```

But even that is **not an inevitable progression**.

Context, conduct, intention, audience and effect matter.

Tequila Nate saying something revolting to twelve adults who
deliberately subscribed to his basement podcast may be no more than
Tequila Nate exercising his freedom to be an arsehole in public.

That matters.

Otherwise any attempt to analyse harmful online ecosystems risks turning
ordinary political disagreement, adolescent stupidity or offensive
speech into a security category.

---

## 🧠 "Radicalisation" Needs Discipline Too

The petition specifically asks government to tackle:

> **"anti-feminist hate groups that target and radicalise young people
> online."**

That makes *radicalisation* an important word.

It also makes it a word that needs defining.

A boy watching dating advice has not thereby been radicalised.

A boy watching misogynistic content has not automatically been
radicalised either.

And:

> **misogyny is not synonymous with terrorism.**

So what does *radicalisation* mean in this policy context?

Possibilities include:

-   adoption of increasingly hostile beliefs;
-   movement into grievance communities;
-   progressive normalisation of abuse or violence;
-   participation in targeted harassment;
-   movement toward criminal offending;
-   mobilisation toward ideological violence.

Those are not interchangeable phenomena.

They imply different interventions and different thresholds.

See [🧠 Radicalisation Is Not a Sentencing
Factor](./🧠_radicalisation_is_not_a_sentencing_factor.md) for the
danger of allowing a broad behavioural concept to slide casually into a
criminal-law register.

## 🥃 Tequila Nate Enters the Ecosystem

Now give Nate an audience.

``` text
Tequila Nate posts
       ↓
some people deliberately follow him
       ↓
clips circulate
       ↓
adjacent creators appear
       ↓
audiences overlap
       ↓
shared language develops
       ↓
grievance becomes social
```

This is already different from one man talking alone.

But it still does not automatically make Nate or his audience criminal,
extremist or violent.

The ecosystem matters because repeated social reinforcement can change
what ideas feel:

-   normal;
-   funny;
-   plausible;
-   socially rewarded;
-   permissible to say;
-   permissible to do.

See [🥃 The Manosphere
Ecosystem](../🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/🥃_the_manosphere_ecosystem.md).

## 📈 Then the Feed Changes the Ecology

Now change one variable.

Instead of Nate acquiring only the audience that actively seeks him out,
a platform begins recommending him.

``` text
Tequila Nate posts
       ↓
platform detects engagement
       ↓
clip receives wider recommendation
       ↓
rage / agreement / argument produces engagement
       ↓
system learns an audience cluster
       ↓
adjacent creators are recommended
       ↓
audience becomes increasingly networked
       ↓
creators learn which rhetoric performs
       ↓
content can specialise or escalate
```

Now the analytical question changes.

> **Who is doing the amplification?**

Possible answers include:

-   Nate;
-   viewers sharing the content;
-   other creators;
-   recommendation infrastructure;
-   advertisers;
-   monetisation systems;
-   communities forming around the content.

Different actors can contribute in different ways.

This is why [📈 How the Feed Changes the
Ecology](../🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/📈_how_the_feed_changes_the_ecology.md)
and [👠 You Are Not Getting the Same
Feed](../🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/👠_you_are_not_getting_the_same_feed.md)
matter.

The content and its distribution cannot always be analysed as though
they are the same object.

## 💰 And Then There Is Money

A microphone can also be a business.

``` text
attention
   ↓
engagement
   ↓
distribution
   ↓
followers
   ↓
advertising / subscriptions / donations
   ↓
creator income and status
```

That creates incentives.

If inflammatory gender conflict reliably generates attention, a creator
does not need to begin with a sophisticated political programme for the
surrounding system to reward increasingly inflammatory performance.

So the useful question is not only:

> `Why does this man hate women?`

It is also:

> **What technical and economic environment rewards him for performing
> increasingly inflammatory misogyny to an increasingly segmented
> audience?**

This does not erase individual responsibility.

It identifies additional actors and incentives.

## 🧪 The Tequila Nate Test

The synthetic case lets us change one fact at a time.

### Nate has twelve listeners

Nate says "women suck" to twelve adults who deliberately subscribe to
his podcast.

What exactly are we proposing the state should do?

Probably nothing.

### Nate is recommended to young users

A recommender system begins serving Nate --- and progressively harder
adjacent material --- to thousands of 14-year-old boys who did not
search for it.

Now there are questions about:

-   platform design;
-   recommendation;
-   age-appropriate experiences;
-   child safety;
-   education;
-   safeguarding;
-   media literacy.

### Nate targets an individual woman

Nate identifies a woman and directs sustained hostile attention toward
her.

Now the relevant questions may include:

-   harassment;
-   stalking;
-   threats;
-   communications offences;
-   platform enforcement;
-   coordinated abuse.

The legal answer depends on the actual conduct and evidence.

### Nate becomes part of an organised network encouraging serious offending

Different problem again.

At that point, depending on what is actually said and done, criminal-law
and public-order thresholds may become relevant.

The point of the exercise is not that Nate inevitably travels from the
first box to the last.

It is precisely the opposite.

> **There is no automatic
> `misogyny → radicalisation → extremism → violence` conveyor belt.**

Policy needs to distinguish the stages because the appropriate response
changes with them.

## ⚖️ Where Stirring Up Hatred Enters

The current hate-crime architecture makes this especially interesting.

The Public Order Act 1986 contains offences concerning stirring up:

-   racial hatred;
-   religious hatred;
-   hatred on grounds of sexual orientation.

The Commons Library briefing prepared for the 7 September debate states
that there is no equivalent offence involving stirring up hatred on the
basis of misogyny.

In its 2021 hate-crime review, the Law Commission recommended extending
stirring-up hatred legislation to **sex or gender**.

Its reasoning expressly considered extremist misogynist "incel" ideology
and its potential to lead to serious criminal offending.

That recommendation speaks much more directly to **upstream
mobilisation** than the new aggravated-offence framework does.

The useful question is therefore not:

> `Should misogyny be illegal?`

It is:

> **What conduct was the Law Commission trying to capture that
> harassment law, communications offences, aggravated offences and
> platform regulation do not already capture?**

According to the Commons Library briefing published on 4 September 2026,
government had not published a formal response to Recommendation 23 or
otherwise indicated that it intended to legislate for a sex/gender
stirring-up offence.

See [⚖️ What the Law Actually Does](./⚖️_what_the_law_actually_does.md).

## ⚖️ Case Law Travels

There is a further reason to be disciplined about thresholds.

Current litigation around Palestine-related speech and proscription offences is producing legal interpretation that does not stay inside the Palestine debate.

Whatever somebody's political view of Palestine, Hamas, protest movements, government policy or the defendants involved:

> **the statutory elements and the case law prevail.**

Government can describe a policy problem one way. Ministers can explain what they want legislation to achieve. Police and prosecutors can develop operational approaches around it.

But once criminal liability is contested, the questions become legal and evidential:

```text
What does the statute actually prohibit?

What elements must the prosecution prove?

What did this defendant actually say or do?

To whom was the expression directed?

What risk actually existed?

What did the defendant know about that risk?

What does existing case law require?

What does freedom of expression require?
```

Those answers do not belong exclusively to the controversy that generated them.

> **Case law travels.**

A restrictive interpretation developed in a Palestine-related prosecution may later constrain an attempt to prosecute a misogynistic speaker.

A broad interpretation developed because government urgently wants to capture one politically unpopular form of speech may later be available in a case involving a speaker, protest movement or political cause its original supporters favour.

That is not an accidental inconvenience.

It is one of the reasons generally applicable law matters.

### 🧭 The Supreme Court Has Already Made the Point Concrete

In **R v ABJ; R v BDN [2026] UKSC 8**, the Supreme Court examined section 12(1A) of the Terrorism Act 2000.

The judgment is useful here not because a terrorism offence and a hypothetical sex/gender stirring-up offence are the same. They are not.

It is useful because the Court had to identify what Parliament's speech offence **actually requires**.

Among other things, the Court emphasised distinctions between:

- support for the aims of an organisation and support for the organisation itself;
- expression that merely reaches somebody and expression **directed** to that person;
- the existence of a risk of encouraging support and the defendant's knowledge of that risk;
- political expression and conduct satisfying the statutory offence.

The Court explained that an expression cannot be “directed” to a person if it reaches them only accidentally or inadvertently. It also held that the section 12(1A) recklessness requirement depends upon an actual risk that a person to whom the expression was directed would be encouraged to support the organisation, the defendant's awareness of that risk, and whether taking that risk was unreasonable in the circumstances.

That is a useful reminder for this entire node:

> **policy language such as “radicalisation”, “extremism”, “harmful content” or “misogynistic mobilisation” cannot substitute for the elements Parliament actually puts into an offence.**

If Parliament creates another speech offence later, courts will have to interpret that legislation too.

### 📉 Publication Is Not the Same Thing as Reach

The 2026 prosecution of **Tony Greenstein** provides a useful evidential illustration, with an important limitation.

Greenstein was prosecuted under section 12(1) of the Terrorism Act 2000 and acquitted by a jury at Kingston Crown Court in August 2026.

Published trial reporting records very small view counts for material relied upon in the case. Those reports are useful evidence that:

```text
something was published online
        ≠
a large audience actually saw it
```

But a jury acquittal does **not** provide a reasoned judgment explaining which evidential feature produced the verdict.

So this node should not claim:

> `Greenstein was acquitted because his reach was small.`

The defensible lesson is narrower:

> **“Posted online” and “meaningfully reached an audience” are different evidential propositions, and a criminal case still has to prove the elements of the offence actually charged.**

No offence, Tony.

### 📡 Then the Platform Question Gets Harder

This distinction becomes especially important for misogynistic content because contemporary distribution can separate the speaker's conduct from the platform's conduct.

Imagine:

```text
Tequila Nate uploads a clip
        ↓
58 people see Nate's original post
        ↓
another user reposts it
        ↓
a recommendation system detects engagement
        ↓
the clip is served repeatedly to young users
        ↓
500,000 people eventually encounter it
```

What is Nate's reach?

What is the reposting user's reach?

What is the platform's distribution?

Which audience did Nate intend to address?

Which consequences did Nate know about?

Which questions belong to criminal law?

Which belong to platform regulation?

The answer cannot safely be obtained by putting `500,000 views` next to Nate's name and treating every stage of distribution as his conduct.

Nor can a platform necessarily answer its own regulatory responsibilities by saying that Nate was the original speaker.

> **Speaker liability and distribution-system responsibility are analytically separate questions.**

## ❄️ The Rule Has to Work for People You Dislike

This is where both sides of the House should be able to meet.

A workable criminal-law principle cannot depend upon whether today's defendant has politics you find sympathetic.

If you want a narrow, evidence-sensitive interpretation when a Palestine protester is prosecuted, you cannot casually demand an expansive, impressionistic interpretation when the speaker is a misogynist.

The reverse is equally true.

```text
DEFENDANT YOU LIKE
        ↓
same statute
same evidential standards
same case law
same procedural safeguards
        ↑
DEFENDANT YOU DISLIKE
```

Policy choices made now can help generate the case law applied later in apparently unrelated cases.

That is why safeguards are not gifts to particular political movements.

They are properties of a legal system.

A useful legislative stress test is therefore:

> **If you construct a legal principle because you desperately want Tequila Nate convicted, would you still endorse that principle when it is applied to a protester whose cause you support?**

If not, go back and design the rule again.

## 🧯 Safeguards Belong in the Same Conversation

If Parliament considers extending speech-related criminal offences,
safeguards are not an annoying afterthought.

They are part of competent legislative design.

Questions include:

-   What conduct threshold applies?
-   Must material be threatening?
-   What mental element must be proved?
-   How is legitimate political argument protected?
-   How is feminist criticism of patriarchy or men prevented from being
    caught by an overbroad sex-hostility provision?
-   How are highly contested debates concerning sex and gender handled?
-   What protection exists for journalism?
-   Academic work?
-   Art?
-   Quotation or documentation of misogynistic material?
-   Survivor testimony?

The existence of difficult boundary cases is not, by itself, an argument
for doing nothing.

It is an argument for knowing what law is being designed before
celebrating its headline.

This also maps directly onto the Independent Review of Public Order and
Hate Crime's concern with whether **thresholds and safeguards are right
and effective**.

## 🪞 The Symmetry Problem

A sex- or gender-based stirring-up offence could not simply mean:

> `misogynistic things men say about women`

Law has to operate through a general statutory category.

That immediately produces harder questions.

What is protected?

-   sex;
-   presumed sex;
-   gender;
-   some combination?

How would the law operate where:

-   women express hostility toward men;
-   political movements make sweeping claims about either sex;
-   feminist and trans political disputes involve highly hostile speech;
-   a misogynistic speaker describes his content as criticism of
    feminism;
-   a feminist documents misogynistic speech in order to criticise it?

Again:

> **Write the law knowing the difficult cases exist.**

That is not white-knighting.

It is legislation.

## 🧒 Young People Change the Policy Surface

The petition is not merely concerned with adult men voluntarily
listening to Tequila Nate.

It specifically identifies groups that **target and radicalise young
people online**.

That brings additional systems into view:

``` text
speech law
platform regulation
education
safeguarding
age-appropriate design
parent / teacher tools
media literacy
community intervention
```

Criminal law is therefore only one intervention surface.

The government's own VAWG strategy recognises this broader environment.
The Commons Library briefing notes its concern with misogynistic online
content, violent pornography and inadequate tools for parents and
teachers, and its description of these influences as contributing to a
culture that normalises abuse and control.

That diagnosis is broader than:

> `prosecute the bad podcast man`

It should produce policy broad enough to match it.

## 🕸️ The Offline-Harm Question Comes Later

Some online grievance communities do connect to offline harassment,
abuse or violence.

That matters.

But the existence of that pathway does not justify treating everyone at
its beginning as though they have already reached its end.

``` text
exposure
   ↓
engagement
   ↓
community
   ↓
reinforcement
   ↓
possible escalation
   ↓
possible harmful conduct
```

Every arrow contains uncertainty, agency, context and opportunities for
interruption.

That is why prevention matters.

It is also why indiscriminate securitisation can be counterproductive.

See [🕸️ From Online Grievance to Offline
Harm](../🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/🕸️_from_online_grievance_to_offline_harm.md).

## 🎯 Questions Worth Asking on 7 September

These questions distinguish speech, distribution, safeguarding and
criminal thresholds rather than collapsing them into one problem.

-   **What does Government mean by "radicalisation" when discussing
    online misogyny and young people?**
-   **What evidence does it use to distinguish ordinary sexist or
    misogynistic speech from organised anti-feminist radicalisation?**
-   **What intervention does Government consider appropriate before
    conduct reaches a criminal threshold?**
-   **What conduct does Government believe existing harassment,
    communications and public-order offences fail to capture?**
-   **Does Government intend to respond formally to Law Commission
    Recommendation 23 on stirring up hatred on grounds of sex or
    gender?**
-   **If it does not intend to implement Recommendation 23, why not?**
-   **If it does, what thresholds and freedom-of-expression safeguards
    does it consider necessary?**
-   **Did Lord Macdonald examine Recommendation 23 or misogynistic
    mobilisation in his Independent Review?**
-   **How does Government distinguish platform amplification from the
    liability of the original speaker?**
-   **What role does Ofcom's women-and-girls guidance play in addressing
    recommendation systems that repeatedly expose young users to
    misogynistic material?**
-   **What tools are being provided to schools, parents and safeguarding
    professionals to recognise harmful trajectories without treating
    ordinary adolescent stupidity as extremism?**
-   **How will Government evaluate whether interventions reduce harm
    rather than merely move communities onto less visible platforms?**

-   **What existing case law does Government expect would govern the
    interpretation of any future sex/gender stirring-up offence?**
-   **What lessons is Government drawing from current litigation concerning
    politically contentious speech and public-order or proscription offences?**
-   **How will Government ensure that policy descriptions such as
    “misogynistic radicalisation” do not displace the statutory test applied by
    police, prosecutors and courts?**
-   **How should speaker reach be distinguished from amplification subsequently
    produced by recommender systems, reposting or other third parties?**
-   **What freedom-of-expression and evidential safeguards does Government
    regard as principles of general application, regardless of whether ministers
    approve of the politics of the speaker?**

There may be good answers to all of these.

That is why they are worth asking.

## 📣 Core Finding

> **The problem with Tequila Nate is not that a democracy permits him to
> own a microphone.**

The policy problem begins when misogynistic speech interacts with
recommendation systems, commercial incentives, audience formation,
targeted harassment, youth safeguarding and --- in a smaller subset of
cases --- mobilisation toward serious harm.

The answer cannot be:

> `stop giving men microphones`

But neither is:

> `it's just speech`

an adequate description of a networked distribution system capable of
turning one microphone into millions of personalised loudspeakers.

Tequila Nate may retain his microphone.

His mother may also continue asking whether he wants meatloaf or chicken
nuggets.

**The Constitution survives.**
