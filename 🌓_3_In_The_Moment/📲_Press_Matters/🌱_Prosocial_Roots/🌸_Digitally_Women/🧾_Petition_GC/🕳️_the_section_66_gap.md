# 🕳️ The Section 66 Gap

**First created:** 2026-09-06 \| **Last updated:** 2026-09-06\
*What happens to proven sex-based hostility when the offence falls
outside the new aggravated-offence route?*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The previous nodes establish two things.

First, the petition asked broadly for crimes motivated by misogyny to be
recorded as hate crimes.

Second, the legal architecture does not contain one generic offence or
mechanism called "hate crime". Parliament has legislated through one
particular branch of that architecture: section 145 of the Crime and
Policing Act 2026 extends the specified aggravated offences in the Crime
and Disorder Act 1998 to hostility based on sex.

That leaves a very specific question.

``` text
ROUTE A

specified Crime and Disorder Act offence
+ statutory sex-hostility test
        ↓
aggravated offence
        ↓
higher statutory maximum potentially available


ROUTE B

other criminal offence
+ evidence of sex-based hostility
        ↓
???
```

> **The `???` is this node.**

**Question answered:** After section 145 commences, where does proven
sex-based hostility go when the underlying offence falls outside the
aggravated-offence list?

## ⚖️ What Section 66 Normally Does

Section 66 of the Sentencing Code provides a different mechanism from
the aggravated offences in the Crime and Disorder Act.

For hostility within its statutory scope, it allows the hostility to
matter at sentencing even where the case is not proceeding as one of the
specified aggravated offences.

In simplified form:

``` text
criminal offence
        +
qualifying hostility
        ↓
court treats hostility
as an aggravating factor
        ↓
sentence remains within
ordinary statutory maximum
```

As the Commons Library briefing explains, the sentencing judge must
state in open court that the offence was aggravated on that basis.

Section 66 does **not** itself create the higher statutory maximum
associated with a separately aggravated offence.

Its significance is different:

> **it provides a way for hostility to remain legally visible at
> sentencing even where it does not transform the underlying conduct
> into a separately aggravated offence.**

That is why the omission discussed below matters.

---

## 🕳️ And Sex Isn't There

The Commons Library briefing prepared for the 7 September 2026 debate
states that section 66 does not refer to hostility on grounds of sex or
gender.

The Crime and Policing Act 2026 did not extend it in that way.

After section 145 is commenced, the architecture therefore has a
conspicuous boundary:

``` text
                         SEX HOSTILITY
                               │
                  ┌────────────┴────────────┐
                  ↓                         ↓
        specified CDA offence          other offence
                  │                         │
                  ↓                         ↓
        aggravated offence                 ?
```

For sex, there is no corresponding section 66 branch identified by the
Commons Library briefing.

So:

> **Parity in one layer of hate-crime law is not parity throughout the
> architecture.**

---

## 🪞 "Parity" Depends on Which Layer You Mean

The government's response to the petition says its aggravated-offence
amendment creates parity across protected characteristics.

Within the **aggravated-offence framework being amended**, that
description has a clear meaning: section 145 extends the specified
aggravated offences beyond race and religion to disability, sexual
orientation, transgender identity and sex.

But a reader can easily hear:

> `parity across protected characteristics`

and infer:

> `sex hostility now receives equivalent treatment everywhere hostility matters in hate-crime law`

That does not follow.

The broader architecture remains uneven.

Both of these statements can therefore be true:

-   section 145 is a substantial expansion of the aggravated-offence
    framework;
-   sex is still absent from the section 66 sentencing-aggravation
    mechanism.

The point is not semantic pedantry.

The two routes do different legal work.

---

## 🧪 Put the Boundary Through a Simple Test

### Case A --- an offence inside the specified framework

Suppose conduct satisfies an offence covered by the Crime and Disorder
Act aggravated-offence regime and the statutory sex-hostility
requirements are proved.

For example, once section 145 is in force:

``` text
harassment
+
statutory sex hostility
        ↓
aggravated-offence route
```

There is an identifiable statutory route.

### Case B --- an offence outside that framework

Now suppose another criminal offence is proved.

There is also compelling evidence of hostility based on sex.

But the offence is not one of the specified offences capable of becoming
aggravated under the Crime and Disorder Act framework.

``` text
other offence
+
same sex hostility
        ↓
no corresponding sex limb
in section 66
```

The hostility does not have the same dedicated statutory hate-crime
route.

That is the gap.

The examples are architectural rather than advice about how any
individual case should be charged or sentenced.

---

## 🔖 Why This Matters to the Petition

The petition asked government to:

> **"record crimes motivated by misogyny as hate crimes"**

That wording is broader than:

> create aggravated versions of a specified group of offences where
> hostility based on sex is proved.

The difference is visible:

``` text
petition language

crimes motivated by misogyny
        ↓
broad category


2026 legislative response

specified offences
+
hostility based on sex
        ↓
aggravated-offence category
```

A petition is not a draft Bill, and government is not obliged to use the
statutory route proposed by petitioners.

Nor does the petition tell us precisely how its authors intended every
offence outside the aggravated framework to be treated.

But the difference in breadth is real.

It creates a legitimate question:

> **Was the petition seeking recognition of misogynistic motivation
> across offending generally, or principally stronger treatment of the
> offences most commonly associated with misogynistic harm?**

And if the answer is the former:

> **what is supposed to happen to the rest?**

---

## 🗃️ Recording Is Not the Same Thing as Sentencing

The petition's choice of the word **record** deserves attention.

These are not interchangeable stages:

``` text
police recording
      ↓
charging / offence selection
      ↓
prosecution
      ↓
conviction
      ↓
sentencing
      ↓
published statistics
```

Creating an aggravated offence is one legal intervention.

Requiring hostility to be recognised at sentencing is another.

Recording hostility operationally is another question again.

So the section 66 gap raises a second set of questions beyond sentence
length:

-   Can police record misogynistic or sex-based hostile motivation when
    the offence cannot proceed in aggravated form?
-   Under what operational definition?
-   Who sets that definition?
-   Does the information survive transfer between police and
    prosecutors?
-   Is it visible to the sentencing court?
-   Does it appear in national statistics?
-   Can cases recorded operationally as misogyny be distinguished from
    the statutory category of hostility based on sex?

See [📚 Definitions and
Registers](../📖_Previously_On_The_Internet/📚_definitions_and_registers.md).

The vocabulary matters because:

> **what the system calls the thing helps determine whether the system
> can later find the thing.**

---

## 📊 The Gap May Also Be a Data Problem

Government's VAWG strategy describes misogyny as part of the upstream
environment in which violence and abuse can be normalised.

If government wants to know whether its interventions work, it needs
some way of observing the phenomenon over time.

That includes questions about:

-   prevalence;
-   offence type;
-   repeat offending;
-   escalation;
-   victimisation;
-   charging;
-   conviction;
-   sentencing;
-   geographic or demographic patterns;
-   outcomes after intervention.

The question is not whether **no data at all** exists.

It is:

> **What data architecture allows government to see the phenomenon it
> says it wants to reduce?**

If sex-hostile motivation is formally visible through one legal route
but less visible through another, the consequences may reach beyond
sentencing.

A fragmented legal category can become a fragmented evidence base.

And a fragmented evidence base makes prevention harder to evaluate.

## 🧭 But the Law Commission Complicates the Obvious Fix

The obvious response to a missing section 66 limb might be:

> `Fine. Add sex to section 66.`

The policy history makes that considerably less simple.

In its 2021 hate-crime review, the Law Commission recommended
**against** adding sex or gender to the protected characteristics for
aggravated offences and enhanced sentencing.

Its position was not that misogynistic harm was trivial.

The Commission was concerned that extending those mechanisms could be
ineffective or counterproductive for women and girls, including in the
contexts of rape and domestic abuse. It also considered the difficulties
created by carving particular offences or contexts back out of an
otherwise expanded framework.

Instead, its recommendations included:

-   extending stirring-up hatred offences to sex or gender;
-   government review of the need for a specific public
    sexual-harassment offence.

The Law Commission records that government accepted its recommendation
against adding sex or gender to aggravated offences and enhanced
sentencing in 2023.

The Crime and Policing Act 2026 subsequently takes a different route for
the specified aggravated offences.

That history changes the useful question.

Not merely:

> **Why did government forget section 66?**

But:

> **Was section 66 deliberately left untouched --- and, if so, what
> mechanism is supposed to perform the function petitioners were asking
> for outside the aggravated-offence framework?**

That is a much better question.

---

## 🧩 Three Possible Explanations

The published material in this cluster does not establish which
explanation is correct.

### 1. A deliberate boundary

Government may have intentionally confined the new sex-hostility
provision to specified aggravated offences, while retaining concerns
about extending enhanced sentencing more generally.

If so, Parliament should be able to hear the policy rationale.

### 2. Staged reform

Government may regard section 145 as one intervention while other
questions remain under consideration through the wider VAWG, hate-crime
or public-order work.

If so, the next stage should be identifiable.

### 3. A policy gap

Government may have answered the part of the problem most readily
addressed through the aggravated-offence framework without establishing
an equivalent mechanism for offending outside it.

If so, the gap needs to be acknowledged before it can be evaluated.

> **The difference between these explanations matters.**

Only the first two imply a designed boundary with an identifiable
account of what happens next.

---

## 🧨 Do Not Solve the Wrong Problem

Even if Parliament eventually decided that sex should be added to
section 66, that would not solve the whole problem described by the
petition.

It would not, by itself, resolve:

-   online misogynistic radicalisation;
-   recommendation systems amplifying grievance communities;
-   misogynistic networks operating below criminal thresholds;
-   safeguarding failures;
-   institutional information gaps;
-   training deficits;
-   survivor support;
-   prevention and early intervention.

So:

> **The existence of a section 66 gap does not make section 66 the
> missing master switch.**

It is one legal seam inside a much larger system.

Harm](../🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/🕸️_from_online_grievance_to_offline_harm.md)

## 🕯️ Then There Is Macdonald

The government's petition response itself pointed to the Independent
Review of Public Order and Hate Crime led by Lord Macdonald of River
Glaven.

The review was asked to examine whether public-order and hate-crime law
remains fit for purpose and whether its thresholds and safeguards are
right and effective.

That makes several questions obvious:

-   Did the Review consider the relationship between the newly extended
    aggravated-offence framework and section 66?
-   Did it consider sex or misogyny specifically?
-   Did it revisit the Law Commission's reasoning?
-   Did it consider Recommendation 23 on stirring up hatred?
-   Did it recommend maintaining the asymmetry?
-   Did it recommend another mechanism?
-   Did it consider recording or data consequences?

The published terms of reference, as summarised in the Commons Library
briefing, do not answer those questions.

The report was submitted to government in May 2026.

As of the Commons Library briefing published on 4 September 2026, it had
not been published.

## 🎯 Questions Worth Asking on 7 September

These questions are deliberately diagnostic rather than accusatory.

There may be good policy reasons for the boundary.

If there are, Parliament should be able to hear them.

-   **Why was sex added to the specified aggravated offences but not
    section 66 of the Sentencing Code?**
-   **Was that distinction deliberate?**
-   **Does the Government continue to accept the Law Commission's
    reasoning against adding sex or gender to enhanced sentencing
    generally?**
-   **If it does, what mechanism does the Government consider
    appropriate for recognising proven misogynistic or sex-based
    hostility where the underlying offence falls outside the
    aggravated-offence framework?**
-   **How will police record misogynistic or sex-based hostile
    motivation in those cases?**
-   **Will that information remain visible as a case moves from police
    recording to CPS charging, conviction and sentencing?**
-   **How will it appear in national statistics, so that Parliament can
    assess prevalence, patterns, escalation and outcomes?**
-   **What definition will be used operationally for recording purposes,
    and who will set it?**
-   **How will Government measure whether the 2026 reform is actually
    reducing misogynistic offending or improving criminal-justice
    outcomes?**
-   **Did Lord Macdonald's Independent Review examine the relationship
    between the new aggravated-offence framework and section 66?**
-   **Did the Review revisit the Law Commission's recommendations on
    sex/gender and stirring up hatred?**
-   **If those questions have already been considered by the Review,
    when will Parliament be able to see its conclusions?**

A generic statement that government takes VAWG seriously does not answer
these questions.

A description of section 145 does not answer them either.

The useful answer would tell Parliament **why the boundary exists and
what happens on the other side of it**.

---

## 🧷 Core Finding

> **The section 66 gap is not proof that the 2026 reform failed. It is
> proof that "sex has been added to hate-crime law" is an incomplete
> description of what Parliament did.**

The serious question is not simply whether the gap should be filled.

It is:

> **why the boundary was drawn there, what happens on the other side of
> it, and whether government can still see and respond to misogynistic
> or sex-based hostile motivation that falls outside the
> aggravated-offence route.**

That question connects criminal-law architecture to recording, data,
prevention and accountability without pretending that one statutory
amendment can solve the entire ecology of misogynistic harm.
