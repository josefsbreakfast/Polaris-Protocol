# 🕯️ Where Is Macdonald?
**First created:** 2026-09-06 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-06  
*Government used the Independent Review of Public Order and Hate Crime as part of its answer to petitioners. The review is complete. Parliament still cannot read it.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The question is not:

> `Why has Government commissioned a review?`

That part is easy.

The Independent Review of Public Order and Hate Crime was commissioned in November 2025. Lord Macdonald of River Glaven was asked to examine whether the relevant public-order and hate-crime framework remains fit for purpose, including whether thresholds and safeguards are right and effective.

Government then **invoked that review in its formal response to e-petition 746640**.

That changes the significance of publication.

The review is no longer merely one internal strand of Home Office policy work.

It became part of the answer given to 114,927 petitioners.

And according to the Commons Library briefing prepared for the 7 September debate:

```text
November 2025
review commissioned
        ↓
11 March 2026
Government cites the review
in its response to Petition 746640
        ↓
May 2026
Lord Macdonald submits final report
        ↓
June 2026
Dan Jarvis says Government is reviewing it
and will publish the report and formal response “shortly”
        ↓
4 September 2026
Commons Library briefing still records it as unpublished
        ↓
7 September 2026
Parliament debates the petition
```

> **The report is complete. The public answer is not.**

**Question answered:** Why does the missing Macdonald report matter specifically to the 7 September petition debate?

---

## 🕯️ Government Made Macdonald Relevant

This point should remain very simple.

Government did not have to cite the Independent Review in its response to the petition.

It chose to.

Its March response told petitioners that, alongside aggravated-offence reform and the VAWG strategy, Government was also considering the fitness of public-order and hate-crime law through the Macdonald review.

So:

```text
petition raises unresolved hate-crime
and public-order questions
        ↓
Government says:
we have an independent review examining
this legal territory
        ↓
review finishes
        ↓
Parliament reaches debate
without the report
```

That is why publication is not a side issue.

> **Government made the review part of its answer. Government therefore made the report part of the evidence Parliament reasonably wants before judging that answer.**

---

## 🗺️ Not Every Petition Question Belongs to Macdonald

This is important because the missing-report argument should not become inflated.

As [🗺️ What Macdonald Was Asked to Test](./🗺️_what_macdonald_was_asked_to_test.md) establishes, the review was not commissioned as a general inquiry into misogyny.

Its remit overlaps most strongly with:

- public-order law;
- hate-crime architecture;
- thresholds;
- safeguards;
- stirring-up and incitement questions;
- harassment, alarm and distress;
- police guidance and training;
- CPS guidance;
- resourcing;
- oversight;
- consistency.

It does **not** obviously own:

- survivor support in education;
- university safeguarding;
- platform recommendation;
- Ofcom regulation;
- all forms of online misogyny;
- the whole VAWG strategy.

So the argument is not:

> `publish Macdonald and the petition is solved`

It is:

> **publish Macdonald so Parliament can see what the expert review concluded about the parts of the problem Government itself routed there.**

---

## 🕳️ The Section 66 Question Is Now Waiting Behind the Door

The 2026 reform extends specified aggravated offences to sex-based hostility.

It does not extend section 66 of the Sentencing Code in the same way.

See [🕳️ The Section 66 Gap](./🕳️_the_section_66_gap.md).

That creates an obvious question:

> **Did Macdonald consider the asymmetry?**

Possible answers include:

- yes, and recommended preserving it;
- yes, and recommended changing it;
- yes, but through another mechanism;
- no, because it fell outside the work undertaken.

All four would be useful information.

At present, Parliament cannot know which is true from the published material.

That is exactly the kind of uncertainty an already-completed review should be able to reduce.

---

## 📣 Recommendation 23 Is Also Waiting Behind the Door

The Law Commission recommended extending stirring-up hatred offences to sex or gender.

That recommendation is particularly relevant to the petition's concern about anti-feminist groups and online radicalisation.

The Commons Library briefing says Government had not, by 4 September 2026:

- published a formal response to Recommendation 23;
- otherwise indicated that it intended to legislate for such an offence.

Macdonald's review covers public-order and hate-crime law, including thresholds and safeguards.

So Parliament can reasonably ask:

- Did he consider Recommendation 23?
- Did he agree with it?
- Did he reject it?
- Did he propose another route?
- Did he identify safeguard problems?
- Did he think existing offences already cover the conduct of concern?

Again:

> **the review may contain an answer to an unresolved question that Government itself placed inside the petition response.**

But Parliament cannot evaluate what it cannot see.

---

## ⚖️ Thresholds and Safeguards Matter More After the Current Speech Cases

The legal environment has not stood still while the report remained unpublished.

Current litigation concerning politically contentious speech and proscription offences has continued to develop generally applicable principles around:

- statutory construction;
- mental elements;
- audience and direction;
- actual risk;
- evidential sufficiency;
- freedom of expression.

See [📣 Men With Microphones](./📣_men_with_microphones.md).

That makes a completed review specifically concerned with **thresholds and safeguards** more, not less, relevant.

The point is not that Palestine-related speech cases and misogyny cases are interchangeable.

They are not.

The point is:

> **case law travels.**

A Government deciding whether and how to expand public-order or hate-speech law needs rules that make sense when applied to speakers it likes and speakers it dislikes.

If Macdonald has already analysed that problem, Parliament should be able to read the analysis.

## 🧮 An Unpublished Review Creates a Translation Gap

[🧮 How Government Translated the Petition](./🧮_how_government_translated_the_petition.md) identifies the broader pattern:

```text
public demand
        ↓
Government decomposes problem
        ↓
law / VAWG / Ofcom / education / review
```

Usually, that decomposition is sensible.

But when one branch becomes:

```text
UNPUBLISHED REVIEW
```

the public cannot tell whether the translation preserved the function.

Government can say:

> `this question is being considered`

But once the report has been delivered, that explanation changes.

The question becomes:

> **What did the consideration conclude?**

A completed but unpublished review is no longer evidence that analysis is underway.

It is evidence that analysis exists somewhere Government can see and Parliament cannot.

---

## ⏳ “Shortly” Has Acquired a Timescale

In June, Dan Jarvis said Government would publish the report and set out its formal response **“shortly”**.

There is always administrative work between receipt and publication.

A report may require:

- legal checking;
- national-security review;
- redaction;
- ministerial consideration;
- coordination of a formal response.

So:

> **delay does not prove impropriety.**

But “shortly” becomes measurable once time passes.

By 4 September:

- the report had been in Government hands since May;
- Parliament was three days away from debating a petition to which the review had been cited as relevant;
- the Commons Library still had to brief MPs without access to the report.

That is enough to make the publication timetable a legitimate parliamentary question.

---

## 🏛️ Parliament Is Being Asked to Debate With an Information Asymmetry

This is the practical problem.

On 7 September:

```text
Government
        ↓
has the completed review

MPs
        ↓
do not have the completed review

petitioners
        ↓
do not have the completed review

Commons Library
        ↓
had to prepare the debate briefing
without the completed review
```

That does not prevent Parliament holding a useful debate.

It does mean Government possesses information directly relevant to at least part of the debate that the rest of the House cannot independently assess.

This is especially awkward because the review concerns precisely the sort of questions MPs are likely to raise:

- legal thresholds;
- safeguards;
- public-order law;
- hate-crime consistency;
- police and CPS practice;
- stirring-up questions.

> **The asymmetry is the problem.**

---

## 🪩 Publishing the Report Is Government's Bit

[🪩 Solidarity Is Multifaceted](./🪩_solidarity_is_multifaceted.md) argues that whole-society action does not mean everybody doing the same work.

Petitioners have already:

- organised;
- written;
- persuaded;
- gathered 114,927 signatures.

MPs can:

- ask questions;
- test the legal architecture;
- create control cases;
- put answers on the record.

The Commons Library has:

- mapped the existing law;
- identified the section 66 gap;
- surfaced Recommendation 23;
- documented the publication chronology.

Lord Macdonald has:

- completed the review Government asked him to conduct.

Government's contribution at this point is unusually straightforward.

> **Publish the bloody report.**

Nobody else can substitute for that function.

---

## 🧯 Publication Does Not Require Automatic Agreement

Publishing the report does not require Government to adopt every recommendation.

Government can say:

- we agree;
- we disagree;
- we accept in part;
- we need further work;
- we reject this recommendation because of X;
- we propose another route.

That is policy.

The problem with non-publication is that Parliament cannot distinguish:

```text
Government disagrees with Macdonald

from

Government agrees but has not acted

from

Government is still deciding

from

Macdonald did not address the issue at all
```

Those are materially different states.

Publication makes disagreement governable.

---

## 🧠 Independence Matters Only If the Work Can Be Examined

An independent review is useful because it can:

- test assumptions;
- examine inconvenient evidence;
- separate expert analysis from immediate political incentives;
- identify trade-offs;
- recommend against Government's preferred approach.

But that value is realised publicly only when the work can be scrutinised.

Otherwise:

```text
independent review
        ↓
independent analysis
        ↓
Government receives it
        ↓
public sees only Government's eventual description
```

At that point, much of the independent function has been lost to the public.

The report does not need to be infallible.

It needs to be inspectable.

---

## 🧪 The Control-Case Question

The Mike Wood/control-case logic from [🪩 Solidarity Is Multifaceted](./🪩_solidarity_is_multifaceted.md) is useful here too.

Government routinely publishes:

- commissioned reviews;
- consultation responses;
- independent reports;
- departmental evidence;
- formal responses.

So a fair question is:

> **What is the normal publication timeline for comparable completed Home Office independent reviews, and what explains any difference here?**

Possible explanations may be perfectly ordinary.

The value of the control case is that it converts:

> `this feels slow`

into:

> **how does this handling compare with the administrative baseline?**

That is a better accountability question.

## 🎯 Questions Worth Asking on 7 September

- **On what date did Government receive Lord Macdonald's final report?**
- **What publication steps remained after receipt?**
- **What did “shortly” mean when Government told Parliament in June that publication would follow shortly?**
- **Why was the report not available to the Commons Library when it prepared the briefing for this debate?**
- **Does Government accept that it made the Review relevant to Petition 746640 by citing it in the formal petition response?**
- **Which parts of the petition does Government consider the Review to have examined?**
- **Did Lord Macdonald consider the relationship between section 145 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026 and section 66 of the Sentencing Code?**
- **Did he consider Law Commission Recommendation 23 on stirring up hatred on grounds of sex or gender?**
- **Did he make recommendations concerning misogynistic or incel mobilisation?**
- **What did he conclude about police guidance, CPS guidance, training, resourcing, oversight and consistency?**
- **Did he address online reach, audience, incitement or other speech thresholds relevant to current public-order cases?**
- **Are any redactions required before publication, and if so, on what basis?**
- **Will Government commit to publishing the report in full except where specific lawful redactions are necessary?**
- **Will the formal Government response be published simultaneously?**
- **If not, will Government publish the report first rather than withholding the independent analysis until its own policy position is complete?**
- **What is the publication date?**

The last question is intentionally boring.

Dates are useful.

---

## 🕯️ Core Finding

> **Government used the Macdonald review as part of its answer to e-petition 746640.**

The review has been completed.

That does not mean it answers every question raised by the petition.

It does mean Parliament should be able to see what it says about the questions that fall within its remit.

The useful demand is therefore narrower than:

> `Macdonald will solve misogyny.`

It is simply:

> **Government cited the review. Lord Macdonald finished the review. Parliament is now debating the issue. Publish the review.**

The people outside Government should not have to reconstruct the conclusions of an independent review by inference from legislation, Written Parliamentary Answers and ministerial adjectives.

> **The candle is lit. We would like the document now.**
