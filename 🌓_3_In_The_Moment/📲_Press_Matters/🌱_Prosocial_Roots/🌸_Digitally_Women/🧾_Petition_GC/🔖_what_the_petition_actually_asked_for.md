# 🔖 What the Petition Actually Asked For

**First created:** 2026-09-06 \| **Last updated:** 2026-09-06\
*Preserve e-petition 746640 as the control specimen before legal,
parliamentary and administrative translation starts doing things to it.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

E-petition 746640 is often easy to compress into one line:

> `make misogyny a hate crime`

That is not what the petition actually contains.

The petition combines criminal-law recognition, recording, online-abuse
law, institutional training, survivor support, education and
anti-feminist radicalisation into one public demand. Those asks operate
at different points in the harm pathway and require different
institutions, powers and thresholds.

This node therefore does one deliberately boring-but-important thing
first:

> **Keep the petition's own language visible before translating it into
> the machinery government already has.**

The later gap analysis then asks which parts were directly answered,
which were translated into adjacent frameworks, and which remain
unresolved in the materials available immediately before the Westminster
Hall debate on 7 September 2026.

**Question answered:** What did 114,927 petitioners actually ask
government to do --- and what happens to those asks once they are
translated into existing law and policy?

---

## 🧾 The Petition

**E-petition 746640:** *Legislate crimes motivated by misogyny are hate
crimes, fund support & training*\
**Closed:** 2 June 2026\
**Signatures:** 114,927\
**Westminster Hall debate:** 7 September 2026\
**Debate opened by:** Tony Vaughan MP

The petition's core request says:

> The Government should amend the Sentencing Act 2020 to record crimes
> motivated by misogyny as hate crimes and introduce tougher laws for
> online abuse, and fund the training of police and prosecutors, and
> support for survivors in education.

Its supporting case adds another explicit demand:

> We want the Government to tackle anti-feminist hate groups that target
> and radicalise young people online.

It also states the petitioners' theory of the problem: young women,
especially students, face harassment, assault and online abuse; misogyny
can fuel this violence; recognition could protect victims, deter
offenders, validate survivors' experiences and make educational and
online environments safer.

---

## 🔖 The Petition Is Not One Ask

At minimum, the petition contains these distinct intervention asks:

``` text
hate-crime recognition
        +
recording misogynistic motivation
        +
tougher laws for online abuse
        +
action on anti-feminist radicalisation
        +
funded police / prosecutor training
        +
survivor support in education
        +
safer educational / online environments
```

These are related.

They are not interchangeable.

A reform that answers one should not quietly acquire credit for answering all of them. **Petitioners asked for a system response, not a game of policy telephone.**

---

## ⚖️ Ask One: Recognise Misogynistic Motivation in Hate-Crime Law

The petition asks government to amend the Sentencing Act 2020 and treat
crimes motivated by misogyny as hate crimes.

The government's 11 March 2026 response translates that demand into a
different legal form: **sex or presumed sex** is to be added to the
protected characteristics covered by specified aggravated offences under
the Crime and Disorder Act 1998.

Section 145 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026 will extend that
aggravated-offence framework to hostility based on sex.

This is a substantive response to the petition.

It is not identical to the petition's wording.

``` text
petition's social-language category:
misogyny

        ↓ translated into

government's statutory category:
hostility based on sex / presumed sex
```

Those concepts overlap. They should not be treated as synonyms without
checking the legal provision actually in play.

See [⚖️ What the Law Actually Does](./⚖️_what_the_law_actually_does.md)
for the legal architecture rather than the political shorthand.

---

## 🕳️ The Hate-Crime Reform Does Not Cover Everything

The House of Commons Library briefing makes the boundary particularly
clear.

The Crime and Disorder Act aggravated-offence framework covers specified
forms of:

-   assault;
-   criminal damage;
-   public-order offending involving harassment, alarm or distress;
-   harassment;
-   stalking.

The aggravated versions can carry higher maximum sentences than their basic equivalents. That is practically significant for offence types already central to many VAWG patterns — particularly assault, harassment and stalking — where the evidence supports the statutory hostility test.

But **sex was not added to section 66 of the Sentencing Code**.

Section 66 therefore does not provide sex-based hostility aggravation
for offences falling outside the new specified aggravated-offence
framework.

So:

> **"Misogyny is now a hate crime" is too broad.**

The reform matters. Its boundary matters too.

See [🕳️ The Section 66 Gap](./🕳️_the_section_66_gap.md).

## 🧾 Ask Two: Recording

The petition specifically uses the word:

> **record**

That should not disappear during legal translation.

Charging architecture, sentencing architecture and recording
architecture are not automatically the same thing.

Neither the 11 March government response nor the Commons Library
material used here identifies how misogynistic motivation will be
comprehensively recorded across the criminal-justice system.

That leaves practical questions:

-   How will police record suspected misogynistic motivation?
-   Will recording depend on an aggravated charge being available and
    used?
-   How will CPS and court data preserve the hostility element?
-   Will national datasets make misogynistic patterns visible across
    offence types?
-   How will consistency be measured?

The current materials do not answer those questions. That does not
establish that no relevant recording mechanism exists elsewhere; it
establishes that the petition's recording ask is not resolved by the
material being relied upon here.

## 📲 Ask Three: Tougher Laws for Online Abuse

The petition explicitly asks government to:

> **introduce tougher laws for online abuse**

The government's formal 11 March response does **not** directly identify
a measure answering that request.

The Commons Library briefing subsequently points to the VAWG strategy,
which in turn identifies the Online Safety Act 2023 and Ofcom's
regulatory framework. Those are plainly relevant to online harm. They
are not automatically the same thing as creating or strengthening
substantive criminal offences for online abuse.

That leaves a translation question:

> **What did government understand "tougher laws for online abuse" to
> mean?**

Possibilities include:

-   new or amended criminal offences;
-   harassment or stalking enforcement;
-   platform duties;
-   illegal-content systems;
-   children's protections;
-   image-based abuse law;
-   some combination of these.

The supplied response does not specify.

See [📲 Online Safety and
Platforms](../🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/📲_online_safety_and_platforms.md) for
the distinction between platform regulation and the wider
social/criminal intervention surface.

## 📣 Ask Four: Anti-Feminist Hate Groups and Radicalisation

This is one of the easiest parts of the petition to lose when the whole
thing is summarised as hate-crime sentencing.

The petition explicitly asks government to:

> **tackle anti-feminist hate groups that target and radicalise young
> people online**

That is principally an **upstream prevention and public-order
question**, not merely a sentencing question after an offence has
occurred.

It potentially engages:

-   ideological ecosystems;
-   group formation;
-   recruitment;
-   recommendation and discovery;
-   lawful and unlawful misogynistic speech;
-   safeguarding;
-   education;
-   early intervention.

The government's VAWG strategy recognises online misogyny and prevention
as serious problems. But recognition of the problem is not itself a
specific mechanism for dealing with anti-feminist groups targeting young
people.

See [🥃 The Manosphere
Ecosystem](../🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/🥃_the_manosphere_ecosystem.md) and
[🧠 Radicalisation Is Not a Sentencing
Factor](../🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/🧠_radicalisation_is_not_a_sentencing_factor.md)
for the distinction between ecosystems, pathways, prevention and
criminal attribution.

## 📣 Recommendation 23 Is Uncomfortably Relevant

The Commons Library briefing surfaces one particularly relevant
unresolved strand of the Law Commission's hate-crime review.

The Law Commission recommended extending the stirring-up hatred
legislation to sex or gender. Its reasoning expressly considered the
growing threat of extremist misogynist incel ideology and its potential
relationship with serious criminal offending.

That does **not** make stirring-up offences a complete answer to online
radicalisation. The latter is a much broader policy problem.

But it makes Recommendation 23 a strikingly relevant legal strand when
the petition itself is asking about anti-feminist hate groups and
radicalisation.

According to the 4 September Commons Library briefing:

-   government had not published a formal response to Recommendation 23;
-   government had not otherwise indicated that it intended to legislate
    to extend stirring-up hatred offences to sex or gender.

So one of the legal recommendations most obviously adjacent to this part
of the petition remains unresolved in the briefing prepared for the
debate.

See [📣 Men With Microphones](./📣_men_with_microphones.md).

## 🎙️ Do Not Criminalise the Entire Neighbourhood

Precision matters here too.

The petition's concern about anti-feminist groups and radicalisation
does not mean every unpleasant male-coded internet space is one legal
category.

``` text
men discussing men's lives
        ≠
bad dating advice
        ≠
sexist speech
        ≠
misogynistic grievance
        ≠
targeted harassment
        ≠
stirring up hatred
        ≠
advocacy / facilitation of violence
```

The policy problem is real enough without flattening every threshold
into the most serious one.

That distinction protects legitimate speech **and** makes it easier to
identify conduct that genuinely crosses a legal or safeguarding
threshold.

## 👮 Ask Five: Fund Police and Prosecutor Training

The petition expressly asks government to:

> **fund the training of police and prosecutors**

This is not decorative implementation detail.

Training can affect:

-   recognition of hostility;
-   evidence gathering;
-   charging decisions;
-   recording;
-   understanding digitally networked conduct;
-   consistency between institutions;
-   survivor experience.

The 11 March government response does not identify a specific funded
police/prosecutor training programme answering this part of the
petition.

The useful questions are therefore practical:

-   What training is planned or already operating?
-   Who provides it?
-   Is there dedicated funding?
-   Does it address sex-based hostility specifically?
-   Does it cover online misogyny, networked harassment and
    radicalisation contexts?
-   How will consistency and outcomes be measured?

If relevant programmes exist elsewhere, they need to be identified
rather than assumed.

---

## 🩸 Ask Six: Support Survivors in Education

The petition specifically names young women and students experiencing
harassment, assault and online abuse, and asks for:

> **support for survivors in education**

The VAWG strategy clearly overlaps with this demand. It includes support
for people affected by violence and abuse and places substantial
emphasis on prevention and intervention in schools, colleges and
universities.

But overlap is not the same as demonstrating that the petition's
specific ask has been fulfilled.

Questions remain:

-   What survivor support is actually available in educational settings?
-   Is there new or protected funding?
-   Who delivers it?
-   Are universities included alongside schools and colleges?
-   Does support cover digitally mediated abuse?
-   What happens when one pattern crosses platform, campus,
    relationship, safeguarding and criminal-justice systems?

This is a **partial / implementation** question rather than an
absence-of-policy claim.

## 🏫 Ask Seven: Safer Educational and Online Environments

The petition's desired outcome is broader than punishment.

It wants safer environments:

-   in education;
-   online;
-   particularly for young people.

Government's VAWG strategy substantially shares this preventive framing. Its ten-year architecture is organised around three primary objectives:

- **prevention and early intervention**, with particular attention to online VAWG and misogyny and action in schools, colleges and universities;
- **relentless pursuit of perpetrators**, including the National Centre for VAWG and Public Protection;
- **support for those affected by violence and abuse**.

Its diagnosis is broader than a generic concern about unpleasant posts. The strategy identifies misogynistic online content, easy access to violent pornography, and inadequate tools for parents and teachers as part of the contemporary problem, and describes these influences as contributing to a culture that normalises abuse and control.

The Online Safety Act architecture also addresses important parts of the
digital environment, particularly illegal content and children's
exposure to harmful or age-inappropriate material.

This is therefore one of the areas where government appears to
**substantially recognise the diagnosis**, while implementation is
distributed across several systems rather than contained in one answer
to the petition.

## 🌱 The Petition Contains a Theory of Harm

The petition is not merely asking for symbolic recognition or longer
sentences.

It describes interacting pathways:

``` text
                     misogyny
                    /        \
                   ↓          ↓
        harassment / abuse    anti-feminist
            / assault         mobilisation
                                  ↓
                             radicalisation
                                  ↓
                         possible further harm
```

That is deliberately **not** a deterministic pipeline.

The petition says misogyny **can fuel** harassment, assault and online
abuse. Separately, it identifies anti-feminist groups that target and
radicalise young people.

The government's later VAWG strategy is notably compatible with this
upstream diagnosis. It describes misogynistic online content, the
normalisation of abuse and control, and online root causes of real-world
violence.

> **Government substantially accepts the petition's upstream problem
> statement even where its strongest new criminal-law response operates
> further downstream.**

## 🧠 The Petition Operates Before and After the Offence

Some asks are downstream:

``` text
crime occurs
      ↓
identify relevant sex-based hostility
      ↓
charge / sentence through the applicable framework
```

Others are upstream:

``` text
misogynistic culture
      ↓
anti-feminist networks / mobilisation
      ↓
normalisation / possible radicalisation
      ↓
escalating behaviour
      ↓
possible offending
```

Others run across both:

-   training;
-   recording;
-   education;
-   survivor support.

Therefore:

> **No single criminal-law reform can logically answer the whole
> petition.**

## 🧮 What Government Did With the Ask

The 11 March response substantially translates the petition into three
existing government mechanisms:

``` text
misogyny / hate-crime recognition
        ↓
Crime and Disorder Act aggravated offences

public-order / hate-crime uncertainty
        ↓
Independent Review of Public Order and Hate Crime

root causes / prevention / survivors
        ↓
VAWG strategy
```

The Commons Library briefing then makes the Online Safety Act / Ofcom
framework visible as part of the wider online-VAWG policy architecture.

This translation is not inherently improper. Government has to turn
public demands into legal and administrative machinery.

The analytical question is:

> **What survives the translation?**

This node keeps that comparison deliberately anchored to the petition's asks. The fuller institutional translation — what government selected, omitted, bundled together or routed elsewhere — belongs in [🧮 How Government Translated the Petition](./🧮_how_government_translated_the_petition.md).

## 🕯️ Macdonald Is Part of Government's Own Answer

Government itself made the Independent Review of Public Order and Hate
Crime relevant to this petition by invoking it in the 11 March response.

At that point, the response said the review was due to report by the end
of March.

The Commons Library briefing records the subsequent chronology:

``` text
March 2026
Government invokes the review in its petition response
        ↓
May 2026
Lord Macdonald submits the final report
        ↓
June 2026
Dan Jarvis says publication and formal response will follow “shortly”
        ↓
4 September 2026
Commons Library briefing still describes the report as unpublished
        ↓
7 September 2026
Westminster Hall debate
```

Government made Macdonald relevant to the petition. Its continuing
absence therefore leaves part of government's own March answer
unavailable for scrutiny immediately before the September debate.

See [🕯️ Where Is Macdonald?](./🕯️_where_is_macdonald.md) and [🗺️ What
Macdonald Was Asked to Test](./🗺️_what_macdonald_was_asked_to_test.md).

## 🧩 THE GAPS ARE IN THE JOINS

The useful conclusion is not:

> `government ignored the petition`

It did not.

Government has:

-   legislated;
-   produced a VAWG strategy;
-   relied on existing online-safety regulation for parts of the digital
    environment;
-   commissioned and invoked a major public-order and hate-crime review.

The harder question is whether the pieces join up:

``` text
criminal law
        ↕
recording
        ↕
online safety
        ↕
education
        ↕
police / CPS training
        ↕
safeguarding
        ↕
survivor support
        ↕
radicalisation prevention
```

> ## **THE GAPS ARE IN THE JOINS.**

A system can contain several individually sensible policies while still
losing the pattern between them.

## 🗣️ Questions Available for 7 September

-   When section 145 comes into force, how will misogynistic or
    sex-based hostile motivation actually be **recorded**?
-   Why was sex added to the specified aggravated-offence framework but
    not section 66?
-   What is government's response to Law Commission Recommendation 23?
-   What specifically is being done about anti-feminist groups targeting
    and radicalising young people online?
-   What did government understand the petition's request for
    `tougher laws for online abuse` to require?
-   What funded training will police and prosecutors receive?
-   What survivor support is being funded or delivered in schools,
    colleges and universities?
-   How will Ofcom/platform work connect to education, safeguarding and criminal justice?
-   What information-sharing arrangements join policing, safeguarding, education, online-safety and VAWG systems when the same pattern crosses institutional boundaries?
-   What evidence and outcome measures will government use to determine whether these interventions actually reduce misogynistic offending, victimisation and upstream harm?
-   Who is responsible for noticing when individually lawful or sub-threshold signals become a cross-system safeguarding problem?
-   And, because government itself put it into the answer: **where is
    the Macdonald report?**

## 📚 Keep the Registers Separate

See [📚 Definitions and
Registers](../📖_Previously_On_The_Internet/📚_definitions_and_registers.md)
before casually swapping between:

-   misogyny;
-   hate crime;
-   aggravated offence;
-   hostility;
-   stirring up hatred;
-   radicalisation;
-   harassment;
-   stalking;
-   online harm;
-   doxxing;
-   swatting.

US operational internet-harm definitions can be useful descriptions of
internet-native tactics. They do not determine the meaning of UK
criminal law.

The methodological rule is simple:

> **Do not translate a social term into a legal term until you have
> checked whether the legal term actually covers the same thing.**

## 🔖 Core Proposition

> **Petition 746640 did not merely ask Parliament to increase sentences
> for misogynistic offending.**
>
> It asked government to recognise and record misogynistic hostility,
> strengthen responses to online abuse, confront anti-feminist
> radicalisation, train police and prosecutors, support survivors in
> education and make young people's online and educational environments
> safer.
>
> Government has answered important parts of that demand.
>
> It has also translated the demand into the legal and administrative
> categories already available to it.
>
> The useful question for 7 September is therefore:
>
> **Which parts of the petition have actually been answered, which have
> been translated into something adjacent, and which remain
> unresolved?**
