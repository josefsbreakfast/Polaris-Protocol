# 🧮 How Government Translated the Petition

**First created:** 2026-09-06 \| **Last updated:** 2026-09-06\
*What happened when a broad public demand about misogyny, online harm,
radicalisation, training and survivor support was translated into
government machinery.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Government did not simply accept or reject e-petition 746640.

It **translated it**.

The petition described a lived and social problem in broad terms:

``` text
misogynistic motivation recognised
        +
recording
        +
tougher laws for online abuse
        +
anti-feminist radicalisation
        +
police and prosecutor training
        +
support for survivors in education
        +
safer online and educational environments
```

The Government's 11 March 2026 response answered principally through
three existing or developing policy structures:

``` text
Crime and Policing Bill
        +
Independent Review of Public Order and Hate Crime
        +
cross-government VAWG strategy
```

By the time of the Commons Library briefing published on 4 September
2026, those strands could be mapped further into:

-   section 145 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026;
-   the still-unpublished Macdonald review;
-   the VAWG strategy and action plan;
-   the Online Safety Act and Ofcom's regulatory work.

That is normal government work.

A petition is a social demand, not a departmental organogram.
Petitioners do not need to know whether the answer belongs in the Home
Office, Ministry of Justice, Department for Education, Ofcom, CPS,
policing, safeguarding or primary legislation.

Government does.

> **The analytical question is whether the function survives the
> translation.**

**Question answered:** What did Government hear when petitioners said
*misogyny*, *record*, *online abuse*, *radicalisation*, *training* and
*support* --- and what machinery did it use in response?

Read alongside:

-   [🔖 What the Petition Actually Asked
    For](./🔖_what_the_petition_actually_asked_for.md) --- *the demand
    before translation*.
-   [⚖️ What the Law Actually Does](./⚖️_what_the_law_actually_does.md)
    --- *the legal architecture produced or relied upon in response*.

## 📥 Input: What the Public Asked For

The petition asked Government to:

> **"amend the Sentencing Act 2020 to record crimes motivated by
> misogyny as hate crimes and introduce tougher laws for online abuse,
> and fund the training of police and prosecutors, and support for
> survivors in education."**

Its supporting explanation also asked Government to tackle:

> **"anti-feminist hate groups that target and radicalise young people
> online."**

It described harassment, assault and online abuse affecting young women,
particularly students, and argued that misogyny could fuel that
violence.

This matters because the petition is not one demand.

It is a **bundle**.

A useful decomposition is:

1.  recognition of misogynistic motivation;
2.  recording;
3.  criminal-law consequences;
4.  tougher responses to online abuse;
5.  anti-feminist groups and radicalisation;
6.  young people's exposure;
7.  police and prosecutor training;
8.  survivor support in education;
9.  safer educational and online environments.

Government therefore had several different functions to translate.

---

## 🔄 Translation One: "Misogyny" → "Sex or Presumed Sex"

This is the clearest translation.

``` text
PETITION

misogyny
        ↓

STATUTORY TRANSLATION

hostility based on sex
or presumed sex
```

Section 145 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026 extends the
aggravated-offence framework in the Crime and Disorder Act 1998 to
include hostility based on sex.

That translation has obvious advantages.

It:

-   fits an existing statutory framework;
-   uses a protected characteristic capable of legal definition;
-   creates aggravated versions of specified offences where the
    statutory test is met;
-   can expose offenders to the higher maximum penalties attached to
    those aggravated offences.

But the translation also changes the object being described.

**Misogyny** can describe:

-   an ideology;
-   a social attitude;
-   a pattern of degradation;
-   a cultural hierarchy;
-   hostility toward women;
-   a structural or networked phenomenon.

**Sex or presumed sex** is a statutory characteristic.

The two concepts overlap.

They are not synonyms.

> **Government translated a social and ideological category into a
> legally administrable protected-characteristic test.**

That may be entirely defensible.

But it means the policy analysis cannot stop at the sentence:

> `misogyny has been added to hate-crime law`

See [⚖️ What the Law Actually Does](./⚖️_what_the_law_actually_does.md).

---

## 🔄 Translation Two: "Crimes Motivated by Misogyny" → Specified Aggravated Offences

The petition's language is broad:

``` text
crimes motivated by misogyny
```

The 2026 legislative route is narrower and more specific:

``` text
specified basic offence
        +
statutory hostility based on sex
        ↓
aggravated offence
```

The specified framework includes highly relevant offending such as
assault, criminal damage, harassment, stalking and certain public-order
offences.

That is substantial.

It is also not the same thing as creating a general mechanism by which
sex-based hostility is recognised across all offending.

The distinction becomes particularly visible in section 66 of the
Sentencing Code, which was **not** extended to sex.

See [🕳️ The Section 66 Gap](./🕳️_the_section_66_gap.md).

So the translation is:

> **a general recognition demand became a specified-offence
> architecture.**

The unanswered policy question is whether that boundary was:

-   deliberate;
-   regarded as sufficient;
-   intended as one stage of wider reform;
-   or simply left unresolved.

---

## 🔄 Translation Three: "Record" → ?

This may be the most revealing gap.

The petition did not merely ask for punishment.

It used the word:

> **record**

Recording is an information-system function.

It can occur at several stages:

``` text
police recording
      ↓
crime classification
      ↓
charging / offence selection
      ↓
prosecution
      ↓
conviction
      ↓
sentencing
      ↓
national statistics
```

The Government's March response foregrounded the creation of aggravated
offences and their higher maximum sentences.

That answers an important **criminal-law** question.

It does not, by itself, answer the **recording** question.

So the translation currently looks something like:

``` text
social demand:
make misogynistic motivation visible
        ↓
government answer:
change offence architecture
        ↓
remaining question:
how does the motivation stay visible
through police / CPS / court / statistics?
```

This is not a semantic complaint.

If Government wants to know whether misogynistic offending is changing,
it needs an information architecture capable of seeing it.

> **What the system can prosecute, what it can record and what it can
> count are related questions. They are not the same question.**

---

## 🔄 Translation Four: "Anti-Feminist Hate Groups Radicalising Young People" → Root Causes and Prevention

The petition is unusually specific here.

It describes:

-   anti-feminist hate groups;
-   targeting;
-   radicalisation;
-   young people;
-   online environments.

The Government response moves toward a broader VAWG prevention frame:

-   root causes;
-   whole-society action;
-   healthy relationships;
-   consent;
-   education;
-   prevention and early intervention.

That is not an irrelevant answer.

The VAWG strategy itself explicitly identifies online misogyny as part
of the contemporary problem and places prevention and early intervention
at the centre of the response.

But something changes during translation:

``` text
anti-feminist groups
targeting and radicalising
young people online
        ↓
societal misogyny
+
education
+
prevention
+
online safety
```

The second frame may be broader.

It may also lose some of the specificity of the first.

If the concern is genuinely about **groups, networks, audience formation
and mobilisation**, then the relevant policy surface may also include:

-   platform recommendation;
-   safeguarding;
-   networked harassment;
-   ideological mobilisation;
-   public-order law;
-   the Law Commission's Recommendation 23 on stirring up hatred.

See [📣 Men With Microphones](./📣_men_with_microphones.md).

## 🔄 Translation Five: "Tougher Laws for Online Abuse" → Online Safety and Ofcom

This translation needs particularly careful attribution.

The Government's **11 March petition response** did not set out a
discrete answer labelled:

> `Here are the tougher laws for online abuse requested by the petition.`

The later **Commons Library briefing**, when describing the Government's
VAWG strategy, points to the Online Safety Act 2023 and Ofcom's
regulatory role.

That brings a major systems-and-processes regime into the picture.

But the regulatory object has changed:

``` text
possible petition reading:

stronger law governing abusive conduct
        ↓

policy architecture surfaced later:

duties on online services
+
platform systems and processes
+
child protection
+
Ofcom regulation and enforcement
```

Both can matter.

They are not interchangeable.

> **Platform regulation is not automatically a substitute for
> substantive criminal-law reform, just as creating a criminal offence
> is not automatically a substitute for safer platform design.**

The right question is which problem each mechanism is intended to solve.

See [📲 Online Safety and
Platforms](../🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/📲_online_safety_and_platforms.md).

## 🔄 Translation Six: "Fund the Training of Police and Prosecutors" → Mechanism Not Yet Identified Here

The petition explicitly asks Government to:

> **fund the training of police and prosecutors**

In the Government response and Commons Library material being mapped in
this cluster, no specific training programme is identified that fully
answers:

``` text
who is trained?
what are they taught?
who delivers it?
what is funded?
how much?
when does it begin?
how is competence measured?
```

That does **not** establish that no relevant training exists elsewhere.

It means the mechanism is not identified in the material currently being
used as the Government's answer to this petition.

The appropriate status is therefore:

> **Translation incomplete / mechanism not identified.**

This distinction matters.

A new offence without operational understanding can still fail at:

-   recognition;
-   evidence gathering;
-   charging;
-   victim communication;
-   case progression.

Legislation and training perform different functions.

---

## 🔄 Translation Seven: "Support for Survivors in Education" → VAWG Support + Education

Here there is a clearer policy home.

The VAWG strategy includes:

-   support for those affected by violence and abuse;
-   prevention and early intervention;
-   action involving schools, colleges and universities.

So the petition's survivor-support and education asks do not simply
disappear.

But identifying a policy home is not the same as identifying an
implementation mechanism.

Questions remain:

-   What support is available?
-   Who delivers it?
-   Is it new or pre-existing?
-   How is it funded?
-   Does it include online abuse?
-   How do universities fit?
-   What happens where a case crosses campus, platform, safeguarding and
    criminal-justice systems?
-   How will survivors know where to go?
-   How will outcomes be measured?

The most accurate status is:

> **Policy home identified; implementation answer still needed.**

## 🕯️ Translation Eight: Remaining Hate-Crime and Public-Order Questions → Macdonald

The Government's petition response also pointed to the Independent
Review of Public Order and Hate Crime led by Lord Macdonald of River
Glaven.

That is a legitimate form of policy translation.

Complicated law can require independent review.

The difficulty is temporal.

``` text
petition raises problem
        ↓
government points to review
        ↓
review examines legal architecture
        ↓
report submitted
        ↓
government reviews report
        ↓
Parliament waits
```

The Commons Library briefing records that Lord Macdonald submitted his
final report in **May 2026**.

In June, Home Office minister Dan Jarvis said Government was reviewing
it and would set out its formal response and publish the report
**"shortly"**.

The Commons Library briefing was published on **4 September 2026**.

The report was still not available to Parliament in the material before
the 7 September debate.

That matters because once Government uses a review as part of its answer
to a petition, the review becomes an **answer dependency**.

## 🧩 Translation Is Not Implementation

A demand can be recognised without being implemented.

A policy can be announced without being commenced.

A law can be enacted without being operationalised.

A strategy can name a problem without establishing who delivers the
solution.

So keep these stages separate:

``` text
recognised
    ↓
translated
    ↓
legislated / programmed
    ↓
commenced
    ↓
implemented
    ↓
measured
```

For example:

``` text
PETITION

support survivors in education
        ↓

TRANSLATION

VAWG strategy includes
support + education
        ↓

IMPLEMENTATION QUESTIONS

who?
what?
funding?
access?
measurement?
```

Likewise:

``` text
PETITION

fund police and prosecutor training
        ↓

TRANSLATION

new legal architecture
        ↓

IMPLEMENTATION QUESTION

where is the training mechanism?
```

> **A policy home is not yet a delivery system.**

## 🧱 Translation Creates Joins

Government has good reasons to decompose a complicated social problem.

Specialisation is useful.

Different institutions genuinely do different jobs.

So one lived problem may become:

``` text
                    petition
                       ↓
        one cross-system social problem
                       ↓
   ┌────────┬────────┬────────┬────────┐
   ↓        ↓        ↓        ↓        ↓
  law      VAWG     DfE     Ofcom    police
                                  ↘
                                   CPS
                                  ↘
                              safeguarding
```

That decomposition may improve expertise.

But it creates interfaces.

And interfaces create another question:

> **Who owns the joins?**

A young person experiencing an online misogynistic environment does not
experience:

``` text
25% platform regulation
20% education policy
15% safeguarding
20% criminal justice
20% VAWG strategy
```

They experience one environment.

The state then decomposes that environment administratively.

If the resulting institutions do not reconnect their information and
responsibilities, the original pattern can disappear between them.

---

## 🔗 The Joins We Need to See

For this petition, particularly important joins include:

-   police ↔ CPS;
-   police ↔ schools and universities;
-   education ↔ safeguarding;
-   safeguarding ↔ platforms;
-   Ofcom ↔ Government VAWG policy;
-   survivor support ↔ educational institutions;
-   online safety ↔ criminal investigation;
-   criminal justice ↔ national data;
-   VAWG prevention ↔ work on misogynistic mobilisation;
-   Macdonald's findings ↔ current legislation and operational policy.

None of those joins necessarily requires one giant institution.

They require identifiable responsibility.

> **Where does the pattern become somebody's job?**

That is the administrative version of the petition's problem.

---

## 📊 Translation Needs Outcome Measures

Government can point to outputs:

``` text
section 145 enacted
strategy published
guidance issued
regulator empowered
review commissioned
```

Those are real outputs.

The petition is ultimately concerned with outcomes:

``` text
less harassment
less assault
less online abuse
safer young women
less harmful mobilisation
better survivor support
better institutional response
```

So each translation needs a corresponding measurement question.

### Section 145

**Output:** aggravated-offence framework extended.

**Outcome questions:**

-   Is sex-hostile offending better identified?
-   Is it charged appropriately?
-   What happens to conviction and sentencing outcomes?
-   Can Government see offending outside the aggravated framework?

### VAWG strategy

**Output:** cross-government strategy and action plan.

**Outcome questions:**

-   Are women and girls safer?
-   Are young people's information environments changing?
-   Are prevention interventions reaching the intended groups?
-   Are survivors receiving better support?

### Online Safety Act / Ofcom

**Output:** statutory duties, guidance and enforcement powers.

**Outcome questions:**

-   Does repeated exposure to harmful misogynistic material change?
-   Do recommendation systems behave differently?
-   Does targeted abuse decline?
-   Are platforms identifying relevant risks earlier?

> **Government cannot know whether the translation worked unless the
> outcome can still be connected to the original problem.**

## 🧠 Government Actually Accepts Much of the Diagnosis

This is important.

The Government response does not treat misogyny as imaginary or
irrelevant.

The VAWG strategy, as described in the Commons Library briefing,
explicitly recognises:

-   entrenched sexist attitudes;
-   misogynistic content online;
-   violent pornography;
-   inadequate tools for parents and teachers;
-   a culture that can normalise abuse and control;
-   the importance of prevention and early intervention.

So the central disagreement is not:

> `Is misogyny a problem?`

Government already accepts much of that diagnosis.

The more interesting question is:

> **Does the institutional response preserve the shape of the problem
> once the problem is distributed across legal, regulatory, educational
> and safeguarding machinery?**

That is a much more useful question for Parliament.

## 🎯 Questions Worth Asking on 7 September

These questions test the translation rather than merely asking
Government to repeat its existing policy announcements.

-   **Which parts of the petition does Government consider fully met,
    and which does it consider still in progress?**
-   **How does Government distinguish the petition's social category
    "misogyny" from the statutory category "hostility based on sex"?**
-   **What was the policy reason for responding through specified
    aggravated offences without extending section 66 of the Sentencing
    Code to sex?**
-   **How will misogynistic or sex-based hostile motivation be recorded
    where a case does not proceed as an aggravated offence?**
-   **What specific Government policy answers the petition's request for
    tougher laws on online abuse?**
-   **What specific intervention addresses anti-feminist groups
    targeting and radicalising young people online?**
-   **What does Government mean by "radicalisation" in that context?**
-   **What funded police and prosecutor training accompanies the new
    legal framework?**
-   **What survivor-support provision in education is new, funded or
    measurable within this programme?**
-   **Who is responsible for joining policing, CPS, education,
    safeguarding, Ofcom and VAWG information where one pattern crosses
    those systems?**
-   **What outcome measures will Government use to determine whether its
    response to misogynistic offending and online harm is working?**
-   **Which outstanding questions were considered by Lord Macdonald's
    Independent Review?**
-   **When will Parliament be able to see those answers?**

There may be good answers.

The point of the questions is to locate them.

## 🧮 Core Finding

> **Government did not simply accept or reject petition 746640. It
> translated it.**

Misogyny became sex-based hostility.

A broad hate-crime demand became specified aggravated offences.

Online abuse became, in part, platform regulation.

Anti-feminist radicalisation became part prevention, part education,
part online safety and part unresolved public-order question.

Survivor support found a home within the wider VAWG strategy.

Remaining questions about the hate-crime and public-order architecture
were, in part, routed toward Macdonald.

Translation is necessary.

> **The test is whether the function survives it.**

The question for Parliament is not whether Government has policies in
all the right folders.

It is whether those policies reconnect into a system capable of
**recognising, recording, interrupting and responding to the problem the
petitioners actually described**.
