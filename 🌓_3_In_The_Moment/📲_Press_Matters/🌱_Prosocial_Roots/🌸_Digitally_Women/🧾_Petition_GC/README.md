# 🧾 Petition GC
**First created:** 2026-09-05 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-06  
*Reading desk for the 7 September 2026 Westminster Hall debate on e-petition 746640: what petitioners asked, what the law now does, what Government translated, what remains unresolved, and what to listen for tomorrow.*

## 🛰️ Orientation

On **7 September 2026**, Westminster Hall is due to debate e-petition 746640:

> **Legislate crimes motivated by misogyny are hate crimes, fund support & training.**

The petition closed on 2 June 2026 with **114,927 signatures**.

Its demands are wider than one change to hate-crime legislation.

Petitioners asked Government to address a connected system involving:

- crimes motivated by misogyny;
- sentencing;
- online abuse;
- anti-feminist hate groups;
- radicalisation of young people;
- police training;
- prosecutor training;
- survivor support in education.

Government's March 2026 response distributed those concerns across several existing or developing policy mechanisms:

```text
PETITION
   ↓
aggravated-offence reform
+
VAWG strategy
+
Independent Review of
Public Order and Hate Crime
+
wider online-safety machinery
```

That response contains real policy.

It also leaves real gaps.

This folder is designed to make both visible without collapsing:

```text
misogyny
        ≠
hate crime
        ≠
public-order offence
        ≠
online harm
        ≠
radicalisation
        ≠
terrorism
```

The aim is not to demand that every unpleasant thing become criminal.

It is to ask whether the legal, institutional and technological systems surrounding misogynistic harm actually join up.

## 🧭 Suggested Reading Order

### 1. [🔖 What the Petition Actually Asked For](./🔖_what_the_petition_actually_asked_for.md)

Start with the petition rather than Government's translation of it.

This reconstructs the original bundle of demands and prevents the debate being reduced to:

> `Should misogyny become a hate crime?`

The petition also asks about online abuse, anti-feminist networks, young people's radicalisation, institutional training and survivor support in education.

---

### 2. [⚖️ What the Law Actually Does](./⚖️_what_the_law_actually_does.md)

Then map the legal architecture.

There is no single offence called “hate crime”.

Different mechanisms do different jobs, including:

- aggravated offences under the Crime and Disorder Act 1998;
- aggravated sentencing under section 66 of the Sentencing Code;
- stirring-up hatred offences under the Public Order Act 1986;
- underlying offences such as harassment, stalking and assault;
- the 2026 extension of specified aggravated offences to sex-based hostility.

This node is the legal reference sheet for the rest of the folder.

---

### 3. [🕳️ The Section 66 Gap](./🕳️_the_section_66_gap.md)

Then look closely at one particularly interesting seam.

Section 145 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026 extends specified aggravated offences to sex-based hostility.

Section 66 of the Sentencing Code was **not** extended in parallel.

That matters because section 66 performs a different function from the aggravated-offence framework.

The useful question is not simply whether Government legislated.

It is:

> **Which conduct now receives sex-based hostility recognition, through which legal mechanism, and what remains outside it?**

---

### 4. [📣 Men With Microphones](./📣_men_with_microphones.md)

Now move from architecture to speech.

Featuring, regrettably, **Tequila Nate**.

This node separates:

- misogynistic speech;
- offensive speech;
- criminal speech;
- audience;
- reach;
- distribution;
- mobilisation;
- evidential thresholds.

It also carries a wider rule for the whole folder:

> **case law travels.**

A legal threshold developed in one politically contentious context can govern a later case that looks completely unrelated.

Good law therefore has to remain intelligible when today's sympathies reverse.

---

### 5. [🧮 How Government Translated the Petition](./🧮_how_government_translated_the_petition.md)

Return to the Government response once the original petition and legal machinery are clear.

This node asks what happened when a public demand entered Whitehall.

Some elements were answered directly.

Some were routed into adjacent programmes.

Some became questions for another institution.

Some remain only partially answered.

The key analytical move is:


### 6. [💄 The Camden Crew and VAWG](./💄_the_camden_crew_and_vawg.md)

Then bring the policy back inside Government.

The current VAWG strategy uses strong systems language:

- prevention and early intervention;
- relentless pursuit of perpetrators;
- support for those affected;
- whole-of-government;
- whole-of-society.

Those principles create an institutional standard.

The difficult test is whether safeguarding and accountability survive proximity to political power.

> **VAWG credibility is infrastructure.**

---

### 7. [🪩 Solidarity Is Multifaceted](./🪩_solidarity_is_multifaceted.md)

Whole-society action does not mean women doing more unpaid safeguarding work.

Nor does useful solidarity require everybody to adopt the same vocabulary, politics or motive.

This node distinguishes **direct solidarity** from **enabling solidarity**.

Mike Wood's Written Parliamentary Questions can produce useful administrative control cases even where his original reason for asking them is entirely different.

Ed Miliband can create regulatory and political room around frontier AI without arriving on behalf of cyberfeminism.

The rule is:

> **You do not have to come into our lane. Bring us something useful from yours.**

Polaris will take ten men who can do a Mike or an Ed over a hundred men who know all the correct words.

---

### 8. [🗺️ What Macdonald Was Asked to Test](./🗺️_what_macdonald_was_asked_to_test.md)

Only now move into the Independent Review of Public Order and Hate Crime.

Lord Macdonald was **not commissioned to conduct a misogyny review**.

This node maps what he was actually asked to examine and tests the overlap with Petition 746640.

That distinction prevents two errors:

- demanding answers from a review it was never commissioned to provide;
- allowing Government to park unrelated petition questions inside a broad review whose remit does not contain them.

---

### 9. [🕯️ Where Is Macdonald?](./🕯️_where_is_macdonald.md)

Then comes the publication problem.

Government itself cited the Macdonald review in its formal response to petitioners.

Lord Macdonald submitted the final report in May 2026.

In June, Government said publication and its formal response would follow **“shortly”**.

The Commons Library briefing published on 4 September still had to describe the report as unpublished.

So by the debate:

```text
Government
        ↓
has the completed review

Parliament
        ↓
does not

petitioners
        ↓
do not

Commons Library
        ↓
prepared its briefing without it
```

The claim is deliberately narrow:

> **Government does not have to agree with an independent review. Parliament should still be allowed to read it.**

## 🔍 The Main Gaps to Watch

### ⚖️ Legal architecture

- Sex-based hostility is being added to specified aggravated offences.
- Section 66 does not receive the same extension.
- Stirring-up hatred legislation does not currently contain an equivalent sex/gender offence.
- Law Commission Recommendation 23 remains an obvious unresolved reference point.

### 📲 Online harms

The petition's online component is broader than:

> `illegal content exists online`

It raises questions about:

- recommendation;
- amplification;
- ordinary entry points;
- networked grievance;
- anti-feminist communities;
- young people's exposure;
- movement between online grievance and offline harm.

See the wider [🌱 Thanks Algorithm](../🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/README.md) cluster.

### 🏫 Training and survivor support

The petition explicitly asks for:

- police training;
- prosecutor training;
- support for survivors in education.

Those demands should not disappear merely because Government can point to adjacent legislation.

### 🕯️ Independent review

Government cited Macdonald.

The report is complete.

The publication date is therefore itself useful information.

## 🧠 Working Principles

### 1. Start with what somebody actually said

Polaris generally prefers:

> `this statement / policy / action is misogynistic`

over:

> `this person is a misogynist`

The first formulation is usually more precise, more testable and more useful.

See [🌱 Misogyny Is in the Roots](../🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/🌱_misogyny_is_in_the_roots.md).

### 2. Do not criminalise by vibes

Misogynistic, disgusting, harmful, offensive and criminal are not synonyms.

### 3. Do not confuse reach with content

A speaker, recommender system and audience occupy different positions in an information system.

Their responsibilities may therefore differ.

### 4. Case law travels

Rules written for people we dislike eventually govern people we like.

### 5. Safeguards are part of good law

The answer to under-enforcement is not automatically broader drafting.

The answer to overreach is not automatically doing nothing.

### 6. Prevention starts before prosecution

A whole-society strategy needs tools between:

```text
nothing
        and
prison
```

### 7. Survivors are not compulsory public servants

Institutions already possess responsibilities of their own.

### 8. Bring your own tools

Useful solidarity can create:

```text
evidence
+
leverage
+
precedent
+
capacity
+
safety
+
room to manoeuvre
```

without requiring ideological assimilation.

### 9. Ask the boring question

Dates, ownership, publication, guidance, budgets, commencement and responsibility often reveal more than rhetoric.

### 10. Compare the answer with the question

A policy can be good and still fail to answer the demand it is being presented as answering.
