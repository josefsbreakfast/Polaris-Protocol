## E-petition 746640 — misogyny / hate crime debate handoff

- **Petition:** E-petition 746640, calling for government action on crimes motivated by misogyny.
- **Closed:** 2 June 2026.
- **Signatures:** 114,927.
- **Westminster Hall debate:** Monday 7 September 2026.
- **Lead:** Tony Vaughan MP, on behalf of the Petitions Committee.

### What the petition asks for

- Amend the Sentencing Act 2020 so crimes motivated by misogyny are recorded/treated as hate crimes.
- Introduce tougher laws addressing online abuse.
- Fund training for police and prosecutors.
- Provide support for survivors, including in education.
- Address anti-feminist hate groups and the online radicalisation of young people.
- Petitioners explicitly connect misogyny, harassment, assault, online abuse and environments in which violence against women and girls can develop.

### Government response — 11 March 2026

Government pointed principally to three strands:

- **Crime and Policing Act 2026:** section 145 will add **sex** to the characteristics covered by aggravated offences under the Crime and Disorder Act 1998.
- **Independent Review of Public Order and Hate Crime:** commissioned after the October 2025 Heaton Park Synagogue attack and conducted by Lord Macdonald of River Glaven.
- **VAWG strategy:** *Freedom from Violence and Abuse*, published December 2025, including prevention/early intervention, perpetrator pursuit and survivor support.

### Important legal distinction: there is no single offence of "hate crime"

Current hate-crime architecture operates through several different mechanisms.

#### 1. Crime and Disorder Act 1998 aggravated offences

Sections 28–32 provide aggravated versions of specified offences, including:

- assault;
- criminal damage;
- certain public-order offences;
- harassment and stalking.

Section 145 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026 — **not yet in force** — extends these aggravated offences to hostility based on:

- disability;
- sexual orientation;
- transgender identity;
- **sex**.

This is a substantive change: where applicable, the aggravated offence can carry a higher statutory maximum than the basic offence.

### 2. The Section 66 sentencing gap

- Section 66 of the Sentencing Code provides a broader sentencing-aggravation mechanism for hate motivation in circumstances not covered by the specific aggravated offences.
- **Sex/gender was not added to section 66 by the Crime and Policing Act 2026.**
- Therefore the general section 66 aggravated-sentencing mechanism **does not apply simply because an offence was motivated by misogyny**.
- This means the 2026 reform is narrower than saying generally that "misogyny has become a hate crime".

Potential question for debate:

> Why was sex added to the specified Crime and Disorder Act aggravated offences but not incorporated into the wider section 66 sentencing framework?

### 3. Stirring up hatred remains unresolved

- Public Order Act 1986 offences currently address stirring up hatred on grounds including race, religion and sexual orientation.
- There is **no equivalent stirring-up offence covering sex/gender or misogyny**.
- In its hate-crime review, the Law Commission recommended extending stirring-up legislation to sex or gender.
- Recommendation 23 specifically discussed extremist misogynist/"incel" ideology and its potential relationship with serious criminal offending.
- According to the Commons Library brief, government has **not formally responded to recommendation 23** and has not indicated an intention to legislate for it.

This is particularly relevant because the petition specifically raises:

> anti-feminist hate groups targeting and radicalising young people online.

### 4. Potential policy mismatch: downstream aggravation vs upstream prevention

The government's VAWG strategy itself recognises:

- entrenched sexist attitudes;
- misogynistic online content;
- violent pornography;
- difficulties for parents/teachers countering harmful material;
- an online culture potentially normalising abuse and control;
- these factors as possible **"online root causes" of real-world violence**.

That produces an important analytical distinction:

**Current/new criminal-law intervention:**

misogynistic hostility
→ specified criminal offence occurs
→ hostility established
→ aggravated offence / increased maximum penalty

**Problem described by the petition and VAWG strategy:**

misogynistic information environment
→ normalisation / radicalisation
→ behavioural change
→ escalating risk
→ harassment / abuse / violence

The first intervention does not necessarily solve the second problem.

### 5. Online Safety Act / Ofcom

- Government also relies upon the Online Safety Act 2023.
- In-scope platforms have duties concerning illegal content and protection of children from harmful/age-inappropriate content.
- Ofcom can enforce those duties.
- Ofcom has issued:
  - illegal-content guidance;
  - guidance concerning protection of women and girls from online harms.

Questions remain about the boundary between:

- platform safety regulation;
- lawful but misogynistic/radicalising material;
- extremist ideological ecosystems;
- criminal "stirring up" thresholds;
- safeguarding and early intervention.

### 6. Macdonald review — major outstanding item

- Home Secretary commissioned Lord Macdonald of River Glaven, former DPP, to review public-order and hate-crime legislation in November 2025.
- Terms of reference ask whether the framework remains "fit for purpose" and whether thresholds and safeguards are appropriate/effective.
- The terms do **not specifically mention misogyny**.
- Dan Jarvis told Parliament in June 2026 that:
  - Macdonald submitted his final report in **May 2026**;
  - government was reviewing it;
  - government would publish the report and formal response **"shortly"**.
- As of the research brief / current discussion, publication remains outstanding.

**Very obvious question for the 7 September debate:**

> Where is the Macdonald report, and does it contain findings relevant to misogyny, sex-based hostility, online radicalisation or the Law Commission's outstanding recommendations?

### 7. VAWG strategy

*Freedom from Violence and Abuse* establishes a ten-year ambition to halve VAWG through three main objectives:

1. prevention and early intervention;
2. "relentless pursuit of perpetrators", including a National Centre for VAWG and Public Protection;
3. support for people affected by violence and abuse.

The prevention strand specifically encompasses:

- online VAWG;
- misogyny;
- schools;
- colleges;
- universities.

### 8. Things worth listening for in Monday's debate

- Section 66 Sentencing Code gap.
- Law Commission recommendation 23.
- Stirring-up hatred offences and misogynistic/incel ideology.
- Publication of the Macdonald review.
- Difference between legislating for **"sex"** and addressing **"misogyny"** as the phenomenon identified by the petition.
- Police and CPS training accompanying section 145.
- Recording/data collection: will misogynistic motivation actually become visible in criminal-justice datasets?
- Survivor support and whether new funding accompanies the legal change.
- Universities/schools and safeguarding.
- Online radicalisation and anti-feminist networks.
- Ofcom/Online Safety Act limitations.
- Whether policy addresses misogyny primarily **after an offence occurs**, or also as a potential precursor/risk signal.
- Information-sharing between policing, safeguarding, education, online-safety and VAWG systems.
- How effectiveness of the reforms will actually be measured.

### Core analytical question

The Crime and Policing Act reform is meaningful, particularly for assault, harassment and stalking.

But the broader question remains:

> Has government created a sufficiently joined-up system for recognising, recording, interrupting and responding to misogynistic hostility and radicalisation — or principally improved the criminal-law response once particular offences have already occurred?
