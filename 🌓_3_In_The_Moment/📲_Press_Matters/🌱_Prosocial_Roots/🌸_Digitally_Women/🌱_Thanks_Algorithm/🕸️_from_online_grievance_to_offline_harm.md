# 🕸️ From Online Grievance to Offline Harm

**First created:** 2026-09-06 \| **Last updated:** 2026-09-06\
*The screen is not a causal magic portal. It is also not a wall.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The internet is not somewhere bodies cease to exist.

People move continuously between:

-   feeds;
-   group chats;
-   relationships;
-   schools;
-   workplaces;
-   streets;
-   homes.

So do:

-   beliefs;
-   grievances;
-   information;
-   social permission;
-   behavioural scripts;
-   threats.

The existing `💔_Violence_IRL/` cluster provides the embodiment bridge:
digital fixation, ideological radicalisation, swarm behaviour and
platform failure can have physical expressions including stalking,
intrusion, assault and coordinated violence.

But avoid the overly simple model:

``` text
bad content
    ↓
radicalisation
    ↓
violence
```

A better starting point is:

``` text
existing person + existing social world
              ↕
digital information environment
              ↕
peers / creators / communities
              ↕
relationships and opportunities
              ↓
possible embodied behaviour
```

> **The screen is not a causal magic portal. It is also not a wall.**

**Question answered:** How can something cultivated, rehearsed,
coordinated or reinforced online become harm to an actual person?

## 📈 Start Where the Previous Node Stops

See [📈 How the Feed Changes the
Ecology](./📈_how_the_feed_changes_the_ecology.md).

That node distinguishes:

-   creation;
-   discovery;
-   recommendation;
-   repetition;
-   reinforcement;
-   active searching;
-   community circulation;
-   creator optimisation.

None of those establishes violent effect.

Keep the sequence explicit:

``` text
content exists
      ≠
person sees it
      ≠
person believes it
      ≠
person behaves accordingly
      ≠
person commits an offence
```

Each transition requires its own evidence.

This node begins after making that distinction.

## 🥃 Grievance Can Supply an Explanatory Frame

See [🥃 The Manosphere Ecosystem](./🥃_the_manosphere_ecosystem.md) and
[🌱 He Only Wanted Dating Advice](./🌱_he_only_wanted_dating_advice.md).

Real experiences can include:

-   loneliness;
-   rejection;
-   humiliation;
-   economic insecurity;
-   relationship breakdown;
-   status anxiety.

Those experiences do not themselves predict violence.

What matters analytically is what happens when an explanatory system
repeatedly says:

-   women caused your suffering;
-   feminism stole your status;
-   female autonomy is unfair;
-   rejection is humiliation;
-   dominance restores masculinity;
-   violence or coercion is justified, funny, inevitable or deserved.

The useful object of study is therefore not merely **anger**.

It is the combination of:

-   grievance;
-   target attribution;
-   entitlement;
-   permission;
-   opportunity;
-   reinforcement.

## 🧠 Belief Is Only One Route

Do not make ideology the only mechanism.

Embodied harm can emerge through several routes:

-   ideological mobilisation;
-   interpersonal coercion;
-   parasocial fixation;
-   stalking;
-   peer escalation;
-   networked sexual violence;
-   doxxing and location exposure;
-   image-based sexual abuse;
-   swarm harassment;
-   lone-actor violence.

These can overlap.

They are not interchangeable.

---

## 🪞 Route One: Gendered Entitlement Becomes Interpersonal Behaviour

Someone does not need a coherent political ideology to behave
misogynistically.

Online cultures can supply:

-   scripts;
-   vocabulary;
-   rationalisations;
-   peer approval.

Examples already cross-linked through [🌹 Demonstrating High
Value](../../../🥃_Males_Online/🌹_Demonstrating_High_Value/README.md)
include:

-   negging;
-   status destabilisation;
-   kino-style incremental escalation;
-   dominance scripts.

The relevant transition can be:

``` text
gendered assumption
      ↓
behavioural script
      ↓
boundary test
      ↓
other person's response
      ↓
stop / adapt / escalate
```

The critical issue is not whether somebody has consumed manosphere
content.

It is what they actually do when another person's autonomy conflicts
with the script.

## 🧤 Route Two: Digital Harassment Becomes Physical Access

Digital harassment can supply information useful for embodied
harassment:

-   identity;
-   workplace;
-   social circle;
-   routines;
-   approximate location;
-   photographs;
-   events attended.

[💄 The Gendered Exposure of
Voice](../../../../../🌑_1_Origin_Points/🪿_Embodied_Information_Ecology/🫀🕸️_Information_Is_Experienced/💔_Violence_IRL/💄_the_gendered_exposure_of_voice.md)
adds an important layer: voice carries gendered and sociolinguistic
information, and digital exposure can create risks of impersonation,
sexualisation, profiling, doxxing and stalking.

The important distinction is simple:

> **Visibility is not consent to access.**

Public participation does not imply permission for:

-   surveillance;
-   tracking;
-   unwanted contact;
-   sexualisation;
-   physical approach.

---

## 🕯️ Route Three: Parasocial Fixation

See [💔 Violence
IRL](../../../../../🌑_1_Origin_Points/🪿_Embodied_Information_Ecology/🫀🕸️_Information_Is_Experienced/💔_Violence_IRL/README.md).

Existing Polaris material identifies parasocial fixation as one possible
digital-to-physical vector.

Creator economies can produce unusual combinations of:

-   repeated access;
-   perceived intimacy;
-   financial exchange;
-   personal disclosure;
-   audience entitlement.

For most people this does not become violence.

For some offenders, perceived intimacy or ownership can become part of:

-   stalking;
-   unwanted contact;
-   intrusion;
-   violence.

Parasociality itself is not pathology.

The relevant problem is entitlement to the person behind the mediated
relationship.

---

## 💄 Visibility Can Become Vulnerability

See [💄 The Gendered Exposure of
Voice](../../../../../🌑_1_Origin_Points/🪿_Embodied_Information_Ecology/🫀🕸️_Information_Is_Experienced/💔_Violence_IRL/💄_the_gendered_exposure_of_voice.md).

Women and gender-diverse people may experience public digital
participation differently because visibility can expose:

-   gender;
-   sexuality;
-   ethnicity;
-   class;
-   accent;
-   location clues;
-   relationships.

The existing node treats voice as both biometric and social information
and asks risk assessment to consider bodily consequences rather than
stopping at `data loss`.

> **A privacy failure can also be a safeguarding failure.**

This is one reason information security, platform safety and violence
prevention cannot always be separated cleanly.

## 🕸️ Route Four: Digitally Networked Sexual Violence

See [🕸️ Digitally Networked Sexual
Violence](../../../../../🌑_1_Origin_Points/🪿_Embodied_Information_Ecology/🫀🕸️_Information_Is_Experienced/💔_Violence_IRL/🕸️_digitally_networked_sexual_violence.md).

The existing Polaris definition is:

> **DNSV = sexual violence + peer reinforcement + networked
> coordination.**

The important contribution here is that online-to-offline harm is **not
only a lone-actor problem**.

Existing DNSV indicators include:

-   non-consensual intimate-image circulation;
-   sexualised group harassment;
-   encouragement or planning of sexual assault;
-   trophy economies;
-   distributed predation;
-   peer validation;
-   cross-platform migration.

This produces a different causal structure:

``` text
individual willingness
        ↕
peer approval
        ↕
status incentives
        ↕
shared material
        ↕
group escalation
```

Harm can therefore be:

-   coordinated;
-   socially rewarded;
-   distributed across several participants.

---

## 👥 Peer Reinforcement Matters

The DNSV material gives us especially useful concepts:

-   distributed agency;
-   social incentive loops;
-   predatory cohesion;
-   trophy circulation;
-   violence rehearsal;
-   reformation after takedown.

This helps us move beyond:

> `bad man saw bad content`

Ask instead:

-   What did peers reward?
-   What produced status?
-   Was escalation applauded?
-   Were boundaries mocked?
-   Was material shared as proof?
-   Did somebody facilitate another person's offending?

Group dynamics can change behaviour without erasing individual
responsibility.

---

## 🎮 Rehearsal, Joking and Social Permission

Some harmful behaviour can first appear in:

-   jokes;
-   fantasies;
-   memes;
-   hypotheticals;
-   sexual boasting;
-   roleplay.

Do **not** assume fantasy or offensive humour predicts offending.

The more useful questions are:

-   Is a real person being targeted?
-   Is private information being exchanged?
-   Is offending being encouraged?
-   Are boundaries being tested?
-   Is evidence of actual abuse being circulated?
-   Is escalation producing social status?

Context separates:

-   offensive speech;
-   ideological expression;
-   rehearsal;
-   facilitation;
-   offending.

## 📸 Route Five: Harm Can Already Be Embodied While Remaining Digital

Avoid implying that harm becomes `real` only when somebody is physically
attacked.

Image-based sexual abuse is already harm.

Doxxing is already consequential.

Sexual harassment is already experienced by a body.

Threats can change:

-   sleep;
-   movement;
-   employment;
-   relationships;
-   participation;
-   perceived safety.

This follows the wider **Embodied Information Ecology** premise:

> **information is experienced.**

So `online → offline` is sometimes misleading.

A better formulation is:

> **digitally mediated harm → embodied consequence**

## 🐝 Route Seven: Swarms and Distributed Harassment

Harassment can be distributed.

One participant may:

-   identify the target.

Another may:

-   find personal information.

Another may:

-   circulate it.

Others may:

-   contact;
-   threaten;
-   sexualise;
-   follow;
-   appear physically.

No single participant needs to perform every step.

This complicates:

-   safeguarding;
-   attribution;
-   moderation;
-   policing.

Network structure can matter even where individual offences remain
individually attributable.

---

## 🚪 Online and Offline Networks Can Be the Same People

Do not imagine two populations:

-   `online people`;
-   `real-world people`.

A misogynistic group chat may consist of:

-   classmates;
-   colleagues;
-   friends;
-   partners;
-   acquaintances.

The target may already know the participants.

Digital coordination can therefore operate inside an existing physical
network.

This is especially important for:

-   schools;
-   universities;
-   workplaces;
-   intimate relationships;
-   friendship groups.

---

## 🧿 The Target's Behaviour Changes Too

Harm should not be measured only by whether the offender makes physical
contact.

Targets may change:

-   route;
-   job;
-   account;
-   name;
-   social life;
-   clothing;
-   public speech;
-   home security;
-   attendance at events.

The threat environment therefore has behavioural effects on the target
as well as the perpetrator.

A system that measures only completed attacks misses:

-   constrained autonomy;
-   silencing;
-   withdrawal;
-   precautionary labour.

## 🧠 Do Not Use Mental Illness as a Wastebasket

The existing [💔 Violence
IRL](../../../../../🌑_1_Origin_Points/🪿_Embodied_Information_Ecology/🫀🕸️_Information_Is_Experienced/💔_Violence_IRL/README.md)
node identifies institutional pathologising as one failure mode when
gendered violence is treated as isolated pathology rather than examined
structurally.

Keep:

-   mental health;
-   misogyny;
-   ideology;
-   coercive control;
-   stalking;
-   peer dynamics

analytically distinct.

One does not automatically explain another.

The useful question is:

> **What evidence exists for each factor in this particular case?**

## 🧯 Safeguarding Needs the Network View

The DNSV material argues that institutions can miss harm when they
classify:

-   offences individually;
-   online activity as mere `indicators`;
-   platform incidents as isolated terms-of-service violations.

For safeguarding, ask:

-   Is this one actor or a group?
-   Are people coordinating?
-   Is material being circulated?
-   Are there admins or facilitators?
-   Is the target aware of the full network?
-   Has the group migrated platforms?
-   Is online activity connected to people with physical access?

> **A network problem cannot always be understood one screenshot at a
> time.**

## 📲 Platform Intervention Has Limits --- and Real Uses

Platforms cannot prevent every physical offence.

They can affect:

-   visibility;
-   recommendation;
-   account access;
-   group persistence;
-   reporting;
-   evidence preservation;
-   friction;
-   location exposure.

The DNSV node specifically identifies concerns around:

-   deletion without forensic preservation;
-   victim notification;
-   cross-platform mapping;
-   re-forming networks.

The detailed intervention question belongs in [📲 Online Safety and
Platforms](./📲_online_safety_and_platforms.md).

Platform responsibility should be specific to the power platforms
actually possess.

## ⚖️ Prevention Is Not Sentencing

This is the crucial hand-off to [🧠 Radicalisation Is Not a Sentencing
Factor](./🧠_radicalisation_is_not_a_sentencing_factor.md).

Prevention can legitimately ask:

-   What environments increase risk?
-   What warning patterns recur?
-   Where could safeguarding intervene?
-   What network dynamics facilitate escalation?

Criminal adjudication asks different questions:

-   What did this defendant do?
-   What did they intend or know?
-   What offence is proved?
-   What legally relevant factors apply?

Do not turn:

> `radicalised online`

into a free-floating aggravating label.

A causal hypothesis used for prevention is not automatically a
sentencing factor.

## 🧭 Analytical Guardrails

Do not infer violence from:

-   offensive speech alone;
-   masculinity content;
-   loneliness;
-   manosphere exposure;
-   mental illness.

Do not ignore:

-   direct threats;
-   stalking behaviour;
-   doxxing;
-   boundary testing;
-   facilitation;
-   peer escalation;
-   target-specific fixation;
-   actual coordination.

Prefer:

-   behaviour;
-   evidence;
-   sequence;
-   network;
-   opportunity

over identity labels.

## 🕸️ The Screen Is Not the Boundary

> **Digital environments can supply information, scripts, audiences,
> targets, reinforcement, coordination and social permission.**
>
> None of those mechanically turns a person violent.
>
> But neither does violence begin only at the moment somebody touches
> another body.
>
> Harassment can constrain movement.
>
> Exposure can facilitate stalking.
>
> Peers can reward escalation.
>
> Sexual violence can be networked.
>
> Digital groups can contain people who also know one another offline.
>
> And information produced on a screen can alter what happens in a home,
> school, workplace or street.
>
> **The internet is part of embodied social life. Analyse the crossing
> carefully rather than pretending there is no crossing at all.**
