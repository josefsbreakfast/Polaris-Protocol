# 📲 Online Safety and Platforms

**First created:** 2026-09-06 \| **Last updated:** 2026-09-06\
*Platforms can change an information environment. They cannot
independently solve the society inside it.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

By this point in the cluster, we have separated several things that are
often compressed into one story:

``` text
misogynistic cultural substrate
        ↓
different users receive different feeds
        ↓
communities and grievance ecosystems overlap
        ↓
ordinary needs can provide entrance points
        ↓
distribution architecture changes availability
        ↓
digital and embodied harms can interact
        ↓
prevention is not the same thing as sentencing
```

The remaining question is practical:

> **What can we reasonably ask platforms and online-safety regulators to
> do?**

The answer is neither:

> `nothing — users are responsible for themselves`

nor:

> `make the internet stop misogyny`

Platforms possess real forms of infrastructural power.

They can alter:

-   recommendation;
-   amplification;
-   discovery;
-   friction;
-   moderation;
-   reporting;
-   children's experiences;
-   evidence preservation;
-   data access;
-   the incentives surrounding creators and communities.

They cannot independently solve:

-   misogyny;
-   loneliness;
-   fascism;
-   abusive relationships;
-   safeguarding failures;
-   criminal violence.

> **The useful intervention surface is the space between pretending
> platforms are neutral pipes and pretending they are sovereign
> governments of human behaviour.**

**Question answered:** What can platforms and regulators reasonably
change, where are the limits of platform intervention, and what still
has to be done elsewhere?

---

## 📈 Begin With the Powers Platforms Actually Have

See [📈 How the Feed Changes the
Ecology](./📈_how_the_feed_changes_the_ecology.md).

That node distinguishes:

-   creation;
-   discovery;
-   recommendation;
-   repetition;
-   reinforcement;
-   active searching;
-   community circulation;
-   creator optimisation.

Those distinctions tell us where intervention can occur.

A platform may not have created a misogynistic belief.

It may still control whether a particular piece of content is:

-   recommended;
-   searchable;
-   monetised;
-   repeatedly surfaced;
-   placed beside adjacent material;
-   allowed to circulate through a large network.

This is why:

> `the platform did not create misogyny`

is true but incomplete.

The relevant governance question is:

> **What did the platform do with the material once it existed?**

---

## 🔎 Discovery

Platforms can influence how easy material is to find.

Possible intervention points include:

-   search ranking;
-   autocomplete;
-   hashtags;
-   trending systems;
-   suggested accounts;
-   related searches;
-   recommendation surfaces.

This does not require deleting lawful material.

There is a large practical difference between:

``` text
content exists somewhere on the service
```

and:

``` text
the service actively helps a user discover it
```

Availability and amplification are different policy questions.

---

## 🧭 Recommendation

Recommendation is one of the clearest platform-controlled surfaces.

Platforms can ask:

-   What content follows ordinary dating or masculinity searches?
-   Are repeated recommendations narrowing toward more hostile material?
-   Are young users receiving different pathways from adults?
-   Are particular creators functioning as bridges between benign and
    harmful ecosystems?
-   Does the system repeatedly recommend material a user has not
    deliberately sought?

See [🌱 He Only Wanted Dating
Advice](./🌱_he_only_wanted_dating_advice.md).

The goal is not to declare every adjacent recommendation harmful.

It is to understand the route.

---

## 📣 Amplification

Amplification is broader than recommendation.

It can include:

-   ranking;
-   virality systems;
-   trending;
-   repost mechanics;
-   notifications;
-   search prominence;
-   creator promotion.

A useful regulatory distinction is:

> **Is the platform merely hosting the material, or is its architecture
> materially increasing its reach?**

That distinction matters particularly where content is:

-   legal;
-   offensive;
-   misogynistic;
-   ideologically extreme

but does not itself cross an illegal-content threshold.

---

## 🪜 Friction Is an Intervention

Platform safety is not only:

``` text
leave up
vs
take down
```

Platforms can add friction.

Examples can include:

-   interrupting rapid forwarding;
-   slowing mass contact;
-   limiting unsolicited messages;
-   restricting discoverability;
-   changing recommendation eligibility;
-   warning before sharing;
-   making blocking easier;
-   preventing repeated contact after blocking;
-   limiting location exposure.

Friction can change behaviour without requiring a platform to adjudicate
every contested belief.

> **Design choices can make harmful conduct harder without making lawful
> disagreement impossible.**

---

## 🚨 Moderation Still Matters

Some material and conduct does require moderation.

Relevant categories can include:

-   threats;
-   harassment;
-   stalking behaviour;
-   doxxing;
-   image-based sexual abuse;
-   coordinated targeting;
-   illegal sexual content;
-   encouragement or facilitation of criminal conduct.

See [🕸️ From Online Grievance to Offline
Harm](./🕸️_from_online_grievance_to_offline_harm.md).

The harder boundary is lawful but harmful or misogynistic material.

That is where moderation policy, recommendation policy and criminal law
should not be lazily collapsed.

A piece of content can be:

``` text
lawful
+
misogynistic
+
eligible for platform policy intervention
```

without therefore being criminal.

Equally:

``` text
lawful
```

does not mean:

``` text
platform must recommend it
```

---

## ⚖️ Illegal Content and Harmful Content Are Different Problems

The policy notes for this cluster identify an important unresolved
boundary between:

-   platform safety regulation;
-   lawful but misogynistic or radicalising material;
-   extremist ideological ecosystems;
-   criminal stirring-up thresholds;
-   safeguarding and early intervention.

That boundary should remain visible.

Criminal law asks whether conduct meets a legal threshold.

Platform governance can also ask:

-   Is this content suitable for recommendation?
-   Is it appropriate for children?
-   Is a community repeatedly facilitating harassment?
-   Is a design feature creating foreseeable safety problems?
-   Is a creator monetising material the service has decided not to
    amplify?

Different questions permit different interventions.

---

## 🇬🇧 Online Safety Act / Ofcom

The government response relevant to the misogyny petition relies in part
on the **Online Safety Act 2023**.

The cluster's research notes identify:

-   duties on in-scope platforms concerning illegal content;
-   protections for children from harmful or age-inappropriate content;
-   Ofcom enforcement;
-   Ofcom guidance concerning illegal content;
-   Ofcom guidance concerning protection of women and girls from online
    harms.

That gives government a real regulatory answer to part of the petition.

It does not automatically answer the whole problem.

The petition is concerned not only with illegal posts but with:

-   anti-feminist hate groups;
-   radicalisation;
-   harassment;
-   abuse;
-   environments in which violence may develop.

The policy question therefore remains:

> **How much of the upstream misogynistic information environment falls
> within the regulatory tools government is relying upon?**

---

## 🌸 Women and Girls Need to Be Visible in Safety Design

Online safety for women and girls is not simply a content-classification
problem.

Risks can involve:

-   sexual harassment;
-   impersonation;
-   doxxing;
-   stalking;
-   image-based abuse;
-   coordinated harassment;
-   unwanted sexual contact;
-   location exposure;
-   networked sexual violence.

The wider Polaris `💔_Violence_IRL/` material makes the central point:

> **digital risk can become embodied risk.**

So platform safety should ask not merely:

> `was prohibited content uploaded?`

but:

-   Can the target stop contact?
-   Can a stalker re-form accounts?
-   Can location information be easily extracted?
-   Can a swarm find and coordinate around a target?
-   Can evidence be preserved?
-   Can the user understand what happened?
-   Can safety settings be used under stress?

---

## 🧒 Children's Protections Are Not an Optional Extra

The petition specifically raises young people and anti-feminist
radicalisation.

Children may encounter:

-   dating advice;
-   masculinity content;
-   sexual material;
-   misogynistic humour;
-   influencer content;
-   grievance communities.

See [🌱 He Only Wanted Dating
Advice](./🌱_he_only_wanted_dating_advice.md).

The right question is not:

> `How do we prevent boys from ever encountering a bad idea?`

It is:

> **What should a service reasonably do when designing recommendation,
> discovery, contact and safety systems for children?**

That can include attention to:

-   age-appropriate recommendation;
-   repeated exposure;
-   unsolicited contact;
-   sexualised content;
-   reporting;
-   default settings;
-   pathways from benign interests into harmful material.

Children's safety also requires:

-   parents;
-   schools;
-   safeguarding;
-   relationships education;
-   viable prosocial alternatives.

A platform cannot supply all of those.

---

## 🥃 Do Not Moderate Male Distress Out of Existence

See [🥃 The Manosphere Ecosystem](./🥃_the_manosphere_ecosystem.md).

A crude intervention can make the problem worse.

Do not treat:

-   loneliness;
-   rejection;
-   men's mental health;
-   dating frustration;
-   masculinity;
-   custody concerns;
-   male victimisation

as prohibited ideological categories.

Some users genuinely need help.

Some communities genuinely provide it.

The relevant distinctions remain:

``` text
distress
≠
entitlement
≠
misogynistic ideology
≠
harassment
≠
violence
```

If platforms or institutions cannot make those distinctions, prosocial
speech may disappear while grievance entrepreneurs become more
attractive.

---

## 💸 Monetisation Is Part of the Safety Surface

See [🥃 The Manosphere Ecosystem](./🥃_the_manosphere_ecosystem.md).

Platforms can influence not only reach but reward.

Ask:

-   Is harmful material monetised?
-   Are creators rewarded for escalating grievance?
-   Can prohibited creators continue earning through adjacent accounts?
-   Do engagement incentives make increasingly inflammatory content
    commercially attractive?
-   Does the service distinguish between hosting and financially
    promoting material?

A platform does not need to prove that content caused a crime before
asking whether it wants to subsidise it.

> **Revenue architecture is also governance architecture.**

---

## 🧿 Safety by Design

A safer platform is not merely one with a larger moderation team.

Safety can be designed into:

-   defaults;
-   contact permissions;
-   blocking;
-   muting;
-   location handling;
-   recommendation eligibility;
-   group creation;
-   forwarding;
-   account recovery;
-   evidence preservation;
-   reporting.

This is especially important because the burden otherwise moves onto the
target.

A user experiencing harassment should not need to become:

-   a threat analyst;
-   an evidence archivist;
-   a privacy engineer;
-   a platform-policy expert

before the service becomes usable again.

> **Safety-by-design asks what predictable harm the architecture can
> prevent before a user has to report it.**

---

## 🧾 Reporting Systems Are Infrastructure

A `Report` button is not a safeguarding system by itself.

Useful reporting requires attention to:

-   accessible categories;
-   contextual evidence;
-   repeated incidents;
-   linked accounts;
-   coordinated behaviour;
-   appeals;
-   response time;
-   preservation;
-   user communication.

Networked harm can be missed when every report is treated as:

> `one post`

See [🕸️ From Online Grievance to Offline
Harm](./🕸️_from_online_grievance_to_offline_harm.md).

The system may need to recognise:

``` text
account A
+
account B
+
group C
+
repeated contact
+
location exposure
=
one developing safety problem
```

## 🔗 Information Has to Travel

A platform may hold one part of the pattern.

A school may hold another.

Police may hold another.

A survivor may be the only person who can see that the incidents are
connected.

This is not an argument for unrestricted data sharing.

It is an argument for designing lawful, proportionate routes through
which genuinely relevant safety information can reach the appropriate
decision point.

The wider `🌸_Digitally_Women/` architecture already identifies this as
a recurring Polaris plumbing problem:

> **having good policy somewhere in the system is not the same as having
> the relevant information available where a decision is being made.**

Platform regulation therefore intersects with:

-   safeguarding;
-   policing;
-   education;
-   survivor support;
-   VAWG systems.

No one institution owns the whole ecology.

---

## 🗄️ Evidence Preservation Matters

Takedown and evidence preservation are different functions.

Rapidly removing harmful material may protect users.

Deleting everything without preserving material needed for:

-   appeals;
-   safeguarding;
-   criminal investigation;
-   network analysis

can create another problem.

The existing digitally networked sexual violence material raises
precisely this tension.

A mature safety system needs to think about:

``` text
stop circulation
      +
protect target
      +
preserve what legitimately needs preserving
```

subject to appropriate legal and privacy safeguards.

## 🔬 Research and Data Access

Public debate about platform harms is often forced to operate with:

-   screenshots;
-   anecdotes;
-   small test accounts;
-   externally visible metrics.

But the questions we actually want answered may concern:

-   recommendation pathways;
-   prevalence;
-   repeated exposure;
-   age differences;
-   network migration;
-   creator incentives;
-   enforcement outcomes.

See [👠 You Are Not Getting the Same
Feed](./👠_you_are_not_getting_the_same_feed.md).

Researchers need enough access to test claims about the system rather
than merely inspect isolated posts.

Platforms also need appropriate protection for:

-   privacy;
-   security;
-   user data;
-   legitimate commercial confidentiality.

The answer is not `release everything`.

It is:

> **create credible routes for independent scrutiny of claims that
> cannot be tested from outside the system.**

---

## 📊 Measure Outcomes, Not Announcements

A policy can exist while the problem persists.

Useful evaluation questions include:

-   Did harmful recommendation pathways change?
-   Did reporting become faster or more accurate?
-   Did repeat harassment decline?
-   Did children encounter less age-inappropriate material?
-   Did coordinated networks simply migrate?
-   Did false positives rise?
-   Did users lose access to benign support?
-   Did survivor confidence improve?
-   Did enforcement become more consistent?

Compliance paperwork is evidence of process.

It is not automatically evidence of safety.

## 🧠 Platform Governance Is Not Criminal Sentencing

See [🧠 Radicalisation Is Not a Sentencing
Factor](./🧠_radicalisation_is_not_a_sentencing_factor.md).

Platform governance can legitimately operate on a different threshold
from criminal punishment.

A service may decide:

> `we will not recommend this category of material to children`

without claiming:

> `the creator has committed a criminal offence`

Likewise, a regulator may assess systemic risk without proving that a
particular recommendation caused a particular crime.

Keep the layers distinct:

``` text
criminal law:
what conduct is prohibited?

platform policy:
what will this service host,
recommend or monetise?

regulation:
what systems and duties
must the service maintain?

safeguarding:
what intervention protects
a person before harm escalates?
```

Conflating these layers makes all four worse.

## 📣 Lawful Misogyny Is Still a Governance Question

One of the difficult spaces is content that is:

-   lawful;
-   misogynistic;
-   degrading;
-   ideologically hostile;
-   potentially attractive to young audiences.

Criminal prohibition is not the only possible response.

Other tools can include:

-   recommendation limits;
-   age-appropriate design;
-   demonetisation;
-   user controls;
-   counter-speech;
-   friction;
-   media literacy;
-   education;
-   prosocial alternatives.

The existence of lawful misogyny is therefore not proof that
online-safety policy has nothing to say.

It is proof that **criminal law is not the whole intervention surface**.

---

## 📣 Nor Should `Harm` Become an Unlimited Censorship Word

The opposite mistake is equally serious.

If `harmful` means:

> `anything somebody considers socially undesirable`

then the category becomes impossible to govern fairly.

Platforms and regulators need:

-   defined risks;
-   evidence;
-   proportionate interventions;
-   procedural safeguards;
-   transparency;
-   routes of challenge.

This matters especially for:

-   sexual speech;
-   feminist speech;
-   queer speech;
-   survivor testimony;
-   political dissent;
-   discussion of violence;
-   minority communities.

Safety systems can themselves create harm when context disappears.

> **The answer to blunt moderation is not no moderation. It is better
> specification.**

Women are:

-   users;

-   moderators;
-   researchers;
-   developers;
-   activists;

-   sex workers;
-   journalists;
-   platform workers;
-   safety experts.

Online-safety policy should not imagine women only as passive recipients

-   safety practices;

## 🛠️ What Platforms Can Reasonably Be Asked to Do

A useful minimum list:

1.  **Understand recommendation pathways**, including for children.
2.  **Distinguish hosting from amplification and monetisation.**
3.  **Reduce foreseeable design-enabled abuse**, including repeated
    unwanted contact and location exposure.
4.  **Provide usable blocking, muting and reporting systems.**
5.  **Recognise coordinated and networked harm**, not merely isolated
    posts.
6.  **Preserve relevant evidence appropriately when harmful material is
    removed.**
7.  **Give users meaningful information about enforcement and appeals.**
8.  **Enable credible independent research and regulatory scrutiny.**
9.  **Measure whether interventions actually reduce harm.**
10. **Design for the people most likely to bear the cost when systems
    fail.**

This is substantial.

It is not omnipotence.

---

## 🚫 What Platforms Cannot Do Alone

Platforms cannot independently provide:

-   healthy relationships;
-   friendship;
-   housing;
-   economic security;
-   good schools;
-   competent policing;
-   trauma care;
-   sexual education;
-   community;
-   functioning safeguarding;
-   gender equality.

They cannot abolish:

-   misogyny;
-   racism;
-   fascism;
-   loneliness;
-   domestic abuse;
-   criminal violence.

Trying to make platform regulation carry all of those problems can
produce two failures at once:

-   impossible expectations for platforms;
-   political neglect of institutions that actually hold the missing
    powers.

> **Do not outsource social policy to the content-moderation queue.**

The strongest version of online safety is not merely:

> `what should be removed?`

It is also:

> **what healthier pathways should exist?**

See:

-   [🌞 We Could Build Nicer
    Things](../📖_Previously_On_The_Internet/🌞_we_could_build_nicer_things.md).

A platform environment can make it easier to find:

-   competent relationship advice;
-   supportive communities;
-   accurate sexual education;
-   non-dominating models of masculinity;
-   survivor resources;
-   accessible moderation;
-   healthier forms of belonging.

Safety is partly subtractive.

It can also be constructive.

## 🇬🇧 The Policy Question for the Petition

The cluster's research notes identify a genuine policy mismatch.

The government can point to:

-   criminal-law reform;
-   the VAWG strategy;
-   the Online Safety Act;
-   Ofcom.

Those interventions matter.

But the petition describes a wider chain:

``` text
misogynistic information environment
        ↓
normalisation / radicalisation
        ↓
behavioural change
        ↓
escalating risk
        ↓
harassment / abuse / violence
```

The unresolved question is therefore not:

> `has government done anything?`

It plainly has.

The sharper question is:

> **Does the combined system recognise and interrupt the problem across
> the whole pathway, or do the interventions remain strongest once
> content is illegal, a child-protection duty is engaged, or an offence
> has already occurred?**

That is the question worth carrying into the wider `🌸_Digitally_Women/`
policy cluster.

## 📲 The Platform Is Part of the Ecology

> **Platforms are not neutral pipes.**
>
> They rank.
>
> They recommend.
>
> They monetise.
>
> They design contact.
>
> They set friction.
>
> They preserve or lose evidence.
>
> They decide what becomes easy to find.
>
> Those are real powers and legitimate objects of regulation.
>
> But platforms are not substitutes for families, schools, communities,
> safeguarding systems, police, courts, healthcare or politics.
>
> **Ask platforms to govern the infrastructure they actually control.
> Then make the rest of the system do its job too.**
