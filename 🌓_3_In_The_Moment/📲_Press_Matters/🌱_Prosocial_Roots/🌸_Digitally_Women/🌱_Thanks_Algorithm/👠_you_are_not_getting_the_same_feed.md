# 👠 You Are Not Getting the Same Feed

**First created:** 2026-09-06 \| **Last updated:** 2026-09-06\
*Sometimes people disagree about what is happening online because they
are not actually observing the same environment.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Women keep explaining this because other people genuinely are not
experiencing the same environment.

Two people can use the same platform.

They can live in the same city.

They can work in the same institution.

They can know some of the same people.

And they can still encounter materially different information about:

-   misogyny;
-   sexual threat;
-   coercive behaviour;
-   harassment;
-   dating;
-   relationships;
-   safety;
-   masculinity;
-   gendered violence.

Sometimes this is because their feeds are literally personalised.

Sometimes it is because their social networks carry different
information.

Sometimes it is because people behave differently around them.

Sometimes previous experience changes what they recognise.

Usually several of these are operating at once.

The resulting epistemic gap matters for journalism, policy, safeguarding
and platform governance.

> **"I haven't seen this" is not the same proposition as "this isn't
> happening."**

**Question answered:** Why can two people sincerely look at "the
internet" and report very different environments?

---

## 👠 You Are Literally Not Getting the Same Feed

On personalised platforms, `the feed` is not one stable public object.

Different users may encounter different material because of combinations
of:

-   accounts followed;
-   previous viewing;
-   searches;
-   likes;
-   comments;
-   shares;
-   watch time;
-   social connections;
-   inferred interests;
-   recommendation systems;
-   platform experimentation;
-   location and language;
-   the communities through which they entered the platform.

The precise mechanisms vary by service and change over time.

The basic consequence does not.

> **Two people opening the same app are not necessarily observing the
> same information environment.**

This creates a problem for ordinary reasoning.

People are accustomed to treating media objects as shared.

If two people buy the same newspaper, they can compare the same front
page.

If two people watch the same television bulletin, they can discuss the
same sequence of stories.

A personalised feed is different.

The interface can look shared while the informational environment is
individualised.

---

## 🪞 Your Feed Also Learns From You

Personalisation is not simply a platform assigning people to demographic
boxes.

Users interact with systems.

Systems respond to those interactions.

The resulting environment can therefore emerge through feedback:

``` text
person encounters content
          ↓
person responds
          ↓
system observes behaviour
          ↓
system selects more content
          ↓
person encounters a changed environment
          ↓
new behaviour produces more signals
```

That does not mean the system perfectly understands the person.

It means behaviour can affect subsequent exposure.

Small differences in:

-   curiosity;
-   watch time;
-   follows;
-   searches;
-   social connections;
-   previous exposure

can produce increasingly different informational surroundings.

So even people of the same age, gender and location should not be
assumed to inhabit identical feeds.

## 💾 You Are Not Getting the Same Social Internet Either

Algorithms are only one layer.

People also inhabit different social networks.

See:

`../📖_Previously_On_The_Internet/💾_guide_to_girl_internet.md`

Women-heavy digital cultures can contain information exchanges that
remain substantially invisible outside them.

For example:

-   screenshots of messages;
-   warnings about particular behaviour;
-   discussions of dates;
-   accounts of harassment;
-   relationship advice;
-   safety information;
-   private jokes carrying shared context;
-   receipts;
-   stories about workplace behaviour;
-   friends processing coercive experiences;
-   discussions of patterns that have appeared repeatedly.

Some of this happens publicly.

Some happens in:

-   group chats;
-   DMs;
-   private accounts;
-   closed communities;
-   disappearing stories;
-   friendship networks.

A person outside those networks may never encounter it.

This is one reason gossip should not automatically be treated as
informationally empty.

Gossip can be wrong.

It can be malicious.

It can reproduce prejudice.

It can become a pile-on.

It can also be one of the mechanisms through which communities exchange
information about behaviour that formal systems have failed to record.

> **Informal information is not automatically reliable. It is also not
> automatically meaningless.**

## 🧾 Receipts Change the Information Ecology

Digital communication creates unusual combinations of informal testimony
and durable artefact.

A conversation may produce:

-   screenshots;
-   timestamps;
-   message histories;
-   photographs;
-   screen recordings;
-   links;
-   archived posts.

This does not eliminate interpretation.

Screenshots can omit context.

Material can be manipulated.

People can misunderstand what they saw.

But it means some forms of social knowledge can travel with supporting
material attached.

This is part of what `Girl Internet` sometimes means by **receipts**.

The result can look strange to somebody encountering only the final
screenshot bundle.

To the people inside the network, it may represent the accumulated
record of a much longer process.

## 👥 Group-Chat Epistemology

A group chat is not a court.

It is not a newsroom.

It is not a research institute.

It can still be an information-processing environment.

One person reports an experience.

Another recognises something similar.

Somebody supplies a screenshot.

Somebody remembers an earlier incident.

Another person disagrees.

Someone adds context.

Someone notices that apparently isolated events may involve the same
behaviour.

This can produce:

``` text
individual observation
          ↓
comparison
          ↓
corroboration / disagreement
          ↓
pattern recognition
          ↓
collective interpretation
```

Sometimes the pattern is real.

Sometimes it isn't.

The important point is that **collective testimony can reveal
information unavailable from one vantage point**.

That is ordinary distributed knowledge production.

Women did not invent it.

Women-heavy networks use it.

## 🚶 You Are Not Getting the Same Offline Feed Either

The epistemic gap does not begin with recommendation systems.

People can inhabit the same physical environment differently.

A street is not experienced identically by everybody walking down it.

Neither is:

-   a workplace;
-   a university;
-   a pub;
-   a conference;
-   public transport;
-   a political organisation;
-   a dating environment.

Different people may encounter different:

-   sexualised attention;
-   threats;
-   propositions;
-   coercion;
-   stalking;
-   intimidation;
-   boundary testing;
-   comments;
-   expectations.

People also behave differently depending on who is present.

A man saying sincerely:

> `I've never heard him speak to women like that.`

tells us something.

It tells us how that person has behaved **within that observer's field
of view**.

It does not settle how he behaves:

-   on dates;
-   in DMs;
-   after rejection;
-   around women without male peers present;
-   inside a relationship;
-   when he believes there will be no social consequence.

> **Absence from one observer's field of view is not absence from the
> system.**

## 🧠 Experience Changes What Becomes Salient

People also notice different things.

Someone who has repeatedly encountered coercive behaviour may recognise
a pattern earlier than somebody who has not.

Someone who has never needed to think about a particular risk may not
register the same cue as significant.

This does not make either person omniscient.

Experience can sharpen perception.

Experience can also create bias.

The answer is not to declare one vantage point inherently correct.

It is to recognise that **position changes available information**.

That matters when institutions compare:

``` text
"I do not see a pattern"
```

with:

``` text
"people repeatedly exposed to this environment
are reporting a pattern"
```

Those statements require investigation.

They should not be collapsed into a popularity contest between
perceptions.

## 👠 "But I Don't See This" Is Data About Your Vantage Point

If a woman says misogynistic material is ubiquitous in her environment
and a man says he rarely encounters it, those observations do not
necessarily contradict each other.

They may be describing different information environments.

Likewise:

-   a parent may not see the feed their child sees;
-   a teacher may not see the group chat;
-   a journalist may not receive the recommendations their interviewee
    receives;
-   a platform researcher may not inhabit the target community;
-   a regulator may see public posts but not DMs;
-   a male colleague may never witness behaviour women discuss
    repeatedly among themselves.

So:

> **"I haven't seen it" can be useful evidence about the observer.**

It can tell us:

-   which environment they occupy;
-   what is visible there;
-   what is not;
-   where the information boundary may sit.

The mistake is converting that limited observation into:

> `therefore it is not happening`

## 🐇 Men Are Not Getting One Feed Either

This is not a claim that:

``` text
women see misogyny
men do not
```

Reality is much messier.

See:

`../📖_Previously_On_The_Internet/🐇_rabbitholes_for_the_boys.md`

Men and boys also inhabit different informational environments.

One boy may receive:

-   football;
-   games;
-   comedy;
-   Warhammer;
-   music.

Another may begin with:

-   gym advice;
-   dating;
-   money;
-   self-improvement;

and encounter increasingly status-heavy or grievance-oriented

Another may deliberately seek political material.

Another may never see much of it.

Demographic category is not destiny.

The relevant concept is **differential exposure**.

## 🌱 The Roots and the Feed Are Different Layers

See:

`./🌱_misogyny_is_in_the_roots.md`

Misogyny does not begin with recommendation.

The surrounding culture already contains gendered assumptions.

People enter platforms with different:

-   histories;
-   relationships;
-   beliefs;
-   vulnerabilities;
-   interests;
-   social networks.

Then:

``` text
ambient cultural material
          ↓
different people encounter
different communities and experiences
          ↓
they interact with different material
          ↓
personalisation responds to those interactions
          ↓
different informational environments emerge
          ↓
people looking at "the internet"
can sincerely report different realities
```

The algorithm is therefore neither the whole explanation nor an
irrelevant technical layer.

It can participate in determining **which part of the wider culture
becomes somebody's immediate environment**.

## 🔬 Collective Testimony Is Not Collective Omniscience

Women's testimony should not be dismissed because another observer has
not personally encountered the same pattern.

That does not require treating women as infallible.

Women's networks can contain:

-   mistakes;
-   rumours;
-   prejudice;
-   motivated reasoning;
-   social pressure;
-   misidentification;
-   pile-ons;
-   false inference.

So can every other human information network.

The stronger proposition is:

> **Repeated observations from differently positioned participants can
> reveal a pattern unavailable from any one vantage point.**

Collective testimony is evidence to investigate.

It should not be dismissed merely because the investigator's personal
environment looks different.

Nor should the word `collective` magically remove the need to:

-   verify;
-   contextualise;
-   distinguish observation from inference;
-   test competing explanations.

The point is epistemic inclusion, not epistemic sainthood.

## 📰 The Journalism Problem

This creates a methodological problem for journalism.

Imagine a reporter researching online misogyny through:

-   a fresh account;
-   their professional social-media feed;
-   search engines;
-   public posts;
-   interviews with prominent organisations.

They may genuinely not be looking at the environment being described.

Relevant material may instead appear through:

-   personalised recommendation sequences;
-   comments;
-   DMs;
-   burner accounts;
-   private groups;
-   Discords;
-   group chats;
-   deleted posts;
-   transient stories;
-   screenshots;
-   niche creators;
-   repeated exposure over time.

A snapshot can therefore miss a pathway.

A search can miss recommendation.

A public account can miss private behaviour.

A journalist's own feed can miss the feed central to the story.

This does not mean journalists should impersonate vulnerable people
recklessly or treat every screenshot as verified evidence.

It means the research design should match the object being investigated.

> **You cannot report a personalised information environment as though
> everybody received the same newspaper.**

## 🏛️ The Policy Problem

The same problem applies to policy.

A policymaker can sincerely open a platform and see nothing resembling
the environment being described by:

-   teenage girls;
-   teenage boys;
-   survivors;
-   teachers;
-   moderators;
-   researchers;
-   particular minority communities.

That observation cannot resolve the question.

A serious investigation may need combinations of:

-   platform data;
-   qualitative testimony;
-   longitudinal observation;
-   multiple test personas;
-   participatory research;
-   recommendation-pathway analysis;
-   attention to comments and private channels where lawful and
    appropriate;
-   demographic and contextual exposure research;
-   preservation of transient material;
-   examination of how feeds change after particular interactions.

The methodological principle is simple:

> **You cannot audit a personalised system exclusively from outside the
> populations whose experiences you are trying to understand.**

Participation does not replace independent scrutiny.

It improves the map of what needs scrutinising.

## 🧪 Test the Pathway, Not Just the Post

Content moderation often asks whether a particular object violates a
rule.

That matters.

Recommendation research may require another question:

> **How did somebody get here?**

A pathway can contain individually ordinary pieces:

``` text
fitness
   ↓
dating
   ↓
masculinity
   ↓
status
   ↓
gender grievance
```

Looking only at the final post loses information about the route.

Likewise, examining a single recommendation does not necessarily
establish a systemic pattern.

Useful investigation may require:

-   sequence;
-   repetition;
-   timing;
-   adjacent recommendations;
-   user interaction;
-   counterfactual accounts;
-   changes over time.

The unit of analysis may need to become **the pathway**, not merely the
content item.

## 🧭 Four Different Questions

When people disagree about an online phenomenon, separate at least four
questions:

### 1. Does the material exist?

A content question.

### 2. Who encounters it?

An exposure question.

### 3. How do they encounter it?

A distribution and pathway question.

### 4. What happens after repeated exposure?

A behavioural, social and causal question.

Evidence for one does not automatically answer the others.

A screenshot can demonstrate existence.

It does not by itself establish prevalence.

A prevalence estimate does not by itself establish algorithmic
causation.

A recommendation pathway does not by itself establish behavioural
effect.

Precision here makes the argument stronger.

## 👠 Same Platform Does Not Mean Same Reality

The most important correction is small.

When two people report different experiences of the same platform, do
not begin by assuming one of them must be lying, exaggerating or failing
to understand the service.

First ask whether they were actually exposed to the same environment.

They may differ in:

-   feed;
-   network;
-   community;
-   history;
-   account age;
-   interactions;
-   private messages;
-   embodied experience;
-   risk;
-   social position.

That produces a different starting point for inquiry:

``` text
Person A: "I see this constantly."

Person B: "I never see this."

Bad inference:
one of them must be wrong

Better question:
what differs between their information environments?
```

That question is answerable.

And it is considerably more useful.

## 👠 YOU ARE NOT GETTING THE SAME FEED

The contemporary internet creates an unusual epistemic problem.

People can occupy interfaces that look almost identical while receiving
different information, inhabiting different communities and accumulating
different evidence about the world around them.

Offline life already worked partly like this.

Personalisation makes the difference technically explicit.

So when somebody says:

> `I don't see that.`

The appropriate next question is often:

> **What are you seeing?**

Then compare environments.

Compare pathways.

Compare networks.

Compare observations.

Investigate the difference.

> ## **YOU ARE NOT GETTING THE SAME FEED.**
>
> And sometimes the disagreement about what is happening online begins
> with the mistaken assumption that you are.
