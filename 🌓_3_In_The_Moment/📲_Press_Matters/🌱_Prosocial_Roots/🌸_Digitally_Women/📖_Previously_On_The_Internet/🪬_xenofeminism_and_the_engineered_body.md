# 🪬 Xenofeminism and the Engineered Body
**First created:** 2026-09-05 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-05  
*If nature is not destiny, who gets to alter bodies, how, and under whose authority?*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

A great deal of politics quietly relies on a sentence nobody quite says aloud:

**this is how bodies are, therefore this is how life must be organised.**

Xenofeminism is useful because it refuses that move.

Bodies are material. Biology is real. Pain is real. Pregnancy is real. Disability is real. Hormones do things. Medicines do things. Technologies have limits.

None of that means the present arrangement possesses moral authority merely because somebody can call it natural.

The political question begins after material reality has been acknowledged:

> **What are we allowed to change?**

And immediately after that:

> **Who is "we"?**

This node is therefore not an argument that every body should be engineered.

It is about the politics that appears once we recognise that bodies, environments and the boundary between them are already technologically mediated — and that some people have far more authority than others over what may be altered.

**Question answered:** If "natural" does not mean "good", who gets to decide what should be changed?

---

## 🪬 Xenofeminism: A Politics for Alienation

Laboria Cuboniks published *Xenofeminism: A Politics for Alienation* in 2015.

The manifesto describes xenofeminism through commitments including:

- techno-materialism;
- antinaturalism;
- gender abolitionism;
- intersectional and universalist political ambition;
- repurposing existing technologies rather than waiting for politically pure tools to arrive.

Its provocation is not that technology will automatically save anyone.

It is that technological and material systems are already political terrain.

If a system has been constructed, maintained, classified, engineered or institutionalised, it can potentially be:

- understood;
- contested;
- hacked;
- repurposed;
- redesigned.

Alienation is therefore not treated simply as something from which politics must retreat.

What is alien can sometimes become usable.

---

## 🌱 Antinaturalism Is Not a War on Nature

The easiest way to misunderstand xenofeminism is to hear **antinaturalism** and imagine hostility toward:

- bodies;
- ecology;
- biology;
- material limits.

That is not the useful distinction.

The target is the inference:

```text
this is natural
      ↓
therefore this is normal
      ↓
therefore this is desirable
      ↓
therefore this should determine social organisation
```

Those propositions do not follow automatically.

Pregnancy may be natural.

So are:

- infection;
- infertility;
- pain;
- ageing;
- poor eyesight;
- death in childbirth.

Human beings have spent an extraordinary amount of history altering natural conditions.

We use:

- glasses;
- anaesthesia;
- antibiotics;
- contraception;
- Caesarean sections;
- vaccines;
- insulin;
- pacemakers;
- prostheses;
- hormones.

The politically interesting question is not whether intervention is artificial.

Almost everything about modern embodied life already contains artifice.

The question is:

> **Who gets access to intervention, who controls it, and whose desired change is treated as legitimate?**

---

## 🧬 The Engineered Body Is Already Here

"The engineered body" can sound futuristic because technological culture likes chrome.

Most bodily engineering is considerably less cinematic.

A person wearing glasses has technologically altered the relationship between body and environment.

So has somebody using:

- a wheelchair;
- an insulin pump;
- a hearing aid;
- an IUD;
- testosterone;
- oestrogen;
- a prosthetic limb;
- a cardiac implant;
- orthodontics;
- a tattoo;
- an AAC device;
- a nerve stimulator;
- fertility treatment;
- cosmetic surgery.

These interventions are not politically identical.

That is the point.

Society already accepts extensive technological mediation of bodies while drawing highly variable lines around:

- necessity;
- vanity;
- health;
- disability;
- gender;
- reproduction;
- sexuality;
- enhancement.

Those lines are not supplied by technology itself.

They are social decisions.

---

## 🩺 Who Gets to Say What the Body Needs?

Once bodily alteration enters medicine, another actor appears:

**the gatekeeper.**

Medical authority can provide essential:

- expertise;
- safety;
- diagnosis;
- monitoring;
- treatment.

It can also decide whether a person's desired change is:

- necessary;
- elective;
- pathological;
- cosmetic;
- premature;
- inappropriate;
- insufficiently evidenced.

This creates a difficult problem.

A person may possess the strongest possible knowledge of:

- what hurts;
- what they can tolerate;
- what function they need;
- what change they want.

A clinician may possess specialist knowledge of:

- anatomy;
- pharmacology;
- risk;
- contraindications;
- treatment outcomes.

Neither form of knowledge becomes useless because the other exists.

The problem appears when one form of expertise is treated as the only form capable of producing legitimate knowledge.

See [🕹️ Cyberfeminism Before the Algorithm](./🕹️_cyberfeminism_before_the_algorithm.md) for Sandy Stone's account of medical gatekeeping and the feedback loops created when patients must learn the narrative institutions require.

---

## ♻️ Permission Changes the Person Asking for Permission

Medical gatekeeping is not merely a door.

It can change behaviour upstream of the door.

If access depends upon demonstrating:

- sufficient pain;
- sufficient impairment;
- sufficient stability;
- sufficient gender conformity;
- sufficient infertility;
- sufficient weight loss;
- sufficient psychiatric acceptability;

people learn what the system rewards.

This does not mean they are lying.

It means institutional criteria become part of the environment in which people learn how to describe themselves.

```text
need
 ↓
eligibility criteria
 ↓
self-presentation
 ↓
institutional interpretation
 ↓
access or refusal
```

The body being assessed is therefore not separate from the system assessing it.

Classification becomes intervention.

---

## 🏥 Necessary for Whom?

"Medically necessary" sounds objective.

Sometimes it is relatively straightforward.

Sometimes it contains a political judgement about what kinds of life are worth supporting.

Consider the difference between asking:

**Will this treatment keep the patient alive?**

and:

**Will this treatment allow the patient to live the life they actually have?**

Function can include:

- walking;
- sleeping;
- communicating;
- working;
- parenting;
- having sex;
- concentrating;
- leaving the house;
- participating in community;
- being recognised as oneself.

A body is not merely a collection of laboratory values.

Medical necessity becomes more humane when the definition of health can accommodate the person's actual life.

---

## ⚧️ Trans Healthcare Makes the Politics Visible

Trans healthcare exposes the instability of the natural/artificial distinction unusually clearly.

Hormones alter bodies.

So do:

- puberty;
- pregnancy;
- menopause;
- endocrine disorders;
- contraceptives;
- cancer treatment;
- ageing.

Surgery alters bodies.

So do:

- accidents;
- childbirth;
- dentistry;
- reconstruction;
- weight change;
- disease.

The politically interesting distinction is therefore not:

**unaltered natural body / technologically altered trans body.**

There is no generally unmediated body waiting on the other side of that slash.

The questions are about:

- autonomy;
- informed consent;
- evidence;
- safety;
- access;
- gatekeeping;
- social recognition.

Technological mediation does not make a body less real.

---

## 🧷 Authenticity Is a Strange Standard for Medicine

Medicine is full of interventions designed to make bodies unlike the condition in which nature left them.

Nobody normally asks whether:

- a repaired cleft palate;
- an artificial hip;
- corrected vision;
- insulin-treated diabetes

is sufficiently authentic.

Yet bodily interventions associated with:

- gender;
- sexuality;
- reproduction;
- appearance

often attract a peculiar demand to justify whether the resulting body is "real".

That is not a technical question.

It is a cultural one wearing a laboratory coat.

---

## 🦾 Disability Technology: Engineer the Person or the World?

Disability politics creates one of the most important constraints on technological utopianism.

Sometimes changing the body is exactly what somebody wants.

Sometimes changing the environment is.

Sometimes both.

A wheelchair can alter the person's mobility.

A ramp alters the environment.

Neither makes sense independently of:

- streets;
- buildings;
- transport;
- employment;
- social support.

The wrong question is:

**Should disability be cured or accommodated?**

That forces enormously different people and impairments into one ideological answer.

Better questions are:

- What does this person want?
- What causes pain or restriction?
- What can technology change?
- What should the environment change?
- What support remains necessary?
- Who controls the decision?

> **Do not make people earn access by agreeing with somebody else's philosophy of what their body ought to become.**

---

## ♿ Antinaturalism Needs Disability Justice

Antinaturalism supplies an important freedom:

**the natural body is not sacred.**

Disability justice supplies an equally important limit:

**neither is the optimised body.**

Without the first principle, people can be told to accept suffering because alteration is unnatural.

Without the second, people can be told that difference itself is a technical failure awaiting correction.

Both positions can erase the person.

A serious emancipatory technological politics must protect:

- the freedom to seek alteration;
- the freedom to refuse alteration;
- the resources required to live either way.

> **Technological emancipation must not quietly become technological normalisation.**

---

## 🫀 Interdependence Is Not Technical Failure

Technological culture often imagines successful engineering as independence.

But human beings are radically interdependent.

We depend upon:

- food systems;
- energy systems;
- medicine;
- roads;
- communications;
- other people;
- ecosystems;
- institutions.

Some forms of dependence are simply so normalised that they disappear from view.

The wheelchair user is described as dependent.

The driver using roads, fuel infrastructure, satellite navigation and an international manufacturing system often is not.

That asymmetry tells us something about what society has decided to notice.

Technology should not merely ask how to make people independent.

It should ask how to make **interdependence safe, dignified and reliable**.

---

## 🧫 Reproductive Technology: Firestone's Question Becomes Less Hypothetical

Reproductive technology is one of the clearest places where the old feminist argument about nature becomes material infrastructure.

Technology already intervenes in:

- contraception;
- conception;
- fertility;
- pregnancy;
- fetal imaging;
- childbirth;
- neonatal survival.

IVF alone separates processes once treated as one natural sequence into multiple:

- clinical;
- laboratory;
- legal;
- financial;
- reproductive

events.

That creates extraordinary possibilities.

It also creates new sites of control.

Ask:

- Who can access fertility treatment?
- Who pays?
- Whose gametes can be used?
- Who owns stored embryos?
- What happens across borders?
- Who performs reproductive labour?
- What information is generated?
- Who receives authority when technologies make previously invisible processes visible?

Technology does not remove reproductive politics.

It gives reproductive politics new machinery.

---

## 🩻 Seeing Is Also an Intervention

Ultrasound is a good example.

It provides valuable medical information.

It also produces an image that can appear to make the fetus independently visible, while the pregnant body and the apparatus producing the image recede from view.

The technology therefore does more than reveal.

It changes:

- representation;
- attention;
- political language;
- perceived agency.

This is a recurring rule:

> **A technology that makes something visible can redistribute authority around what has been seen.**

See the wider [Observation Becomes Intervention](../../../../../../🌑_1_Origin_Points/🪿_Embodied_Information_Ecology/♻️🧿_Observation_Becomes_Intervention/) cluster for the general problem.

---

## 🧪 Hormones Are Technology Too

Hormonal technologies are so ordinary that their technological character can disappear.

They include interventions associated with:

- contraception;
- fertility;
- menopause;
- endocrine disease;
- gender transition;
- cancer;
- menstrual suppression.

The same molecular or physiological system can therefore be described through very different political registers depending upon:

- who wants the intervention;
- why;
- at what age;
- for which body;
- under which diagnosis.

This makes hormones particularly useful for examining the difference between:

- pharmacological risk;
- moral discomfort;
- political controversy.

Those are not interchangeable categories.

---

## 💄 Beauty, Modification and the Suspiciously Selective Natural Body

Modern societies tolerate a remarkable amount of deliberate bodily modification.

Consider:

- hair dye;
- shaving;
- orthodontics;
- tattoos;
- piercings;
- bodybuilding;
- cosmetic dentistry;
- injectables;
- cosmetic surgery;
- laser hair removal.

Some interventions are treated as ordinary grooming.

Others are medicalised.

Others are moralised.

Others are celebrated as self-improvement.

The distinction cannot be explained by "natural versus artificial".

The more useful questions are:

- What norm is being enforced?
- Is the person choosing freely?
- What social penalty attaches to refusal?
- What risks are being accepted?
- Who profits?
- Is the modification reversible?
- Does access depend on wealth?
- Who gets called vain, disordered or empowered?

Choice occurs inside culture.

That does not make choice unreal.

It means the environment around choice belongs in the analysis.

---

## 🪞 There Is No Pure Choice Outside Society

One bad response to bodily norms is to insist that any choice shaped by society is therefore false.

That standard would invalidate almost every human preference.

People choose inside:

- families;
- markets;
- beauty standards;
- religions;
- subcultures;
- gender systems;
- medical systems;
- peer groups.

The relevant political task is not to discover a mythical preference untouched by society.

It is to improve the conditions under which people can:

- understand options;
- refuse;
- consent;
- change their minds;
- access resources;
- survive the consequences.

Autonomy is not isolation from influence.

It is meaningful agency within a world where influence is unavoidable.

---

## 📱 Femtech: When the Body Becomes a Dataset

Menstruation, fertility, pregnancy and sexual-health technologies can provide people with information that was previously:

- difficult to collect;
- inconvenient to track;
- dependent on clinical access.

That can be genuinely useful.

It can also transform intimate bodily processes into:

- accounts;
- databases;
- behavioural profiles;
- commercial assets.

The political question is therefore not whether tracking is empowering or exploitative.

It can be either, both, or neither depending on the system.

Ask:

- What data is collected?
- Where is it stored?
- Who can access it?
- What is inferred?
- What happens if the company is sold?
- Can the user meaningfully delete it?
- Does intimate data cross into advertising, insurance, employment, policing or legal processes?

The technologically legible body is also a governable body.

---

## 🔐 Privacy Is Bodily Infrastructure

Privacy is sometimes discussed as though it were an abstract digital preference.

For embodied life, it can be infrastructure.

Privacy can determine whether somebody can safely:

- seek healthcare;
- explore sexuality;
- transition;
- obtain reproductive information;
- leave an abusive relationship;
- organise politically;
- work;
- communicate.

The body does not stop at the skin when the systems governing the body depend upon data.

A politics of engineered bodies therefore needs a politics of:

- databases;
- identity;
- authentication;
- access logs;
- retention;
- inference.

---

## 💳 Follow the Payment Rails

Bodily autonomy also depends on material resources.

A treatment can be legally available and practically inaccessible.

A person can be legally permitted to work while being unable to:

- bank;
- advertise;
- receive payment;
- use ordinary platforms.

This is why the sex-work discussion in [🕹️ Cyberfeminism Before the Algorithm](./🕹️_cyberfeminism_before_the_algorithm.md) belongs to the same political problem.

The engineered body is not merely the body under a surgeon's hand.

It is the body whose options are shaped by:

- money;
- payment infrastructure;
- employment;
- housing;
- insurance;
- public services.

> **Bodily autonomy without material resources can become autonomy in theory only.**

---

## 🧷 Do Not White-Knight People Out of Their Essential Resources

This principle travels.

A protective intervention can remove:

- income;
- healthcare;
- housing;
- community;
- mobility;
- information;
- digital access;
- safer working conditions.

Good intentions do not replenish those resources.

This is particularly important where policy concerns people already described as:

- vulnerable;
- marginalised;
- exploited;
- at risk.

Those descriptions can become reasons to listen less precisely to the person concerned.

That is backwards.

> **The more consequential the intervention, the more important the knowledge of the people carrying its consequences becomes.**

Do not white-knight people out of the things keeping them alive.

---

## 🧠 Enhancement: Better According to Whom?

Transhumanist and enhancement debates make the normative problem explicit.

Suppose technology can improve:

- memory;
- strength;
- endurance;
- lifespan;
- attention;
- sensory capacity.

Improvement still requires a standard.

Better for:

- what task?
- which environment?
- whose values?
- at what cost?
- with what side effects?

A trait useful in one environment may be irrelevant or costly in another.

Once enhancement becomes competitive, another problem appears.

An optional technology can become functionally compulsory when:

- employers expect it;
- schools reward it;
- militaries require it;
- insurers price around it;
- peers adopt it.

The freedom to enhance therefore needs a companion freedom:

> **the freedom not to be made obsolete for refusing.**

---

## 🤖 AI and the Body

Generative AI can appear disembodied because its most visible outputs are:

- text;
- images;
- audio;
- code.

Its consequences are not.

AI increasingly intersects with embodied systems through:

- healthcare;
- diagnosis;
- triage;
- assistive technology;
- accessibility;
- reproductive information;
- biometric classification;
- employment;
- insurance;
- sexual content;
- identity verification.

It can help translate, describe, communicate and navigate.

It can also encode somebody else's model of:

- normality;
- risk;
- credibility;
- attractiveness;
- sex;
- gender;
- disability.

The old cyberfeminist question therefore returns in a new form:

> **Whose body is inside the model of the world?**

---

## 🧬 Biotech Makes the Governance Question Harder

As biotechnology expands, the line between:

- treatment;
- prevention;
- selection;
- enhancement

can become difficult to maintain.

Genetic and reproductive technologies intensify old questions:

- What counts as disease?
- What counts as difference?
- What should be prevented?
- Who gets to decide?
- What happens when parental choice becomes population pattern?
- What happens when markets decide which traits are valuable?

Disability justice is indispensable here.

A technology capable of reducing suffering may be welcome.

A society capable of deciding that certain people should cease to exist is something else entirely.

Those propositions must not be lazily collapsed into either:

**all intervention is eugenic**

or:

**all technological prevention is progress.**

Precision matters.

---

## ⚖️ The Difference Between Choice and Programme

This distinction is essential.

There is a political difference between:

- an individual choosing an intervention for their own body;

and:

- an institution deciding which bodies should be altered.

There is a further difference between:

- making an option available;

and:

- arranging society so refusal carries unacceptable penalties.

The same technology can participate in:

- autonomy;
- coercion;
- care;
- normalisation;

depending on the surrounding system.

So ask not merely:

**What can this technology do?**

Ask:

**What relationship does this system create between the person, the technology and everybody with power over access to it?**

---

## 💸 Political Economy: Who Gets the Nice Cyborg Future?

Technological possibility is distributed through money.

That means any politics of bodily transformation must ask:

- Who owns the patents?
- Who manufactures the device?
- Who pays for maintenance?
- Who receives public funding?
- Who must buy privately?
- Who gets a clinical trial?
- Who bears experimental risk?
- Who supplies the data?
- Who can travel for treatment?
- What happens when support is withdrawn?

A brilliant technology that exists only for wealthy people is still politically interesting.

It is not universal emancipation.

Nor should innovation policy assume that a cheaper future automatically solves present exclusion.

People live in the present tense.

---

## 🌍 Infrastructure Before Individualism

Xenofeminism is most useful when it prevents bodily autonomy from collapsing into consumer choice.

An individual cannot purchase their way out of every structural problem.

The relevant infrastructure includes:

- healthcare;
- housing;
- transport;
- childcare;
- broadband;
- education;
- clean environments;
- public space;
- legal recognition;
- social care.

Sometimes the most transformative technology is not inside the body.

It is the lift that works.

The bus that arrives.

The medication that remains funded.

The form that can be completed.

The database that does not expose you.

The payment system that does not quietly eject you.

Engineering the world is also bodily politics.

---

## ❤️‍🩹 WE DO NOT LIVE SINGLE-ISSUE LIVES

The principle carried from disability justice remains decisive:

> ## **WE DO NOT LIVE SINGLE-ISSUE LIVES.**

A reproductive technology policy is also potentially about:

- disability;
- class;
- race;
- migration;
- sexuality;
- healthcare access.

A trans-health policy is also potentially about:

- housing;
- employment;
- family;
- disability;
- privacy;
- poverty.

A disability technology policy is also potentially about:

- labour;
- transport;
- care;
- procurement;
- data;
- design.

Bodies do not arrive at institutions as single policy categories.

Technology policy should stop pretending they do.

## 🧭 A Practical Test for Bodily Technology

When assessing a technology that changes, supports, measures or governs bodies, ask:

| Question | What it catches |
| --- | --- |
| What does the person want? | paternalism disguised as protection |
| What material problem is being addressed? | abstraction from lived conditions |
| Who defines success? | hidden normative standards |
| Who controls access? | gatekeeping |
| Who pays? | class and resource exclusion |
| What happens if the person refuses? | coercive normalisation |
| What data is created? | surveillance and secondary use |
| Can the intervention be stopped or reversed? | lock-in and autonomy |
| What alternatives exist? | false choice |
| Could the environment change instead? | unnecessary individualisation |
| Who bears failure? | risk displacement |
| What happens to the most marginalised user? | intersectional blind spots |

The table is not a substitute for expertise.

It is a way of noticing where expertise is missing.

---

## 🧿 The Core Guardrail

A useful emancipatory position can hold all of these at once:

- bodies are material;
- material conditions can be altered;
- nature does not settle politics;
- technology does not automatically produce progress;
- people can legitimately want to change their bodies;
- people can legitimately refuse to change their bodies;
- environments can disable;
- medicine can liberate;
- medicine can gatekeep;
- technological access depends on resources;
- lived expertise matters;
- specialist expertise matters;
- neither should erase the other.

That is harder than choosing a slogan.

It is also considerably more useful.

---

## 🌞 From Engineered Bodies to Engineered Worlds

The next move is outward.

If:

- technological systems are political;
- supposedly natural arrangements can be redesigned;
- bodies and environments are interdependent;
- access and resources determine whether autonomy is real;

then the obvious question is no longer only what we should do to bodies.

It is:

> ## **WHAT KIND OF ENVIRONMENT WOULD MAKE MORE PEOPLE FREE?**

That is the hand-off to [🌞 We Could Build Nicer Things](./🌞_we_could_build_nicer_things.md).

The constructive technological question is not merely how to perfect the human.

It is how to build systems in which more kinds of humans can live well.
