**First created:** 2026-09-05 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-07

## 🛰️ What This Folder Is For

A great deal of contemporary discussion begins too late.

It begins with:

- TikTok;
- Andrew Tate;
- incels;
- the manosphere;
- algorithmic radicalisation;
- online violence against women and girls;
- generative AI;
- the latest platform scandal.

Those things matter.

They are not the beginning of the story.

Women have been building, using, moderating, writing, playing, organising, making art, making money, forming relationships and developing political cultures through digital systems for decades.

Networked misogyny has a history too.

So do feminist theories of technology.

So do queer, trans, disability and intersectional critiques of supposedly neutral systems.

And so do attempts to imagine and build something better.

`📖_Previously_On_The_Internet/` exists to put that history back underneath the present.

> **You do not need to read this folder from beginning to end.**

It is designed for **searchable, printable, grab-and-go use**.

Use the question you are trying to answer and take the relevant route.

## 💾 Women Were Already Here

### [💾 Women Have Always Been Online](./💾_women_have_always_been_online.md)

**Question answered:** How did changing internet architecture change women's participation, power and exposure?

This node corrects the historical framing.

Women did not suddenly arrive on an otherwise naturally male internet.

Follow women across successive architectures as:

- builders;
- programmers;
- users;
- moderators;
- writers;
- artists;
- fans;
- gamers;
- sex workers;
- activists;
- researchers;
- community organisers;
- creators.

At every architectural transition, ask:

- What became easier?
- What became harder?
- What became visible?
- Who gained power?
- Who acquired access to whom?

## 🌸 Feminism Is Not One Account

### [🌸 Guide to Online Feminisms](./🌸_guide_to_online_feminisms.md)

Use this when "feminists online" is being treated as though it describes one coherent political bloc.

Online feminist cultures contain different:

- traditions;
- generations;
- political commitments;
- vocabularies;
- strategic assumptions;
- conflicts.

Knowing which feminism somebody is drawing from can explain why two people who both call themselves feminists may disagree profoundly about:

- sex;
- gender;
- pornography;
- sex work;
- policing;
- prisons;
- race;
- disability;
- capitalism;
- technology;
- trans politics;
- reform;
- abolition.

Do not flatten disagreement into incoherence.

Sometimes people are working from genuinely different political traditions.

## 🪞 The Backlash Has a History

### [🪞 The Backlash Was Also Networked](./🪞_the_backlash_was_also_networked.md)

**Question answered:** If women could organise online, what happened when misogynists learned to organise there too?

Women and feminist movements gained new capacities from networked communication.

Their opponents did too.

The useful relationship is:

```text
networked feminist mobilisation
             ↕
networked antifeminist countermobilisation
```

This node traces recurring dynamics through earlier online gender conflict, harassment cultures, rape-joke and free-speech battles, gaming-community boundary policing, antifeminist communities, grievance formation, Dickwolves, GamerGate and the environments preceding later manosphere ecosystems.

It does **not** manufacture a neat deterministic genealogy.

These communities and movements are not all the same thing.

The recurring affordances and political dynamics are the point.

## 📰 For Journalists, Policymakers and People Who Have Been Handed This in a Hurry

You do not need to become chronically online.

You do need to know enough not to mistake unfamiliarity for meaninglessness.

A few useful rules:

- Do not assume a female-coded space is politically or informationally trivial.
- Do not assume a meme expresses literal belief.
- Do not assume an ironic register means nobody is serious.
- Do not assume screenshots are merely gossip.
- Do not assume every member of a network shares an ideology.
- Do not assume one highly visible platform created a culture that predates it.
- Do not treat GamerGate as the beginning of online misogyny.
- Do not treat the manosphere as one organisation.
- Do not treat women only as victims of technological systems.
- Do not treat technical expertise as the only expertise relevant to technological governance.
- Do not confuse criticism of a technological arrangement with opposition to technology.
- Ask what changed in the architecture.

When confused, return to:

[📚 Definitions & Registers](./📚_definitions_and_registers.md)

and:

## 🌱 The Larger Argument

This folder is not arguing that the old internet was good.

It was not.

It is not arguing that women form one coherent online culture.

They do not.

It is not arguing that every contemporary misogynistic movement descends neatly from one earlier community.

It does not.

It is not arguing that algorithms invented misogyny.

They did not.

It is not arguing that technology is inherently emancipatory.

It isn't.

The larger argument is:

> **Architecture changes affordances.**
>
> **Affordances change relationships.**
>
> **Relationships create cultures.**
>
> **Cultures interact with power.**
>
> **Power shapes the next architecture.**

That is a feedback environment.

Once you can see the loop, the contemporary internet becomes considerably easier to read.

And once the current configuration stops looking inevitable, another question becomes available:

> **What would we rather build?**
