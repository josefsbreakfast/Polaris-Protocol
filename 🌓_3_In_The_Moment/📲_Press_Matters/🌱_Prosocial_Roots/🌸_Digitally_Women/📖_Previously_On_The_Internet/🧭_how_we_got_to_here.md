# 🧭 How We Got to Here
**First created:** 2026-09-05 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-05  
*A route map through the technological, cultural and feminist histories underneath the contemporary internet.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

This is the capstone for `📖_Previously_On_The_Internet/`.

It does not propose another Year Zero.

It does not argue that one forum, one platform, one movement, one algorithm or one scandal caused the contemporary internet.

It does something simpler:

> **Put the pieces back in chronological and architectural order.**

If you arrive at contemporary debates through misogynistic influencers, algorithmic radicalisation, online harassment, platform power, AI, safeguarding or the manosphere, it is easy to mistake the latest visible manifestation for the beginning of the story.

It isn't.

Women were already online.

Women were already building things.

Women were already forming communities.

Women were already being harassed.

Women were already organising politically.

Antifeminists were already organising too.

And feminists, queer people, trans people, disabled people, artists, researchers and technologists were already asking what different technological futures might look like.

The internet changed.

The relationships between people changed with it.

So did the distribution of power.

**Question answered:** How did we get from women participating in early digital cultures to the contemporary politics of platforms, algorithms, misogyny and AI?

---

## 🧱 Read the Architecture, Not Just the Scandals

A useful chronology is technological rather than merely event-based:

```text
early networks and forums
          ↓
web communities
          ↓
blogs and participatory publishing
          ↓
social networking
          ↓
smartphones
          ↓
platform consolidation
          ↓
algorithmic feeds
          ↓
creator economy
          ↓
generative AI
```

At every transition, ask:

- What became easier?
- What became harder?
- What became visible?
- What became persistent?
- What became searchable?
- Who gained power?
- Who lost control?
- Who acquired access to whom?
- Who became dependent on infrastructure they did not govern?

This prevents technological history becoming a list of famous websites.

The architecture matters because architecture changes behaviour.

## 💾 Women Were Already There

Start with:

[💾 Women Have Always Been Online](./💾_women_have_always_been_online.md)

Women did not suddenly arrive on an otherwise naturally male internet and begin experiencing online harm.

Across successive digital architectures, women have participated as:

- programmers;
- builders;
- users;
- moderators;
- writers;
- artists;
- fans;
- gamers;
- sex workers;
- activists;
- researchers;
- community organisers;
- creators.

Women are also intersectional.

The history includes women who are simultaneously:

- queer;
- trans;
- disabled;
- racialised;
- poor;
- migrants;
- sex workers;
- carers;
- members of religious or cultural minorities;
- members of multiple overlapping communities.

Those identities do not disappear when somebody opens a browser.

> **We do not live single-issue lives.**

Neither does internet history.

## 🌐 Early Networks: Community Before the Feed

Early digital communities were not egalitarian utopias.

But their architecture differed substantially from contemporary algorithmic platforms.

Forums, bulletin boards, mailing lists, IRC, specialist communities and early web spaces could allow people to:

- gather around shared interests;
- adopt pseudonyms;
- build specialist knowledge;
- create community norms;
- moderate locally;
- maintain archives;
- develop identities;
- find people they could not easily find geographically.

Women and marginalised communities used those affordances extensively.

So did hostile actors.

The important point is not that early internet architecture was safe.

It is that discovery, distribution and governance worked differently.

A hostile person often had to **find the community**.

That distinction becomes increasingly important later.

## 🪭 Web Communities: Culture Becomes Infrastructure

As the web expanded, apparently frivolous digital spaces became substantial information environments.

See:

[💾 Guide to Girl Internet](./💾_guide_to_girl_internet.md)

Fandom, forums, blogs, LiveJournal, Tumblr and their descendants supported practices including:

- collaborative interpretation;
- writing;
- art;
- identity experimentation;
- relationship discussion;
- political education;
- mutual aid;
- screenshots and receipts;
- distributed investigation;
- community memory;
- social sanction;
- collective humour.

Later spaces such as Bookternet, stan cultures, beauty and fashion networks, creator communities and group chats inherit parts of this ecology.

Apparently frivolous does not mean informationally empty.

> **People make knowledge wherever people gather.**

That remains true when the knowledge is being exchanged through fanfiction, lipstick recommendations, memes or a group chat.

## 🧰 The Maker Internet Was Never Naturally Male Either

Contemporary maker cultures offer an easy corrective to the persistent assumption that women mainly occupy the social or decorative layers of the internet.

Search for things such as **cyberdecks** and the boundaries become immediately less convincing.

Women, young women, trans women and queer women participate in spaces involving:

- hardware modification;
- fabrication;
- electronics;
- aesthetic computing;
- DIY interfaces;
- open-source experimentation;
- repair;
- speculative design.

Technical culture and cultural expression were never cleanly separable.

A computer can be functional and beautiful.

A machine can also be identity, art, play and community.

## 🪞 The Backlash Was Networked Too

Women and feminist movements gained new capacities from networked communication.

Their opponents did too.

See:

[🪞 The Backlash Was Also Networked](./🪞_the_backlash_was_also_networked.md)

The useful relationship is not a neat genealogy.

It is:

```text
networked feminist mobilisation
             ↕
networked antifeminist countermobilisation
```

Different communities, ideologies and conflicts should not be collapsed into one movement.

But recurring political dynamics matter:

- boundary policing;
- grievance formation;
- rape-joke and free-speech conflicts;
- harassment;
- antifeminist organising;
- community raids;
- reputational attacks;
- contested moderation;
- arguments about who belongs.

The backlash learned to use the network too.

## 🐺 Before GamerGate

GamerGate should not become Year Zero for online misogyny.

Pre-GamerGate conflicts already demonstrate contested gender politics inside digital communities.

The Dickwolves controversy is useful precisely because it sits in this earlier historical neighbourhood.

Cross-reference:

`🌑_1_Origin_Points/🪿_Embodied_Information_Ecology/♻️🕸️_The_Feedback_Environment/🐺_dickwolves_survivors_guild.md`

The preliminary dynamics of later large-scale harassment campaigns were already visible in smaller pockets of networked culture:

- a community conflict;
- exposure beyond the originating community;
- boundary disputes;
- harassment crossing between spaces;
- competing claims about humour, speech and harm;
- targets becoming objects of wider attention.

There has always been some degree of **encroachment between networked spaces**.

The scale, discoverability and amplification mechanisms changed.

---

## 🎮 GamerGate: Not the Beginning, but a Visibility Event

GamerGate matters.

It does not need to be the origin of everything in order to matter.

It made several already-existing dynamics unusually visible at once:

- gendered harassment;
- cross-platform mobilisation;
- community boundary policing;
- antifeminist grievance;
- networked targeting;
- reputational warfare;
- platform moderation failures;
- conflict over journalism, identity and cultural power.

It also demonstrated how difficult it was becoming to contain conflict inside one community.

A dispute could move.

A target could be followed.

A narrative could be replicated across platforms.

A crowd could assemble without sharing one coherent ideology.

That is an architectural development as much as a cultural one.

---

## 📱 Smartphones Changed Access to People

The smartphone transition matters because the internet stopped being somewhere many people deliberately went.

It became something carried continuously.

That changed:

- availability;
- notification;
- photography;
- location;
- messaging;
- social visibility;
- audience access;
- harassment persistence;
- expectations of response.

The boundary between online and offline life became increasingly artificial.

A person could now carry:

- their friends;
- their work;
- their abuser;
- their audience;
- their political community;
- strangers;
- the news;
- the platform

in the same object.

That is an extraordinary concentration of social infrastructure.

---

## 🏢 Platform Consolidation Changed Power

As internet activity consolidated around a smaller number of major platforms, community governance increasingly encountered corporate governance.

Platforms acquired substantial power over:

- discovery;
- moderation;
- identity;
- advertising;
- monetisation;
- visibility;
- data;
- archives;
- access to audiences.

The relationship increasingly became:

```text
community
    ↓
platform infrastructure
    ↓
corporate rules
    ↓
community
```

People could build enormous communities without owning the environment those communities inhabited.

That creates leverage.

A policy change can alter:

- livelihoods;
- safety;
- discoverability;
- speech;
- community memory;
- access to essential resources.

The platform is no longer merely hosting culture.

It is shaping the conditions under which culture can exist.

---

## 🧮 Then Discovery Became Algorithmic

Chronological and subscription-based systems do not eliminate power.

But recommender systems change the problem.

The question is no longer only:

> **What did I choose to follow?**

It becomes:

> **What did the system decide to place in front of me next?**

That matters enormously for political and gendered content.

Recommendation systems can connect:

- adjacent interests;
- grievance;
- identity;
- entertainment;
- lifestyle content;
- political messaging;
- influencers;
- increasingly specialised communities.

The route does not have to begin with somebody searching for an extremist ideology.

It can begin with an ordinary question.

The contemporary consequences belong in:

## 🐇 Rabbitholes for the Boys

See:

[🐇 Rabbitholes for the Boys](./🐇_rabbitholes_for_the_boys.md)

Men and boys have been deliberately and commercially pipelined for years.

That process can involve mixtures of:

- self-improvement;
- fitness;
- dating;
- money;
- masculinity;
- status;
- humour;
- sexual anxiety;
- grievance;
- loneliness;
- political identity.

Not every person entering one of these topics travels to the same destination.

Not every creator belongs to the same movement.

Not every recommendation pathway is deliberately radicalising.

The analytical point is that contemporary architecture makes **movement between adjacent informational environments cheap**.

The rabbit hole has acquired an escalator.

## 🎥 The Creator Economy Put People Inside the Distribution System

The creator economy changes the incentives again.

Individuals can become:

- media outlets;
- brands;
- political educators;
- entertainers;
- sales channels;
- community leaders;
- parasocial figures.

Their income may depend on:

- attention;
- retention;
- subscriptions;
- sponsorship;
- controversy;
- algorithmic distribution.

This does not make creators uniquely malicious.

It means personality, commerce, politics and community can now occupy the same feed.

The audience may experience a creator as a trusted person.

The platform may experience the same relationship as engagement.

The advertiser may experience it as inventory.

Those are different descriptions of the same connection.

---

## 🧠 Online and Offline Stopped Being Useful Opposites

By this point, asking whether something happened "online" or "in real life" often obscures more than it clarifies.

Digital systems can shape:

- relationships;
- employment;
- elections;
- healthcare;
- education;
- policing;
- sexuality;
- reputation;
- money;
- physical safety;
- political mobilisation.

Online behaviour can produce offline consequences.

Offline institutions can govern online behaviour.

The same person inhabits both.

There is no little internet person who disappears when the phone screen turns off.

---

## 🤖 Generative AI Is Another Architectural Transition

Generative AI does not erase the previous internet.

It sits on top of, learns from, interacts with and increasingly mediates parts of it.

The transition raises old questions in new forms:

- Who supplied the information?
- Who performed the labour?
- Whose work became training material?
- What becomes easier to generate?
- What becomes easier to impersonate?
- What becomes easier to automate?
- What happens to search and discovery?
- Who governs the model?
- Who can inspect the system?
- Who carries the cost of error?

It also creates constructive possibilities.

That matters because feminist technological history is not only a history of harm.

## 🕹️ Meanwhile, Feminists Were Imagining Technology Too

There is a second chronology running alongside the architectural one.

Very roughly:

```text
Firestone
    ↓
Haraway / Stone
    ↓
cyberfeminisms
    ↓
intersectional, queer, trans,
postcolonial and disability critiques
    ↓
feminist HCI / feminist STS
    ↓
design justice / disability justice
    ↓
xenofeminism
    ↓
solarpunk and other constructive traditions
    ↓
we could build nicer things
```

This is not a single intellectual lineage.

People disagree.

Movements overlap imperfectly.

Some rejected labels used by others.

Some early technological feminism reproduced exclusions later thinkers had to confront.

That disagreement is part of the history.

What matters here is that feminist and adjacent traditions have repeatedly refused to treat technology as something men build while women wait to discover the consequences.

## 🕹️ Cyberfeminism Before the Algorithm

See:

[🕹️ Cyberfeminism Before the Algorithm](./🕹️_cyberfeminism_before_the_algorithm.md)

Cyberfeminism asked what networked technology might do to:

- gender;
- embodiment;
- identity;
- community;
- reproduction;
- power;
- political organisation.

Its history includes utopianism, art, theory, experimentation and substantial internal critique.

Donna Haraway's cyborg became one influential route into thinking beyond supposedly stable boundaries between:

- human and machine;
- nature and technology;
- body and information.

Sandy Stone's work is important in the neighbouring history of trans studies, medical authority, self-representation and the politics of who is permitted to speak about embodied experience.

Later critiques make the exclusions visible too.

Race, colonialism, disability, economic access and the material infrastructure underneath apparently disembodied cyberspace cannot be treated as optional additions.

## 🪬 Xenofeminism and the Engineered Body

See:

[🪬 Xenofeminism and the Engineered Body](./🪬_xenofeminism_and_the_engineered_body.md)

Xenofeminism makes the argument deliberately interventionist.

Nature is not automatically just.

Technology is not automatically liberatory.

Bodies, environments and institutions can be altered.

The political question is who gets the capacity to alter them, under what conditions and toward what ends.

That opens directly into contemporary questions around:

- reproductive technology;
- trans healthcare;
- disability;
- medical authority;
- femtech;
- bodily data;
- enhancement;
- accessibility;
- technological autonomy.

It also reconnects technological politics to material bodies.

The internet was never actually disembodied.

---

## ♿ Disability Justice Changes the Unit of Analysis

Disability justice contributes something essential to this history.

It refuses the idea that people can be understood through one isolated axis at a time.

Its emphasis on:

- intersectionality;
- leadership of those most impacted;
- cross-movement solidarity;
- wholeness;
- sustainability;
- interdependence;
- collective access;
- collective liberation

changes how technological design can be understood.

A system is not accessible merely because somebody eventually found the accessibility menu.

A person does not stop being racialised, gendered, poor, queer, a migrant, a parent or a worker when they become disabled.

> **We do not live single-issue lives.**

Technology does not interact with single-issue lives either.

## 🌞 We Could Build Nicer Things

See:

[🌞 We Could Build Nicer Things](./🌞_we_could_build_nicer_things.md)

The constructive lineage matters because critique alone can accidentally naturalise the object being criticised.

If every discussion begins with today's platform architecture and asks only how to make it slightly less harmful, the architecture itself disappears from political view.

The alternative is not:

```text
harmful technology
       ↕
no technology
```

We can change:

- objectives;
- ownership;
- interfaces;
- governance;
- accessibility;
- research priorities;
- models;
- incentives;
- relationships;
- exit;
- the problems technology is pointed at.

This is where feminist HCI, feminist STS, design justice, accessibility, community technology, queer experimentation, rehabilitative technology and solarpunk become constructive rather than merely critical.

> **Different assumptions produce different machines.**

## 🗳️ Governance Was Always Part of the Question

There is one more route into the present.

Technology policy is too often treated as though only technologists are qualified to scrutinise technological systems.

They aren't.

Engineers matter.

So do:

- procurement specialists;
- MPs;
- regulators;
- clinicians;
- finance specialists;
- journalists;
- survivors;
- disabled users;
- community organisers;
- researchers;
- workers;
- ordinary users.

You do not need to understand every layer of a machine-learning system to ask:

- What did it cost?
- Who evaluated it?
- What evidence supports its use?
- What happens when it fails?
- What data does it consume?
- What dependencies does it create?
- Can it be rolled back?
- Who benefits?
- Who carries the liability?

> **Accountability is not anti-innovation.**

Governance is part of the technological system.

## 🧭 So: How Did We Get to Here?

Not through one conspiracy.

Not through one platform.

Not through one misogynist.

Not through one feminist movement.

Not through one algorithm.

Not through one technological invention.

We got here through interacting changes in:

- architecture;
- culture;
- economics;
- politics;
- gender;
- community;
- business models;
- governance;
- devices;
- distribution;
- identity;
- technological imagination.

A compressed version looks like this:

```text
people build networks
        ↓
communities form
        ↓
women participate, create and organise
        ↕
gender conflict and countermobilisation develop
        ↓
networks become larger and more connected
        ↓
smartphones make access persistent
        ↓
platforms consolidate infrastructure
        ↓
algorithms increasingly govern discovery
        ↓
creators become distribution systems
        ↓
grievance can be monetised and recommended
        ↓
online/offline distinction becomes increasingly useless
        ↓
generative AI introduces another mediation layer
```

Running beside it:

```text
women and marginalised communities
use technology
        ↓
analyse technology
        ↓
criticise its exclusions
        ↓
develop alternative theories
        ↓
develop alternative design practices
        ↓
ask what else technology could be for
```

Both histories matter.

## ⚠️ History Is Not Destiny

This is the most important distinction in the cluster.

> **How we got here is not the same question as why we had to get here.**

There was no law of computing requiring:

- surveillance advertising;
- engagement optimisation;
- recommender-driven grievance;
- inaccessible systems;
- concentrated platform ownership;
- behavioural capture;
- women becoming permanent subjects of online-safety policy.

Those are historical arrangements, business models, political decisions and design choices.

Some emerged gradually.

Some solved real problems while creating others.

Some became entrenched because they were profitable.

Some became infrastructural before democratic institutions understood their significance.

None acquires inevitability merely by existing now.

> **History explains the present configuration. It does not sanctify it.**

## 🌱 A Note Before You Leave

It is easy to finish a history of the internet feeling as though every architectural transition simply increased the available surface area for somebody to behave terribly.

That is not the whole record.

The same history contains:

- friendship;
- art;
- knowledge;
- mutual aid;
- organising;
- queer community;
- disability community;
- survival;
- technical experimentation;
- scientific collaboration;
- political imagination;
- people finding one another.

The network is not one thing.

Neither are the people inside it.

Understanding how harmful systems emerged gives us something more useful than despair.

It gives us design information.

> **We know more about what breaks people now.**
>
> **We can use that knowledge when deciding what to build next.**

And if this entire cluster has become a bit much, recuperative leave in the lesbian greenhouse remains conceptually available.

The computers are monitoring the tomatoes.
