# 💾 Women Have Always Been Online

**First created:** 2026-09-05 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-05  
*Women did not arrive late to the internet. The history keeps forgetting where they were.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

**Question answered:** How did changing internet architecture change women’s participation, power and exposure?

- Correct the framing:
  - women were not late arrivals;
  - women were not only victims;
  - women were not merely “users” of systems built elsewhere.
- Women have been:
  - builders;
  - programmers;
  - moderators;
  - writers;
  - artists;
  - fans;
  - gamers;
  - sex workers;
  - researchers;
  - activists;
  - community organisers;
  - creators;
  - makers.
- The important history is not only:
  - who was online;
- but:
  - what the architecture allowed people to do;
  - how discoverable they became;
  - who could reach them;
  - how communities governed themselves;
  - what happened when scale increased.

---

## 🧮 A Repeating Test

At each technological transition ask:

1. **What became easier?**
2. **What became harder?**
3. **What became visible?**
4. **Who gained power?**
5. **Who acquired access to whom?**

This prevents the chronology becoming a list of websites.

---

## 💻 Early Networks, BBSs And Forums

- Women were present in early networked communities.
- Relevant forms:
  - BBSs;
  - Usenet;
  - mailing lists;
  - niche forums;
  - early commercial online services.
- What became easier:
  - finding people with shared interests;
  - pseudonymous participation;
  - community formation beyond geography;
  - specialist knowledge exchange.
- What remained hard:
  - access to hardware;
  - technical literacy;
  - cost;
  - social assumptions about who “belonged” in computing.
- Important point:
  - early networked space was never naturally male;
  - male dominance in visibility, narrative and institutional memory can make it look that way retrospectively.

---

## 🌐 The Web Makes Communities Legible

- Personal websites;
- forums;
- fan pages;
- web rings;
- early blogging;
- online shops;
- community archives.
- Women increasingly:
  - published;
  - documented;
  - organised;
  - taught;
  - archived;
  - sold things;
  - built niche publics.
- What became easier:
  - publishing without institutional permission;
  - finding niche audiences;
  - maintaining persistent archives.
- What became harder:
  - privacy;
  - separation between identities;
  - control over context once material travelled.

---

## 📝 Blogs, LiveJournal And Networked Voice

- Blogging mattered because:
  - women could publish at length;
  - build readerships;
  - develop expertise publicly;
  - form political and cultural communities;
  - document lived experience.
- LiveJournal and similar spaces mixed:
  - friendship;
  - fandom;
  - politics;
  - diary;
  - community;
  - pseudonymity.
- This is an important precursor to later:
  - Tumblr;
  - Twitter;
  - creator culture;
  - personal-brand politics.
- What changed:
  - the private/public boundary became more porous;
  - community writing became searchable and persistent.

- Fandom is not an afterthought.
- Women-heavy fandom spaces became sites of:
  - metadata;
  - archives;
  - tagging;
  - translation;
  - collaborative writing;
  - historical preservation;

- AO3 is a major later example.

## 🎮 Women Were Gaming Too

- Women did not enter games culture with GamerGate.
- Women were:
  - players;
  - modders;
  - writers;
  - journalists;
  - developers;
  - forum participants;
  - streamers;
  - fandom participants.
- Gender conflict in gaming spaces predates GamerGate.
- Dickwolves belongs here as a pre-GamerGate case study.
- Useful analytical role:
  - demonstrates that:
    - boundary policing;
    - rape-joke disputes;
    - feminist criticism;
    - harassment;
    - free-speech framing;
    - community identity conflict
  - were already present.
- GamerGate is therefore:
  - escalation and consolidation;
  - not Year Zero.
- Cross-link:
  - `./🪞_the_backlash_was_also_networked.md`

## 📱 Social Networking Changes Access

- Facebook;
- Twitter;
- Tumblr;
- Instagram;
- later TikTok.
- What became easier:
  - public networking;
  - mobilisation;
  - discovery;
  - creator growth;
  - real-time collective response.
- What became harder:
  - maintaining separation between audiences;
  - avoiding harassment;
  - controlling virality;
  - preserving context.
- Who acquired access to whom:
  - strangers to individuals;
  - journalists to private communities;
  - employers to personal expression;
  - harassers to social graphs;
  - creators to enormous audiences.

---

## 📲 Smartphones Collapse The Boundary Further

- The internet becomes:
  - always present;
  - embodied;
  - location-adjacent;
  - camera-equipped;
  - notification-driven.
- What changes for women:
  - easier documentation;
  - easier emergency communication;
  - easier organising;
  - easier creator work;
  - easier harassment;
  - easier stalking;
  - easier unwanted access.
- The phone becomes:
  - archive;
  - camera;
  - workplace;
  - social network;
  - safety tool;
  - vulnerability surface.

---

## 🏢 Platform Consolidation Changes Power

- Earlier internet:
  - more fragmented;
  - community-specific;
  - often self-governed.
- Later internet:
  - fewer large platforms;
  - stronger central moderation;
  - more commercial dependence;
  - more algorithmic distribution.
- Women gain:
  - scale;
  - visibility;
  - commercial opportunity.
- Platforms gain:
  - control over discovery;
  - moderation;
  - monetisation;
  - account survival.
- Important tension:
  - visibility can be power;
  - dependence can also be vulnerability.

---

## 📈 Algorithmic Feeds Change Discovery

- Users increasingly encounter:
  - what platforms predict will engage them,
  - not only what they explicitly chose to follow.
- This changes:
  - discoverability;
  - virality;
  - harassment scale;
  - niche formation;
  - creator incentives;
  - ideological exposure.
- Women-heavy spaces become:
  - easier to find;
  - easier to monetise;
  - easier to extract from;
  - easier to target.

## 💰 The Creator Economy

- Women become major:
  - creators;
  - influencers;
  - educators;
  - commentators;
  - sellers;
  - cultural intermediaries.
- Creator work blurs:
  - labour;
  - identity;
  - intimacy;
  - advertising;
  - community.
- Benefits:
  - direct audience access;
  - independent income;
  - niche markets;
  - cultural power.
- Costs:
  - parasocial pressure;
  - harassment;
  - dependence on platforms;
  - reputational volatility;
  - monetisation rules;
  - constant visibility.
- Particularly important for:
  - beauty;
  - fashion;
  - fitness;
  - sex work;
  - fandom;
  - education;
  - politics.

## 🪟 Visibility Is Not The Same As Power

- Women becoming more visible online does not necessarily mean:
  - they gained equivalent control over the systems distributing that visibility.
- Visibility can produce:
  - audience;
  - money;
  - influence;
  - harassment;
  - surveillance;
  - reputational exposure.
- Power also depends on:
  - moderation;
  - platform rules;
  - ownership;
  - payment infrastructure;
  - discovery systems;
  - legal protections.

## 🧭 The Long View

A compressed architecture history:

```text
early networks / BBS / forums
        ↓
web communities
        ↓
blogs / LiveJournal / fandom archives
        ↓
social networking
        ↓
smartphones
        ↓
platform consolidation
        ↓
algorithmic feeds
        ↓
creator economy
        ↓
generative AI
```

At every stage:

> **women were already there.**

What changed was:

- scale;
- discoverability;
- persistence;
- portability;
- commercialisation;
- governance;
- exposure.

---

## 🧠 The Analytical Point

- Women did not enter a naturally male internet.
- Women were part of building:
  - technical infrastructure;
  - cultural infrastructure;
  - social infrastructure;
  - governance;
  - archives;
  - markets.
- The historical error is often:
  - looking only for women where male-coded institutions already recognised importance.
- When women-heavy participation gets recoded as:
  - fandom;
  - gossip;
  - lifestyle;
  - community work;
  - moderation;
  - aesthetics;
- its infrastructural role can disappear from the historical account.
- The better question is:
  - **what work was being done, regardless of what the culture called it?**
