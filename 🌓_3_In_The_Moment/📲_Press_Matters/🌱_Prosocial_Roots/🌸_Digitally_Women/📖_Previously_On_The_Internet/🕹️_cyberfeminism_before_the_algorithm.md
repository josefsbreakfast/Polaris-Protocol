# 🕹️ Cyberfeminism Before the Algorithm
**First created:** 2026-09-05 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-05  
*Before feminist technology politics became dominated by platform harms and algorithmic safety, it was already asking what bodies, identities, relationships and worlds technology might make possible.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Contemporary technology policy often begins after the machine has already been built.

A platform exists. A recommender system exists. A market exists. Harm appears. Women and marginalised people are described as vulnerable users. The policy question becomes how to make the existing system hurt them less.

That question matters.

It is not the only question feminist technological politics has asked.

Long before the current argument about algorithms, feminists, queer and trans thinkers, artists, disability activists and adjacent movements were already asking larger and stranger questions:

- What counts as natural?
- What counts as human?
- Who gets to define a normal body?
- Can technology redistribute reproductive labour?
- Can identity be technologically destabilised?
- Who gets access to technological transformation?
- Who gets to narrate a technologically mediated body?
- If technology can alter the material conditions of life, what should we alter?
- Who gets to decide what counts as improvement?

Cyberfeminism is useful here not because it supplies one doctrine. It does not.

It is better understood as an intellectual and artistic neighbourhood: argumentative, international, sometimes utopian, sometimes deliberately obscene, sometimes exclusionary, and often considerably more ambitious than the contemporary language of online safety suggests.

**Question answered:** Before we started asking how to make platforms less harmful, what did feminists imagine technology could actually become?

## 🔥 Before Cyberfeminism Had a Name

Shulamith Firestone's *The Dialectic of Sex* (1970) predates cyberfeminism, but it belongs in its prehistory.

Firestone treated reproductive biology not merely as a private fact but as part of the material organisation of sex inequality. Her technological imagination therefore reached into pregnancy, childbirth, reproductive labour and the organisation of the family.

The important move is not that every prediction or prescription should be inherited.

It is that **biological reality can be materially real without being politically sacred**.

Once technology can alter a condition previously treated as given, a new political question appears:

> **What authority should "nature" have over the organisation of human life?**

That question will recur through cyberfeminism, posthumanism, disability politics, reproductive technology and xenofeminism.

## 🧬 Donna Haraway: Enter the Cyborg

Donna Haraway's 1985 *A Cyborg Manifesto* became one of the major catalysts for later cyberfeminist thought.

The cyborg is easy to flatten into a woman with mechanical parts.

That misses most of the point.

Haraway's cyborg unsettles boundaries that had been treated as clean and politically reliable:

- human / machine;
- nature / culture;
- organism / technology;
- physical / technological;
- male / female;
- original / constructed.

The provocation is anti-essentialist. There is no simple natural condition of being female that automatically supplies a complete political programme or guarantees solidarity.

That created possibilities for thinking about:

- coalition without sameness;
- technologically mediated identity;
- queer and hybrid subjectivities;
- the instability of supposedly natural categories.

It also generated serious feminist disagreement. Anti-essentialism can open political possibilities while making it harder to explain what, exactly, a movement organised around women is organising around.

That tension was never fully resolved. It remains useful.

---

## 🦾 The Cyborg Escapes Its Author

The cyborg rapidly travelled beyond Haraway's socialist-feminist argument.

Popular technology culture often retained the exciting part — human-machine enmeshment — while dropping much of the feminist and political critique around it.

That migration matters.

Ideas do not remain under the control of the people who formulate them. A political metaphor can become:

- an aesthetic;
- a product category;
- a futurist fantasy;
- an advertising language;
- a technological identity.

The cyborg therefore supplies an early warning about technological culture itself:

> **A radical account of technology can be absorbed by technological culture while its politics are left behind.**

## 🪞 Sandy Stone: Whose Story Is This, Anyway?

Sandy Stone's 1987 *The Empire Strikes Back: A Posttranssexual Manifesto* gives this history another essential question:

> **Who gets to write the technologically mediated body?**

Stone examined a world in which trans women had been heavily:

- medically classified;
- diagnosed;
- researched;
- narrated;
- instructed;
- interpreted;

while frequently being denied authority over the discourse describing them.

Physicians, psychiatrists, researchers, autobiographical conventions and feminist critics could all participate in constructing the legible trans subject.

Stone's answer was not merely better representation. It was counter-discourse: trans people speaking as trans subjects rather than remaining objects in somebody else's explanatory system.

This matters well beyond trans studies.

A body can be changed by technology.

A body can also be **interpreted by an institution**, and that interpretation can change how the person learns to present the body back to the institution.

## ♻️ The Diagnostic Feedback Loop

Stone's discussion of medical gatekeeping provides an unusually clear example of observation becoming intervention.

```text
institution defines acceptable narrative
        ↓
patient learns required narrative
        ↓
patient performs it to obtain treatment
        ↓
institution observes conformity
        ↓
institution treats conformity as validation
```

The observer is no longer merely observing.

The classification helps produce the behaviour subsequently treated as evidence for the classification.

This is a recurring problem anywhere people must become institutionally legible in order to obtain:

- healthcare;
- benefits;
- protection;
- recognition;
- access;
- permission.

See the wider Polaris work on [Observation Becomes Intervention](../../../../../../🌑_1_Origin_Points/🪿_Embodied_Information_Ecology/♻️🧿_Observation_Becomes_Intervention/) for the general feedback problem.

## 👁️ Passing, Legibility and Self-Erasure

Stone also complicates the politics of visibility.

Passing can provide:

- safety;
- treatment;
- employment;
- social legibility;
- ordinary privacy.

It can also require:

- a plausible history;
- conformity to institutional expectations;
- historical erasure;
- disappearance into an accepted category.

The useful question is therefore not:

**visibility good / passing bad.**

It is:

> **What does political legibility cost when safety may depend on illegibility?**

That question did not disappear when life moved online.

It became one of the central questions of pseudonymity, disclosure, identity, community safety and platform governance.

## ⚔️ Feminist Conflict Did Not Begin on Twitter

Stone wrote partly in response to Janice Raymond's *The Transsexual Empire* and against a wider environment in which trans women could be treated as instruments of patriarchy rather than speaking political subjects.

The history deserves precision.

Stone did not need to deny that medical or autobiographical accounts of trans femininity could reproduce sexist conventions in order to reject the totalisation of trans women as patriarchal artefacts.

That distinction matters.

Many arguments now experienced as internet culture-war material have much older:

- intellectual;
- medical;
- feminist;
- institutional

histories.

Twitter did not invent the dispute.

Tumblr did not invent the people in it.

## 🧵 *Patchwork Girl*: Hypertext Has a Body

Shelley Jackson's 1995 hypertext work *Patchwork Girl* makes the theory materially strange.

The work reimagines a female Frankensteinian monster as:

- queer;
- scarred;
- composite;
- bodily multiple;
- technologically reconstructed.

Readers move through fragments and links. The interface is not merely carrying the argument. The form participates in it.

The body becomes navigable.

The text becomes bodily.

Authorship, monstrosity, reproduction, sexuality and technological form become difficult to separate.

This is worth remembering because feminist technological thought was never confined to people writing essays about machines.

Artists were using the machines themselves to think.

## 💾 1991: Cyberfeminism Names Itself

The term **cyberfeminism** emerged around 1991 through more than one route.

VNS Matrix in Australia and British cultural theorist Sadie Plant are commonly associated with independent early uses of the term; Nancy Paterson was also writing explicitly about cyberfeminism in Canada in the early 1990s.

There is no need to manufacture one clean origin point.

### VNS Matrix

VNS Matrix treated the electronic environment as somewhere women, bodies, sexuality, fluids, art and political consciousness could be inserted aggressively rather than politely admitted.

Their *Cyberfeminist Manifesto for the 21st Century* was:

- artistic;
- sexual;
- funny;
- confrontational;
- deliberately excessive;
- network-native while also moving through billboards, radio, print and other media.

This matters.

Cyberfeminism was not simply a seminar about women gaining access to computers.

Some of it was women arriving in technological culture and making the furniture nervous.

### Sadie Plant

Plant's work connected women, machines and technological development to the destabilisation of established gender orders.

Her importance is historical even where later readers may be less persuaded by the technological optimism.

The point is not to turn every early cyberfeminist claim into prophecy.

It is to recover the scale of the imagination.

---

## 🌍 Cyberfeminism Was International

Cyberfeminism should not be narrated as America, then Britain, then everybody else.

The 1990s landscape included work and organising in:

- Australia;
- Britain;
- Canada;
- Germany;
- Russia;
- and other networked art and feminist communities.

The Cyber-Femin Club in St Petersburg brought together women working with new media in the mid-1990s.

The First Cyberfeminist International, organised through the Old Boys Network in Kassel in 1997, famously resisted fixing the movement into one definition. Its **100 Anti-Theses of Cyberfeminism** answered the definition problem by saying what cyberfeminism was *not*.

That refusal is part of the history.

Cyberfeminism was never one thing.

---

## 🌀 An Intellectual Neighbourhood, Not a Family Tree

Different cyberfeminisms emphasised different things:

- networks;
- art;
- bodies;
- sexuality;
- posthumanism;
- feminism;
- technological optimism;
- technological critique;
- anti-essentialism;
- identity experimentation.

Some strands contradicted others.

Some people whose work belongs in the intellectual neighbourhood did not use the cyberfeminist label.

Some actively preferred other traditions.

That matters particularly when the history reaches race, colonialism, disability and non-Western technological cultures.

Do not retrospectively annex everybody who thought interesting things about women and machines.

## 🌐 Cyberspace Looked Like an Escape Hatch

Early networked life created real possibilities for:

- pseudonymity;
- alternative identity;
- queer and trans community;
- geographically dispersed organising;
- sexual exploration;
- escaping local social surveillance;
- finding people who simply did not exist in one's immediate physical environment.

Some early digital theory overestimated cyberspace's capacity to dissolve embodied hierarchy.

But the optimism was not wholly imaginary.

People really could do things online that were difficult, dangerous or impossible locally.

The mistake was treating the loosening of one constraint as the disappearance of embodiment itself.

## 🪞 Bodies Followed Us Online

Race did not disappear.

Gender did not disappear.

Disability did not disappear.

Class did not disappear.

Geography did not disappear.

Colonial relations did not disappear.

Neither did:

- money;
- states;
- labour;
- infrastructure;
- housing;
- healthcare;
- bodies.

The better formulation is:

> **The internet did not dissolve embodied hierarchy. It networked embodied people.**

That is why the history of women online cannot be written as a story of virtual people floating free of material life.

See [💾 Women Have Always Been Online](./💾_women_have_always_been_online.md) for the technological chronology of women's participation and exposure, and [💾 Guide to Girl Internet](./💾_guide_to_girl_internet.md) for the cultures that grew inside those environments.

## 🌍 Race, Colonialism and the Universal Cyberwoman

One major criticism of canonical cyberfeminism is that a supposedly universal technological woman could quietly turn out to be:

- white;
- Western;
- economically secure;
- culturally legible to the academy producing the theory.

The correction is not simply to say that intersectionality was later "added".

Some women did not regard cyberfeminism as an adequate name for their technological politics in the first place.

Black feminist digital thought, Black cyberfeminism, Afrofuturist traditions, postcolonial analysis and other frameworks bring different histories and political priorities.

Artists including Coco Fusco, Shu Lea Cheang and Prema Murthy explored race, gender, performance and emerging digital forms without requiring one universal cyberfeminist subject.

The choice of another intellectual tradition can itself be part of the critique.

---

## 🗾 Do Not Colonise Everybody Into Cyberfeminism

Japanese cyberpunk provides an obvious warning.

Works such as *Ghost in the Shell* can be useful when thinking about:

- gender;
- embodiment;
- technological identity;
- human-machine boundaries.

That does not make them secretly European cyberfeminism waiting to be correctly labelled.

Nor should Japan be reduced to a Western fantasy of the hyper-technological future.

Comparison is useful.

Categorical annexation is not.

## 💸 Access Is Not Empowerment by Definition

One of the strongest critiques of 1990s technological optimism is brutally material:

**getting online costs something.**

Access depends on combinations of:

- money;
- hardware;
- telecommunications infrastructure;
- literacy;
- geography;
- electricity;
- time;
- political conditions.

Technology also sits inside:

- labour systems;
- extraction;
- land;
- supply chains;
- colonial histories;
- private ownership.

Giving somebody access to a system therefore does not settle whether participation is empowering.

Ask instead:

- Who owns the infrastructure?
- Who pays?
- Who extracts value?
- Who supplies the labour?
- Whose land and resources support the system?
- Does participation improve the person's material conditions?
- Can the person leave?

> **Access to a system is not the same thing as power over the system.**

## 🦾 Disability: Who Gets to Be a Cyborg?

Disability critique makes the cyborg considerably less metaphorical.

Disabled people may already live with:

- prosthetics;
- implants;
- mobility technology;
- communication devices;
- pharmaceuticals;
- medical monitoring;
- assistive software;
- bodies routinely interpreted through technological systems.

That makes it politically awkward when disability becomes evocative aesthetic material for theories written without disabled people at their centre.

Later disability critiques of Haraway ask, in effect:

- Who gets to use disability metaphorically?
- Who actually lives with the machinery?
- Who controls access to it?
- Who is expected to be repaired?
- Whose body is classified as deficient?
- Who gets to refuse?

The cyborg is different when the charger is essential.

## ♿ Disability Justice Is More Than Accessibility

The disability justice framework developed in the mid-2000s from organisers dissatisfied with disability politics that could centre white, straight men with physical disabilities while treating other structures of power as secondary.

The critique was not merely representational.

A right can exist while the conditions necessary to exercise it remain profoundly unequal.

A right to education does not guarantee equitable education.

Healthcare access does not guarantee that a patient is believed.

A login does not create digital power.

A ramp does not settle poverty.

Disability justice therefore asks about systems, relationships and the whole person.

The ten principles articulated by Sins Invalid include:

- intersectionality;
- leadership of those most impacted;
- anti-capitalist politics;
- cross-movement organising;
- recognising wholeness;
- sustainability;
- cross-disability solidarity;
- interdependence;
- collective access;
- collective liberation.

These are not merely principles for making websites more accessible.

They are a political theory of how people live together.

## ❤️‍🩹 WE DO NOT LIVE SINGLE-ISSUE LIVES

Audre Lorde's formulation belongs near the centre of this node because it corrects an extraordinary amount of bad technological thinking at once:

> ## **WE DO NOT LIVE SINGLE-ISSUE LIVES.**

A person does not encounter a system sequentially as:

```text
woman
then
disabled
then
Black
then
poor
then
queer
then
technology user
```

The whole person encounters the system at once.

That means technological politics cannot sensibly extract:

- gender;
- race;
- disability;
- class;
- sexuality;
- geography;
- migration status

into independent modules and expect the result to describe anybody's life.

Systems meet the whole person even when institutions do not.

---

## 🫂 Leadership of Those Most Impacted

One disability justice principle is especially important for technological governance:

**people most affected by a system possess expertise about that system.**

That does not mean lived experience answers every empirical question.

It does mean policy becomes dangerously stupid when it treats the people carrying the consequences as merely:

- vulnerable;
- emotional;
- anecdotal;
- objects of protection.

Patients know things about healthcare systems.

Disabled people know things about access.

Sex workers know things about sex-work safety.

Trans people know things about medical gatekeeping.

Communities living under a technological intervention know things about what that intervention actually does.

Expertise is distributed.

Institutions should behave accordingly.

---

## 🫀 Interdependence Versus Technological Normalisation

Some technological futurisms treat:

- dependence;
- disability;
- ageing;
- bodily limitation

primarily as defects to overcome.

Disability justice complicates that assumption.

Human beings are already dependent upon:

- other people;
- infrastructure;
- ecosystems;
- medicine;
- food systems;
- communication systems;
- technologies.

Independence is not the absence of dependence.

Often it is dependence whose infrastructure has become sufficiently ordinary that nobody notices it.

But there is an equal mistake available in the opposite direction.

Pain does not become politically sacred because it is natural.

Illness does not become emancipatory because somebody fears technological normalisation.

People can want:

- pain treatment;
- prosthetics;
- mobility aids;
- communication technology;
- hormones;
- reproductive technology;
- neural interfaces;
- accessible environments;

without agreeing that liberation means transforming everybody toward one supposedly optimal body.

> **Technological emancipation must not quietly become technological normalisation.**

## 🌱 Naturalism and Antinaturalism

These terms have several philosophical meanings. The relevant question here is narrower:

**what political authority should "nature" possess?**

Material embodiment matters.

Bodies:

- bleed;
- reproduce;
- hurt;
- age;
- heal;
- require care.

A feminist politics that pretends material bodies do not exist will fail people living in them.

But material reality does not generate political obligation by itself.

The bad inference looks like this:

```text
natural
   ↓
therefore good
   ↓
therefore necessary
   ↓
therefore politically immutable
```

Antinaturalism rejects that jump.

> **"It occurs in nature" and "therefore society should be organised around it" are different propositions.**

There is, however, an equally lazy opposite:

```text
technology
   ↓
therefore progress
   ↓
therefore liberation
```

Technology can:

- emancipate;
- constrain;
- normalise;
- surveil;
- connect;
- extract;
- heal;
- injure.

The political questions remain.

Who controls it?

Who can access it?

Who bears the risks?

Whose body is the default?

Who decides what counts as improvement?

## 🌿 Posthumanism Is Not Transhumanism

These terms are frequently collapsed because both disturb familiar boundaries around "the human".

They do different work.

### Posthumanism

Posthumanist thought challenges the autonomous, self-contained human as the obvious centre and unit of analysis.

Humans exist in relationships with:

- animals;
- ecosystems;
- infrastructures;
- machines;
- tools;
- environments.

The emphasis is often on:

- relationality;
- interdependence;
- materiality;
- ecology;
- critique of anthropocentrism.

Posthumanism is therefore not simply a dream of escaping the body through superior technology.

### Transhumanism

Transhumanism is generally more explicitly concerned with deliberate technological enhancement or transformation of human capacities.

Its recurring subjects include:

- longevity;
- cognition;
- augmentation;
- enhancement;
- bodily transformation.

Feminist questions immediately follow:

- Who receives the enhancement?
- Who defines enhancement?
- What happens to people who remain unenhanced?
- Which differences become defects?
- Who owns the technology?
- What political economy distributes it?

Neither posthumanism nor transhumanism is identical to cyberfeminism.

Neither automatically produces emancipation.

## 🧭 Six Questions, Not Six Boxes

| Framework | Useful shorthand question |
| --- | --- |
| Cyberfeminism | What can feminist politics do with technological and networked life? |
| Posthumanism | Was the autonomous human ever the right unit of analysis? |
| Transhumanism | What could deliberate technology enable humans to become? |
| Disability justice | Who is supported, excluded or normalised by the system? |
| Antinaturalism | Why should "natural" settle the political question? |
| Xenofeminism | What material conditions could we deliberately redesign? |

These traditions overlap, disagree, borrow from one another and contain substantial internal disagreement.

The table is navigation.

It is not taxonomy.

## 🩻 The Sonographic Fetus: Technology Does Not Automatically Liberate

Reproductive imaging provides an especially useful complication.

Sonography can destabilise distinctions between:

- body;
- image;
- organic;
- digital.

At the same time, the fetal image can appear independently visible and independently legible, as though detached from the pregnant person's body and from the technological apparatus producing the image.

A technology capable of disrupting one conceptual boundary can therefore strengthen another political hierarchy.

> **Technology can destabilise one boundary while reinscribing another.**

This is why "technology changes the body" is not yet a political programme.

The question remains what the representation does, who receives authority through it, and whose embodiment becomes easier to ignore.

## 🧷 Do Not White-Knight People Out of Their Essential Resources

Sex work is a particularly unforgiving stress test for whether intersectional technological politics has understood any of the preceding argument.

Online sex work can sit at intersections of:

- gender;
- sexuality;
- disability;
- race;
- migration;
- poverty;
- housing insecurity;
- caring responsibilities;
- queer and trans life;
- financial exclusion;
- platform governance.

Not every sex worker occupies all or most of these categories.

But people with fewer safe alternatives can be disproportionately exposed when governments or private infrastructure providers alter the conditions under which sexual labour can occur.

Protective interventions have material consequences.

For people already living at marginalised intersections, losing access to one system can mean losing access to:

- income;
- food;
- housing;
- banking;
- payment services;
- healthcare;
- community;
- safety information;
- client-screening tools;
- digital infrastructure;
- safer ways of working.

> **You cannot protect somebody by treating the resources they need to survive as politically disposable.**

## 💳 The Infrastructure Can Become the Prohibition

The United States' 2018 FOSTA-SESTA regime and Mastercard's 2021 adult-content standards are different interventions by different kinds of institutions.

They nevertheless illuminate the same infrastructural point.

Governance does not have to operate by simply arresting the person selling sexual services.

It can operate through:

- intermediary liability;
- platforms;
- advertising;
- payment rails;
- identity verification;
- hosting;
- banking;
- content moderation.

Mastercard's adult-content requirements brought private payment infrastructure directly into questions of identity verification, consent, content review and platform compliance.

FOSTA-SESTA altered the liability environment around online services and sex work.

The consequences of such interventions must be evaluated where the risk goes, not only where legislators or companies intended it to go.

> **If somebody can theoretically speak but cannot advertise, host, receive payment, maintain an account or safely identify clients, infrastructure has already substantially governed their choices.**

## 🚨 Trafficking Language Does Not Remove the Obligation to Think

Human trafficking, coercion and sexual exploitation are real harms.

They require:

- prevention;
- investigation;
- survivor support;
- serious safeguarding.

That does not mean every intervention justified through anti-trafficking language is effective or harmless.

Categories can collapse:

- trafficking;
- coercion;
- exploitation;
- consensual adult sex work;
- pornography;
- sexual expression.

When they do, people whom a policy intends to protect can lose resources and safety infrastructure without the conditions producing their vulnerability disappearing.

The relevant question is not only:

**What harm was this intervention intended to prevent?**

It is also:

**What happened to the people afterwards?**

For somebody doing survival work, removing one available option does not remove:

- rent;
- hunger;
- dependants;
- disability;
- discrimination;
- immigration precarity;
- the need to survive until tomorrow.

It changes the available choices.

> **If you cannot understand how an anti-trafficking intervention could remove essential resources from the very people most exposed to exploitation, you are not yet in a position to design that intervention safely.**

Listen to the people most affected.

Follow the resources.

Follow where the risk moves.

Do not white-knight people out of the things keeping them alive.

## 🪜 Rights Are Not the End of the Question

The disability justice critique of rights-only politics generalises well.

```text
RIGHTS
  ↓
Who formally has access?

JUSTICE
  ↓
What conditions make that access meaningful?

INTERSECTIONAL JUSTICE
  ↓
What happens to the whole person moving through all
of those conditions simultaneously?
```

Examples are easy to find:

- a right to education does not guarantee equitable education;
- internet access does not guarantee meaningful digital power;
- healthcare access does not guarantee being believed;
- reproductive rights do not guarantee material reproductive autonomy;
- employment rights do not guarantee an accessible labour environment.

Formal permission is one layer.

Material possibility is another.

## 💻 The Name Fades; the Practices Do Not

"Cyberfeminism" became less prominent as a label after the dot-com era.

The practices did not disappear.

They proliferated through:

- blogging;
- fandom;
- gaming;
- social media;
- mothers' networks;
- queer and trans communities;
- digital art;
- feminist internet theory;
- communities outside Western technological centres.

The label became less fashionable while digital life became more embedded in ordinary social life.

Women did not leave.

The questions did not leave.

The architecture changed around them.

## 📱 Then the Platform Era Arrives

As digital life moved through:

- social networking;
- smartphones;
- platform consolidation;
- algorithmic feeds;

the political emphasis increasingly moved toward:

- harassment;
- moderation;
- recommender systems;
- platform power;
- online safety.

Those are necessary questions.

But there is a danger in allowing them to become the whole feminist technological imagination.

The political demand can shrink from:

> **What kinds of worlds could technology help us make?**

to:

> **Please make the existing system hurt us less.**

We should keep asking the second question.

We should recover the first.

## ⚡ What Cyberfeminism Got Wrong

There is no need for hagiography.

Cyberfeminist traditions could:

- overestimate cyberspace's capacity to dissolve embodiment;
- underestimate economic barriers to participation;
- insufficiently analyse race;
- insufficiently analyse disability;
- insufficiently analyse class;
- insufficiently analyse colonialism;
- understate the importance of material infrastructure;
- confuse technological possibility with political emancipation.

Some canonical writing was:

- culturally specific;
- academically inaccessible;
- heavily dependent on Western reference points.

Some people it purported to encompass preferred other frameworks entirely.

Failed predictions are not useless.

They tell us which constraints the theory failed to see.

---

## 🌱 What Cyberfeminism Saw Early

Cyberfeminism and its intellectual neighbours also saw things that now look remarkably ordinary:

- bodies and technologies are not cleanly separate;
- digital identity can be multiple and politically consequential;
- categories presented as natural can be institutional and historical;
- networks can create new communities and forms of agency;
- medical technology changes representation as well as bodies;
- technological systems are political environments;
- technological design redistributes power;
- the future of technology is therefore a feminist question.

That is a substantial inheritance.

## 🪬 Hand-Off: Xenofeminism and the Engineered Body

Laboria Cuboniks' *Xenofeminism: A Politics for Alienation* inherits some of these questions and makes the antinaturalist challenge unusually explicit.

Xenofeminism is:

- techno-materialist;
- antinaturalist;
- interested in structural transformation;
- gender-abolitionist in a specific sense.

Gender abolition here should not be flattened into eliminating:

- femininity;
- masculinity;
- individual identities.

The target is gender functioning as a grid through which asymmetric power is organised.

## 🧠 The Analytical Point

Feminist technology politics was never only about:

- representation;
- safety;
- inclusion.

It has also asked:
