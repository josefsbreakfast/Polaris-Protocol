# 🌸 Guide to Online Feminisms
**First created:** 2026-09-07 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-07  
*When somebody says “feminists online”, whom exactly do they mean?*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

There is no single political actor called **online feminism**.

There are feminists online.

There are feminist organisations online.

There are feminist arguments, subcultures, campaigns, professional networks, survivor communities, academic traditions, political coalitions, friendship groups, creators, publications, hashtags, counterpublics and extremely committed reply sections.

They overlap.

They disagree.

They sometimes cooperate for one campaign and fight bitterly over the next.

And platforms can make those disagreements look flatter, larger, more unified, more hostile, or more representative than they actually are.

So when reporting says:

> **“Feminists online reacted...”**

the first useful question is:

> **Which feminists?**

Then:

> **Where? About what? In which tradition or coalition? And how do we know they are representative of anything beyond the material we can actually see?**

This node is a field guide, not a complete history of feminist theory.

Its job is to stop a plural political ecology becoming a fictitious single actor.

---

## 🌿 Feminism Is Already Plural Before It Reaches The Internet

Online fragmentation did not invent feminist disagreement.

Feminist traditions have long disagreed about:

- the relationship between sex and gender;
- capitalism and class;
- race and colonialism;
- sexuality;
- the family;
- reproductive labour;
- pornography;
- prostitution and sex work;
- policing and prisons;
- disability;
- religion;
- nationalism;
- trans politics;
- state power;
- reform versus structural transformation;
- and what liberation itself is supposed to require.

The internet did not place one coherent movement into a machine and break it.

It placed **already heterogeneous political traditions** into new infrastructures for speech, coalition, visibility, conflict and mobilisation.

That distinction matters.

## 🗺️ A Rough Field Guide

These categories are deliberately broad.

People can belong to several traditions at once. Organisations can change. Coalitions can be tactical. Labels can be accepted, rejected, imposed by opponents, or mean different things in different countries.

Treat this as orientation, not taxonomy carved into stone.

### 🌷 Liberal Feminisms

Often emphasise equal rights, representation, legal reform, access to institutions, anti-discrimination protections and women's ability to participate on equal terms within existing political and economic structures.

Online work may cluster around:

- workplace discrimination;
- representation;
- reproductive rights;
- political participation;
- harassment;
- equal access;
- institutional reform.

Critics from other feminist traditions may argue that liberal approaches insufficiently challenge deeper structures of class, race, family, labour or economic power.

That disagreement is internal to feminism, not evidence that one side is secretly not feminist.

---

### 🧱 Radical Feminisms

Radical feminist traditions generally treat women's oppression as structurally rooted in sex, gender hierarchy, male power, sexuality, reproduction or patriarchal social organisation.

Different radical feminists disagree substantially among themselves.

Online disputes involving radical feminism can become especially intense around:

- pornography;
- prostitution and sex work;
- sexual violence;
- reproductive politics;
- gender;
- trans inclusion;
- male violence;
- separatism;
- institutional power.

Do not use **radical feminist** as a synonym for every feminist position that sounds severe, anti-male, gender-critical or controversial.

Name the actual argument.

---

### 🧶 Socialist, Marxist and Materialist Feminisms

These traditions place particular emphasis on the relationship between gendered oppression and material organisation:

- paid work;
- unpaid labour;
- reproduction;
- property;
- capitalism;
- class;
- welfare;
- care;
- housing;
- economic dependency.

Online discussion may therefore connect apparently “women's issues” to:

```text
rent
wages
childcare
healthcare
care work
employment
migration
public services
family formation
```

The important point is that gender is being analysed through material relations, not treated as a free-floating culture war.

---

### 🪡 Black and Intersectional Feminisms

Black feminist traditions have long challenged accounts of “women” that silently centre white women and accounts of race that silently centre men.

Intersectional analysis asks how structures such as race, gender, class, disability and other forms of power can interact rather than operating as isolated lanes.

Online this can make visible experiences that older mass-media or institutional feminist spaces excluded.

It can also produce a recurring distortion:

> **intersectionality becomes internet shorthand for “include every identity”.**

That is much thinner than using intersectional analysis to examine how structures and institutions produce differently situated forms of vulnerability, power or exclusion.

---

### 🌈 Queer Feminisms

Queer feminist traditions often examine how gender, sexuality, normativity, family structures, bodies and social categories are produced and regulated.

Online queer feminist spaces may overlap heavily with:

- LGBTQ+ organising;
- trans politics;
- sexual-health activism;
- anti-carceral politics;
- disability communities;
- sex-worker organising;
- cultural criticism.

They are not politically uniform.

“Queer feminist” tells you something about a field of analysis and coalition.

It does not tell you somebody's position on every feminist dispute.

---

### 🏳️‍⚧️ Trans Feminisms

Trans feminist traditions examine gendered power with trans lives and experiences included within the analytical frame rather than treated as an external problem for feminism to solve.

They may address:

- misogyny and transmisogyny;
- healthcare;
- violence;
- employment;
- reproductive autonomy;
- bodily governance;
- family;
- policing;
- media representation;
- gendered institutions.

Trans feminists may also disagree with one another about theory, law, tactics and coalition.

Do not turn trans feminism into merely **the opposing team** in reporting about gender-critical feminism.

That reproduces the conflict frame before explaining either body of thought.

---

### ♿ Disability Feminisms

Disability feminist analysis examines how gender interacts with disability, illness, dependency, care, bodily autonomy and institutional power.

It can expose assumptions hidden inside apparently universal feminist demands.

For example:

- Who is expected to perform unpaid care?
- Who can physically access a protest?
- Who can leave an abusive household?
- Whose reproductive choices are respected?
- When does “independence” become a demand to survive without support?
- Who is believed about their own body?

Online spaces can be especially important for disabled feminist participation because digital access may enable forms of political and social involvement that physical spaces do not.

That makes platform accessibility itself part of the politics.

---

### 💋 Sex-Worker-Led and Sex-Positive Feminisms

These are related but not identical traditions.

Sex-worker-led feminism centres the political knowledge, rights, safety and material conditions of people who sell sexual services.

Sex-positive feminist traditions broadly resist treating sexuality, sexual pleasure or sexually explicit expression as inherently degrading to women.

Online debates may concern:

- decriminalisation;
- labour rights;
- platform bans;
- payment processors;
- pornography;
- sexual autonomy;
- exploitation;
- trafficking;
- censorship.

These debates can become exceptionally polarised because other feminist traditions may understand the same industries through frameworks of male power, coercion or exploitation.

Do not collapse:

```text
SEX WORKER
        =
SEX-POSITIVE FEMINIST

or

CRITIC OF THE SEX INDUSTRY
        =
RADICAL FEMINIST
```

Establish the actual position.

---

### 🌍 Postcolonial, Decolonial and Global Feminisms

These traditions challenge feminist frameworks that universalise political assumptions drawn from particular Western histories.

They may examine:

- colonialism;
- empire;
- war;
- development;
- borders;
- labour;
- migration;
- religion;
- nationalism;
- humanitarianism;
- racialised ideas of rescue.

Online international coalitions can make these traditions more visible to one another.

They can also create context collapse when a concept developed within one political or historical setting is rapidly imported into another.

Internationalism is not the same thing as universal sameness.

---

### 🪴 Ecofeminisms and Other Traditions

This list is not exhaustive.

Feminist politics also includes ecofeminist, anarchist, abolitionist, religious, Indigenous, psychoanalytic, cultural, conservative or reformist traditions and many hybrid formations that resist tidy placement.

The point is not to build the perfect family tree.

It is to make one reporting error harder:

> **“Feminists believe X.”**

Probably some do.

Which ones?

---

## 🕸️ Coalitions Are Not The Same Thing As Traditions

A political coalition can contain people with incompatible theories.

People who profoundly disagree about sex work may campaign together on abortion access.

People who disagree about policing may support the same survivor in a particular case.

People who disagree about gender may oppose the same piece of surveillance legislation.

People who would never join the same feminist organisation may share a hashtag for forty-eight hours.

So:

```text
SHARED CAMPAIGN
        ≠
SHARED IDEOLOGY

SHARED HASHTAG
        ≠
SHARED ORGANISATION

SHARED ENEMY
        ≠
SHARED POLITICAL PROJECT
```

Coalitions are often **issue-specific and temporary**.

That is normal politics.

---

## #️⃣ Hashtags Can Aggregate Without Organising

A hashtag can:

- make testimony searchable;
- aggregate dispersed experiences;
- create a campaign marker;
- help journalists locate participants;
- connect strangers;
- produce a sense of scale;
- allow counter-speech to gather around the same term.

It can also make thousands of unrelated acts of posting look like one centrally organised movement.

A hashtag has no executive committee merely because it has a name.

Ask:

- Who coined it?
- Who amplified it?
- Was there an organisation behind it?
- Did participants share demands?
- Was there moderation or governance?
- Did its meaning change as it spread?

---

## 🗣️ Testimony Aggregation Changes What Becomes Visible

One of the most politically important affordances of networked media is the ability to place many individually familiar experiences beside one another.

A single account may be dismissed as:

```text
unfortunate
personal
ambiguous
isolated
```

Thousands of recognisably similar accounts can produce a different question:

> **Is this a pattern?**

That can alter:

- journalism;
- institutional response;
- public vocabulary;
- survivor recognition;
- research questions;
- political agendas.

But aggregation also requires evidential discipline.

Repeated testimony can reveal a social pattern without proving every allegation, every causal explanation or every proposed remedy.

Recognition and adjudication are different functions.

---

## 🌐 Decentralised Organising Is Still Organising

Online feminist mobilisation often lacks the organisational forms older political reporting expects.

There may be:

- no headquarters;
- no membership list;
- no single spokesperson;
- no agreed manifesto;
- no leader authorised to negotiate for everyone.

That does not mean nothing organised is happening.

Coordination can occur through:

- shared documents;
- group chats;
- hashtags;
- informal networks;
- creator ecosystems;
- professional groups;
- local chapters;
- mutual aid;
- repeated norms and practices.

But decentralisation should not be romanticised either.

Informal systems can reproduce hierarchy while making power harder to locate.

Ask:

> **Who can actually set norms, direct attention, exclude people, move resources or define the story?**

---

## 🌍 International Coalition Changes Scale

Digital networks allow feminist campaigns, concepts and testimony to cross borders rapidly.

That can:

- connect movements;
- circulate tactics;
- make local abuses internationally visible;
- create solidarity;
- allow diaspora participation;
- challenge national media silos.

It can also flatten context.

A legal term, racial category, feminist dispute or policing practice may not travel cleanly between countries.

The fact that two movements use the same English-language hashtag does not mean their political environments are equivalent.

---

## 📣 Call-Outs Are A Political Form, Not One Behaviour

“Call-out culture” is often used as though it names one stable practice.

It can include very different things:

- public criticism;
- survivor disclosure;
- evidential documentation;
- accountability demands;
- intra-movement dispute;
- ridicule;
- ostracism;
- harassment;
- dogpiling;
- warning others about risk.

These should not be morally or analytically bundled merely because they occur in public.

Ask what happened.

Then name it.

---

## 🪺 Counterpublics Let Marginalised People Build Their Own Publics

Mainstream public spheres do not distribute attention or credibility equally.

Online feminist spaces can function as **counterpublics**: places where people excluded, trivialised or misdescribed elsewhere can develop language, analysis, community and political claims among themselves before addressing a wider public.

This matters particularly for:

- survivors;
- disabled people;
- queer and trans communities;
- racialised women;
- sex workers;
- geographically isolated people;
- people excluded from professional or institutional platforms.

Counterpublics are not automatically virtuous.

They can develop their own hierarchies, exclusions and bad information.

But analysing them only as echo chambers misses their democratic function.

---

## 🚀 Virality Changes Which Feminism Becomes Visible

The feminism most visible online is not necessarily:

- the largest;
- the most representative;
- the most institutionally powerful;
- the most theoretically influential;
- or the most common among women.

It may simply be the material best adapted to the platform.

Virality tends to favour things that are:

- emotionally legible;
- surprising;
- conflict-rich;
- compressible;
- visually recognisable;
- identity-signalling;
- easy to quote;
- easy to oppose.

This can make feminist politics look like a sequence of maximalist declarations when much feminist work is comparatively boring:

```text
committee meetings
service provision
research
union organising
casework
policy drafting
legal advocacy
mutual aid
teaching
```

The boring work is still politics.

## 🫗 Context Collapse Makes Different Audiences Collide

A feminist argument may begin inside a small community whose members share:

- vocabulary;
- history;
- assumptions;
- humour;
- previous disputes.

Then somebody screenshots it.

Now the audience includes:

- opponents;
- journalists;
- employers;
- politicians;
- strangers;
- automated systems;
- people in other countries.

The original statement has not changed.

Its interpretive environment has.

This is **context collapse**.

It helps explain why online political language can look bizarrely absolute when extracted from the conversation in which it developed.

Context is not an excuse for everything.

It is evidence.

---

## 🎰 Platform Incentives Reward Some Feminist Behaviours Over Others

Platforms are not neutral containers for pre-existing politics.

Their design can shape:

- what becomes visible;
- which disputes recur;
- which creators become central;
- how quickly conflict escalates;
- whether nuance travels;
- how communities monetise attention;
- how moderation affects participation.

A platform that rewards engagement may repeatedly surface conflict because conflict generates engagement.

A short-form platform may reward compressed claims.

A creator economy may reward recognisable political brands.

A recommendation system may repeatedly connect adjacent communities.

None of this means:

> **the algorithm caused feminism to fragment.**

It means political behaviour occurs inside technical systems with incentives and affordances worth examining.

---

## 🧩 Movement Fragmentation Can Be Real Without Meaning Movement Failure

Online feminism can fragment around:

- ideology;
- identity;
- generation;
- geography;
- platform;
- tactics;
- personality;
- institutional affiliation;
- specific controversies.

Some fragmentation is destructive.

Some reflects genuine political disagreement.

Some creates more specialised communities.

Some allows people previously marginalised inside feminist movements to build their own institutions.

Some is simply what happens when millions of people are no longer represented by a handful of gatekeepers.

Do not assume:

```text
MORE VISIBLE DISAGREEMENT
        =
LESS POLITICAL CAPACITY
```

Sometimes visibility reveals conflict that already existed.

---

## 🥊 Countermobilisation Is Part Of The Ecology

Online feminist politics does not develop alone.

It develops alongside:

- anti-feminist movements;
- misogynistic communities;
- men's-rights politics;
- conservative movements;
- religious movements;
- nationalist politics;
- platform entrepreneurs;
- trolls;
- harassment networks;
- critics from elsewhere on the left;
- commercial media.

Opposition can influence feminist strategy and internal politics.

A movement repeatedly attacked on one issue may become organised around defending that boundary.

A hostile screenshot economy can reward defensive purity.

A harassment campaign can alter who remains willing to speak.

So when analysing feminist fragmentation, ask what **countermobilisation** is doing to the movement too.

## 🔮 Platform Visibility Is Not Movement Representation

This deserves its own rule.

```text
WHAT THE PLATFORM SHOWED YOU
        ≠
WHAT FEMINISTS THINK
```

Nor does:

```text
WHAT WENT VIRAL
        =
WHAT MOST WOMEN THINK
```

Nor:

```text
TEN ACCOUNTS WITH LARGE FOLLOWINGS
        =
A MOVEMENT LEADERSHIP
```

Useful reporting asks about:

- sampling;
- platform demographics;
- organisational membership;
- polling;
- institutional influence;
- offline activity;
- selection effects.

If the evidence is ten viral posts, report ten viral posts.

Do not promote them into the Central Committee of Women.

## 📰 A Quick Guide For Journalists

Before writing **“feminists online...”**, ask:

1. **Which feminist tradition or coalition is actually visible here?**
2. **Are these organisations, activists, creators, academics, survivors, anonymous accounts, or a mixture?**
3. **Is the evidence a hashtag, a sample of posts, organisational statements, polling, interviews, or something else?**
4. **Does the group have a shared position, or only a shared moment of attention?**
5. **Is the platform amplifying conflict-rich material?**
6. **Are opponents or countermovements shaping what becomes visible?**
7. **Has context collapsed between communities or countries?**
8. **Does online visibility correspond to offline organisation or institutional influence?**
9. **Are you describing a feminist disagreement as though one side represents feminism itself?**
10. **Could you replace “feminists” with the actual noun?**

Usually, question ten improves the sentence immediately.

Instead of:

> **Feminists criticised the policy.**

try:

> **Several women's-rights organisations criticised the policy.**

or:

> **A group of trans feminist activists criticised the policy.**

or:

> **Posts using the campaign hashtag criticised the policy.**

or:

> **The proposal divided feminist organisations.**

Those are different claims.

Good.

## 🧭 What This Node Does Not Attempt

This node does not decide:

- which feminist tradition is correct;
- which theory best explains women's oppression;
- which coalition should dominate;
- whether every claimed feminist position deserves the label;
- or how every historical feminist dispute should be resolved.

That would require several libraries and at least one extremely tense conference.

Its narrower job is methodological:

> **Do not turn political plurality into a fictional unitary actor merely because the participants all use the internet.**

## 🧠 The Useful Mental Model

Do not imagine:

```text
              ONLINE FEMINISM
                    │
                    ▼
             ONE POLITICAL VIEW
```

Imagine something closer to:

```text
feminist traditions
        ↕
organisations
        ↕
survivor networks
        ↕
campaign coalitions
        ↕
creators and publications
        ↕
hashtags and counterpublics
        ↕
platform incentives
        ↕
opponents and countermovements
        ↕
offline institutions and politics
```

The network changes over time.

The edges matter.

The disagreements are part of the object being studied.

## 🌸 The Question To Keep

When somebody says:

> **“Feminists online...”**

ask:

> **Whom exactly do they mean?**

If the answer is:

> **well, you know, feminists**

the reporting is not finished.
