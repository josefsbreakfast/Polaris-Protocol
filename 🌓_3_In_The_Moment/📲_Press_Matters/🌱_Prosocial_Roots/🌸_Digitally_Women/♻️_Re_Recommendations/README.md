# ♻️ Re: Recommendations  
**First created:** 2026-09-06 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-06  
*When institutions keep being told recognisably similar things after serious failures, what happened between recommendation and recurrence?*

---

## 🛰️ Start Here

This folder is about a deceptively simple problem.

Across UK inquiries, inspections, super-complaints, independent reviews and major institutional investigations concerning violence against women and girls, sexual violence, domestic abuse, stalking, predatory behaviour, police misconduct and safeguarding, some recommendations recur.

Again.

And again.

That observation is useful.

But:

> **“They were told this before” is the beginning of the analysis, not the end of it.**

A recurring recommendation can mean several different things.

```text
recommendation
      ↓
not implemented

or

implemented partially

or

implemented locally but not universally

or

implemented successfully
      ↓
failure moved downstream

or

implemented successfully
      ↓
later decayed

or

works ordinarily
      ↓
fails under status / institutional pressure

or

looks similar
      ↓
actually addresses a different failure

or

public evidence is insufficient
to know what happened
```

Those are not interchangeable diagnoses.

This cluster exists to tell them apart.

---

## ♻️ The Governing Question

The folder asks:

> **When institutions keep being told recognisably similar things after serious failures, what happened between recommendation and recurrence?**

That means following recommendations beyond publication.

Not only:

```text
WHAT WAS RECOMMENDED?
```

but:

```text
WHO OWNED IT?

WAS IT ACCEPTED?

WAS IT IMPLEMENTED?

DID IMPLEMENTATION CREATE
THE INTENDED CAPABILITY?

DID THE CAPABILITY SURVIVE
THE NEXT CASE?

COULD INFORMATION CROSS
THE NECESSARY JOINS?

DID IT SURVIVE TIME,
RESTRUCTURE AND STAFF TURNOVER?

DID IT SURVIVE STATUS,
REPUTATION AND INCONVENIENCE?

DID THE INSTITUTION LEARN?
```

The object is not to produce a larger pile of recommendations.

It is to understand what makes a safeguard **operational, durable and dependable**.

## ♻️ [`as_per_my_last_review`](./♻️_as_per_my_last_review.md)

### The receipts

This is the evidential spine of the folder.

It crosswalks recurring institutional safeguards and recommendations across relevant UK inquiries, reviews, inspections and major investigations, concentrating primarily on approximately 2015–September 2026 while retaining older landmark material where later work explicitly revisits or tests it.

The research is interested in recurring families including:

- pattern recognition across incidents;
- information sharing and retrieval;
- stalking and harassment;
- domestic abuse;
- sexual offending and predatory behaviour;
- police vetting and misconduct;
- professional standards;
- escalation and risk assessment;
- survivor reporting and institutional response;
- audit, supervision and implementation;
- institutional ownership.

Its job is not merely to notice that two reviews used similar words.

It asks whether later material:

- repeats an earlier safeguard;
- tests implementation;
- identifies partial implementation;
- identifies a different failure in the same chain;
- demonstrates improvement;
- demonstrates decay;
- leaves the outcome unknown.

This is where the analytical nodes get their receipts.

## ♻️ [`why_are_we_recommending_this_again`](./♻️_why_are_we_recommending_this_again.md)

### Recurrence is a diagnostic question

This node takes the crosswalk and asks what recurrence actually means.

Its central move is to reject the lazy equation:

```text
RECOMMENDATION REPEATED
        =
INSTITUTION IGNORED IT
```

Sometimes that is true.

Sometimes it is not.

The missing hinge may instead be:

- ownership;
- implementation;
- coverage;
- training;
- supervision;
- technology;
- information retrieval;
- synthesis;
- escalation;
- audit;
- corrective action;
- maintenance;
- authority.

The useful question is:

> **Where did the recommendation-to-safeguard chain break?**

That question structures everything downstream.

## 🧠 [`did_the_safeguard_survive_the_next_case`](./🧠_did_the_safeguard_survive_the_next_case.md)

### A policy document is an artefact. A safeguard is a capability.

Acceptance is not implementation.

Implementation is not embedding.

Training is not necessarily changed behaviour.

Audit is not corrective action.

And none of those things alone establishes that the safeguard existed when somebody next needed it.

The stress test is:

```text
recommendation
        ↓
owner
        ↓
formal response
        ↓
implementation
        ↓
operational change
        ↓
training
        ↓
measurement
        ↓
supervision
        ↓
audit
        ↓
corrective action
        ↓
NEXT RELEVANT CASE
```

Then ask:

> **When the safeguard was needed again, was institutional behaviour actually different?**

The node also keeps a crucial discipline:

> **UNKNOWN is a finding.**

Where public evidence cannot establish durability or effectiveness, the analysis should say so.

## 🕸️ [`everyone_had_a_piece`](./🕸️_everyone_had_a_piece.md)

### Information can exist without a system possessing the picture

Modern safeguarding is distributed.

Police, employers, professional standards, safeguarding teams, specialist services and other bodies may each hold legitimate pieces of information.

The central distinction is:

```text
EVERYBODY KNEW SOMETHING
        ≠
ANYBODY KNEW ENOUGH
        ≠
SOMEBODY OWNED THE WHOLE RISK
```

This node therefore moves beyond generic calls for “better information sharing.”

Information must be:

```text
sent
received
recorded
retrievable
connected
interpreted
escalated
acted upon
```

And responsible synthesis requires:

- provenance;
- identity resolution;
- duplicate-source recognition;
- correction;
- lawful and proportionate access;
- competing explanations;
- end-to-end ownership.

Five copies of one allegation are still one origin.

A large file is not automatically a good risk assessment.

The governing question becomes:

> **Who owned the meaning of the pieces together?**

Or, more operationally:

> **Measure the joins, not only the nodes.**

## 📦 [`recommendation_decay`](./📦_recommendation_decay.md)

### Installation is an event. Maintenance is a function.

Some safeguards really are implemented.

They work.

Then they weaken.

This node distinguishes:

```text
FAILED IMPLEMENTATION
```

from:

```text
IMPLEMENTED
        ↓
OPERATIONAL
        ↓
WORKING
        ↓
TIME / CHANGE
        ↓
DEGRADATION
```

Decay can follow:

- staff turnover;
- training drift;
- funding cliffs;
- restructuring;
- technology replacement;
- measurement drift;
- audit fatigue;
- workaround fossilisation;
- ownership drift;
- loss of scandal-level attention.

It introduces the practical idea of **safeguard half-life**:

> **How long can this protection remain dependable without refresh, audit, rehearsal, investment or use?**

And it makes change control part of safeguarding:

> **Which existing safeguards depend on the thing we are changing?**

A software migration, restructure or outsourcing decision should not be allowed to delete a protection merely because nobody remembered that the protection depended on the old architecture.

The next scandal should not be the monitoring system.

## ⚖️ [`status_reputation_and_safeguarding`](./⚖️_status_reputation_and_safeguarding.md)

### Does the safeguard survive prestige?

Safeguarding systems encounter people with different amounts of power.

Rank, wealth, authority, institutional value, expertise, celebrity, political access and social reputation can all affect how information is interpreted.

So can the reputation of the person reporting.

The test is:

```text
SAME INFORMATION
      +
DIFFERENT SUBJECT
      =
DIFFERENT INSTITUTIONAL RESPONSE?
```

The node distinguishes legitimate power-sensitive risk assessment from prestige deference.

Its core rule is:

> **Status may change the risk assessment. It should not silently change the evidential rules.**

It examines:

- threshold drift;
- sunk institutional costs;
- reputation as pseudo-evidence;
- selective safeguard decay;
- conflicts of interest;
- differential scrutiny;
- counterfactual testing;
- structural independence.

It also preserves the inverse safeguard: negative information must be capable of correction.

Integrated safeguarding cannot become a permanent suspicion ratchet.

The endpoint is not naïve identical treatment.

It is:

> **Greater power should not purchase weaker scrutiny — and the additional risks created by power should actually be assessed.**

## 🧭 [`institutional_learning_or_crisis_absorption`](./🧭_institutional_learning_or_crisis_absorption.md)

### The capstone

Institutions need to survive crises.

But surviving a crisis is not the same as learning from it.

A system can become very good at:

- commissioning reviews;
- accepting recommendations;
- producing action plans;
- delivering training;
- reorganising teams;
- creating dashboards;
- documenting decisions;
- providing assurance;
- restoring institutional equilibrium.

All of that can involve serious, sincere work.

The question is where the work terminates.

### Crisis absorption

```text
CRISIS
        ↓
INSTITUTION RESPONDS
        ↓
STABILITY / LEGITIMACY / FUNCTION
RECOVER
```

### Institutional learning

```text
FAILURE MECHANISM UNDERSTOOD
        ↓
CAPABILITY CHANGES
        ↓
BEHAVIOUR CHANGES
        ↓
CHANGE IS TESTED
        ↓
WEAKNESS IS CORRECTED
        ↓
CAPABILITY SURVIVES
        ↓
NEXT CASE IS HANDLED DIFFERENTLY
```

A system can become more auditable without becoming more capable.

An organisation can also learn the wrong lesson: becoming excellent at crisis management, communications or procedural defensibility while remaining poor at recognising the original risk.

The final test is therefore:

> **What can the institution reliably do now that it could not reliably do before?**

## 🧬 The Diagnostic Vocabulary

Taken together, the folder gives us a more precise vocabulary for recurring recommendations.

### 🔴 Non-implementation

The safeguard was recommended but never became operational.

### 🟠 Partial implementation

Meaningful implementation occurred, but coverage or embedding remained incomplete.

### 🗺️ Local variation

The safeguard exists in some institutions or areas but is not reliably available across the system.

### 🪜 Downstream migration

The original bottleneck was improved, exposing a later failure in the chain.

For example:

```text
policy fixed
        ↓
training becomes bottleneck
        ↓
training fixed
        ↓
information retrieval becomes bottleneck
        ↓
retrieval fixed
        ↓
synthesis becomes bottleneck
```

A familiar recommendation family can therefore recur even where earlier work produced genuine progress.

### 🕸️ Integration failure

The relevant pieces existed but the architecture did not produce sufficient synthesis, ownership or action.

### 📦 Decay

A previously operational safeguard weakened over time.

### ⚖️ Status-contingent failure

The safeguard operates ordinarily but weakens around rank, prestige, reputation, institutional value or conflict.

### 🧩 New failure mode

A later recommendation resembles an earlier one but addresses a materially different mechanism.

### ⚪ Insufficient evidence

The available public record does not establish what happened.

### 🟢 Durable learning

The safeguard became operational, remained operational and produced evidence of changed institutional behaviour when later tested.

That is a much more useful taxonomy than:

```text
DONE
/
NOT DONE
```

## 🧪 The Cluster-Wide Stress Test

For any recurring recommendation, ask in order:

### ♻️ Recurrence

- Where have we seen this recommendation family before?
- Is it genuinely the same protected function?

### 🧬 Mechanism

- What failure was the recommendation supposed to correct?
- What capability was supposed to exist afterwards?

### 🧠 Implementation

- Who owned it?
- Was it accepted?
- Was it implemented?
- Did it change operational behaviour?

### 🕸️ Integration

- Did relevant information reach the right place?
- Could it be retrieved and connected?
- Was provenance preserved?
- Who owned the combined picture?

### 📊 Assurance

- Was the protected function measured?
- Was it audited?
- Did findings produce corrective action?

### 📦 Durability

- Did the safeguard survive staff turnover?
- Funding change?
- Restructure?
- New software?
- Loss of public attention?

### ⚖️ Status resilience

- Did the same evidential discipline survive powerful or institutionally valuable subjects?
- Were conflicts recognised and independently scrutinised?

### 🧪 Reality

- What happened in the next relevant case?

### 🧭 Learning

- Can the institution demonstrate a durable capability it did not reliably possess before?

That sequence turns recommendation recurrence into an inspectable implementation history.

## 🪴 What This Folder Is Not Saying

This is not an argument that:

> **nothing ever changes.**

The evidence includes real implementation, improvement and institutional learning.

Nor is it an argument that:

> **every later failure proves an earlier review was useless.**

A reform can succeed and later decay.

It can solve one bottleneck and expose another.

It can work in most places and remain incomplete elsewhere.

And a superficially similar recommendation can address a different mechanism.

The point is precisely to preserve those distinctions.

Otherwise criticism becomes less accurate — and successful reforms become harder to learn from.

## 🌱 Why This Matters

VAWG and safeguarding policy repeatedly confronts a difficult problem.

After a serious case, the public record can accumulate:

```text
review
recommendations
response
action plan
inspection
new review
new recommendations
```

Without a longitudinal implementation view, every document risks being read as a new beginning.

It is not.

Institutions have histories.

Safeguards have histories.

Recommendations have implementation histories.

And later cases can tell us whether those histories became operational memory.

This folder therefore treats the **space between reviews** as evidence.
