# 📦 Recommendation Decay

**First created:** 2026-09-06 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-06  
*A safeguard can be genuinely installed, genuinely useful, and still disappear by degrees.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Not every recurring recommendation proves that the first recommendation failed.

Sometimes the earlier safeguard was:

- accepted;
- implemented;
- embedded into practice;
- useful;
- and demonstrably operational.

Then time passed.

Staff changed. Teams moved. Funding ended. Software was replaced. Training became stale. Audit slipped. A workaround became permanent. The people who remembered why an odd-looking procedure existed left the building.

And eventually the next case exposed a familiar failure.

That is a different phenomenon from failed implementation.

It is **recommendation decay**.

> **Installation is an event. Maintenance is a function.**

---

## 📦 Failure and Decay Are Not the Same Thing

Keep the distinction clean.

### 🧱 Failed implementation

```text
recommendation
        ↓
acceptance / attempted implementation
        ↓
capability never becomes dependable
```

The safeguard never really arrives.

### 📦 Recommendation decay

```text
recommendation
        ↓
implementation
        ↓
capability becomes operational
        ↓
works / is evidenced
        ↓
time passes
        ↓
maintenance weakens
        ↓
capability degrades
        ↓
next case exposes the loss
```

The safeguard **did** arrive.

The institution failed to keep it.

Those diagnoses imply different remedies.

If implementation never occurred, finish the implementation.

If a functioning safeguard decayed, find out what sustained it, what changed, and why the maintenance architecture did not catch the loss.

---

## 🧠 First Prove That It Existed

Decay should not become a polite label for an implementation gap.

Before calling something recommendation decay, look for evidence that the protected capability genuinely operated.

That might include:

- implementation records;
- inspection findings;
- case audits;
- operational data;
- evidence of changed practice;
- independent evaluation;
- later cases in which the safeguard worked;
- sustained use over some period.

This is why [`🧠 Did the Safeguard Survive the Next Case?`](./🧠_did_the_safeguard_survive_the_next_case.md) comes first.

The sequence is:

```text
DID IT EXIST?
        ↓
DID IT WORK?
        ↓
DID IT KEEP WORKING?
```

Only the third question is decay.

---

## 🕰️ Safeguards Have Maintenance Needs

A safeguard is not necessarily permanent merely because it has been embedded once.

Some capabilities depend on continual renewal.

A useful way to think about this is **safeguard half-life**.

Not as a fake precision metric.

As a maintenance question:

> **How long can this protection remain dependable without refresh, audit, rehearsal, investment or use?**

Different safeguards will have different answers.

A statutory duty may remain formally stable for years while operational knowledge around it drifts.

A specialist skill may degrade quickly without practice.

A database control may work until the next software migration.

A multi-agency pathway may weaken after staff turnover.

A dedicated team may disappear at the next restructure.

A training intervention may have a very short useful life if new starters never receive it.

So maintenance cadence should follow the nature of the capability.

---

## 🧬 The Decay Mechanisms

Recommendation decay can happen through several routes.

### 🧑‍💼 Staff turnover

The people trained during the reform period leave.

Their replacements inherit the job but not necessarily the same:

- training;
- context;
- supervision;
- institutional memory.

A safeguard dependent on particular people is fragile unless the capability survives recruitment and succession.

### 🎓 Training drift

A course exists.

Then:

- refresher training stops;
- completion becomes the metric;
- content ages;
- local adaptations diverge;
- new staff miss the original programme;
- supervisors no longer reinforce it.

The training has not technically vanished.

Its operational effect may have.

### 💷 Funding cliffs

A reform receives:

- pilot funding;
- transformation funding;
- scandal-response funding;
- a fixed-term grant.

The capability grows.

Then the dedicated money ends.

If the work was never transferred into durable baseline funding, the safeguard may contract with it.

### 🧱 Restructuring

Teams merge.

Functions move.

Responsibilities are rewritten.

A specialist post disappears.

A national programme becomes business as usual.

Every individual organisational change may be defensible.

The combined effect can quietly sever the safeguard's original chain.

### 💾 Technology replacement

A system can support a safeguard perfectly well until somebody replaces the system.

Then a field disappears.

A flag no longer migrates.

Search behaves differently.

Historic data becomes awkward to retrieve.

An integration breaks.

The policy survives the migration.

The capability does not.

### 📊 Measurement drift

What gets measured can change.

A safeguard initially has:

- explicit indicators;
- dashboards;
- quality checks;
- senior attention.

Later those measures are merged into broader reporting or disappear.

Once nobody can see whether the capability is weakening, deterioration becomes easier to miss.

### 🧾 Audit fatigue

A new safeguard may be audited intensively after a scandal.

Over time:

```text
quarterly
    ↓
annual
    ↓
occasional
    ↓
when something goes wrong
```

The absence of bad news begins to substitute for evidence that the safeguard still works.

### 🩹 Workaround fossilisation

A temporary workaround is introduced while the real solution is built.

Then the workaround becomes normal.

The underlying dependency is never repaired.

Years later everybody assumes the awkward process is the safeguard.

It may be much less reliable than the intended design.

### 🧭 Ownership drift

The original owner leaves.

Responsibility moves between teams.

The organisational chart changes.

Nobody explicitly abolishes the safeguard.

Nobody is quite sure who now owns its continued operation either.

### 📰 Salience decay

The original scandal created urgency.

Ministers asked questions.

Senior leaders watched the metrics.

Resources moved.

Training happened.

Then public attention moved elsewhere.

The safeguard had been held partly in place by **attention**.

When attention disappeared, the organisation discovered whether it had actually built maintenance underneath it.

## 🧠 Institutional Memory Is More Than an Archive

A report sitting on a website is not institutional memory.

A recommendation database is not necessarily institutional memory either.

Useful institutional memory preserves:

```text
WHAT WAS THE FAILURE?

WHY WAS THIS SAFEGUARD CREATED?

WHAT FUNCTION MUST NOT BE LOST?

WHO OWNS IT NOW?

WHAT DOES IT DEPEND ON?

HOW DO WE KNOW IT STILL WORKS?
```

That explanatory layer matters.

Otherwise a future team may inherit a peculiar-looking procedural step with no idea why it exists.

Then someone quite reasonably asks:

> **Do we still need to do this?**

Perhaps the step is redundant.

Perhaps it is inefficient.

Or perhaps it exists because the organisation previously discovered, at considerable human cost, what happens without it.

Institutional memory lets the next team know which question it is answering.

---

## ✂️ Beware the Efficiency Review

Decay can arrive wearing the clothes of sensible administration.

A new leader sees:

- duplicate checks;
- manual review;
- specialist sign-off;
- a seemingly redundant field;
- an expensive dedicated team;
- an awkward escalation stage.

The organisation wants to simplify.

Good.

Some safeguards should be redesigned.

But before deleting friction, ask:

> **Is this friction accidental bureaucracy, or is it deliberately carrying risk?**

A safeguard may look inefficient precisely because it requires the system to stop, connect information, seek another view or document a decision.

Efficiency work therefore needs a **safeguard impact check**.

Otherwise the institution can optimise itself back into an old failure mode.

---

## 🔧 Change Control Is Safeguarding

This gives us one of the most useful maintenance rules.

Whenever an organisation materially changes:

- case-management software;
- data architecture;
- team structure;
- outsourcing arrangements;
- professional standards;
- vetting;
- referral pathways;
- training;
- audit;
- staffing model;
- national guidance;

somebody should ask:

> **Which existing safeguards depend on the thing we are changing?**

Then test them.

A simple model:

```text
PROPOSED CHANGE
        ↓
DEPENDENCY CHECK
        ↓
AFFECTED SAFEGUARDS IDENTIFIED
        ↓
MIGRATION / MITIGATION PLAN
        ↓
IMPLEMENT CHANGE
        ↓
RE-TEST SAFEGUARD
```

The re-test matters.

A safeguard surviving on the old system tells us nothing about whether it survived the migration.

---

## 💾 The Software Migration Test

Information systems deserve their own version because they can quietly rewrite organisational memory.

Before migration:

- Which fields carry safeguarding meaning?
- Which flags trigger review?
- Which identifiers connect records?
- Which search functions support pattern recognition?
- Which audit trails matter?
- Which corrections or outcomes must travel with historic records?

After migration:

- Did the fields survive?
- Did the flags survive?
- Can staff still search what they could search before?
- Can historic and current records still be connected?
- Did permissions change?
- Did duplicate records appear?
- Did correction history survive?
- Are the audit trails still usable?

A data migration is therefore not only an IT project.

Where systems carry safeguarding functions, it is also a **safeguard migration**.

## 🕸️ Interfaces Decay Too

[`🕸️ Everyone Had a Piece`](./🕸️_everyone_had_a_piece.md) deals with distributed knowledge and integration ownership.

Those joins also require maintenance.

A multi-agency pathway can work because:

- people know each other;
- referral routes are current;
- contact details work;
- thresholds are understood;
- meetings occur;
- somebody chases missing responses.

Then personnel change.

The written protocol remains exactly the same.

The working relationship does not.

So interface maintenance needs its own checks:

```text
Is the route still live?

Does the receiving team still exist?

Are thresholds still aligned?

Do people know who owns the hand-off?

Are referrals acknowledged?

Can unresolved cases still be escalated?
```

The paper pathway can survive while the operational pathway decays.

---

## 📊 Measure Durability, Not Just Launch

Implementation dashboards naturally focus on beginnings.

```text
policy issued
staff trained
forces onboarded
system deployed
actions completed
```

For durability, add later questions.

```text
12 months later — still operating?

24 months later — still operating?

after leadership change — still operating?

after restructure — still operating?

after IT migration — still operating?

after funding change — still operating?

after the next relevant case — still operating?
```

A mature implementation programme should eventually become a maintenance programme.

Otherwise success is measured at precisely the moment when attention is highest.

## 🧪 The Maintenance Architecture

A durable safeguard ideally has:

```text
SAFEGUARD
        ↓
named enduring owner
        ↓
stable operational standard
        ↓
induction + refresher training
        ↓
system dependency register
        ↓
routine measurement
        ↓
periodic audit
        ↓
corrective action
        ↓
change-control test
        ↓
re-test after major change
```

Not every safeguard needs identical machinery.

But the basic functions should exist somewhere.

### 📦 Enduring owner

Someone owns continued capability, not merely implementation completion.

### 📚 Knowledge transfer

New staff inherit the safeguard and the reason for it.

### 🎓 Refresh

Skills and guidance remain current.

### 🧰 Dependency register

The institution knows which systems, teams, contracts and processes the safeguard relies on.

### 📊 Measurement

There is some way to detect deterioration before a catastrophic case does it for us.

### 🧾 Audit

The capability is periodically tested rather than assumed.

### 🔧 Corrective action

Audit findings have owners, deadlines and verification.

### 🔄 Change control

Major organisational changes trigger a safeguard dependency check.

### 🧪 Re-test

After the change, somebody confirms that the protected function still works.

---

## 🚨 The Next Scandal Should Not Be the Monitoring System

Without maintenance architecture, organisations can end up using major failures as their most sensitive quality-assurance mechanism.

The pattern becomes:

```text
safeguard installed
        ↓
attention falls
        ↓
capability weakens
        ↓
no one notices
        ↓
serious case
        ↓
review
        ↓
recommendation
        ↓
safeguard reinstalled
```

That is an extremely expensive way to perform regression testing.

The better question is:

> **What signal should have told us the safeguard was degrading before somebody was harmed?**

That might be:

- audit failure;
- falling training coverage;
- increased case errors;
- unresolved referrals;
- missing data;
- staffing thresholds;
- supervision quality;
- user feedback;
- repeated workarounds;
- rising exception rates.

A maintenance system should be capable of noticing its own weakening.

---

## 🪴 Control Cases Matter Here Too

Decay analysis should preserve successful reform.

If a safeguard:

- improved practice;
- remained operational;
- survived leadership change;
- survived technical change;
- continued to perform years later;

that is valuable evidence.

It tells us what **durable implementation** looks like.

The aim of this cluster is not to prove that institutions inevitably forget.

It is to distinguish:

```text
NEVER IMPLEMENTED

from

IMPLEMENTED BUT UNEVEN

from

IMPLEMENTED AND DURABLE

from

IMPLEMENTED THEN DEGRADED
```

Without those distinctions, we cannot learn from either failure or success.

## 🧭 A Recommendation-Decaying Checklist

When a familiar failure reappears after an earlier successful reform, ask:

### 🧠 Evidence of prior success

- Was the safeguard actually operational?
- What evidence showed that it worked?
- For how long?
- Was success local or systemic?

### 🕰️ Timeline

- When was it implemented?
- When was it last independently tested?
- When did the later failure occur?
- What changed in between?

### 🧑‍💼 People

- Did key staff leave?
- Was knowledge transferred?
- Did new starters receive equivalent training?
- Did supervision change?

### 💷 Resources

- Did dedicated funding end?
- Did staffing reduce?
- Was the function absorbed into a larger team?
- Did workload make the safeguard impractical?

### 💾 Systems

- Was software replaced?
- Were fields, flags or search functions lost?
- Did data migrate correctly?
- Did access permissions change?

### 🧱 Organisation

- Was there a restructure?
- Did ownership move?
- Did interfaces change?
- Did a specialist function disappear?

### 📊 Assurance

- Were metrics still collected?
- Was audit cadence maintained?
- Were findings acted upon?
- Could deterioration be seen before the later case?

### 🔧 Change control

- Did anybody test the safeguard after major change?
- Was there a dependency register?
- Was regression testing performed?

### 📦 Diagnosis

Then classify:

```text
FAILED IMPLEMENTATION
PARTIAL IMPLEMENTATION
LOCAL VARIATION
DECAY
NEW FAILURE MODE
INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE
```

Do not call it decay unless there is evidence that the safeguard previously functioned.

## ♻️ Why This Changes the Meaning of Recurrence

Suppose Review A recommends a safeguard in 2018.

The institution implements it in 2019.

Audits show improvement in 2020 and 2021.

A restructure occurs in 2022.

Training coverage falls in 2023.

The supporting system changes in 2024.

A 2025 case reveals the old failure mode.

Review B recommends something recognisably similar in 2026.

That history should not be summarised as:

> **They were told this in 2018 and ignored it.**

The more useful account is:

> **The 2018 intervention appears to have produced a functioning safeguard. Subsequent organisational changes weakened the capability, and assurance mechanisms did not detect or correct that deterioration before the later case.**

That is both fairer and more actionable.

The remedy is no longer simply:

> implement Recommendation A.

It becomes:

> **build maintenance and change-control architecture around Recommendation A.**

---

## 🧠 Institutional Learning Has to Survive Personnel

There is a simple test for whether an institution has learned something.

Imagine every person involved in the original review leaves.

Does the safeguard survive?

If the answer is no, the lesson may have been held by people rather than by the institution.

Real institutional learning needs to survive:

- retirements;
- promotions;
- transfers;
- elections;
- ministerial changes;
- chief officer changes;
- reorganisations;
- vendor changes;
- scandal cycles.

That does not mean freezing organisations forever.

It means encoding important learning deeply enough that change does not accidentally erase it.

## 📦 Core Finding

A recommendation does not have only an implementation status.

It has a **maintenance state**.

The full lifecycle is closer to:

```text
recommend
        ↓
accept
        ↓
implement
        ↓
embed
        ↓
operate
        ↓
maintain
        ↓
audit
        ↓
refresh
        ↓
survive change
        ↓
perform in next case
```

The important question is therefore not only:

> **Did we implement it?**

It is:

> **What keeps this safeguard alive after everybody stops talking about the review?**

Because a safeguard that depends indefinitely on scandal-level attention is not yet a durable safeguard.

And if a safeguard matters enough to install, it matters enough to maintain.
