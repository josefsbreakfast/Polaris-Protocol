# 👾 One Man Can Never Be Our Hero
**First created:** 2026-09-06 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-06  
*What Keir Starmer’s record on violence against women and girls can teach us about the danger of making women’s safety dependent on the judgment of powerful individuals.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

This is not a node arguing that Keir Starmer has never done anything useful for women, girls or survivors. That would be both too easy and contradicted by parts of his record.

It asks a harder question.

Starmer is an unusually useful case study because, on paper, he should have been unusually good at this. He was a human-rights lawyer and Director of Public Prosecutions. His tenure at the Crown Prosecution Service confronted serious failures involving rape, child sexual exploitation, survivor credibility and the criminalisation of vulnerable women and girls. Some of those failures produced meaningful corrective reforms. As Prime Minister, he has repeatedly presented violence against women and girls as a major political priority.

And yet women’s and girls’ safety has still repeatedly proved negotiable when it collides with other powerful interests.

That does not make every episode below equivalent. It does not establish a single motive. It does not require the conclusion that Starmer does not care about women. It asks instead what happens when concern for women and girls meets prosecutorial culture, institutional convenience, immigration policy, fiscal priorities, technology companies, diplomatic strategy, powerful political men or the requirements of statecraft.

The recurring question is simple:

> **When the interests of women and girls conflict with an institution or person possessing substantially greater power, which interest is initially protected — and what has to happen before that ordering changes?**

## ⚖️ The Pattern Is Not “Starmer Always Fails”

That would be an inaccurate reading of the record.

There are occasions on which Starmer responded meaningfully to failures affecting survivors. The rape-retraction cases produced changed oversight. Rochdale helped drive changes in how CSE evidence was understood. The later CPS guidance on child sexual abuse contained considerably more sophisticated recognition of grooming, coercion, trauma and the unstable boundary between apparent offending and victimisation. Victims’ Right to Review shifted some power back towards people affected by prosecutorial decisions.

These reforms matter.

They also reveal something about the governing pattern.

Again and again, the strongest intervention appears **after an existing arrangement has produced conspicuous harm**.

The rough sequence is:

> **warning or vulnerability → accommodation of existing power → harm or institutional failure → evidential threshold crossed → corrective intervention**

A leader who learns is preferable to one who refuses to learn.

But a political system in which vulnerable people repeatedly have to supply the evidence that finally makes accommodation untenable is not adequately protective.

That distinction becomes especially important when the powerful interest is not merely an institution capable of making mistakes, but a powerful person or system that may itself be producing the risk.

## 🪞 When The Powerful Man Is The Risk

Sexual violence, coercive control, trafficking, institutional abuse and authoritarian violence share an unpleasant feature: **power affects the production of evidence**.

Power can determine:

- who is believed;
- whose behaviour is regarded as normal;
- who controls records and access;
- who can hire lawyers and advisers;
- whose reputation creates institutional hesitation;
- whether frightened or inconsistent testimony is interpreted as trauma or unreliability;
- how expensive intervention becomes;
- how many respectable people can explain why maintaining a relationship is necessary.

This is why an essentially reactive model of protection becomes dangerous.

If the operating principle is *maintain the relationship until the evidence becomes overwhelming*, the person possessing the most power may also possess the greatest capacity to prevent the evidence becoming overwhelming.

The lesson from CSE should have travelled considerably further than CSE: **do not require vulnerable people to defeat the credibility advantages of powerful actors before protection becomes available.**

## 🕯️ Jewish Women Are Not Israel

There is another layer to this history for Jewish women after 7 October 2023.

The sexual violence committed against Israelis was real and deserves investigation, recognition and justice. Nothing about concern for Palestinian women requires minimising violence against Israeli women.

But Jewish women are not interchangeable with Israel, and Israeli women’s violated bodies are not political property.

Where sexual violence against Israeli women becomes part of the moral language used to justify or sustain war, Jewish survivors elsewhere can find themselves involuntarily folded into that narrative. A government can simultaneously tell Jewish women that sexual violence against women like them is of enormous moral significance while supporting actions whose foreseeable consequences place another population of women and girls at extraordinary risk.

That contradiction deserves examination in its own right.

The rhetorical question for later corpus work is therefore precise:

> **How often, and in what contexts, did British political language after 7 October personify or feminise Israel while Palestinian women remained linguistically submerged within the category of civilians?**

That should be answered by collecting the speeches and statements rather than assumed in advance.

## 🌊 Gaza Is Also A VAWG Question

No sophisticated knowledge of the Middle East was required to predict that mass displacement, deprivation, militarisation and destruction of healthcare would have gendered consequences.

Conflict does not suspend pregnancy, menstruation, childbirth, caring responsibilities, domestic abuse, sexual exploitation or the need for community protection. Destroying civilian infrastructure changes the conditions under which all of those things occur.

Nor does protecting Palestinian women require pretending Palestinian men are incapable of harming women. Feminism does not require idealising men before recognising their social function.

In armed conflict, men within families and immediate communities may provide physical protection, income, transport, childcare, advocacy, accompaniment and material support. Killing, detaining, injuring or displacing large numbers of those men can therefore remove parts of the social infrastructure upon which women depend — even while women may simultaneously face patriarchal or interpersonal violence within their own communities.

No opinion about Hamas is required to recognise that.

Likewise, no state’s armed forces should be presumed incapable of sexual or gendered violence. Allegations concerning Israeli personnel require the same evidential discipline as allegations concerning anyone else: investigation, differentiation between allegation and established fact, and accountability where evidence supports it.

The appropriate policy question is therefore not whether British ministers could have predicted every individual atrocity.

It is:

> **What gendered harms should a government claiming exceptional expertise in VAWG reasonably have anticipated from the policies it supported?**

## 📺 When Statecraft Enters The Survivor’s Living Room

Diplomacy requires governments to deal with people they would not choose as friends.

It does not follow that every form of ceremony, intimacy, praise or deference is diplomatically compulsory.

That distinction matters in the case of Donald Trump.

Many people may remember the *Access Hollywood* recording principally as an old political scandal. Survivors may experience information of that kind differently. Threat-relevant information can remain extraordinarily salient because remembering danger is one of the ways human beings try not to encounter it again.

Trump was subsequently found civilly liable for sexually abusing E. Jean Carroll. The judge later explained that the conduct established by the jury fell within the ordinary contemporary meaning of rape, despite the narrower terminology of the applicable New York law.

Against that background, the extraordinary ceremonial treatment attached to a state visit carries meaning beyond foreign-policy protocol.

A survivor can understand why Britain requires a relationship with the President of the United States and still experience the spectacle of exceptional honour and deferential friendship as profoundly disturbing.

That is not irrationality. It is a political cost of the diplomatic choices being made.

The Mandelson appointment makes the distinction sharper still.

Britain could not choose the American president. Britain **could** choose its ambassador.

The relevant question therefore extends beyond whether security vetting technically permitted the appointment:

> **Why did a Prime Minister who repeatedly grounded part of his political authority in his experience of sexual violence consider this an acceptable moral and political risk?**

## ♻️ We Have Already Been Told

Britain does not suffer from an absence of recommendations about violence against women and girls.

There have been inquiries, reviews, strategies, inspectorate reports, safeguarding reforms, specialist prosecutors, new offences, new guidance, institutional apologies, online-safety regimes, lessons-learned exercises and another round of recommendations explaining what earlier recommendations failed to achieve.

The recurring-recommendations problem changes the diagnosis.

We increasingly do not need another exceptionally clever person to discover that women are exposed to violence.

We need political and administrative systems in which **known protections cannot perpetually be subordinated to whichever competing interest possesses more power this week**.

Starmer is useful here precisely because he is not the easy case.

Give the hypothetical male leader the favourable CV:

- human-rights lawyer;
- Director of Public Prosecutions;
- professional exposure to rape and sexual-abuse cases;
- experience of institutional failure involving exploited girls;
- evidence that he can learn from those failures;
- an explicit political commitment to reducing VAWG;
- and, eventually, the office of Prime Minister.

Now ask whether women and girls consequently become the immovable object in his decision-making.

They do not.

Sometimes their interests prevail. Sometimes reforms are meaningful. Sometimes previous mistakes are corrected.

But sometimes women’s safety remains **tradeable** against other priorities.

That is the more important finding.

## 🧿 The Wrong Question

The wrong question is:

> **Is Keir Starmer good or bad for women?**

It collapses institutions into personality and politics into moral biography.

The better questions are:

- How cheaply can increased exposure to violence be traded away?
- Which competing interests are sufficient to outweigh it?
- Whose assurances are accepted when that calculation is made?
- How much evidence must women produce before the ordering changes?
- Does intervention happen before predictable harm, or principally after it?
- What happens when the person being accommodated is himself part of the risk environment?
- What protections remain when the unusually qualified man gets the judgment wrong?

Those questions survive a change of prime minister.

They also survive a change of party.

## 🧱 The Structural Lesson

The conclusion is not that men cannot help women.

Men can govern well. Men can be feminist allies. Men can prosecute abusers, reform institutions, challenge other men and make decisions that materially improve women’s lives.

Nor does anyone occupying senior office need personally to possess every embodied experience of the people governed.

The problem begins when **the safety of people who bear a risk is made dependent upon the discretionary understanding of somebody who does not bear it**.

No Prime Minister can acquire the embodied experience of having been socialised and persistently assessed as female simply by studying VAWG policy. Expertise helps. Listening helps. Survivor involvement helps. Institutional memory helps.

None is a substitute for structures that continue working when individual judgment fails.

This matters particularly in 2026, as authoritarian politics and organised far-right movements create increasingly visible threats across borders. A protection architecture built around the expectation that the responsible man at the top will eventually recognise that things have gone too far is a poor architecture for an environment in which powerful men may themselves be producing, enabling or normalising the danger.

The lesson is therefore not:

> **find a better man.**

It is:

> **build systems which do not require an exceptionally good one.**

## 🕯️ A Final Personal Note

I write this as a Jewish woman and as a survivor of rape who has experienced sustained digital and in-person harassment of kinds that will be depressingly familiar to women who have spent time writing in public.

During this period, highly sensitive details connected to my rape and trauma were exposed or used against me. At the relevant time, some of that information was not information I had placed into the public domain. I have subsequently spoken much more openly about what happened to me, partly because circumstances made continued privacy increasingly impossible. That should not obscure what was private when this began.

My political expression throughout has been ordinary lawful political expression. People are entitled to dislike it. Disagreement with a woman’s politics does not create an entitlement to her medical history, the details of her rape, or knowledge of precisely how to frighten her.

I pursued lower-level routes before approaching the Prime Minister. I did so because I believed I had exhausted the realistic alternatives available to me and was still unable to obtain straightforward answers about what was happening or how I was supposed to protect myself. I appealed to Keir Starmer as my Prime Minister. I received no response or acknowledgement from his office.

I cannot establish from that sequence what happened behind government doors, nor do I attribute subsequent events to Downing Street without evidence. I can record what happened to me: the in-person harassment subsequently ceased; significant digital harassment continued.

I have now approached the Investigatory Powers Tribunal as part of the continuing administrative trail arising from these events. Part of the purpose of doing so is precisely to establish whether conduct within the Tribunal’s jurisdiction had anything to do with what I experienced, rather than assuming that it did. There are questions here to which I do not presently know the answers. That is why there is a process for asking them.

This occurred after years in which I had repeatedly heard Keir Starmer’s experience as Director of Public Prosecutions invoked as evidence that women and survivors should regard him as unusually trustworthy on violence against women and girls. It occurred while I watched Britain maintain and sometimes publicly celebrate relationships with extraordinarily powerful men whose conduct, associations, governments or exercise of power raised profound questions about sexual and gendered violence. It also occurred while Palestinian women and girls were experiencing the foreseeable gendered consequences of war, displacement, deprivation and destruction of civilian and healthcare infrastructure.

I had become more publicly engaged with Gaza partly because, as a trainee doctor, I believed attacks on healthcare required me to think seriously about my professional and ethical obligations. I was carrying that alongside profound personal strain, including two close family bereavements. None of this made me exceptional. It made me a woman trying to exercise ordinary professional, political and civic judgment while already carrying rather too much.

The subsequent disclosures arising from the Epstein files have made this harder, not easier. My concern here is not reducible to one individual appointment or one politician. It is also about the processes by which the British government has responded to what has emerged: what is investigated, what is disclosed, how decisions are explained, and how institutions respond when questions about powerful people intersect with sexual exploitation and abuse.

There may be legitimate legal, investigative or national-security reasons why particular information cannot presently be made public. I do not dismiss that possibility. But secrecy can be necessary and still have a human cost. For survivors watching institutions manage another enormous set of revelations concerning sexual exploitation, powerful men and institutional access, prolonged uncertainty and opacity can be profoundly difficult to live alongside.

That matters particularly in the political environment in which I am writing. Women and minorities are watching increasingly organised and escalatory forms of far-right politics and violence. Jewish women do not observe that development from some abstract historical distance.

As a Jewish woman writing in Britain in 2026, **I do not currently have confidence that my government would reliably protect me when my safety conflicts with sufficiently powerful competing interests.**

That is not an allegation that I know what every institution has done, nor a claim that every unanswered question conceals wrongdoing. It is a statement about confidence. Governments cannot demand that confidence as an article of faith. They have to produce the conditions in which it is reasonable.

Nor would changing the individual at the top solve the problem that this node describes.

A former Director of Public Prosecutions, human-rights lawyer and Prime Minister may possess knowledge that another politician does not. He may sincerely care. He may introduce good reforms. None of those things give him the embodied experience of moving through the world while being persistently assessed as female, nor can credentials guarantee that women’s safety will remain non-negotiable when it collides with sufficiently powerful competing interests.

That is not an argument that men cannot help women, govern well, or contribute enormously to ending violence against us. It is an argument against making our safety dependent upon their individual judgment.

Women, girls and others exposed to gendered violence need institutions capable of protecting us when the person at the top gets the balance wrong — including when he is clever, experienced, well-intentioned and convinced that he understands.

One man can never be our hero because no one man should ever *have* to be.  

Moreover, a man who consents to grasp at Jewish femininity to demonise Palestinian men, never had adequate plans for how to *be* such a hero.  

As much as I am still deeply frustrated with Jewish masculinities, I will gladly take the awkward growing of more emancipatory possibilities where they so exist, even where they annoy me, even when I disagree on so much, even where we are arguing like chickens, because this is at least a place where honesty may actually grow.  

Let us turn a page, and look for more sustainable routes to equity and justice.  
> “From the ashes, we will rise.”
>
> - Naji al-Ali

## 📚 Source Spine

This node is deliberately a working analytical node. Before publication, individual timeline rows should be pinned to primary or high-quality secondary sources, with particular attention to chronology and personal versus institutional responsibility.

Priority source sets include:

- CPS / parliamentary material on Rochdale CSE and the subsequent change in credibility assessment;
- Alison Levitt QC’s review of the Savile charging decisions;
- Starmer-era CPS material concerning rape-retraction prosecutions, false allegations, child sexual abuse guidance and Victims’ Right to Review;
- the Casey national audit and CCRC material concerning criminalised CSE survivors;
- the 2025 *Freedom from Violence and Abuse* strategy and responses from specialist VAWG organisations;
- UN Women and other UN reporting on the gendered consequences of the war in Gaza;
- Starmer speeches and statements after 7 October 2023 for direct analysis of gendered rhetoric and personification;
- official and high-quality reporting concerning Trump’s second UK state visit and the Carroll litigation;
- released government material and parliamentary evidence concerning the Mandelson appointment and Epstein-related due diligence;
- official government, parliamentary and investigative material concerning the continuing response to Epstein-file disclosures.

The evidential rule throughout is straightforward: **do not convert leadership responsibility into personal casework responsibility; do not convert an allegation into a finding; do not convert chronology into causation; and do not neutralise what the surviving facts mean for survivors.**

## 🏮 Footer

*👾 One Man Can Never Be Our Hero* is a living node of the **Polaris Protocol**. It uses Keir Starmer’s career as a longitudinal case study in the limits of individualised protection: what happens when expertise, good intentions and corrective reform remain vulnerable to competing concentrations of political, institutional and geopolitical power. Its purpose is not to locate a perfect political protector, but to help move VAWG governance away from dependence on one.
