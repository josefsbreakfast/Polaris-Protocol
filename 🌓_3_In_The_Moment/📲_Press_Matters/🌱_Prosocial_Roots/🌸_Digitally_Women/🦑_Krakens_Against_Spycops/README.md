# 🦑 Krakens Against Spycops
**First created:** 2026-09-20 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-20  
*Consent, information, gender, undercover policing, institutional power, and what happens when the people inside an operation are missing from its risk model.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

This cluster is about **Spycops**.

It is also about a larger problem.

What happens when the state deliberately changes another person's information environment in order to exercise power?

Undercover policing necessarily involves deception.

That does not mean every consequence of deception becomes operationally available.

The history examined here includes political infiltration, constructed identities, identities derived from deceased children, intimate relationships, children conceived during undercover deployments, real-life spouses and families, management and oversight failures, and people who spent years trying to establish what had happened to them.

The current argument includes an additional collision:

Britain is still trying to decide how to finish learning from that history while questions about misogyny, violence against women and girls, political surveillance, covert policing and institutional trust are very much alive.

So this cluster asks:

```text
WHAT HAPPENED?
      ↓
WHO KNEW?
      ↓
WHO WAS MISSING
FROM THE RISK MODEL?
      ↓
WHERE WAS THE LINE?
      ↓
WHAT DID THE INSTITUTION
LEARN?
      ↓
WHAT WOULD MAKE THE
BETTER CHOICE EASIER
NEXT TIME?
```

The answer is not:

**men bad. police bad. punish everybody.**

It is also not:

**different times. complicated job. go home.**

The working proposition is more useful:

> **The state can authorise its officer. It cannot authorise her consent.**

And the institutional proposition is:

> **The people harmed get to know that telling the truth changed something.**

There is an olive branch in one hand.

There is, occasionally, an enormous novelty foam finger reading:

**BRO WTF**

in the other.

Both are load-bearing.

## 2. 🌸 Consent Is A Shitload Of Context

The cluster's central consent model is simple.

```text
SHE SAYS YES
      ↓
TO WHOM DOES SHE BELIEVE
SHE IS SAYING YES?
      ↓
NAME
HISTORY
OCCUPATION
POLITICAL POSITION
REASON FOR BEING THERE
RELATIONSHIP CONTEXT
FUTURE INTENTIONS
      ↓
ACTUAL PERSON:
STATE AGENT OPERATING
A CONSTRUCTED IDENTITY
```

Then:

> **Would she have made the same choice with that information?**

Affected women have said they would not.

That does **not** mean every deception automatically vitiates consent for the purposes of English criminal law.

The criminal-law test is narrower than the ordinary human question:

> **Would I have chosen this sexual relationship if I had known who you actually were and why you were in my life?**

Keep both questions.

Do not pretend they are identical.

Do not pretend the second one does not matter because it is not identical to the first.

The information was not necessarily ancillary to the choice.

**The information could be part of the conditions under which the choice became possible.**

## 3. 🪪 Authorised By Fucking Whom?

Undercover policing requires authorised deception.

That fact cannot settle every later boundary.

```text
STATE → OFFICER

YOU MAY UNDERTAKE
THIS OPERATION
```

is not:

```text
PERSON ↔ PERSON

DO I WANT THIS
SEXUAL RELATIONSHIP?
```

The first decision belongs to the state.

The second does not.

Hence the cluster's shortest proposition:

> **The state can authorise its officer. It cannot authorise her consent.**

This is not an argument that undercover policing has no legitimate purpose.

It is an argument that **legitimate purpose does not abolish category boundaries**.

## 4. 🌸 Political Women Are Still Women

A woman does not stop being a person because she has politics.

She does not stop having a private life because she attends meetings.

She does not become less capable of sexual harm because she campaigns.

She does not become operational scenery because she is useful to an intelligence picture.

```text
POLITICAL WOMAN
      ↓
WOMAN
      ↓
PERSON
      ↓
BODY
      ↓
PRIVATE LIFE
      ↓
SEXUAL AUTONOMY
      ↓
RIGHTS
```

The state may investigate crime.

It may investigate serious threats.

It may use covert methods where lawfully necessary and proportionate.

Fine.

The people encountered during those operations remain people.

This should not be controversial enough to require its own cluster.

And yet.

## 5. 🏠 The Person Was In The Operation But Missing From The Model

One of the recurring Polaris questions is not merely:

> **Who was targeted?**

It is:

> **Who was affected by the system without being represented inside the system's own model of risk?**

For deceptive intimate relationships:

```text
OPERATIONAL MODEL

OFFICER
TARGET
COVER
INTELLIGENCE
MANAGEMENT
PUBLIC ORDER
SECURITY
RISK OF EXPOSURE
```

Where is:

```text
THE PERSON
WHO THINKS
THIS IS HER BOYFRIEND?
```

Then widen the model.

Where is the wife?

Where is the child?

Where is the family of the deceased child whose identity became cover material?

Where is the friend whose private conversation became intelligence?

Where is the person who is not the target but whose home, body, trust or biography has become part of the operation?

A person does not need to appear in the target box to absorb operational consequences.

That is why **collateral** cannot mean **imaginary**.

## 6. 🧠 This Is Sex-Positive

For avoidance of an extremely tedious misunderstanding:

**men are allowed to be sexual beings.**

Police officers are allowed to be sexual beings.

Adults are allowed:

- fantasies;
- explicit fiction;
- consensual kink;
- homosexuality;
- bisexuality;
- heterosexuality;
- receptive sex;
- penetrative sex;
- casual sex;
- committed sex;
- sexual humour;
- weird books;
- weird thoughts;
- and, within the bounds of consent and context, being extremely fucking horny.

Fantasy is not conduct.

Reading a book is not confession.

Writing sexual fiction does not establish an intention to sexually harm somebody.

A sexual joke is not automatically harassment.

The relevant questions are about:

**information, autonomy, power, deception, institutional authority, capacity to refuse, and what happens when somebody says no.**

So:

> **Sex-positive does not mean violence-negative.**

Nor does taking sexual coercion seriously require treating ordinary male sexuality as suspicious.

Healthy masculinity requires room for desire.

It also requires men to learn how to possess desire without requiring somebody else's fear, submission or discomfort to prove it.

## 7. 🍆 Yes, There Is A Dick Joke. It Is Also A Control Variable.

The publicly visible history of deceptive undercover sexual relationships is heavily gendered.

Male officers deceiving women dominate the known record.

That does not prove that no male victim ever existed.

Historical sexual records can be incomplete for obvious reasons, especially across periods in which gay and bisexual men faced criminalisation, discrimination and profound risks around disclosure.

But the gender pattern creates a legitimate counterfactual.

If sex could genuinely become operationally necessary to maintain cover, were straight male officers ever expected to cross **their own** sexual boundaries with men?

Hence the deliberately vulgar question:

> **How much cock did you take for Queen and country?**

And, for the contemporary service:

> **How much cock are you prepared to take for the King's peace?**

Nothing about sex between men is inherently degrading.

That is the point.

If male sexual boundaries remained comparatively inviolable while women's did not, we should ask whether what became operationally negotiable was really **sex**.

Or whether it was **women**.

That is a hypothesis to test.

Not a conclusion to smuggle in through a joke.

The joke merely has excellent transport capacity.

## 8. 👮 This Is Not A Good Men™ Catalogue

The constructive side of the cluster is not:

> **Here are three approved men. Become them.**

Norwell Roberts, Neil Basu and Mark Rowley provide different kinds of public material from different places in or around the history of Metropolitan policing.

That does not make them interchangeable.

It does not make their public image their complete character.

We do not work with them.

Police officers may know things about them that members of the public do not.

Good.

The proposition is narrower:

> **recognisable police authority does not require swagger, sexual entitlement, humiliation, contempt or an inability to change one's mind.**

The better examples may be people nobody outside the reader's own station has heard of.

Ask:

> **Who's your idea of a decent bloke?**

Then:

> **Why?**

What does he do when embarrassed?

What happens when he is wrong?

Can he use authority without enjoying somebody else's submission?

What does he do when a weaker person is frightened?

Can he challenge his mates?

Can he hear *no*?

Can he change his mind without experiencing learning as humiliation?

Nobody needs to copy the entire man.

Identify the quality.

Practise that.

## 9. 📺 Pick The Grey Scenes

Fiction is useful here too.

Not because *Life on Mars*, *Ashes to Ashes*, *Whitechapel* or any other police drama provides an approved fictional role model.

Please do not emerge from this README with:

> **Polaris says become Gene Hunt.**

Jesus Christ.

Use the difficult scenes.

Pick moments where:

- two legitimate objectives collide;
- information is incomplete;
- loyalty and duty point in different directions;
- the character you like is behaving badly;
- the irritating character has a point;
- doing the humane thing costs something;
- doing the expedient thing transfers the cost onto somebody weaker;
- challenging a colleague risks belonging;
- admitting an error risks status.

Then ask:

> **What would I want myself to do?**

And:

> **What would I like to believe I would do?**

Then the only question that really matters:

> **What do I need to do now to make sure I am more likely to be that person?**

```text
WHAT DO I BELIEVE
I WOULD DO?
      ↓
WHAT WOULD IT
ACTUALLY COST?
      ↓
WHAT PRESSURES MIGHT
MAKE ME DO SOMETHING ELSE?
      ↓
WHAT SKILLS / HABITS /
RELATIONSHIPS / RULES /
SAFEGUARDS WOULD HELP?
      ↓
CAN I PRACTISE THEM
BEFORE THE HARD MOMENT?
```

Someone saying:

> *Fuck. I think I'd probably have gone along with the team.*

may have produced considerably more useful information than somebody immediately announcing that naturally they would have been the lone incorruptible hero.

Okay.

Why?

What would have made dissent easier?

Now we are no longer asking individuals to magically **be better men**.

We are asking institutions to:

> **build environments in which people have a fighting chance of being the person they want to be.**

That is a governance problem.

Excellent.

We know how to work with those.

## 10. ♻️ Accountability Is Supposed To Produce Recovery

The cluster does not require this binary:

```text
WE WERE GOOD OFFICERS

OR

WE DID SOMETHING WRONG
```

Both can occupy the same human history.

A person may sincerely have believed he was serving the public while participating in a system that harmed somebody.

A professional culture can normalise conduct that later generations understand differently.

A legitimate objective can be pursued through illegitimate means.

A protective institution can fail to protect.

Recognition does not require ritual self-annihilation.

A person can say:

> I understood this differently then.

> I can see something now that I did not see adequately at the time.

> I would not do it now.

> I would not permit somebody under my command to do it now.

That is useful evidence.

Then ask:

- What changed your mind?
- What did you fail to see?
- What would have helped?
- What did the culture reward?
- What made challenge difficult?
- What would you teach a young officer now?
- What safeguard would have made the better choice easier?

> **You do not have to hate the man you were to decide that the man you are now knows better.**

Institutions should be allowed to grow up too.

The people harmed do not owe forgiveness in exchange.

Legal and professional consequences remain possible where warranted.

But accountability that produces no learning is an extremely expensive way to manufacture archives.

## 11. 🏏 The Point Of A Linesman Is Not The Red Card

The point of a boundary is not merely that somebody can be punished after crossing it.

The line needs to be visible **before** the difficult moment.

```text
HARM
  ↓
DISCLOSURE
  ↓
LISTEN
  ↓
ESTABLISH FACTS
  ↓
ACKNOWLEDGE
  ↓
UNDERSTAND MECHANISM
  ↓
CHANGE INCENTIVES
  ↓
BUILD SAFEGUARD
  ↓
TEST SAFEGUARD
  ↓
SHOW PEOPLE WHAT CHANGED
  ↓
KEEP MONITORING
```

That is the feedback loop.

Sometimes the red card is necessary.

Sometimes the useful intervention is much earlier:

> **hang on, lads. I don't actually want to become the bloke who does this.**

A good system makes that sentence easier to say.

It gives the officer a line he can see.

A colleague who will back him.

A manager who can hear bad news.

A rule that does not disappear under operational pressure.

A route for challenge.

A route for civilians to be heard.

A memory of what happened last time.

That is what **institutional learning** is for.

## 12. 🫀 Trust Is An Output

The cluster is also interested in the difference between:

```text
BEING SAFE
      ≠
BEHAVING SAFELY
      ≠
BEING PERCEIVED AS SAFE
```

Nobody receives an entitlement to another person's trust merely by declaring themselves trustworthy.

```text
I AM SAFE
≠
YOU MUST FEEL SAFE

I AM TRUSTWORTHY
≠
YOU MUST TRUST ME

I HAVE AUTHORITY
≠
YOU MUST LIKE ME

I MEAN WELL
≠
YOU CANNOT BE FRIGHTENED

I HAVE BEEN AUTHORISED
≠
YOU HAVE CONSENTED
```

Perceived safety is produced through history.

Race.

Class.

Sex.

Disability.

Sexuality.

Immigration status.

Politics.

Previous police contact.

Personal trauma.

Knowledge of the institution.

The behaviour of the particular officer.

A public style that reassures one woman may not reassure another.

The institutional goal therefore cannot be:

**make everybody feel safe.**

It can be:

**behave more safely, correct unsafe behaviour, make boundaries visible, and give people evidence over time that the system works.**

Trust is not a prerequisite citizens owe the state.

**Trust is an output the state helps produce through behaviour.**

## 13. 📋 Evidence Rules For This Cluster

This material is funny in places.

The evidence rules are not.

Use the following distinctions:

### **Documented**

Supported by primary records, judgments, Inquiry evidence, official reports or similarly direct material.

### **Institutional admission**

An organisation has itself acknowledged the point.

This is stronger than an outside allegation but does not automatically settle every related question.

### **Survivor / affected-person account**

A person's account of what happened to them.

Preserve attribution where facts remain contested.

### **Party's position**

A submission, campaign position, institutional argument or litigant's claim.

Do not silently upgrade it into an independent finding.

### **Reported**

A media report supported by identified sourcing.

Prefer the underlying primary material where available.

### **Our reading**

Polaris analysis of documented material.

Keep interpretation visibly separate from the underlying fact.

### **Unknown**

Unknown.

Leave it there.

Do not complete the pattern because the missing arrow would make the story excellent.

## 15. ❓ What This Cluster Does Not Claim

This cluster does **not** claim:

- that every undercover officer formed sexual relationships;
- that every deception about identity is rape under English law;
- that every intimate relationship described in the historical record had identical facts;
- that all managers knew everything officers did;
- that every officer shared Roger Pearce's 2026 view;
- that current Metropolitan Police policy permits sex as an intelligence tactic;
- that no male victim of undercover sexual deception ever existed;
- that political-surveillance asymmetries prove partisan conspiracy;
- that every historic policing failure resulted from misogyny;
- that fiction reveals its author's private intentions;
- that buying or reading a book establishes sexual aggression;
- that public presentation proves private character;
- that institutional reform requires everybody to agree about the past;
- that changing the machinery of the Public Inquiry necessarily means abandoning accountability;
- or that several unresolved political or institutional information streams become one connected plot merely because they coexist.

**Unconnected does not mean irrelevant.**

**Unknown does not mean secretly known.**

**Capability does not mean use.**

**Access does not mean influence.**

**Benefit does not establish causation.**

**Strategic usefulness does not establish strategic intent.**

Do not draw missing arrows.
