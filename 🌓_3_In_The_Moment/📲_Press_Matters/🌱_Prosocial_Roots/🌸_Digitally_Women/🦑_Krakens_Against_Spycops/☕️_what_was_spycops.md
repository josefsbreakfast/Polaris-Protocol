# ☕️ What Was Spycops?

**First created:** 2026-09-20 \| **Last updated:** 2026-09-20\
*Sit down. Unfortunately Britain has lore.*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🛰️ Orientation

If you have arrived here from the September 2026 news cycle and are
wondering why women, dead children, political activists, undercover
police officers, Special Branch, a public inquiry, Palestine Action and
an increasingly irritated squid are all in the same folder:

Hello.

This is the background node.

**"Spycops"** is the widely used name for a long-running scandal
concerning undercover policing in Britain, particularly the infiltration
of political, activist and social-justice groups by specialist police
units.

The central historical units are the Metropolitan Police **Special
Demonstration Squad (SDS)**, which began in 1968 and operated until
2008, and the **National Public Order Intelligence Unit (NPOIU)**, which
operated from around 1999 until 2011.

The story is not simply that Britain had undercover police.

States sometimes have legitimate reasons to use undercover policing.

The scandal concerns **who was infiltrated, what information was
gathered, what officers did while living inside those communities, what
management knew, what safeguards existed, what happened to people caught
inside those operations, and how long it took the state to establish
what the fuck had happened.**

Among the issues eventually exposed were:

-   infiltration of political and campaigning groups;
-   collection of extensive information about people's political,
    working, family and private lives;
-   undercover officers forming deceptive sexual and intimate
    relationships with women;
-   officers using the identities of deceased children to construct
    cover identities;
-   surveillance connected to justice campaigns;
-   questions about disclosure and possible miscarriages of justice;
-   management and oversight failures;
-   and the question of how far practices extended beyond the best-known
    specialist units.

In 2015, after years of journalism, campaigning, litigation, reviews and
disclosures, the government established the **Undercover Policing
Inquiry (UCPI)**.

It was expected to conclude within three years.

It is 2026.

The inquiry is still going.

☕️

Get comfortable.

## 1. 🕵️ Why Did Britain Have Spycops?

The SDS began in **1968**, initially as the Special Operations Squad,
against a background of concern about large demonstrations and public
disorder.

Its officers were deployed covertly into political organisations and
protest movements.

The basic operational idea is easy enough to understand.

If police believe a demonstration may produce serious disorder, an
undercover officer inside an organising group may provide information
that cannot be obtained from standing outside the meeting with a
notebook.

Over time, however, the SDS became a long-term intelligence operation.

Its officers did not merely appear at demonstrations.

They lived inside political communities.

They attended meetings.

They joined organisations.

Some became active organisers.

They developed friendships.

They collected information about individuals.

The UCPI's 2023 interim report found that reporting commonly included
extensive details about people's political views, personalities, jobs,
relationships, families and private lives.

Some of the political activity being reported was lawful.

That matters.

The scandal is therefore not adequately described as:

> **Police secretly watched criminals.**

The historical reality is much stranger.

Police officers were living under constructed identities inside
political and activist communities, gathering intelligence across the
boundary between public-order policing, political activity and ordinary
private life.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2. 🪧 Political Affiliation Is Doing Something Very Weird Here

This is one of the easiest features of the story to lose once everything
has been translated into institutional language.

The UCPI's terms of reference explicitly include the extent to which
undercover policing targeted **political and social-justice campaigns**.

Think about what that means from the other direction.

``` text
PERSON PARTICIPATES IN
POLITICAL / CAMPAIGNING ACTIVITY
        ↓
STATE DECIDES THE COMMUNITY
WARRANTS COVERT INFILTRATION
        ↓
UNDERCOVER OFFICER ENTERS
THE SOCIAL ENVIRONMENT
        ↓
OFFICER BECOMES FRIEND /
COLLEAGUE / COMRADE /
APPARENT PRIVATE CITIZEN
        ↓
STATE ACCESS NOW EXTENDS
INTO ORDINARY HUMAN RELATIONSHIPS
```

Again: political organisations are not immune from lawful investigation.

Participation in political activity does not make every subsequent
intrusion automatically unlawful.

But neither does legitimate intelligence-gathering create an unlimited
zone of permissible intrusion around everybody an undercover officer
encounters.

That becomes especially important when the state-created identity enters
someone's **sexual life**.

There is a profoundly strange social sorting problem here.

A woman's political affiliation, friendship network or presence in a
campaigning community can become the route by which an undercover state
agent enters her social environment.

If he then forms an intimate relationship with her while concealing that
state role, her sexual autonomy has become entangled with a political
surveillance operation.

That is not a trivial side issue.

And it is worth performing a simple perspective test.

Imagine reading that another state's political police had:

-   infiltrated political organisations;
-   created long-term false identities;
-   embedded male agents inside activist communities;
-   gathered extensive information about people's private lives;
-   used identities derived from dead children;
-   and had agents form sexual relationships with women who did not know
    who those men actually were.

Would we instinctively describe that merely as:

> **some officers having inappropriate relationships?**

Sit with that.

## 3. 🧸 The Dead Children

Yes.

This part is real.

By the early 1970s, SDS officers were routinely researching the birth
and death records of children who had died young and using those
identities as the basis for undercover cover names.

The UCPI found that by early **1974** this had become established SDS
practice.

The earlier Operation Herne review identified **42 covert identities**
that were confirmed or considered highly likely to have used details
belonging to deceased children.

Why?

A long-term undercover identity has to survive scrutiny.

A completely invented person may have no birth record.

No historical trace.

No documentary past.

A real identity provides administrative depth.

So:

``` text
A CHILD EXISTS
↓
THE CHILD DIES
↓
THE STATE RECORD REMAINS
↓
POLICE RESEARCH THE RECORD
↓
BIOGRAPHICAL INFORMATION BECOMES
MATERIAL FOR AN UNDERCOVER IDENTITY
↓
AN ADULT POLICE OFFICER
INHABITS THAT IDENTITY
↓
LIVING PEOPLE MEET
THE CONSTRUCTED PERSON
```

The UCPI found that no surviving SDS manager could explain precisely
when or why the routine practice began.

It also found that, with possible exceptions, there was no evidence that
anyone gave thought to the propriety of using deceased children's
identities.

The assumption appears to have been that families would never know.

This is one of those sentences that tells you an extraordinary amount
about a system:

> **The harm was discounted because the people affected were not
> expected to discover it.**

Families eventually did discover it.

The Home Office's 2026 consultation on the future of the inquiry
explicitly identifies **family members of deceased children whose
identities were used** among the people particularly affected by its
work.

The child was dead.

The information was not.

## 4. 🌸 Then There Were The Women

Long-term deployment produced another problem.

Undercover officers had to become socially convincing.

They formed friendships.

And male undercover officers formed sexual relationships with women they
encountered while deployed under their cover identities.

The UCPI's first interim report describes this as becoming a **perennial
feature** of the SDS's subsequent history.

That wording matters.

The inquiry did **not** conclude, for the early period it had examined,
that sexual relationships were generally deployed as an authorised
tactic for gaining entry into groups.

It found that some managers would have disapproved of them.

But it also found that sexual relationships between male undercover
officers and women encountered during deployments were common knowledge
among many undercover officers.

And one finding deserves to be written in enormous letters.

A manager recognised that sexual relationships could endanger:

-   operational security;
-   and officers' careers.

The inquiry found:

> **the likely impact on the women was not considered.**

There it is.

The woman was physically present in the operation.

**She was absent from the risk model.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 5. 💔 "Relationship" Is An Inadequate Word

Institutional documents often need neutral nouns.

Unfortunately, nouns can hide mechanisms.

An **"undercover relationship"** sounds like two adults had an
ill-advised workplace romance.

From the woman's side, the informational environment could look rather
different.

She might not know:

-   his real name;
-   his real occupation;
-   his actual biography;
-   that he had entered her community as part of a police operation;
-   that information from that community was being passed to the state;
-   that the apparent private citizen standing in front of her was
    simultaneously an undercover state agent.

Women affected by these deployments have therefore used language centred
on sexual abuse, coercion and, in some campaigning and survivor
accounts, rape or state-sanctioned rape.

Those descriptions should not be silently converted into criminal-law
findings that a court has not made.

But nor should administrative terminology erase what women are trying to
describe.

There are several distinct questions:

``` text
SEXUAL CONSENT
        ↓
DECEPTION
        ↓
UNDERCOVER IDENTITY
        ↓
OPERATIONAL PURPOSE
        ↓
STATE KNOWLEDGE
        ↓
MANAGEMENT / AUTHORISATION
        ↓
INSTITUTIONAL RESPONSIBILITY
```

The Metropolitan Police itself eventually apologised to women deceived
into long-term intimate relationships.

In its 2015 apology, it described those relationships as **abusive,
deceitful, manipulative and wrong**, acknowledged violations of the
women's human rights, and called the conduct an abuse of police power.

It also explicitly said the women had not brought the relationships upon
themselves.

That is a long way from:

> some cops had girlfriends at work.

## 6. 🪪 Where Does The State End When The Badge Comes Off?

This is one of the conceptual problems the scandal leaves behind.

An undercover officer does not stop being an agent of the state because
the person he is observing cannot see his warrant card.

If he gathers intelligence, that intelligence belongs to the operation.

If he attends meetings, his presence is operational.

If he reports on activists, that reporting is state activity.

If he maintains a cover identity, the identity exists because of the
deployment.

So what happens when the same officer enters somebody's intimate life?

There is a useful analogy.

**An arrest does not cease to be an exercise of state power because the
officer making it is wearing plain clothes.**

Likewise, the disappearance of the badge cannot by itself answer whether
conduct inside an undercover deployment is properly characterised as
purely private.

That does **not** mean every action an undercover officer takes is
automatically authorised by the state.

Officers can violate rules.

They can act without permission.

They can conceal conduct from supervisors.

Individual misconduct and institutional authorisation are not
interchangeable concepts.

But neither can an institution have it both ways:

``` text
WHEN THE OFFICER GATHERS INTELLIGENCE:
AGENT OF THE STATE

WHEN THE SAME OPERATIONAL IDENTITY
ENTERS A WOMAN'S BED:
JUST SOME BLOKE
```

The real questions are harder.

What did management authorise?

What did management know?

What should management have known?

What risks did deployment design predict?

What safeguards existed?

What happened when officers crossed boundaries?

What did reporting reveal?

What incentives encouraged silence?

And what responsibility does an institution carry for harms made
possible by an operational identity it created and sustained?

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 7. 📡 Why Did Nobody Stop It?

There is no single answer covering forty years of deployments.

That is partly why there is an inquiry.

But the early findings already show a familiar systems problem.

The UCPI found that sexual relationships occurred among officers, that
knowledge among undercover officers existed, and that effective
preventative measures were not put in place.

Managers' attention could focus on the risks most legible to the
organisation:

``` text
WILL THE COVER BE BLOWN?
WILL THE OPERATION FAIL?
WILL THE OFFICER'S CAREER BE DAMAGED?
```

The woman introduces another category:

``` text
WHAT WILL THIS DO TO HER?
```

The early system did not reliably integrate that question.

This is not merely an historical curiosity.

It is a safeguarding-design problem.

**What harms can a system perceive?**

**Whose harms count as operational risks?**

**Who can report bad news?**

**Where does that information travel?**

**What happens when acknowledging the harm threatens the operation
itself?**

The surveillance system could collect remarkable amounts of information
about people outside it.

It was considerably less successful at observing itself.

## 8. 🧑‍⚖️ The Scandal Did Not Appear Because The System Volunteered It

By the late 2000s and early 2010s, journalists, activists and people
subjected to undercover policing were exposing increasingly serious
allegations.

Women deceived into sexual relationships were central to bringing the
story into public view.

Former undercover officer **Peter Francis** also made disclosures about
historical SDS activity.

Institutional responses followed.

The Metropolitan Police established **Operation Herne** to investigate
allegations about the SDS.

The **Stephen Lawrence Independent Review**, led by Mark Ellison QC,
examined undercover-policing issues connected to the investigation of
Stephen Lawrence's murder and concerns surrounding the Lawrence family.

Questions arose about undercover officers' involvement in cases that
could have implications for criminal convictions and disclosure to
prosecutors.

The Home Office commissioned further review of its own historical
knowledge.

By 2014, Ellison concluded that the scale and nature of the questions
could require a public inquiry capable of testing evidence and making
definitive findings.

In **March 2015**, Home Secretary Theresa May announced a statutory
inquiry.

By July, its terms of reference covered undercover policing throughout
England and Wales since 1968, including the extent to which political
and social-justice campaigns had been targeted.

The feedback route looked something like:

``` text
PEOPLE EXPERIENCE SOMETHING
        ↓
PEOPLE COMPARE EXPERIENCES
        ↓
WOMEN INVESTIGATE
        ↓
ACTIVISTS INVESTIGATE
        ↓
JOURNALISTS INVESTIGATE
        ↓
FORMER OFFICERS DISCLOSE
        ↓
LITIGATION / REVIEWS
        ↓
INSTITUTIONAL EVIDENCE ACCUMULATES
        ↓
PUBLIC INQUIRY
```

The people being observed became observers of the observing system.

Very inconvenient.

Very cybernetic.

## 9. 🪧 And The Campaigners Kept Going

This history matters because **Spycops was not simply discovered by an
internal audit**.

People who had been infiltrated spent years assembling information.

Women compared stories.

Activists identified officers.

Journalists followed records.

Campaigners demanded disclosure.

Families sought answers.

Litigation forced facts into forums where institutions had to respond.

The state's information advantage was enormous.

It had personnel files.

Operational records.

Cover identities.

Intelligence reports.

Management structures.

Legal resources.

The people outside the system often had fragments:

a photograph;

a memory;

an old relationship;

a strange disappearance;

a name that did not quite resolve;

somebody else's eerily similar story.

The reconstruction of Spycops therefore reverses the original
informational relationship.

``` text
THEN:

STATE
SEES
ACTIVISTS

ACTIVISTS
CANNOT SEE
STATE


LATER:

ACTIVISTS + WOMEN + JOURNALISTS
+ LAWYERS + INQUIRY
        ↓
TRY TO RECONSTRUCT
THE STATE'S HIDDEN SYSTEM
```

That reversal has taken years.

## 10. 🧑‍⚖️ Kate Wilson And Mark Kennedy

One of the clearest later cases concerns **Kate Wilson** and undercover
officer **Mark Kennedy**.

Kennedy was deployed between 2003 and 2009 to gather intelligence
concerning political activists.

He operated under the identity **Mark Stone**.

Within months of beginning the deployment, he entered an intimate sexual
relationship with Wilson.

The relationship lasted until 2005.

The Investigatory Powers Tribunal later recorded that Kennedy realised
Wilson would help him infiltrate the activities of people around the
Sumac Centre in Nottingham and that he insinuated himself into her
private and family life.

The Tribunal proceedings concerned multiple rights under the European
Convention on Human Rights.

The police admitted infringements of several of Wilson's rights; the
Tribunal went on to make further findings concerning the deployment,
supervision and discrimination.

Kennedy's case matters because it strips away the comforting idea that
deceptive sexual relationships belonged only to the distant beginnings
of the SDS.

This was the **2000s**.

And Kennedy worked for the **NPOIU**, not the original SDS.

The problem had crossed institutional generations.

## 11. 🗂️ SDS Was Not The Whole Story

This is essential for understanding the argument happening in 2026.

### Special Demonstration Squad --- SDS

Metropolitan Police Special Branch.

Established in 1968.

Long-term infiltration of political and activist organisations.

Disbanded in 2008.

### National Public Order Intelligence Unit --- NPOIU

Established around 1999.

Operated nationally.

Also deployed undercover officers into activist and campaigning
environments.

Mark Kennedy was one of its officers.

The NPOIU ceased operating as a separate unit in 2011.

### Other undercover policing

The UCPI's remit is wider still.

It covers undercover policing in England and Wales since 1968, not
merely these two famous units.

That breadth matters because an institutional inquiry is not only
asking:

> **Which officers did terrible things?**

It is asking:

> **What systems produced, permitted, failed to detect, failed to stop
> or subsequently corrected those things?**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 12. ⚖️ So Britain Established A Public Inquiry

The **Undercover Policing Inquiry** was announced in 2015.

Its purpose includes examining:

-   the contribution undercover policing made to preventing and
    detecting crime;
-   how undercover operations were conducted;
-   their effects on individuals and the public;
-   how officers were selected, trained, managed and supervised;
-   what oversight existed;
-   and what lessons should shape future undercover policing.

This matters because the inquiry is not premised on the proposition that
**undercover policing itself is illegitimate**.

A serious inquiry has to be capable of holding two thoughts
simultaneously:

> Undercover policing can provide legitimate and sometimes important
> intelligence.

and:

> The existence of a legitimate policing technique does not legitimise
> every deployment, target, intrusion or act undertaken within it.

That distinction is the whole game.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 13. 🗓️ Unfortunately, It Has Taken Fucking Forever

The inquiry was originally expected to conclude within **three years**.

More than **ten years** later, it remains ongoing.

That is a serious problem.

People harmed by undercover policing have waited years for answers.

Former officers and their families have remained caught in proceedings.

Costs have grown enormously.

Evidence becomes harder to reconstruct as time passes.

The Home Office is entitled to ask why this has taken so long and
whether remaining objectives can be delivered faster.

But:

> **"This inquiry has taken too long" does not logically entail "the
> questions it has not reached no longer matter."**

The inquiry divided its work into tranches.

The first interim report, published in 2023, covered SDS deployments
from **1968 to 1982**.

And even that report explicitly said several important issues were
better addressed once all relevant evidence was available, including:

-   the impact on women deceived into sexual relationships;
-   the impact on surviving relatives of deceased children whose
    identities were adopted;
-   and the purpose of gathering intelligence on justice campaigns.

The inquiry itself therefore warned readers:

**this was a work in progress.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 14. ✂️ What Hasn't Been Finished?

This is why the 2026 consultation matters.

The Home Office is reconsidering the structure, scope and format of
remaining inquiry work.

The unfinished programme includes later undercover policing.

That includes the **NPOIU**.

It includes other undercover policing beyond the SDS and NPOIU.

And it includes examination of **current and future undercover-policing
practice**.

So the history does not end neatly with:

``` text
BAD OLD POLICE
↓
SCANDAL
↓
INQUIRY
↓
FIXED
```

The actual picture is:

``` text
DECADES OF UNDERCOVER POLICING
↓
PEOPLE DISCOVER HIDDEN PRACTICES
↓
YEARS OF CAMPAIGNING / LITIGATION
↓
PUBLIC INQUIRY ESTABLISHED
↓
INQUIRY TAKES FAR TOO LONG
↓
SOME HISTORY RECONSTRUCTED
↓
LATER SYSTEMS STILL TO BE EXAMINED
↓
GOVERNMENT ASKS HOW MUCH OF
REMAINING PROCESS SHOULD CONTINUE
AND IN WHAT FORM
↓
2026
```

That is where the current cluster begins.

## 15. 🌸 Political Women Are Still Women

There is a final conceptual point worth making before returning to the
present.

The people infiltrated by these units are often described by their
political categories.

Activists.

Protesters.

Anarchists.

Socialists.

Environmentalists.

Anti-racist campaigners.

Animal-rights activists.

Campaign groups.

Those labels can subtly transform the person in the reader's head.

**Political affiliation does not remove embodiment.**

An activist still has a private life.

A protester still has sexual autonomy.

A campaigner still forms attachments.

A person under legitimate investigation still has rights.

A woman does not become less capable of sexual violation because the
state dislikes, distrusts, monitors or investigates the political
environment around her.

And that produces the deeply socially weird question underneath Spycops:

> **What level of sexual and intimate intrusion are we willing to
> tolerate as an incidental consequence of somebody's proximity to
> political surveillance?**

That question should feel uncomfortable.

It is uncomfortable.

If the same historical facts were presented to us as the conduct of a
foreign state's political-policing apparatus, the vocabulary we
instinctively reached for might be considerably harsher.

That observation does not settle the legality of every deployment or
relationship.

It is a useful check against becoming numb to extraordinary conduct
merely because the bureaucracy responsible for it is familiar.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 16. 🦑 So, What Was Spycops?

It was not one officer.

It was not one girlfriend.

It was not one unit.

It was not one bad decision.

It was a long-running system of undercover political policing whose
officers entered human communities deeply enough that the boundary
between **intelligence operation and ordinary life** became dangerously
porous.

The system could know enormous amounts about the people it observed.

Those people could know almost nothing about the system observing them.

Some officers carried identities constructed from dead children's
records.

Some women shared beds, homes, families and futures with men whose
identities and purposes were not what they appeared to be.

Some of the people under observation were engaged in lawful political
activity.

Some intelligence was genuinely useful to public-order policing.

Some deployments concerned real criminality.

Some practices were later acknowledged by police themselves as profound
abuses of power.

And decades later Britain is still trying to reconstruct which was
which, who knew what, what was authorised, what was tolerated, what was
hidden, what changed, and whether the lessons actually propagated
through the institutions responsible.

That is Spycops.

Or, more precisely:

**that is why "Spycops" became a scandal rather than merely another word
for undercover policing.**

Unfortunately, now you have the lore.
