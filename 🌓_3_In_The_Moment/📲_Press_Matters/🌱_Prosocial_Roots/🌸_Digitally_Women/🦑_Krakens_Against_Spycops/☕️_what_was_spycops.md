# ☕️ What Was Spycops?
**First created:** 2026-09-20 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-20  
*Sit down. Unfortunately Britain has lore.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

“Spycops” is the name commonly used for a long-running British undercover-policing scandal involving covert officers infiltrating political, campaigning and activist communities.

That sentence is accurate.

It also does almost no justice to what actually happened.

The story includes:

- the Metropolitan Police Special Demonstration Squad (**SDS**), created in 1968 and operating until 2008;
- the later National Public Order Intelligence Unit (**NPOIU**), operating from 1999 to 2011;
- long-term undercover identities;
- extensive reporting on political and social activity;
- officers entering friendships and homes;
- officers taking positions inside organisations they infiltrated;
- identities derived from children who had died;
- sexual and intimate relationships conducted under false identities;
- children conceived in those relationships;
- wives, partners and families in officers’ real lives;
- reporting touching trade unions, anti-racist campaigns and the campaign for justice for Stephen Lawrence;
- miscarriages of justice and questions about disclosure;
- management, supervision and authorisation;
- and, eventually, a public inquiry established in **2015** to work out what the fuck had been going on.

This node is the historical floor for the rest of the cluster.

It is not the place where every current political collision gets resolved.

It is the place where we establish enough of the record that later nodes can ask harder questions without quietly turning allegations into findings or findings into vibes.

The basic problem is already serious enough.

We do not need to help it.

---

## 1. ☕️ So What Was Spycops?

The **Special Demonstration Squad** began in 1968 after disorder surrounding demonstrations against the Vietnam War.

It became a secret Metropolitan Police Special Branch unit using officers under long-term cover inside political and activist organisations.

The Inquiry’s Tranche 1 report describes deployments settling into periods of around four years. Officers did not merely stand at the back of public meetings in sunglasses looking conspicuous.

They became people.

Or, more precisely, they **presented themselves as people who did not exist**.

```text
POLICE OFFICER
    ↓
COVER NAME
    ↓
COVER HISTORY
    ↓
POLITICAL IDENTITY
    ↓
FRIENDSHIPS
    ↓
SOCIAL LIFE
    ↓
TRUST
    ↓
ACCESS
    ↓
INFORMATION
```

That depth was operationally useful precisely because the people around the officer did not know they were interacting with the state.

The SDS was not the whole later story.

The **National Public Order Intelligence Unit** also deployed undercover officers into political and campaigning environments. The present Undercover Policing Inquiry is structured to examine the SDS first, then the NPOIU, then other undercover policing in England and Wales across the period from 1968 to the present, alongside management, oversight and current and future practice.

So when this cluster says **Spycops**, it is using a convenient public label for a much larger set of questions about undercover policing, political surveillance, intimacy, information and state power.

---

## 2. 🪧 Political Life Became An Access Route

The Inquiry’s record is particularly striking because much SDS activity was directed into left-wing, anarchist, anti-racist, environmental and other campaigning environments.

The Tranche 1 interim report is not coy about the historical distribution.

It records long-running infiltration of Trotskyist organisations and extensive reporting on left-wing and anarchist groups. For the period it examined, the Chair concluded that the **great majority** of SDS deployments did not meet the criteria that could have justified infiltration on grounds of threats to public order or the safety and well-being of the state.

Some deployments did provide useful public-order intelligence.

Some officers performed difficult work conscientiously and believed they were acting lawfully in the public interest.

Both facts belong in the record.

So does this one:

> the Inquiry Chair concluded that, for a police unit, the ends did not justify the means used by the SDS during the period examined.

That matters because political affiliation was not merely information being collected at arm’s length.

It could become the route through which an officer entered somebody’s ordinary social world.

```text
POLITICAL ACTIVITY
      ↓
TARGET / COMMUNITY OF INTEREST
      ↓
UNDERCOVER OFFICER
      ↓
FRIEND
      ↓
COMRADE
      ↓
HOUSEMATE / PARTNER / LOVER
      ↓
PRIVATE LIFE BECOMES
PART OF THE INFORMATION ENVIRONMENT
```

Political organisations are not immune from lawful investigation.

Nor does lawful investigation create an unlimited zone of permissible intrusion around every human being encountered during it.

That distinction is going to become important.

## 3. 🚩 And What About The Far Right?

This needs precision because the historical asymmetry is real, but the easiest explanation is not necessarily the correct one.

During the period covered by the Tranche 1 interim report, **the SDS did not deploy undercover officers into extreme-right organisations**.

The Inquiry Chair did **not** find that this resulted from political bias by those responsible for SDS targeting.

The report records two principal reasons: Special Branch believed it already had good sources within the extreme right, and officers considered infiltration dangerous because an undercover officer might be exposed to violence or required to participate in it to establish credibility.

That does not make the asymmetry analytically uninteresting.

Quite the opposite.

It gives us a rather peculiar incentive question:

> **Can being harder and more dangerous to infiltrate protect a political environment from a particularly intrusive form of surveillance?**

If one community is comparatively easy for an officer to enter, while another presents unacceptable operational danger, the resulting surveillance burden can become uneven **without anybody needing to sit in a room and announce a partisan policy**.

That is a systems question.

It is not proof that police preferred the far right.

It is a reason to examine how operational incentives distribute state intrusion.

The later Inquiry record is broader than Tranche 1, so this is not the place to turn one historical period into an eternal rule.

But the line belongs on the map.

## 4. 🧸 The Dead Children

Yes.

This part is real.

SDS officers used the identities of children who had died to construct undercover identities.

The logic was brutally practical.

A completely invented person may have no documentary past.

A real birth identity has one.

```text
CHILD IS BORN
    ↓
CHILD DIES
    ↓
PUBLIC RECORD REMAINS
    ↓
OFFICER RESEARCHES RECORD
    ↓
IDENTITY BECOMES COVER MATERIAL
    ↓
ADULT OFFICER LIVES
UNDER THAT IDENTITY
```

The Tranche 1 evidence showed the practice becoming established during the early 1970s.

One of the most disturbing features is not merely that it happened.

It is the informational assumption underneath it:

**the family was not expected to find out.**

That is a recurring systems failure worth noticing.

```text
IF PERSON DOES NOT KNOW
THEY HAVE BEEN HARMED
        ↓
HARM BECOMES EASY
TO DISCOUNT
```

Except people do sometimes find out.

Records survive.

Officers speak.

Journalists investigate.

Families ask questions.

Databases become searchable.

People compare notes.

The information environment changes.

And a decision that once appeared operationally invisible arrives decades later in somebody else’s life.

The Metropolitan Police has apologised to families whose deceased children’s identities were used.

The Home Office’s 2026 consultation explicitly identifies those families as among the people directly affected by the Inquiry.

The dead child was not administrative raw material.

The surviving family was not imaginary.

## 5. 🌸 The Women

Some undercover officers formed sexual relationships with women they encountered while deployed.

Some relationships were brief.

Some lasted years.

Some women believed they were building lives with the men concerned.

At least some officers were married in their real identities.

Children were conceived in some of the undercover relationships.

The women did not know that the men with whom they were having sex were police officers operating constructed identities and gathering intelligence.

That sentence is the centre of an enormous amount of the later argument.

In 2015, settling civil claims brought by seven women, the Metropolitan Police issued an unusually stark apology.

It described long-term intimate relationships as **“abusive, deceitful, manipulative and wrong”**, acknowledged violations of the women’s human rights and abuse of police power, accepted failures of supervision and management, and said the women had been **“deceived pure and simple.”**

That matters.

The proposition that these relationships presented a serious institutional problem is not merely an accusation being made from outside policing in 2026.

The Metropolitan Police itself has already acknowledged substantial wrongdoing.

The Inquiry is still trying to establish the scale, mechanisms, management knowledge, institutional responsibility and lessons.

## 6. 🧠 Consent Is A Shitload Of Context

This cluster is going to return to this repeatedly, so we may as well put the foundation down now.

Consent does not happen inside an informational vacuum.

When somebody chooses whether to enter a sexual relationship, the information they understand about the other person can matter enormously.

```text
SHE SAYS YES
     ↓
TO WHOM DOES SHE BELIEVE
SHE IS SAYING YES?

NAME
HISTORY
OCCUPATION
POLITICAL POSITION
REASON FOR BEING THERE
RELATIONSHIP CONTEXT
FUTURE INTENTIONS
```

Now compare:

```text
PERSON SHE BELIEVES EXISTS

versus

POLICE OFFICER
OPERATING A CONSTRUCTED IDENTITY
AS PART OF A COVERT DEPLOYMENT
```

For affected women who have said they would not have entered the sexual relationship had they known the truth, the concealed information was not trivia surrounding the consent.

**It was part of the conditions under which the choice became possible.**

There is an important legal firewall here.

This node is **not** claiming that every deception about identity or circumstance automatically makes sexual activity rape under English criminal law.

Criminal offences have specific elements and the law governing consent by deception is narrower than the ordinary-language question:

> **Would I have chosen this sexual relationship if I had known who you actually were and why you were in my life?**

Those are related questions.

They are not identical questions.

Do not flatten them.

## 7. 🪪 The State Can Authorise Its Officer. It Cannot Authorise Her Consent.

Undercover policing necessarily involves authorised deception.

Otherwise it would be remarkably unsuccessful undercover policing.

The state can create legal and operational frameworks governing what an officer may do.

It can authorise a deployment.

It can authorise surveillance within the law.

It can approve expenditure.

It can establish cover.

It can decide that intelligence is necessary and proportionate.

But there is a category boundary here:

```text
STATE → OFFICER

YOU ARE AUTHORISED
TO PERFORM AN OPERATION
```

is not the same transaction as:

```text
PERSON ↔ PERSON

DO I WANT TO HAVE
SEX WITH YOU?
```

The state cannot issue the second person's consent on her behalf.

That sounds painfully obvious.

Unfortunately Britain has lore.

The deeper conceptual work belongs in [🏏 Where Have All The Linesmen Gone?](./🏏_where_have_all_the_linesmen_gone.md).

For the history node, the important fact is simply that sexual relationships occurred inside operational environments built around concealed state identities, and that the Met has itself acknowledged serious abuse and institutional failures in relation to some of them.

## 8. 👶 Children Were Not Operational Debris

The sexual relationships do not end analytically with the two adults.

Children were conceived.

One particularly well-documented case concerns former SDS officer **Bob Lambert**, who, while deployed as “Bob Robinson”, had a relationship with a woman known publicly as **Jacqui** and fathered a son.

Evidence and submissions before the Inquiry describe Lambert continuing to parent the child in his cover identity before disappearing from that life when the deployment ended.

Lambert has accepted that fathering a child in his undercover identity was wrong. He has also accepted that the sexual relationships he had while undercover were wrong.

There remain disputed questions around what colleagues and managers knew, and when.

That distinction matters.

**The existence of the child and relationship is not the disputed bit.**

Some of the institutional knowledge around it is.

For the child, the information problem is staggering.

```text
MY FATHER
    ↓
HIS NAME
    ↓
HIS HISTORY
    ↓
WHY HE LEFT
    ↓
WHO I AM
```

can later become:

```text
HE WAS A POLICE OFFICER
USING A COVERT IDENTITY
DURING AN OPERATION
```

A child did not consent to become part of an intelligence operation.

Nor does the possibility that disclosure itself may cause pain make permanent concealment benign.

It tells us that **the original deception created a situation in which truth itself could later become traumatic information**.

That is a very expensive kind of secrecy.

## 9. 💍 There Were Families On The Other Side Of The Cover Identity Too

The constructed undercover life was not the officer’s only life.

Some officers had wives, partners and children outside the deployment.

Kate Wilson’s case, for example, concerned an intimate relationship with Mark Kennedy while Kennedy was married and had children.

That does not make the officer’s spouse equivalent to the woman deceived through the undercover identity.

The informational relationships are different.

The harms may be different.

The legal questions are different.

But it does mean that the operational system could have consequences travelling in more than one direction.

```text
REAL-LIFE FAMILY
        ←
     OFFICER
        →
UNDERCOVER SOCIAL WORLD
        ↓
FRIENDSHIPS / SEX /
INTIMACY / CHILDREN
```

The Home Office’s 2026 consultation expressly includes **families of undercover officers** among the people likely to have been significantly affected.

That is useful.

Because it keeps us from reducing the story to:

**OFFICER versus TARGET.**

There were other human beings in the information environment.

A system designed around maintaining cover can become very good at seeing operational risk while becoming remarkably bad at seeing everybody who will eventually have to live with the operation.

---

## 10. 🏠 The Woman Was In The Operation But Missing From The Risk Model

This is one of the core Polaris observations.

The woman was physically present.

She was speaking to the officer.

She was sharing information.

She may have been sharing her home.

Her friends.

Her body.

Her plans.

Her vulnerabilities.

Her political world.

She was therefore plainly inside the **causal system**.

But she was not necessarily represented inside the **operational model** as somebody to whom the operation itself might be doing harm.

That distinction matters.

```text
OPERATIONAL MODEL

TARGET
OFFICER
COVER
INTELLIGENCE
MANAGEMENT
PUBLIC ORDER
SECURITY
RISK OF EXPOSURE
```

Where is:

```text
THE PERSON
WHO THINKS
THIS IS HER BOYFRIEND?
```

Modern surveillance law uses the concept of **collateral intrusion**: interference with the private and family lives of people who are not themselves the intended subjects of surveillance must be considered and, where practicable, minimised.

The historical record helps explain why that idea matters.

People outside the formal target box can still absorb enormous operational consequences.

## 11. 🏳️‍🌈 Do Not Mistake The Visible Record For Every Person Who Could Have Been Harmed

The publicly documented sexual-deception cases are strongly gendered.

Male officers deceiving women are central to the record.

That is important evidence.

It should not be converted into the stronger claim that **no man was ever sexually deceived by an undercover officer** unless the evidence can actually support that.

There are obvious reasons why a historical record of intimate conduct may be incomplete.

People may never have discovered an officer’s identity.

People may not have disclosed a relationship.

Records may be missing.

People may have died.

And for much of the period under examination, gay and bisexual men lived within a Britain in which sexuality itself could expose them to criminalisation, police attention, discrimination or profound social risk.

So the correct historical position is:

> **the known and publicly documented pattern is heavily gendered; the complete population of people affected may not be knowable from the visible record.**

That uncertainty does not weaken the gender analysis.

It makes the research question better.

Later in this cluster we are going to ask a rather impolite but useful counterfactual about whether male officers’ own sexual boundaries were treated as operationally negotiable in anything like the same way.

That belongs in `🏏`.

For now:

**do not erase the women we can see, and do not manufacture certainty about everybody we cannot.**

## 12. 🕸️ Was Intimacy Becoming Network Infrastructure?

Activist communities are social systems.

People do not merely attend meetings and then disappear into individual filing cabinets.

They have friends.

Partners.

Homes.

Parties.

Arguments.

Shared transport.

Political disagreements.

Trust.

```text
PERSON
  ↓
FRIEND
  ↓
PARTNER
  ↓
HOME
  ↓
SOCIAL NETWORK
  ↓
POLITICAL NETWORK
  ↓
TRUST
  ↓
INFORMATION
```

That makes intimacy operationally interesting even before anybody has sex.

An officer who is trusted can learn things that an obvious police observer cannot.

But this is precisely why the next arrow needs examination rather than assumption:

```text
INFILTRATE GROUP
      ↓
BECOME TRUSTED
      ↓
FRIENDSHIPS
      ↓
SOCIAL LIFE
      ↓
ACCESS
      ↓
SEX
```

Hang on.

**That is a new arrow.**

Show your working.

The existence of an operational reason to become socially embedded does not automatically establish an operational necessity for sexual intimacy.

One of the historical questions is therefore not merely:

> Did officers have sex while undercover?

It is:

> **How did a policing system distinguish between the social intimacy necessary to maintain cover and sexual relationships that crossed a different boundary?**

And if the distinction was inadequately drawn, taught, supervised or enforced:

**where were the linesmen?**

## 13. 👁️ Who Was Actually Watching The Watchers?

The historical answer is not “nobody”.

There were managers.

There were authorisations.

There was Special Branch hierarchy.

There were relationships with the Security Service.

There was Home Office funding.

Later there were statutory surveillance frameworks.

The more useful question is:

> **What did the oversight system actually see?**

The Inquiry has repeatedly had to examine:

- selection;
- training;
- tasking;
- authorisation;
- supervision;
- reporting;
- management knowledge;
- misconduct;
- intelligence handling;
- collateral intrusion;
- and whether managers understood what officers were doing inside their cover identities.

A formal control is not automatically an effective control.

```text
RULE EXISTS
    ≠
BEHAVIOUR IS VISIBLE

MANAGER EXISTS
    ≠
MANAGER KNOWS

AUTHORISATION EXISTS
    ≠
EVERY CONSEQUENCE
HAS BEEN MODELLED
```

This is why the Inquiry cannot be reduced to a catalogue of individual officers behaving badly.

Individual conduct matters.

So does the machinery around it.

## 14. ✊ Anti-Racist Campaigning Is Part Of This History Too

The Spycops record cannot honestly be narrated as though it concerns one homogeneous population of white environmental activists.

The Inquiry has examined reporting touching the **Stephen Lawrence family campaign**, **Duwayne Brooks**, anti-racist activism and justice campaigning.

The Inquiry’s opening materials record earlier findings that an SDS deployment into activist groups seeking to influence the Lawrence campaign produced reporting containing personal information about the Lawrence family, and that reporting on Duwayne Brooks included material that an earlier independent review concluded should have been stopped.

The precise questions of targeting, authorisation, management knowledge and motive have required further Inquiry examination.

That distinction is important.

There is documented reporting.

There are also contested questions about why it happened and how far responsibility travelled.

This is one reason the later cluster must be careful with phrases such as **“safe masculinity.”**

Police authority is not received in an informational vacuum either.

Race changes the history standing in the room.

So can class, sexuality, disability, immigration status, political history and previous police contact.

A manner that reassures one person may not reassure another.

The institutional obligation is not to compel everybody to **feel safe**.

It is to behave lawfully and ethically, build systems that identify unsafe conduct, and give people evidence over time that those systems work.

## 15. ⚖️ The Kate Wilson Case Made The Institutional Dimension Very Difficult To Ignore

Kate Wilson was deceived into an intimate relationship with NPOIU undercover officer Mark Kennedy.

Her litigation before the Investigatory Powers Tribunal became one of the clearest legal examinations of the institutional consequences of undercover sexual deception.

The Tribunal found serious violations of her Convention rights.

The case matters historically because it prevents the story being reduced to:

> **one officer had an inappropriate affair.**

The legal scrutiny extended into authorisation, supervision, management and the operation of the undercover system.

Again, this node is not the full legal analysis.

The point is simpler.

By the time the Public Inquiry was examining these issues, affected women had already done years of investigative, legal and public work forcing the state to confront conduct that had occurred behind covert identities.

The information did not simply float naturally into the public record.

People had to drag it there.

## 16. 🔦 How Did Anybody Find Out?

This may be one of the most important pieces of the history.

The system was secret.

The relationships were secret.

The officers’ real identities were secret.

The unit was secret.

The people affected were therefore not standing outside the system with a complete evidential package waiting for somebody to process their complaint.

They had fragments.

Suspicion.

Contradictions.

Documents.

Memories.

Other people.

Journalists.

Campaigners.

Lawyers.

Eventually, patterns.

The Met’s own 2015 apology credited the **“courage and tenacity”** of the women for bringing the relationships to light.

That matters cybernetically.

```text
SECRET SYSTEM
    ↓
PERSON EXPERIENCES
SOMETHING WRONG
    ↓
INCOMPLETE INFORMATION
    ↓
COMPARE NOTES
    ↓
INVESTIGATE
    ↓
IDENTIFY PATTERN
    ↓
LITIGATION / JOURNALISM /
CAMPAIGNING / DISCLOSURE
    ↓
STATE RECORD CHANGES
```

The people harmed became part of the state’s error-detection mechanism.

That is not how a healthy safeguarding system ideally discovers its own failure.

But it is how this one became visible.

## 17. 🏛️ Why Is There A Public Inquiry?

The Undercover Policing Inquiry was established in **2015** under the Inquiries Act 2005 to examine undercover policing in England and Wales since 1968.

It was originally expected to take about three years.

It has taken rather longer.

The structure matters.

The Inquiry has divided its work into modules and tranches examining:

- deployments and their impact;
- management and oversight;
- legal and regulatory frameworks;
- the SDS;
- the NPOIU;
- other undercover policing;
- and current and future practice.

As of **20 September 2026**, the Home Office is consulting on the future structure, scope and format of the remaining work.

The consultation says that, once Sir John Mitting completes his work on SDS Tranches 1–3, the work still not covered under the existing plan includes:

- **Tranche 4:** the NPOIU, 1999–2011;
- **Tranche 5:** other undercover policing beyond the SDS and NPOIU, 1968–present;
- **Module 3:** current and future undercover-policing practice.

The Home Office says ministers are concerned about duration, cost and delays to outcomes, while also saying the remaining process must deliver truth, accountability and learning.

Those are the government’s stated positions.

Whether changing the machinery can finish the outstanding work without losing necessary truth-finding, participation, disclosure, accountability or institutional learning is a live question.

That belongs primarily in the current nodes.

Historically, however, the important point is:

**the story is not finished.**

## 18. ⏳ Why Has It Taken So Long?

There is no single honest answer.

The Inquiry spans decades.

It deals with covert identities, anonymity, national-security material, deceased witnesses, old records, disputed recollections, legal challenges, enormous disclosure exercises and people whose lives were profoundly affected by the events under examination.

The Metropolitan Police says its disclosure work has involved tens of thousands of documents and hundreds of thousands of pages.

That does not automatically establish that every year of delay was necessary.

Nor does the fact of delay establish that the Inquiry has produced no value.

Those are different questions.

A process can be:

```text
IMPORTANT
AND
TOO SLOW

EXPENSIVE
AND
STILL CONTAIN
UNANSWERED QUESTIONS

IMPERFECT
AND
PRODUCING INFORMATION
THAT DID NOT EXIST BEFORE
```

This cluster is going to be extremely annoying about refusing false binaries.

Sorry.

## 19. 🧭 What Still Has To Be Learned?

The remaining questions are not merely historical trivia.

They include questions about:

- the NPOIU;
- other undercover policing beyond the two best-known units;
- management and oversight;
- legal and regulatory architecture;
- what safeguards now exist;
- whether those safeguards address the failure modes revealed by the historical record;
- and how undercover policing should operate in future.

That final step is why this history belongs in **Prosocial Roots** rather than being preserved only as a museum of institutional horror.

The useful sequence is:

```text
HARM
  ↓
DISCOVERY
  ↓
EVIDENCE
  ↓
MECHANISM
  ↓
ACKNOWLEDGEMENT
  ↓
LEARNING
  ↓
SAFEGUARD
  ↓
TEST
  ↓
FEEDBACK
```

If we stop at:

```text
HARM
  ↓
DISCOVERY
  ↓
BRO WTF
```

we have not finished.

The `BRO WTF` is important.

It is not, on its own, a governance system.

## 20. 🌱 The Point Is Not To Freeze Everybody In Their Worst Historical Understanding

There is a humane way to read this history without making it toothless.

Some officers and managers may have sincerely believed that what they were doing served the public.

Some may have operated inside professional cultures where conduct now regarded as unacceptable was normalised, inadequately questioned or poorly supervised.

That does not erase the people harmed.

Nor does acknowledging harm require every person who participated in the institution to decide that his entire life was secretly evil.

People can learn.

Institutions can learn.

A former officer can say:

> I understood this differently then.

> I can see something now that I did not see adequately at the time.

> I would not do that now.

> I would not permit somebody under my command to do it now.

That is not an escape from accountability.

It is **extremely useful accountability information**.

Because then we can ask:

- What changed your mind?
- What did you fail to see?
- What would have helped you see it?
- What did management reward?
- What did the culture make difficult to challenge?
- What would you tell a young officer now?
- What rule or safeguard would have made the better decision easier?

The point is not to hate the man you were.

The point is to make sure the man you are now knows better — and that the institution does too.

There is an olive branch here for anybody prepared to take it.

There is also, respectfully:

**bro. what the fuck.**

Both can be true.

## 21. 🏏 History Is Useful When It Makes The Next Line Easier To See

This node does not ask every police officer to become politically identical to the people who were surveilled.

It does not require every officer to become a particular kind of man.

It does not require pretending that undercover policing has no legitimate purpose.

It asks something much more practical.

When a difficult operational choice arrives:

> **What kind of officer would you like to believe you would be when doing the right thing costs you something?**

And then:

> **What do you need now to make that person more likely to turn up when the moment actually comes?**

Clearer rules?

Better supervision?

A colleague willing to challenge you?

A manager who does not punish dissent?

Training that treats civilians as people inside the risk model?

A culture in which admitting uncertainty is not weakness?

An institution capable of saying **we got that wrong** without collapsing into either denial or ritual self-destruction?

Those are not abstract masculinity questions.

They are professional-system questions.

The history matters because it gives us situations in which the line was missing, blurred, ignored or insufficiently enforced.

The next node asks where that line actually is.
