# 🗺️ Information Has to Travel
**First created:** 2026-09-06 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-06  
*Who knew what, and was there a route by which the person capable of acting could know it too?*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The first three `LOOK_UP` nodes establish that:

- warning behaviour may already be visible;
- legitimate intervention exists before the most serious offence;
- misogyny can sometimes contribute useful contextual information to a wider behavioural risk picture.

That still leaves a systems problem.

Information can exist without being **available where it matters**.

```text
observed
≠
disclosed
≠
recorded
≠
retrievable
≠
accessible
≠
shared
≠
received
≠
understood
≠
acted upon
```

A family member can know something.

A survivor can have reported something.

A university can have recorded something.

A platform can have moderated something.

A police force can have logged something.

A specialist service can have assessed something.

And the person making the next relevant decision may still know none of it.

The governing question is therefore:

> **Who knew what, and was there a route by which the person capable of acting could know it too?**

---

## 📡 “Nobody Knew” Contains Several Different Claims

When a serious outcome occurs, institutions may say that relevant information was not known.

That can describe very different states:

```text
never observed

observed but never disclosed

disclosed but not recorded

recorded but not retrievable

retrievable but inaccessible

accessible but not shared

shared but not received

received but not connected

connected but misunderstood

understood but not acted upon
```

Those are not semantic variations.

They are different failure modes with different remedies.

If the information was never observable, a better database would not have helped.

If it was reported but badly recorded, the problem may be intake.

If it was recorded but siloed, the problem may be access or referral architecture.

If it reached the right institution but sat in a queue, the problem may be capacity.

If several fragments arrived but nobody recognised their combined significance, the problem is pattern recognition.

So:

> **Information existing somewhere in a system is not the same thing as information being operationally available.**

---

## 📮 A Referral Is a Transmission, Not an Outcome

`🌱_before_the_offence.md` asks where a concern goes once somebody recognises it.

The answer cannot stop at:

```text
referral made
        ↓
problem transferred
```

A functioning pathway looks more like:

```text
information sent
        ↓
information received?
        ↓
usable?
        ↓
triaged?
        ↓
accepted?
        ↓
combined with existing information?
        ↓
action?
        ↓
feedback / continuity?
```

An organisation can successfully complete its own **send** operation while the wider safeguarding system achieves very little.

This matters for accountability.

Useful measures include not only:

- number of referrals made;

but:

- number received;
- number successfully matched to a person/case;
- number accepted;
- time to assessment;
- time to intervention;
- reasons for rejection;
- whether the referring party receives meaningful feedback where appropriate;
- what happens to responsibility while the referral is pending.

> **A referral is not the same thing as an intervention.**

---

## 🏡 Informal Knowledge Has an Interface Problem

Much prevention-relevant knowledge begins outside formal institutions.

A friend may say:

> “He's becoming obsessed with her.”

A parent may say:

> “Something has changed dramatically.”

A housemate may say:

> “He's turning up outside her work.”

A woman may say:

> “He isn't accepting that I've ended this.”

These are ordinary-language observations.

Formal systems tend to organise information through professional categories:

```text
stalking
coercive control
harassment
domestic abuse
fixation
safeguarding
radicalisation
disciplinary misconduct
risk
```

That creates a translation problem:

```text
human observation
        ↓
???
        ↓
institutionally legible information
```

A good safeguarding interface should not require a family member, friend or survivor to diagnose the category correctly before somebody will listen.

> **People should be able to report what happened. Systems should do the classification.**

That does not mean every observation warrants escalation.

It means the reporting interface should not make professional vocabulary a prerequisite for being heard.

---

## 🧭 Classification Should Follow the Information

A person may not know whether the conduct they are describing is:

- stalking;
- harassment;
- domestic abuse;
- a safeguarding concern;
- a disciplinary matter;
- an online-safety problem;
- something requiring specialist VAWG support;
- something else entirely.

Requiring the reporter to select the correct institutional door can create predictable loss.

```text
wrong door
        ↓
wrong form
        ↓
wrong category
        ↓
“not for us”
        ↓
information disappears
```

A better system asks:

> **What happened?**

Then:

> **Who needs to know?**

The taxonomy exists to help the institution organise its response.

It should not become an obstacle course for the person supplying the information.

---

## 🧱 Information Has to Travel Does Not Mean Share Everything

There is an obvious bad solution to fragmented information:

```text
MORE DATA
+
MORE SHARING
=
SAFER
```

That does not follow.

Poorly governed information-sharing can create:

- privacy violations;
- irrelevant information accumulating indefinitely;
- gossip becoming institutional fact;
- inaccurate records propagating;
- context being stripped away;
- reputational contamination;
- chilling effects on help-seeking;
- discriminatory profiling;
- enormous volumes of low-quality material obscuring useful signals;
- information being reused for purposes the person who disclosed it could not reasonably anticipate.

The objective is therefore not maximum information flow.

> **The objective is useful information reaching an appropriate decision-maker through a lawful, proportionate and accountable route.**

That requires restraint as well as connectivity.

---

## 🎯 Purpose Matters

Before information travels, ask:

> **For what purpose?**

Safeguarding somebody is not the same activity as:

- criminal investigation;
- disciplinary action;
- clinical care;
- platform moderation;
- counter-extremism;
- employment vetting;
- intelligence collection.

Information that is appropriate and necessary for one purpose is not automatically appropriate for every other purpose.

So any information architecture needs to retain questions such as:

- Why is this information being collected?
- Why does this recipient need it?
- What decision can they make with it?
- Is the information relevant to that decision?
- Is sharing proportionate?
- What safeguards apply?
- How long should it remain available?
- Can the subject or reporter correct materially inaccurate information where appropriate?

Purpose limitation is not an irritating obstacle to prevention.

It is part of making prevention trustworthy enough for people to use.

---

## 🧹 Data Quality Is Safeguarding

Once information moves between systems, provenance matters.

A receiving organisation should be able, where relevant, to distinguish:

```text
direct observation

first-person disclosure

third-party report

professional assessment

allegation

substantiated finding

inference

historical information

correction / dispute
```

These are not equivalent kinds of information.

If those distinctions disappear during transmission, integration can become **rumour laundering**:

```text
someone says X
        ↓
record says X
        ↓
second organisation receives record
        ↓
“Organisation A says X”
        ↓
third organisation receives it
        ↓
“multiple sources indicate X”
```

One originating claim has now acquired the appearance of independent corroboration merely by travelling.

That is the opposite of good pattern recognition.

The aim should be:

> **more context, not merely more copies.**

---

## 🧾 Provenance Has to Travel With the Claim

Useful information-sharing should preserve enough metadata to answer:

- Who originally supplied this?
- When?
- Was it directly observed or reported by somebody else?
- What exactly was alleged or observed?
- Was it investigated?
- What was the outcome?
- Has the assessment changed?
- Is the information disputed?
- Has anything material subsequently corrected it?
- Is this genuinely a second source, or merely a copy of the first?

Without provenance, the receiving system may know **more statements** while knowing less about what any of them mean.

---

## 🔄 Corrections Have to Travel Too

Information systems often propagate concern more easily than correction.

That creates a structural bias.

Imagine:

```text
risk information
        ↓
shared to A
        ↓
copied to B
        ↓
summarised for C
```

Then later:

```text
mistaken identity corrected
        or
assessment materially revised
        or
context changes meaning
        ↓
correction remains at source
```

The wider system now contains several live versions of information that the originating institution no longer treats the same way.

So:

> **If a system can propagate risk information, it needs some mechanism for propagating material correction.**

Relevant updates can include:

- mistaken identity;
- factual correction;
- changed professional assessment;
- new contextual information;
- findings that did not substantiate an allegation;
- information showing that two records were incorrectly linked;
- other material changes in status.

This is fairness infrastructure.

It is also data-quality infrastructure.

A system making safeguarding decisions from stale or materially inaccurate information is not safer because it possesses more data.

---

## 🔮 Information Has to Accumulate Without Becoming Immortal

The next node, `🔮_pattern_recognition_not_incident_processing.md`, requires institutional memory.

But memory without review becomes sediment.

The system needs both:

```text
DON'T FORGET EVERYTHING
EVERY TIME
```

and:

```text
DON'T REMEMBER EVERYTHING
FOREVER AS EQUALLY TRUE
```

Useful longitudinal records therefore need some combination of:

- provenance;
- dates;
- context;
- status;
- relevance;
- correction;
- appropriate retention;
- review;
- links to genuinely related information;
- separation from unrelated material.

The design problem is not simply whether institutions remember.

It is whether they can remember **well**.

---

## 🔗 Linking Is an Analytical Act

Joining two records is not neutral.

The link itself makes a claim:

> **These things belong in the same picture.**

Sometimes that is obvious.

Sometimes it is not.

Bad linkage can create:

- mistaken identity;
- guilt by association;
- false pattern;
- duplicated allegations appearing independent;
- irrelevant political or social material contaminating a safeguarding assessment.

Good linkage therefore needs reasons.

Ask:

- Same person?
- Same target?
- Same relationship?
- Same behaviour?
- Same grievance?
- Same timeframe?
- Same source copied several times?
- What justifies treating these records as connected?

Pattern recognition depends upon joining information.

Safeguards depend upon remembering that **joining information is itself a judgement**.

---

## 🚪 Information Also Needs Somewhere to Arrive

Perfect transmission into an overwhelmed service is not a functioning prevention system.

```text
excellent report
        ↓
excellent referral
        ↓
correct recipient
        ↓
six-month queue
```

Technically:

> information travelled.

Operationally:

> risk did not.

So we need to distinguish:

```text
information transfer
```

from:

```text
operational transfer of responsibility
```

Questions include:

- Has the recipient accepted responsibility?
- Does the sender know?
- Does the person at risk know?
- What happens during the waiting period?
- Who can respond if circumstances escalate?
- Who is checking whether the information remains current?
- What happens if the recipient rejects the referral?

This returns us to the question from `🌱_before_the_offence.md`:

> **Who owns the risk between services?**

---

## 🩸 How Many Times Does She Have to Tell You?

Survivor reporting is an unusually useful test of information architecture.

Ask:

> **How many times does the same person have to tell the same story?**

Some repetition may be unavoidable.

Seven complete retellings to seven organisations tells us something else.

It can indicate that:

- systems cannot retrieve earlier information;
- institutions cannot communicate;
- referrals do not carry sufficient context;
- the survivor has become the interoperability layer.

But the solution is not uncontrolled circulation of everything somebody has ever disclosed.

Good architecture has to hold both principles:

```text
reduce unnecessary repetition
```

and:

```text
do not turn disclosure into
uncontrolled distribution
```

That means thinking about:

- consent where relevant;
- necessity;
- purpose;
- access;
- confidentiality;
- survivor safety;
- what information actually needs to move;
- what can remain where it was disclosed.

**Joined-up working is not synonymous with everybody gets the file.**

---

## 🕳️ Silence Can Be Produced by the System

Information flow also depends upon whether people are willing and able to report.

If disclosure predictably produces:

- uncontrolled sharing;
- disbelief;
- retaliation;
- repeated retelling;
- loss of privacy;
- no meaningful response;
- burdensome administration;
- unexplained closure;

people may rationally stop supplying information.

The system can then experience the resulting absence as:

> `no reports`

when the actual condition is:

> **the reporting channel became too costly to use.**

That makes trust part of information architecture.

A system that frightens away its own information sources cannot solve the problem by demanding more data.

## 🗺️ Map the Journey, Not Just the Database

A useful review should be capable of tracing one piece of relevant information through its whole journey.

For example:

```text
OBSERVATION
Who noticed?

        ↓

DISCLOSURE
Who was told?

        ↓

RECORD
What was written down?

        ↓

CLASSIFICATION
How was it categorised?

        ↓

ACCESS
Who could retrieve it?

        ↓

REFERRAL
Where was it sent?

        ↓

RECEIPT
Did the next service receive it?

        ↓

INTEGRATION
Was it connected to existing information?

        ↓

INTERPRETATION
What did the combined picture mean?

        ↓

ACTION
What happened?

        ↓

FEEDBACK
Did anyone know the outcome?

        ↓

UPDATE
Could later information or correction change the record?
```

That map is much more revealing than asking simply:

> `Was information shared?`

## 🧪 Questions for an Information-Sharing System

### Relevance

- What information actually needs to travel?
- What would be noise?
- What would be actively misleading?

### Purpose

- Why is it being shared?
- What can the recipient legitimately do with it?

### Provenance

- Where did the information originate?
- Is it observation, allegation, assessment or inference?
- Are apparently multiple sources genuinely independent?

### Routing

- Who needs it?
- Is there a clear route?
- What happens if the first route is wrong?

### Receipt

- Do we know it arrived?
- Is somebody responsible for triage?

### Integration

- Can it be connected to relevant previous information?
- Who decides that two records belong together?

### Capacity

- Can the recipient actually act?
- What happens while they cannot?

### Correction

- Can materially inaccurate or superseded information be updated?
- Do corrections propagate as effectively as concerns?

### Privacy and safeguards

- Who can access it?
- For how long?
- Can it be reused for another purpose?
- What controls prevent gossip, profiling or mission creep?

### Feedback

- Does the sender know whether responsibility transferred?
- Does the person at risk know what to do if circumstances change?

That is the difference between **information sharing** as a slogan and information architecture as an operational system.

## 🌉 The Bridge Out of `LOOK_UP`

At this point, the problem is becoming larger than misogyny or VAWG.

We now have:

```text
SIGNAL
        ↓
RECORD
        ↓
TRANSMISSION
        ↓
INTEGRATION
        ↓
INTERPRETATION
        ↓
ACTION
```

Those are questions about **how institutions know things together**.

They lead naturally towards:

- interoperability;
- institutional memory;
- data fusion;
- governance;
- provenance;
- access control;
- purpose limitation;
- feedback loops;
- correction mechanisms;
- human judgement;
- responsibility for the assembled picture.

That is why this node sits inside `LOOK_UP` while also facing outward.

The prevention problem is not solved by making every institution know everything.

It is solved when relevant information can:

```text
reach the right place
+
retain enough context
to remain meaningful
+
be corrected when wrong
+
meet somebody with
authority and capacity
to respond
```

That is not merely information sharing.

> **It is information architecture.**

---

## 🧭 Core Finding

Information does not become useful merely because it exists.

Nor does a system become joined-up merely because data can technically move.

Good prevention requires a much more disciplined chain:

> **relevant information, with provenance and context, moving for a legitimate purpose through an accountable route to somebody capable of doing something useful with it.**

And the inverse matters too.

Information that is:

- irrelevant;
- stale;
- duplicated;
- stripped of provenance;
- materially wrong;
- impossible to correct;
- shared without purpose;
- sent somewhere with no capacity to act

can make a system **less** capable of recognising genuine risk.

So the governing question remains wonderfully simple:

> **Who knew what, and was there a route by which the person capable of acting could know it too?**
