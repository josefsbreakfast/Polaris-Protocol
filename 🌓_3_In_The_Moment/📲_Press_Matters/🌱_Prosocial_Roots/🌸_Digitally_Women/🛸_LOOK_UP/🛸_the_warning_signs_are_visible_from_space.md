# 🛸 The Warning Signs Are Visible From Space
**First created:** 2026-09-06 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-06  
*Sometimes the problem is not signal detection. It is that the system has successfully detected twelve different pieces of the same problem and filed them in twelve different conceptual drawers.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

`🌱_Thanks_Algorithm/` establishes that misogyny can form part of a wider online grievance and radicalisation ecology with offline consequences.

`🛸_LOOK_UP/` asks the next question:

> **What happens when those risks become observable?**

Prevention discussions often assume that the difficult problem is finding a faint signal before something terrible happens.

Sometimes it is.

Sometimes, however, the relevant behaviour is already:

- repeated;
- escalating;
- reported;
- documented;
- visible to peers;
- visible to institutions;
- distributed across several records or systems.

The failure is not necessarily that nobody saw anything.

It may be that nobody assembled what was seen into one intelligible risk picture.

**Question answered:** When do scattered, individually dismissible observations become a pattern somebody ought to recognise?

---

## 🛸 The Signal May Not Be Subtle

A serious outcome can be preceded by behaviour recorded under very different headings:

- stalking;
- fixation;
- misogynistic hostility;
- coercion;
- sexual misconduct;
- harassment;
- threats;
- escalating grievance;
- repeated boundary violations.

Any one observation may be ambiguous.

Any one report may fall below a particular institutional threshold.

Any one person may know only a fragment.

But the relevant analytical object is not always the individual fragment.

Sometimes it is:

```text
one report
+
another report
+
stalking
+
threats
+
sexual misconduct
+
fixation
+
escalating grievance
+
repeated misogynistic hostility
+
another victim
+
another institution
        ↓
a materially different risk picture
```

At some point, asking whether each item is individually dramatic enough becomes the wrong question.

The warning signs may, collectively, be visible from space.

## 📡 Five Different Failure Modes

It helps to distinguish failures that can otherwise be collapsed into:

> `Nobody knew.`

### 1. Signal detection failure

Nobody could reasonably identify the relevant behaviour or information.

### 2. Signal transmission failure

Somebody observed or received relevant information, but it did not reach the person or institution capable of acting on it.

### 3. Signal integration failure

Different people or institutions possessed relevant fragments, but nobody assembled them.

### 4. Interpretation failure

The information was available or assembled, but its significance was misunderstood or repeatedly minimised.

### 5. Action failure

The risk was recognised, but proportionate safeguarding, referral, investigation or other intervention did not follow.

These are different problems.

They require different remedies.

A new reporting system does little for an interpretation failure.

Better risk assessment does little if the information never reaches the assessor.

More data does little if nobody owns the combined picture.

`LOOK UP` is particularly interested in the latter four.

## 🔭 Visible Does Not Mean Obvious

This distinction is essential.

After serious violence, earlier events can suddenly look like an inevitable trajectory.

That creates a powerful hindsight problem.

A prevention system should resist:

- cherry-picking;
- retrospective inevitability;
- treating eccentricity as dangerousness;
- treating lawful beliefs as pre-crime;
- constructing a sinister narrative from unrelated facts;
- assuming every warning behaviour points towards the eventual outcome.

The stronger question is narrower:

> **What information was actually available at the time, what did it reasonably indicate then, and what response did that information justify then?**

Prevention does not require somebody to predict the final offence.

A stalking report can justify a stalking response without anybody predicting murder.

Escalating threats can justify assessment without certainty that violence will follow.

Sexual misconduct can warrant safeguarding action without proving a future serious sexual offence.

Repeated boundary violations can matter before anybody knows where the trajectory ends.

> **Risk recognition is not prophecy.**

The intervention can be proportionate to the problem already visible rather than to the worst imaginable future.

## 🧩 One Person, Many Administrative Identities

The same person's behaviour may appear differently depending on who encounters it.

```text
university
→ conduct complaint

police
→ harassment incident

employer
→ disciplinary concern

platform
→ abusive-content report

ex-partner
→ coercive behaviour

another woman
→ sexual misconduct disclosure

friend
→ increasingly violent grievance

school / university
→ safeguarding concern
```

Each description may be accurate.

Each institution may process its own incident competently.

Yet the system can still fail to recognise:

> **these are observations about the same behavioural trajectory.**

A person does not become seven different people because seven databases use different categories.

---

## 🗄️ Category Fragmentation

Institutions organise information according to their own functions.

Human behaviour does not respect those taxonomies.

A wider pattern involving:

- misogyny;
- stalking;
- fixation;
- grievance;
- sexual entitlement;
- coercion;
- threats;
- harassment;
- violent fantasy;
- repeated boundary violations

may consequently be distributed across completely different administrative categories.

No individual category necessarily contains enough information to express the whole pattern.

This produces an uncomfortable possibility:

> **Accurate filing can coexist with catastrophic understanding.**

The problem is not necessarily that the records are wrong.

It is that the categories used to process them do not themselves reconstruct the person, relationship or trajectory those records describe.

## ⏳ Temporal Fragmentation

Patterns also disappear across time.

```text
Incident A
→ assessed on Monday
→ closed

six months

Incident B
→ assessed as today's problem
→ closed

another institution

Incident C
→ different category
→ closed

another victim

Incident D
→ assessed alone
```

Every individual decision may ask:

> `Does this incident meet our threshold?`

But prevention sometimes requires a second question:

> **Does this incident change what is already known?**

A new event can matter even when it is not independently severe.

It may:

- confirm repetition;
- show escalation;
- identify another victim;
- establish fixation;
- contradict an earlier benign interpretation;
- reveal movement across settings;
- change the meaning of earlier information.

That distinction is developed fully in [🔮 Pattern Recognition, Not Incident Processing](./🔮_pattern_recognition_not_incident_processing.md).

## 👥 Victim Fragmentation

Different victims may each possess one part of the pattern.

They may:

- never meet;
- report to different organisations;
- describe different conduct;
- use different language;
- live in different places;
- have no way of knowing another report exists.

That creates a basic information asymmetry.

A woman experiencing one incident may have **one data point**.

An institution may have seven.

So:

> `Why didn't she realise there was a pattern?`

may be exactly backwards.

The institution may be the only actor structurally capable of discovering repetition across victims.

That creates responsibility for institutions to think about what their information systems can reveal without transferring the work of institutional intelligence back onto the people being harmed.

See [🩸 Survivor Support and Reporting](./🩸_survivor_support_and_reporting.md).

## 🥃 Misogyny Can Be One Signal Among Others

Misogynistic language alone does not establish violence risk.

Nor does holding misogynistic beliefs establish that somebody will commit an offence.

The useful question is whether persistent misogynistic hostility, gendered grievance, entitlement or rigid domination narratives can become **additional information** when they occur alongside behaviour such as:

- fixation;
- stalking;
- threats;
- coercive conduct;
- escalating grievance;
- sexual boundary violations;
- concrete targeting behaviour.

The analytical model is therefore not:

```text
MISOGYNY
   ↓
DANGER
```

It is closer to:

```text
behaviour
+
context
+
change over time
+
multiple observations
+
relevant grievance / hostility
        ↓
risk picture
```

That is the handoff to [🥃 Misogyny as a Risk Signal](./🥃_misogyny_as_a_risk_signal.md).

## 📣 The Warning Sign May Already Have Been Reported

Before concluding that information was unavailable, ask:

- Was it disclosed?
- Was it recorded?
- Where?
- Under what category?
- Was the record retrievable?
- Was it linked to the relevant person?
- Did subsequent decision-makers see it?
- Could they lawfully and practically access it?
- Was there a referral route?
- Did anybody own the combined picture?
- If action was taken, what happened to the information afterwards?

“Not known” can conceal several different states:

```text
never observed

observed but never reported

reported but not recorded

recorded but not retrievable

retrievable but not shared

shared but not assembled

assembled but misread

understood but not acted upon
```

Those states should not be treated as equivalent.

This leads directly to [🗺️ Information Has to Travel](./🗺️_information_has_to_travel.md):

> **Who knew what, and was there a route by which the person capable of acting could know it too?**

## 🏫 Sometimes the Observatory Is Ordinary

Recognising warning behaviour does not automatically require a new national surveillance apparatus.

Useful observations may already arise through ordinary institutions and relationships:

- schools;
- colleges;
- universities;
- workplaces;
- health services;
- safeguarding teams;
- police;
- specialist VAWG services;
- families;
- friendship groups;
- online communities.

Educational institutions are particularly important because young people spend substantial time there and changes in behaviour may become visible through:

- peers;
- staff;
- disciplinary processes;
- safeguarding concerns;
- disclosures;
- online conduct affecting the educational environment.

This is why [🍎 Schools, Colleges and Universities](./🍎_schools_colleges_and_universities.md) treats education as more than somewhere to deliver an **“Andrew Tate is bad” assembly**.

The policy question is not automatically:

> `What giant new surveillance system do we need?`

Sometimes it is:

> **Could institutions already encountering the behaviour become better at recognising, recording, referring and responding to it?**

## 🚫 LOOK UP Is Not Minority Report

This cluster is not an argument for:

- predictive policing;
- ideological profiling;
- mass monitoring;
- criminalising unpopular beliefs;
- treating misogyny as proof of dangerousness;
- treating boys expressing grievance as latent terrorists;
- indiscriminate institutional information-sharing;
- punishment before an offence;
- retrospective claims that everybody “should have known”.

The proposition is much more ordinary:

> **Observe behaviour that is actually occurring.**  
> **Record what matters.**  
> **Hear people reporting harm.**  
> **Notice meaningful change.**  
> **Join information where there is a lawful and proportionate reason to join it.**  
> **Intervene at the level the evidence justifies.**

That is prevention without prophecy.

## 🌱 Before the Final Offence

The criminal-justice system is necessarily organised around legal thresholds.

Safeguarding has a different temporal problem.

It often needs to become interested while the available information still looks like:

```text
concerning behaviour
        ↓
disclosure / reporting
        ↓
recording
        ↓
assessment
        ↓
safeguarding / referral
        ↓
intervention
        ↓
investigation / prosecution
where appropriate
```

That does not mean inventing an offence before an offence exists.

It means recognising that there are legitimate forms of intervention other than:

```text
nothing
        or
criminal prosecution
```

See [🌱 Before the Offence](./🌱_before_the_offence.md).

## 🩸 The People Experiencing It Are Part of the Information Environment

The person experiencing repeated harm may be the first person capable of describing its shape.

That does not mean institutions can outsource pattern recognition to survivors.

A reporting system that repeatedly requires somebody to:

- retell;
- reconstruct;
- cross-reference;
- explain significance;
- identify escalation;
- prove institutional history;
- locate earlier records

is effectively asking the person experiencing the harm to operate as an unpaid information-integration layer.

Worse, if each new recipient then treats the disclosure as a fresh anecdote, the system receives the labour while discarding the intelligence.

Survivor support therefore has an information function as well as a care function.

If reporting is inaccessible, unsafe or apparently futile, institutions lose information they may need for prevention.

That is why this cluster ends with [🩸 Survivor Support and Reporting](./🩸_survivor_support_and_reporting.md).

## 🔮 The Core Diagnostic

When reviewing a serious outcome, do not begin with:

> `Were there warning signs?`

Break the question apart.

### What was observable?

What behaviour, reports, disclosures or changes actually existed before the outcome?

### Who observed it?

Victims? Peers? Teachers? Employers? Platforms? Police? Health professionals? Several of them?

### What was recorded?

And in what category?

### What could be connected?

Did lawful, proportionate routes exist for relevant information to travel or be assembled?

### What did the information mean at the time?

Not with hindsight.

At the time.

### What response was justified then?

Not necessarily prediction.

Not necessarily prosecution.

Perhaps safeguarding, support, referral, assessment, boundary-setting, investigation or another proportionate intervention.

This converts:

> `How could anybody have known?`

into a set of questions institutions can actually answer.

## 🛸 Core Finding

Sometimes the central prevention failure is genuinely that nobody could have known.

But sometimes:

```text
somebody knew A
+
somebody knew B
+
somebody knew C
+
somebody knew D
```

and the system never produced:

```text
A + B + C + D
```

The point is not to manufacture inevitability after the event.

It is to distinguish **unpredictable outcomes** from **recoverable information failures**.

The strongest prevention analysis therefore asks:

> **What was visible beforehand?**

> **Who held it?**

> **What did it reasonably mean then?**

> **Could it travel?**

> **Could it be assembled?**

> **What proportionate action was available?**

And when the answers reveal that the same escalating pattern was already scattered across victims, institutions, categories and time, the final question becomes rather less subtle:

> **Why did nobody have responsibility for looking up?**
