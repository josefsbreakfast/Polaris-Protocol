# 🌱 Before the Offence
**First created:** 2026-09-06 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-06  
*Safeguarding starts earlier than sentencing. If prevention is supposed to happen before prosecution, somebody has to be supported to do the work in the middle.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The previous node asks what happens when warning behaviour is already visible.

This node asks what can legitimately happen **before the most serious offence occurs**.

The basic chain is:

```text
behaviour
        ↓
disclosure / reporting
        ↓
recording
        ↓
assessment
        ↓
safeguarding / referral
        ↓
intervention
        ↓
investigation / prosecution
where appropriate
```

The criminal-justice system necessarily becomes clearest once conduct satisfies an offence.

Prevention cannot depend exclusively on reaching that point.

There is a large and important territory between:

```text
nothing concerning is happening
```

and:

```text
we now have an obvious serious offence to prosecute
```

Safeguarding already occupies much of that territory. The original `LOOK_UP` brief therefore places it upstream of sentencing and prosecution, particularly around stalking, coercive control, harassment, escalating threats and other behavioural precursors. fileciteturn29file1

> **Before the offence does not mean before there is behaviour worth responding to.**

It means before the final or most serious outcome that prevention is trying to avoid.

---

## 🚫 This Is Not Pre-Crime

“Early intervention” can become dangerous language if its object is left vague.

This node is not proposing:

- predicting offences from thoughts;
- punishment for lawful beliefs;
- ideological profiling;
- treating misogyny as proof of future violence;
- mass monitoring;
- criminalising boys or men for grievance;
- bypassing ordinary legal thresholds.

The relevant object is **behaviour and circumstances that already justify some proportionate form of response**.

That response does not have to be prosecution.

A stalking concern can justify assessment because stalking is concerning now.

A safeguarding disclosure can justify support because somebody needs support now.

Escalating threats can justify attention because threats are occurring now.

Repeated sexual boundary violations can justify institutional action because boundaries are already being violated.

> **Prevention does not require proving the future.**

---

## 🛟 Safeguarding Is the Missing Middle

Policy conversations can accidentally produce a binary:

```text
DO NOTHING
        or
PROSECUTE
```

Real prevention needs more options.

Depending on the behaviour, setting, powers available and needs of the people involved, the middle can include:

- listening and documenting;
- safety planning;
- survivor support;
- behavioural boundaries;
- educational intervention;
- disciplinary processes;
- specialist referral;
- stalking or domestic-abuse assessment;
- appropriate health or therapeutic support;
- risk management;
- lawful and necessary information-sharing;
- police action where an existing offence or protective power applies;
- further investigation where evidence warrants it.

Not every intervention is punitive.

Not every intervention is statutory.

Not every concern needs to become a criminal case.

But **nothing** cannot be the only alternative to criminal prosecution.

## 🏡 Safeguarding Is Formal and Informal

Prevention rarely begins with a professional.

People already live inside information and care networks:

```text
family
friends
siblings
partners
housemates
classmates
colleagues
faith / community networks
sports clubs
online friends
        ↓
sometimes
        ↓
formal safeguarding / specialist services
```

These informal networks may notice:

- sudden fixation;
- escalating hostility;
- deterioration in relationships;
- repeated boundary crossing;
- changes in routine;
- violent or degrading talk becoming more intense;
- obsessive attention towards a particular person;
- fear expressed by somebody close to them;
- several apparently separate incidents that are actually connected.

They may also provide early forms of response:

- checking in;
- challenging behaviour;
- supporting somebody experiencing harm;
- helping somebody access appropriate care;
- encouraging disclosure;
- helping retain relevant information;
- seeking specialist advice;
- supporting a formal referral where one is warranted.

This does **not** turn families, friends or communities into amateur threat-assessment units.

It recognises something much more ordinary:

> **Human beings already live inside safeguarding ecologies.**

---

## 🧯 Informal Networks Are Not Free Public Services

Informal systems have serious limits.

Families and communities can:

- minimise behaviour;
- protect a relative;
- misunderstand risk;
- become frightened;
- lack specialist knowledge;
- disagree about what is happening;
- be part of the abusive environment themselves;
- expose a victim to further danger;
- simply have nowhere useful to refer somebody.

So informal networks are not a substitute for properly functioning services.

They can notice.

They can support.

They can sometimes interrupt.

But when the problem exceeds what ordinary relationships can safely hold, there needs to be somewhere for it to go.

That gives us a deceptively simple prevention question:

> **Where do we take this before somebody has committed the offence everybody is waiting for?**

---

## 🚑 Look at What We Are Asking Safeguarding to Do

Once the rest of `LOOK_UP` is assembled, the workload hidden inside the word **safeguarding** becomes striking.

We are effectively asking systems to:

- receive information safely;
- recognise relevant behaviour;
- distinguish concerning conduct from unpopular beliefs;
- identify escalation;
- remember previous incidents;
- connect repeated reports;
- assess different categories of behaviour together;
- recognise vulnerability;
- support people experiencing harm;
- understand referral pathways;
- share information lawfully;
- avoid both underreaction and overreaction;
- document decisions;
- coordinate across institutions;
- understand relevant online behaviour;
- use misogyny or grievance as context without turning either into a predictive shortcut;
- preserve civil liberties;
- recognise when another agency needs to become involved.

That is not a small administrative function.

If prevention policy expects more sophisticated early recognition, the systems expected to provide it need corresponding support.

## 🫗 A Missed Pattern Does Not Have One Explanation

When safeguarding fails to catch something, it is tempting to conclude:

```text
BAD SYSTEM
```

Sometimes that is correct.

But other possibilities include:

```text
GOOD SYSTEM
+
IMPOSSIBLE CASELOAD
```

or:

```text
GOOD PEOPLE
+
BAD INFORMATION
```

or:

```text
GOOD LOCAL PROCESS
+
NO CROSS-INSTITUTIONAL PLUMBING
```

or:

```text
GOOD SAFEGUARDING MODEL
+
INSUFFICIENT SPECIALIST KNOWLEDGE
```

or:

```text
EVERYBODY FOLLOWED
THEIR INDIVIDUAL PROCESS
+
NOBODY OWNED THE PATTERN
```

These are not interchangeable diagnoses.

They imply different remedies:

- staffing;
- training;
- better recording;
- better referral routes;
- specialist support;
- clearer ownership;
- information-sharing architecture;
- workload reduction;
- stronger institutional accountability.

A prevention strategy should therefore ask not only:

> `Why didn't safeguarding catch it?`

but:

> **What prevented safeguarding from performing the function we expected it to perform?**

---

## 📈 Prevention Policy Creates Work

“Prevention and early intervention” sounds upstream and economical.

Operationally, it creates tasks.

If policy expands what institutions are expected to notice, somebody needs:

- time;
- staff;
- training;
- supervision;
- specialist advice;
- usable recording systems;
- referral capacity;
- information-sharing protocols;
- decision-making authority;
- somewhere to send the referral.

Otherwise:

```text
NEW THING WE WANT
PEOPLE TO NOTICE
        ↓
same staff
+
same caseload
+
same systems
+
same referral bottlenecks
        ↓
“Why wasn't this spotted?”
```

That is not a prevention strategy.

It is an **unfunded detection expectation**.

---

## 🪣 Recognition Is Useless Without Somewhere to Refer

Even excellent recognition can fail at:

```text
recognise
→ record
→ assess
→ refer
→ ?
```

The question mark matters.

Ask:

- Is there a service?
- Does it accept this type of referral?
- What is its threshold?
- Is there a waiting list?
- Is specialist VAWG support available?
- Can an education safeguarding team do anything beyond documenting the concern?
- Is policing appropriate at this stage?
- Is there a useful route for somebody displaying escalating but non-criminal behaviour?
- What happens when the referral is declined?
- Who follows up?
- Who knows whether the next service actually received the information?

And especially:

> **Who owns the risk between services?**

A referral is not the same thing as an intervention.

A referral sent into a saturated system may simply move the administrative location of the problem.

---

## 🏚️ Risk Can Flow Backwards

When formal capacity runs out, responsibility does not disappear.

It often moves backwards through the ecology:

```text
specialist service full
        ↓
school / university holds it

institution cannot resolve it
        ↓
family / friends hold it

informal network cannot manage it
        ↓
person experiencing the harm holds it
```

This creates **risk reservoirs in informal networks**.

The people closest to the situation may know that something is wrong while lacking:

- authority;
- expertise;
- protection;
- resources;
- referral access;
- any safe way to change the situation.

An overloaded formal system can therefore externalise both safeguarding work and risk onto households, friendship groups and survivors.

---

## 🍎 Education Makes the Capacity Problem Obvious

The `LOOK_UP` cluster asks schools, colleges and universities to take seriously the fact that they are already environments where:

- behavioural changes become visible;
- peers notice escalation;
- victims disclose;
- online conduct enters offline relationships;
- safeguarding concerns emerge.

That is a real opportunity.

But every new expectation generates operational questions:

> **Notice it with what training?**

> **Record it where?**

> **Assess it using what framework?**

> **Refer it to whom?**

> **With what staff?**

> **What happens when the referral is declined?**

Otherwise:

```text
“schools should notice misogynistic radicalisation”
        ↓
another national ambition
        ↓
an exhausted safeguarding lead's inbox
at 4:47pm
```

See [🍎 Schools, Colleges and Universities](./🍎_schools_colleges_and_universities.md).

## 🧠 Overloaded Systems Can Catch the Wrong Thing

More detection does not automatically mean better detection.

An overloaded system operating with vague concepts may over-triage what is:

- loud;
- novel;
- easy to categorise;
- reputationally frightening;
- immediately visible.

Meanwhile quieter but behaviourally significant patterns may remain fragmented.

The teenager loudly repeating obnoxiously misogynistic internet material may be easier to spot than somebody engaging in:

- persistent stalking;
- coercive behaviour;
- targeted fixation;
- repeated sexual boundary violations;
- escalating intimidation.

That creates a salience problem.

So `🥃_misogyny_as_a_risk_signal.md` needs to retain an important discipline:

> **Misogyny may contribute information. It cannot become a shortcut that replaces behavioural assessment.**

## 🗺️ Informal Knowledge Needs a Route Into Formal Systems

A family member knowing something is not the same as a safeguarding system knowing it.

A friend seeing escalation is not the same as somebody with relevant authority receiving a usable report.

A victim telling several people is not necessarily the same as information reaching the institution capable of acting.

The informal/formal boundary therefore creates its own information-plumbing questions:

- Where can somebody seek advice without immediately demanding criminal enforcement?
- Can information be recorded without forcing an inappropriate pathway?
- When should a concern be escalated?
- What consent or confidentiality rules apply?
- Who can join information?
- How does the person reporting know what happened next?
- Is there a route back if the situation changes?

That is the handoff to [🗺️ Information Has to Travel](./🗺️_information_has_to_travel.md), whose governing question is:

> **Who knew what, and was there a route by which the person capable of acting could know it too?** fileciteturn29file10

## 🔮 Safeguarding Also Needs Memory

The previous node establishes that a system can possess many fragments without assembling them.

Safeguarding therefore cannot be understood only as responding well to today's incident.

It also needs some capacity to ask:

> **Did today's information change what we already knew?**

Repeated low-level reports, multiple victims, different categories of misconduct and fragments held by separate institutions may each look insufficient alone while becoming significant together. That is the specific problem reserved for [🔮 Pattern Recognition, Not Incident Processing](./🔮_pattern_recognition_not_incident_processing.md). fileciteturn29file6

## 🩸 When Capacity Fails, Survivors Become the Database

Institutional overload often produces compensatory labour.

People experiencing harm may end up:

- maintaining timelines;
- retaining screenshots;
- finding old reference numbers;
- repeating disclosures;
- explaining previous incidents;
- identifying escalation;
- chasing referrals;
- carrying records between organisations;
- explaining why several individually “minor” events belong together.

In effect:

```text
institutional memory fails
        ↓
survivor remembers

systems do not connect
        ↓
survivor cross-references

referrals disappear
        ↓
survivor chases

new recipient starts again
        ↓
survivor reconstructs
```

That is not merely exhausting.

It means the system has externalised information-management work onto the person already experiencing the harm.

The original `LOOK_UP` brief therefore treats inaccessible, unsafe or futile reporting not only as a support failure but as an information failure: institutions lose knowledge necessary for prevention and pattern recognition. fileciteturn29file0

See [🩸 Survivor Support and Reporting](./🩸_survivor_support_and_reporting.md).

## 🧪 Questions for Any “Early Intervention” Proposal

Before announcing a new prevention duty, ask:

### Signal

- What behaviour or circumstance is the system being asked to notice?
- Is it defined clearly enough to avoid ideological profiling?
- What evidence makes it relevant?

### Observer

- Who is realistically likely to see it first?
- Family?
- Friends?
- School?
- University?
- Employer?
- Specialist service?
- Police?
- Platform?

### Response

- What proportionate intervention is actually available at this stage?
- Does it require consent?
- Does it require statutory authority?
- Is it supportive, disciplinary, protective, investigative or criminal?

### Capacity

- Who performs the work?
- How many cases are they already carrying?
- What training and supervision do they receive?
- Is specialist advice available?

### Referral

- Where does the concern go?
- What is the receiving service's threshold?
- What happens if it is declined?
- Who owns the risk while everybody waits?

### Information

- What gets recorded?
- What can lawfully travel?
- Can later information change an earlier assessment?
- Can patterns across incidents or victims become visible?

### Accountability

- Who knows whether the intervention happened?
- Who reviews failures?
- Is success measured by referrals made, or by whether useful help actually arrived?

That is what **early intervention** looks like when translated from aspiration into infrastructure.

## 🌱 Core Finding

The `LOOK_UP` cluster suggests a possibility worth taking seriously:

> **Some safeguarding systems may be overwhelmed, under-supported, poorly connected, looking for the wrong signals, or being asked to perform functions they were never resourced to perform.**

Those possibilities should be tested rather than assumed.

But they matter because prevention policy routinely assigns safeguarding more work.

If Government, institutions and communities want intervention to happen before the most serious offence, safeguarding cannot remain the vague box in the middle of the diagram.

We need to know:

```text
who notices
        ↓
who listens
        ↓
who records
        ↓
who assesses
        ↓
who supports
        ↓
who can intervene
        ↓
where information travels
        ↓
where referrals land
        ↓
who owns the risk
```

And informal networks belong in that map too.

Families, friends, peers and communities may be the first people to notice and the first people to help.

They should not become the last functioning service.

> **Otherwise every new prevention strategy quietly becomes another instruction to an already overloaded ecology to please notice more things.**
