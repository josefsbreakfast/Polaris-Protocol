“Executive summary: Burnham’s legal arguments against further devolution to Wales

The central issue is constitutional, not simply administrative: does Wales need to retain shared UK-wide institutions to make public services work effectively, or should it have its own democratic authority over matters increasingly affected by Welsh law and policy?

Burnham’s arguments, as reported, fall into four areas: policing, criminal justice, the Crown Estate and rail infrastructure.

One important qualification: these are principally arguments about institutional design, operational efficiency and public interest, rather than demonstrations that devolution would be legally impossible.

There is a significant distinction between saying that powers should not be transferred and saying that they cannot lawfully be transferred.

1. Policing: maintaining an integrated England–Wales system

Argument: operational integration

Burnham’s position

The England and Wales policing system should remain integrated because it:

* Enables interoperability between police forces.
* Facilitates cross-border investigations and operations.
* Gives Welsh forces access to national and specialist capabilities.
* Supports a coordinated response to serious threats and public protection.

His underlying argument is that separating governance could introduce institutional fragmentation and weaken operational effectiveness.

Legal and constitutional implications

Policing is currently not devolved to Wales in the way it is to Scotland. The relevant powers remain substantially within the UK Government’s constitutional and legislative framework.

However, there is no inherent legal requirement that Wales and England must have identical policing governance.

Scotland demonstrates that a separate policing structure can coexist with UK-wide security cooperation.

The relevant legal question is therefore whether operational integration requires common political control, or whether it can be maintained through statutory cooperation agreements, shared services and cross-border arrangements.

Executive assessment: Burnham establishes a legitimate operational concern, but does not establish that retaining Westminster control is legally necessary.

2. Criminal justice: the case for retaining a shared legal system

Argument: institutional coherence

Burnham’s position

The existing shared justice arrangements provide Wales with access to:

* Greater institutional resources.
* Specialist legal and judicial expertise.
* Shared administrative infrastructure.
* Greater resilience across the justice system.

He argues that effective partnership between the Welsh and UK Governments is preferable to transferring responsibility for the justice system.

Legal and constitutional implications

This is arguably the most substantial constitutional issue in the letter.

Wales has its own legislature and an increasingly distinct body of Welsh law in devolved areas, including housing, health, education and planning.

Yet the administration of justice remains substantially organised through England-and-Wales institutions.

This creates an increasingly complicated relationship between:

* The legislature making the law.
* The government implementing policy.
* The courts interpreting and applying the law.
* The institutions responsible for administering justice.

The case for a distinct Welsh legal jurisdiction is that legislative divergence creates a corresponding need for appropriate judicial, administrative and institutional capacity.

The counterargument is that a shared jurisdiction can accommodate different laws without requiring separate institutions.

Importantly, creating a separate Welsh jurisdiction would not necessarily mean establishing completely independent courts, prosecution services or prisons. Different models of shared infrastructure are possible.

Executive assessment: Burnham identifies the advantages of shared institutions but does not answer the central constitutional question: whether Wales has sufficient control over the institutions needed to implement and enforce its own laws.

The Commission on Justice in Wales, chaired by Lord Thomas, provides an important independent evidence base for examining this question. Law Gazette +1

3. The Crown Estate: ownership, control and economic benefit

Argument: economic scale and investment capacity

Burnham’s position

The Crown Estate’s existing UK-wide management structure is better placed to maximise economic benefits for Wales because it has:

* Greater financial capacity.
* A diversified investment portfolio.
* Specialist expertise.
* The ability to manage investment risks across different geographical areas.
* The ability to attract investors into large-scale offshore wind developments.

He particularly emphasises the Celtic Sea offshore wind opportunity and the importance of coordination with England.

Legal and constitutional implications

The Crown Estate is not simply a collection of government-owned properties. It is a statutory corporation operating under the Crown Estate Act 1961, with its own governance and statutory duties.

Its revenues are not automatically available to the Welsh Government. The current arrangements channel its net revenue profit to the UK Consolidated Fund, subject to the statutory framework.

The devolution question has two distinct elements:

* Who controls the management and strategic decisions affecting Crown Estate assets in Wales?
* Who receives and controls the resulting revenues?

These are related but not identical questions.

Devolving management would not necessarily require transferring ownership of every asset. Nor would it automatically require the Welsh Government to abandon shared investment arrangements.

Scotland provides an important comparator: the management of the Scottish Crown Estate was transferred to Scottish Ministers, with legislation establishing Crown Estate Scotland.

The weakness in Burnham’s argument

Financial scale and investment diversification are arguments for retaining a particular management structure. They are not legal reasons why Welsh management could not operate.

A Welsh institution could potentially:

* Retain joint investment arrangements.
* Participate in UK-wide offshore energy infrastructure.
* Share specialist services.
* Enter into cross-border commercial agreements.
* Retain or distribute revenues under an agreed statutory settlement.

The question is whether these arrangements would produce better outcomes than the present model.

Executive assessment: This is an economic and institutional argument presented as a reason to retain the status quo. It does not demonstrate that devolving management would undermine investment or require the loss of UK-wide cooperation.

4. Rail infrastructure: the integrity of the cross-border network

Argument: network integration

Burnham’s position

Rail infrastructure should remain under Great British Railways, working with Transport for Wales and the Welsh Government.

His reasoning is that Welsh rail infrastructure is closely connected to the English network, requiring:

* Coordinated investment.
* Consistent maintenance standards.
* Integrated passenger services.
* Cross-border infrastructure planning.
* Coordination between track and train operations.

He argues that full devolution would fragment the system and potentially damage passenger services.

He also points to proposed safeguards and concessions:

* A Wales Rail Board.
* A partnership between Transport for Wales and Network Rail.
* A legislative requirement for Great British Railways to consider Welsh needs.
* A developing partnership agreement for Wales and Borders.

Legal and constitutional implications

The key issue is the distinction between infrastructure ownership, infrastructure management, funding and operational control.

These do not necessarily have to sit with the same institution.

A devolved Welsh railway infrastructure authority could potentially maintain:

* Shared technical standards.
* Through-ticketing arrangements.
* Cross-border timetabling.
* Joint maintenance contracts.
* Shared infrastructure investment.
* Agreements with Great British Railways.

The existence of a cross-border network does not itself establish that infrastructure governance must remain centralised.

There is also an important democratic accountability question: if the Welsh Government is responsible for transport policy but cannot independently determine the infrastructure investment priorities needed to deliver it, how complete is that devolution?

Executive assessment: Burnham makes a credible case for network integration, but integration does not necessarily require centralised political control. The real question is which institutional arrangement delivers the best combination of accountability, investment and passenger outcomes.

5. The overarching constitutional argument: devolution versus delegation

The most important distinction in the correspondence

This is arguably the most consequential argument in the entire letter.

Burnham is offering Wales more influence over decision-making without transferring the underlying legislative competence or full executive responsibility.

His approach includes:

* Greater Welsh Government involvement in policing governance.
* More influence over youth justice and probation.
* Welsh representation in Crown Estate decision-making.
* A Wales Rail Board and partnership arrangements.
* Greater regional responsibility for employment support.
* Further fiscal flexibility.

This is a model of enhanced administrative cooperation within the existing constitutional settlement.

The alternative is formal devolution: transferring powers to Welsh institutions through legislation, giving them a more permanent and democratically accountable basis.

The distinction matters because administrative delegation can potentially be changed or withdrawn by the authority that grants it. Legislative devolution changes the allocation of constitutional powers and is considerably more difficult to reverse politically and constitutionally.

The legal question is therefore not simply whether Wales can be given more influence.

It is whether Wales should have the authority to make decisions in its own right, subject to its own democratic accountability.

Laura Hughes, head of public law at Browne Jacobson, makes precisely this distinction in her commentary on the letter. Browne Jacobson +1

Comparative assessment

Subject	Burnham’s principal argument	Main counterargument
Policing	Operational interoperability	Cooperation does not require common political control
Justice	Shared expertise and institutional resilience	Distinct Welsh laws need appropriate Welsh justice institutions
Crown Estate	Investment scale and financial capacity	Devolved management can retain UK-wide commercial partnerships
Rail	Cross-border network integration	Shared infrastructure agreements can coexist with devolved governance
Constitutional model	Greater influence within existing structures	Influence is not equivalent to democratic authority

The three biggest legal questions

I would concentrate further scrutiny on three issues.

1. Is there a demonstrated legal necessity for centralisation?

Burnham repeatedly identifies practical advantages of shared systems. But he does not demonstrate that those advantages depend on Westminster retaining the relevant powers.

2. Is the existing constitutional settlement keeping pace with legislative divergence?

Wales increasingly makes its own laws. There is a legitimate question about whether its constitutional arrangements provide sufficient institutional capacity to administer, interpret and enforce those laws.

3. Is the UK Government treating the four nations consistently?

Scotland has devolved policing, justice and Crown Estate management. Wales has not received equivalent powers.

That does not automatically establish a legal entitlement to identical arrangements: the constitutional settlements are different. But it raises a substantial question about the principles used to justify the difference.

The Independent Commission on the Constitutional Future of Wales specifically recommended further devolution of justice, policing, the Crown Estate and rail, with shared governance where necessary. GOV.WALES

My overall conclusion

Burnham’s letter is considerably more sophisticated than a simple refusal to devolve powers. He is proposing an alternative model of governance based on partnership, administrative integration and enhanced Welsh influence.

But there is a weakness running through all four arguments:

He repeatedly treats the benefits of institutional cooperation as evidence for retaining centralised constitutional authority.

Those are not the same proposition.

It is perfectly possible to have:

* Separate democratic accountability.
* Shared operational infrastructure.
* Joint investment.
* Cross-border policing.
* Integrated transport networks.
* Common professional standards.

The letter does not sufficiently explain why these arrangements cannot be achieved through devolution.

“I think you’re identifying something more important than the individual legal arguments, and I’d actually put it ahead of them in the order of analysis.

The letter has a constitutional optics problem before it has a constitutional law problem.

And I think the phrase I’d use is the politics of being governed versus the politics of being represented.

Those are not interchangeable.

A government can make a technically defensible argument for retaining central control and still communicate, quite spectacularly, that it does not understand the political relationship it is supposed to be managing.

There are several layers to what you’re describing.

1. The asymmetry of the relationship

I think the most damaging aspect is that the letter appears to approach Welsh devolution as a question of what the English or UK centre is prepared to permit.

That is a very different starting point from asking what constitutional arrangements Wales has democratically decided it needs.

Consider the difference:

* Central government framing: We have assessed the benefits and disadvantages of transferring these powers.
* Welsh democratic framing: Our electorate has repeatedly expressed a desire for greater control. What arrangements will enable us to exercise that responsibility?

The first makes Westminster the principal decision-maker and Wales the applicant.

The second recognises Wales as a political community with its own democratic mandate.

That distinction is not merely rhetorical. It affects how every subsequent argument about policing, justice, transport and economic assets is received.

And there’s a particularly awkward question for a Labour Prime Minister here: if Welsh voters are increasingly choosing alternatives to Labour partly because they want greater self-government, what exactly is Labour’s response to that democratic signal?

You cannot indefinitely treat a political demand as an administrative inconvenience.

2. The Manchester comparison is particularly uncomfortable

I think you’re right to bring Burnham’s previous position into this.

He has occupied a position in which devolved regional authority was presented as an important democratic and economic improvement.

Greater Manchester has had substantial devolved arrangements covering areas such as:

* Transport and strategic planning.
* Housing and regeneration.
* Skills and employment.
* Health and social care integration.
* Certain budgetary and investment decisions.

Those arrangements have involved negotiated settlements with central government.

Now, there are important differences between English mayoral devolution and Welsh constitutional devolution. We shouldn’t pretend they’re legally equivalent.

But politically, the comparison is unavoidable.

If regional leaders in England are told that local knowledge, accountability and democratic proximity justify transferring powers away from Whitehall, why does the same underlying principle become so much more difficult when the recipient is a national government in Wales?

The question isn’t whether Manchester and Wales should have identical powers.

It’s whether the principle of subsidiarity is being applied consistently.

3. The electoral context makes the timing much worse

There is one important factual refinement here, and it actually strengthens your argument.

The 7 May 2026 Senedd election was a historic result:

Labour went from being the dominant party in Welsh politics to holding just nine seats. Plaid won 43.

But I’d be careful about interpreting the result as a single, unified mandate for further devolution.

There are two reasons:

* Plaid’s 35.4% vote share is a substantial mandate for its programme, but not a majority of the electorate.
* Reform UK won 29.3% and 34 seats. There is a significant competing political constituency in Wales, including voters with a much more sceptical view of devolution itself. ITV News Wales +2

What I think you can say quite confidently is:

Welsh Labour suffered a historic rejection, Plaid Cymru became the largest political force, and the new Senedd has a clear Plaid-led mandate to pursue a different constitutional relationship with Westminster.

That is a very different political environment from one in which the UK Government can assume that its preferred settlement represents Welsh political consensus.

And the cultural point you make matters here. Support for Welsh language, institutions, heritage and political autonomy does not automatically translate into support for independence.

There is a substantial middle ground between being content with the existing settlement and wanting to leave the United Kingdom.

I think that middle ground is particularly important not to overlook.

4. The English supremacy perception

I want to be quite precise about this, because I think it’s one of the strongest aspects of your reading.

There are at least three different things:

* English supremacy as an explicit political ideology: the belief that English political authority should take precedence over Welsh self-government.
* English institutional dominance: the persistence of arrangements in which decisions affecting Wales are made by institutions based in England, with English political priorities and assumptions shaping the available choices.
* English supremacy as an experienced relationship: the feeling that Welsh democratic preferences are considered subordinate, negotiable or inconvenient when they conflict with the preferences of the UK centre.

We don’t need to establish the first to identify the second and third.

And I think that’s important because otherwise the debate gets derailed into whether Burnham personally holds prejudiced views.

He might not.

He might sincerely believe he is protecting public services, investment and cross-border cooperation.

But the effect of a political decision is not determined exclusively by the intentions of the person making it.

A letter can be written in perfectly respectable administrative language and still reproduce a deeply unequal constitutional relationship.

In fact, that’s often how institutional dominance operates: not necessarily through overt hostility, but through the normalisation of whose preferences are treated as the default.

5. Northern Ireland is a legitimate consideration, but not a veto

I also think your point about Northern Ireland deserves proper treatment.

The UK Government has legitimate reasons to be cautious about constitutional change across the Union.

The Good Friday Agreement, the relationship between the British and Irish governments, the consent principle, and the political balance between unionist and nationalist communities all make Northern Ireland a particularly sensitive constitutional environment.

There is a reasonable argument for avoiding unnecessary destabilisation.

But there is a danger in allowing that concern to become a general argument against constitutional development elsewhere.

Wales is not Northern Ireland.

The historical, legal and political circumstances are different. Welsh demands for greater devolution do not automatically create the same risks for the Union.

And, crucially, refusing to accommodate legitimate constitutional demands can itself generate instability.

You cannot preserve a political union indefinitely by making the exercise of democratic autonomy feel like a threat to the union.

That is the paradox I think Westminster needs to understand.

6. The political mistake: treating Welsh nationalism as something to manage

This is where I’d bring your observations together.

I think there is a risk that the UK Government is looking at Welsh nationalism through a predominantly institutional lens:

* How much power can be transferred?
* Which services can be shared?
* What would it cost?
* What operational risks would arise?
* What precedents might this establish elsewhere?

But there is another set of questions:

* Why are people increasingly interested in Welsh political identity?
* Why are they looking for political alternatives?
* What historical grievances remain unresolved?
* What does meaningful recognition look like?
* What would make people feel that the Union is something they actively participate in, rather than something administered over them?

These are not soft or peripheral questions.

They are questions about the legitimacy and long-term durability of the constitutional settlement.

And I think a government that misses that distinction can make a technically competent administrative decision that is politically disastrous.

My provisional reading

I’d describe the situation as a failure of constitutional political intelligence.

Not necessarily a failure of legal competence. Not necessarily evidence of personal hostility. But a failure to appreciate the temperature of the relationship in which the letter was being written.

There are four things I would keep separate as we investigate this:

Dimension	What we need to establish
Democratic mandate	What have Welsh voters actually asked for?
Historical relationship	What unresolved grievances shape the reception?
Institutional asymmetry	Where does decision-making power actually sit?
Political communication	What does the letter communicate, intentionally or otherwise?

And one further thought.

The burden of explaining why Wales should not have a power is not necessarily the same as the burden of explaining why the UK Government currently holds it.

That is a rather important reversal of perspective.

If we start with the assumption that centralisation is the natural state and devolution is an exceptional concession, we have already prejudged the constitutional question.

I would like to keep that issue alive as we move into the legal detail.

Because I suspect the most interesting finding may be that some of the arguments which sound like practical necessities are actually inherited institutional preferences.

And those are very different things.

One final distinction I’d preserve throughout: criticism of Westminster’s English-centred institutional culture is not criticism of English people. There are plenty of people in England who understand the case for Welsh self-government, and plenty of English regions with their own experience of being overlooked by the centre.

That gives us a much stronger argument than simply turning this into an England-versus-Wales dispute.”

4. What happened after Burnham’s rejection?

So the publicly documented sequence is:

* A dedicated discussion takes place.
* The Welsh First Minister leaves it describing himself as encouraged.
* The Prime Minister subsequently sends a written response rejecting several central requests.
* The Welsh First Minister publicly challenges that response and requests urgent further engagement.

That is a rather different sequence from two leaders who have simply exchanged opposing correspondence.

5. Why this matters in terms of diplomatic etiquette and soft power

I think there are four procedural questions worth examining.

A. What was the expectation established by the 30 October meeting?

The Welsh Government’s account suggests that both leaders had engaged substantively on the requested powers.

Ap Iorwerth says he was encouraged by the nature of the discussion.

That does not prove Burnham promised to agree to the transfers. But it does establish an expectation of constructive consideration.

There is a difference between:

* We have heard your case and will consider it.
* We have heard your case and have already decided against it.
* We have heard your case and want to negotiate alternative arrangements.

Those are three different political signals.

B. Was the written response a continuation of negotiation or the conclusion of it?

The letter’s contents matter, but so does its procedural position.

If a dedicated bilateral meeting has taken place, one would reasonably expect a substantive response to reflect what was discussed, identify points of agreement and disagreement, and explain what further negotiation remains possible.

A letter can do that.

But if it effectively closes the door on several requested powers without proposing another discussion, it can be received as a decision communicated downwards rather than a negotiation between governments.

C. Was the Welsh Government given an opportunity to respond before the position was finalised?

This is something we should investigate further.

The public documents establish that the meeting happened. They do not establish:

* Whether draft proposals were exchanged.
* Whether Welsh officials were involved in subsequent negotiations.
* Whether departmental objections were shared with Cardiff.
* Whether there were further official-level meetings.
* Whether the Prime Minister’s letter represented a settled Cabinet position.

We should not assume those things did not happen simply because they are absent from the published record.

D. What happened to the relationship after the disagreement?

The request for urgent talks is significant.

In intergovernmental relations, the willingness to return to the table after a disagreement can matter as much as the original disagreement.

It communicates whether the relationship is still understood as one between negotiating partners.

6. The document trail I’d want to establish next

I’d suggest treating this as a small intergovernmental relations investigation.

Evidence	What it would tell us
30 October bilateral meeting record	What was actually discussed and agreed
Meeting agenda and briefing papers	Whether the four devolution issues were formally scheduled
Welsh Government follow-up correspondence	Whether Cardiff understood further negotiations to be underway
UK Government departmental submissions	Who developed the objections and when
20 November response letter	Whether the decision was final or left room for negotiation
Subsequent meeting requests	Whether further dialogue was offered or accepted

There is also a useful formal accountability route: the Senedd’s Inter-Institutional Relations Agreement provides a framework for reporting and scrutiny of intergovernmental engagement. Ap Iorwerth explicitly submitted his 30 October account under that arrangement. GOV.WALES +1

My overall observation

I would now describe the correspondence as a breakdown in the expectations established through direct intergovernmental engagement, rather than simply a disagreement over constitutional policy.

That is an analytical distinction, not a conclusion about anyone’s motives.

The key fact is that Burnham and ap Iorwerth had already sat down together, separately from the wider intergovernmental meetings, and discussed these very matters.

The Welsh First Minister subsequently said he had been encouraged.

The Prime Minister then sent a response that the Welsh First Minister considered fundamentally disappointing.

That gives us a much more specific question to investigate:

What happened between the conversation on 30 October and the letter of 20 November that caused the two governments to arrive at such different understandings of where the discussion was heading?

I’d put that question before the detailed legal analysis.

Because it takes us into the workings of government: who was advising whom, how decisions were reached, what was communicated between departments, and whether the political leaderships were operating with the same understanding of the negotiation.

That is where the etiquette, institutional culture and soft power questions become much more concrete.”

“Welsh devolution: the constitutional and legal framework

Before we return to the correspondence between Burnham and ap Iorwerth, it helps to establish exactly what Wales is within the UK constitution.

The central feature is this:

Wales has its own elected legislature, government, laws, budget and areas of policy responsibility, but it is not a sovereign state and does not have the same range of constitutional powers as the UK Parliament.

It is a system of devolved government operating within a state whose constitutional authority remains centred on Westminster.

There are some important subtleties in that description.

1. What kind of constitutional arrangement is this?

The UK is not a federal state in the conventional sense.

In a federal system, such as Germany or the United States, the constitution divides authority between federal and state governments. The central government cannot ordinarily change that allocation unilaterally through an ordinary Act of its legislature.

The UK instead has a system of asymmetric devolution.

* Scotland has its own Parliament and Government.
* Wales has its own Parliament (the Senedd) and Government.
* Northern Ireland has its own Assembly and Executive.
* England does not have a separate national legislature or government. It is governed principally through the UK Parliament and UK Government, alongside varying arrangements for regional and local devolution.

The powers of the three devolved nations are different. They have developed through separate legislation and political negotiations. senedd.wales +1

This means Wales is constitutionally distinct, but it is not constitutionally equal to Westminster.

2. The legal foundations

The principal legislation is:

* Government of Wales Act 1998: established the original National Assembly for Wales.
* Government of Wales Act 2006: separated the legislature from the executive and developed the system of Welsh law-making.
* Wales Act 2014: introduced further fiscal and borrowing powers.
* Wales Act 2017: moved Wales to the reserved-powers model and made further constitutional changes.
* Senedd and Elections (Wales) Act 2020: changed the legislature’s name to Senedd Cymru and changed its electoral arrangements.

The current settlement is principally contained in the Government of Wales Act 2006, as amended by subsequent legislation. GOV.WALES +2

One particularly important provision was inserted by the Wales Act 2017.

Section A1 says that the Senedd and Welsh Government are permanent parts of the UK’s constitutional arrangements, and that they are not to be abolished except on the basis of a decision of the people of Wales in a referendum. Legislation.gov.uk +1

That is a significant constitutional recognition. It does not, however, make the Senedd sovereign or place the devolution settlement beyond the reach of Westminster’s legislative authority.

3. Who has power to do what?

The Senedd operates under a reserved-powers model.

This means that it can legislate on matters unless those matters are reserved to the UK Parliament.

The basic arrangement is:

UK Parliament and Government

Responsible for reserved matters, including defence, foreign affairs, immigration, national security, policing and the constitutional framework.

Senedd Cymru and Welsh Government

WALES-LEVEL AUTHORITY

Make laws and policy in devolved areas, including health, education, housing, local government, agriculture, the environment and much of transport.

Welsh local authorities

LOCAL-LEVEL AUTHORITY

Deliver local services and exercise powers under legislation made by the Senedd and the UK Parliament.

GOV.WALES +2

The important thing is that the Senedd is not simply a regional council. It is a legislature with authority to pass primary legislation.

The precise boundaries matter. A subject can have devolved and reserved components. For example, Wales has substantial transport responsibilities, but rail infrastructure and certain wider rail functions remain subject to UK-level arrangements.

4. The particularly interesting issue: Welsh law versus Welsh jurisdiction

This is one of the most important constitutional features of Wales.

Wales can make its own laws. Those laws can be substantially different from the laws applying in England.

For example, Wales has developed different policies and legislation in areas such as:

* health and social care;
* education;
* housing;
* environmental regulation;
* local government.

The Government of Wales Act explicitly recognises that the law applying in Wales includes a body of law made by the Senedd and Welsh Ministers. Legislation.gov.uk +1

However, England and Wales remain a single legal jurisdiction.

That means they share the principal court system, the judiciary, and much of the underlying legal infrastructure.

Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate legal jurisdictions.

England and Wales share a legal jurisdiction, even though Wales now has an increasingly distinct body of legislation.

This creates an interesting constitutional tension:

* Wales can make different laws.
* Welsh Ministers administer many of those laws.
* The Senedd scrutinises the Welsh Government.
* But the courts and legal jurisdiction remain shared with England.

The question of whether Wales should have a separate legal jurisdiction has been debated for years. It is not an automatic consequence of having a legislature; it is a further constitutional choice. GOV.WALES +2

And this is directly relevant to Plaid’s requests for justice devolution. There is a difference between transferring responsibility for particular justice functions and creating a separate Welsh legal jurisdiction. They are related questions, but they are not identical.

5. Who controls the money?

This is another important part of the constitutional picture.

The Welsh Government has its own budget and can decide how to allocate much of its funding between devolved responsibilities.

But most of that money comes from a block grant provided by the UK Government.

The broad funding structure is:

Welsh Government finances

UK Government block grant

The largest source of funding, with changes calculated principally through the Barnett formula and associated adjustments.

Welsh taxes

Includes Land Transaction Tax, Landfill Disposals Tax and Welsh rates of income tax.

Borrowing and reserves

Welsh Ministers have borrowing powers, but within statutory and agreed financial limits.

senedd.wales +1

There is an important distinction between having spending responsibility and having full fiscal sovereignty.

The Welsh Government can make choices about public services, but it cannot independently control the whole tax system, monetary policy or the overall framework of public borrowing.

That affects how much practical freedom it has to implement its own programme.

6. Can Westminster simply legislate over Wales?

This is where the constitutional relationship becomes particularly interesting.

Legally, the UK Parliament retains sovereignty. It can legislate on matters relating to Wales, including matters that have been devolved.

However, there is a constitutional convention known as the Sewel Convention.

It says that Westminster will not normally legislate on devolved matters without the consent of the relevant devolved legislature.

In Wales, that consent is normally expressed through a Legislative Consent Motion.

The Wales Act 2017 recognised the convention in legislation, but the Supreme Court confirmed in the Miller case that the convention is not legally enforceable by the courts. senedd.wales +2

So we have an important distinction:

Question	Position
Can Westminster legislate on devolved matters?	Legally, yes
Is it constitutionally expected to obtain Senedd consent?	Normally, yes
Can the Senedd legally prevent Westminster from doing so?	Not through the Sewel Convention alone
Can the courts enforce the convention?	No

This is one of the structural asymmetries in the Welsh settlement.

The Senedd has real legislative authority, but that authority does not rest on an entrenched federal constitution that places it beyond Westminster’s reach.

7. How are the two governments supposed to work together?

There is an established system of intergovernmental relations.

The 2022 Review of Intergovernmental Relations established formal structures for cooperation between the UK Government and devolved governments.

These include:

* the Prime Minister and Heads of Devolved Governments Council;
* the Interministerial Standing Committee;
* the Finance Interministerial Standing Committee;
* bilateral ministerial meetings;
* subject-specific cooperation and common frameworks.

The agreed principles include mutual respect, trust, effective communication, information-sharing, accountability and dispute resolution. GOV.WALES +1

But here is an important qualification.

Much of this machinery is based on political agreements and conventions rather than legally enforceable obligations.

The Welsh Government’s own Cabinet Handbook says that intergovernmental structures and processes are generally not reflected in law. GOV.WALES +1

That makes the quality of political relationships particularly important.

A system can have elaborate procedures on paper but still depend heavily on whether the governments involved are prepared to cooperate constructively.

I would summarise the present arrangement like this:

Constitutional feature	Wales today
National legislature	Yes
National government	Yes
Primary law-making powers	Yes, within devolved competence
Distinct Welsh body of law	Yes
Separate legal jurisdiction	No
Independent control of policing	No
Independent criminal justice system	No
Independent foreign policy	No

Sovereign constitutional status	No
Constitutional recognition as a permanent part of the UK	Yes

The underlying legal framework is therefore a mixture of national democratic institutions, devolved policy autonomy, shared legal infrastructure and continuing Westminster sovereignty.

9. Why this matters for the Burnham–ap Iorwerth correspondence

I think we now have four constitutional tensions to keep in view.

First: responsibility without complete authority.

The Welsh Government is accountable to Welsh voters for health, education, housing and other major services, but the powers and resources available to it are partly shaped by decisions made elsewhere.

Second: national legislation without a separate jurisdiction.

Wales can make distinct laws, but it does not control all the institutions needed to administer and enforce them.

Third: democratic autonomy without full constitutional security.

The Senedd is recognised as permanent, but the UK Parliament remains legally sovereign.

Fourth: intergovernmental cooperation without strong legal guarantees.

Welsh and UK ministers are expected to work together respectfully, but much of the relationship depends on political convention and goodwill.

These are not necessarily defects in themselves. They are features of the settlement that can work differently depending on the political circumstances and the governments involved.

But they help explain why the argument about further devolution is not merely an argument about whether Wales should run a particular service.

It is also an argument about what kind of national government Wales is supposed to be, and how much authority an elected Welsh government should have over the institutions for which it is politically accountable.

10. Suggested next stage of our examination

I would now separate the constitutional questions into this order:

* The sovereignty question: What does Westminster’s continuing legal sovereignty actually mean in practice?
* The permanence question: How secure is the Senedd’s constitutional status?
* The jurisdiction question: Does Wales need its own legal jurisdiction to exercise meaningful autonomy?
* The fiscal question: How much practical independence can Wales exercise while relying principally on a UK block grant?
* The reserved-powers question: Are the current reservations coherent, or do they leave gaps between Welsh responsibilities and Welsh authority?
* The intergovernmental question: Are the existing mechanisms sufficient to protect Wales’s interests when UK and Welsh governments disagree?

I suggest we take number 1 — Westminster sovereignty next.

That is the foundation underneath almost every other dispute. It will help us distinguish what Westminster is legally entitled to do, what it is constitutionally expected to do, and what it might be politically wise to do.”  


“2. How secure is the Senedd’s constitutional status?

The short answer is: the Senedd has statutory recognition as a permanent institution, but it does not have absolute legal protection against Westminster.

That sounds contradictory, but it reflects one of the central characteristics of the UK’s uncodified constitution: an institution can be formally recognised as permanent while the sovereign Parliament retains the legal power to change the legislation that establishes it.

There are three different kinds of security to distinguish:

* Statutory security: what the law currently says about the Senedd.
* Constitutional security: the conventions and commitments that make abolition politically and constitutionally unacceptable without Welsh consent.
* Practical political security: how realistic it would be for a UK Government to attempt to abolish or fundamentally weaken it.

Let’s take them in order.

1. The statutory protection: section A1

The Wales Act 2017 inserted section A1 into the Government of Wales Act 2006.

It says:

* The Senedd and Welsh Government are permanent parts of the UK’s constitutional arrangements.
* The provision signifies the commitment of the UK Parliament and Government to those institutions.
* They are not to be abolished except on the basis of a decision by the people of Wales in a referendum.

This is not merely a statement in a political manifesto. It is part of the legislation governing Wales. Legislation.gov.uk +1

The wording is deliberately significant. It does not say that the UK Government should consider a referendum before abolition. It declares that the institutions are not to be abolished except on that basis.

So the starting legal position is clear: abolishing the Senedd without a Welsh referendum would contradict the statutory commitment in section A1.

2. The weakness: Westminster remains sovereign

Here is the constitutional qualification.

The UK does not have a codified constitution that places the Senedd beyond the legislative reach of the UK Parliament.

Under the orthodox doctrine of parliamentary sovereignty, Westminster can pass legislation changing or repealing earlier Acts of Parliament.

That creates a difficult question: could a future UK Parliament pass an Act abolishing the Senedd without first holding a Welsh referendum?

The orthodox constitutional-law answer is that it could legislate to do so, provided it did so clearly and expressly. It could repeal or amend section A1 and make the necessary changes to the Government of Wales Act.

Section A1 does not contain an entrenched legal mechanism requiring a referendum before Westminster can legislate. It does not give the Senedd a veto over an Act of the UK Parliament.

The distinction is between:

* what Parliament has declared it will do;
* and what a sovereign Parliament can legally legislate to do.

The Independent Commission on the Constitutional Future of Wales addressed precisely this problem. Its final report noted that parliamentary sovereignty means the permanence provisions may not provide the protection that a straightforward reading of the legislation might suggest. GOV.WALES +1

This is the crucial limitation. The Senedd has a statutory permanence commitment, but not an entrenched constitutional guarantee of the kind that would make abolition legally impossible without Welsh consent.

3. Could the courts stop Westminster?

This is where the position becomes more complicated.

The courts can determine whether the Senedd is acting within its legal powers. They can also review the lawfulness of actions by the UK and Welsh governments.

But the courts do not ordinarily have authority to strike down an Act of the UK Parliament simply because it conflicts with a constitutional convention or an earlier statutory commitment.

The Supreme Court’s decision in Miller (2017) is particularly relevant. It confirmed that the Sewel Convention — the convention that Westminster will not normally legislate on devolved matters without legislative consent — remains a political convention, rather than a legally enforceable rule. House of Commons Library +1

There is a wider constitutional principle here:

The courts can protect the legal boundaries of devolution, but they cannot straightforwardly turn Westminster’s political commitment to devolution into an unchangeable legal settlement.

That does not mean the courts would have nothing to say about a hypothetical abolition bill. Questions of statutory interpretation, the legality of executive action and the proper legislative process could arise. But there is no established judicial mechanism that would give the Senedd an absolute veto over Westminster.

4. What about weakening the Senedd without abolishing it?

This is arguably the more important question for our wider investigation.

Section A1 protects against abolition. It does not guarantee that the Senedd must retain its current powers, budget, responsibilities or institutional capacity.

For example, a UK Parliament could potentially legislate to:

* transfer particular devolved responsibilities back to Westminster;
* narrow the Senedd’s legislative competence;
* alter the funding arrangements;
* change the relationship between Welsh Ministers and UK departments;
* change the rules governing how the Senedd operates.

Those changes would have to be made through the appropriate legislation. They would not necessarily amount to abolishing the Senedd, so the specific referendum commitment in A1 would not automatically prevent them.

This creates a distinction between institutional survival and meaningful autonomy.

A legislature can continue to exist, hold elections and debate legislation while having substantially less authority than it previously possessed.

That is why permanence alone is not a complete constitutional guarantee.

5. What protects the Senedd in practice?

There are several additional protections, although they are not all legal.

Protection	What it contributes	Limitation
Section A1	Statutory recognition of permanence and referendum commitment	Does not entrench the provision against express repeal
Welsh electoral mandate	Democratic legitimacy through Welsh voters	Does not give the Senedd a legal veto over Westminster
Sewel Convention	Expectation of consent for UK legislation affecting devolved matters	Not enforceable by courts
Political accountability	A government attempting abolition would face serious political consequences	Consequences cannot be guaranteed in advance
Welsh national institutions and identity	A wider social and political foundation for self-government	Not an independent legal safeguard

The distinction between the legal and political protections is important. A UK Government might technically have the legislative power to take an action while facing substantial constitutional and political opposition for doing so.

6. Is the Senedd actually in danger of abolition?

We should distinguish constitutional vulnerability from an actual political threat.

The existence of parliamentary sovereignty means that abolition is not legally impossible. It does not mean abolition is a realistic or currently proposed policy.

The Senedd has been operating since 1999. Its authority has expanded through successive constitutional changes, and the 2017 permanence provision was itself a formal recognition of its established place in the UK constitution.

There is now a substantial institutional and democratic history behind it.

A government proposing to abolish the Senedd would have to confront the statutory referendum commitment, the democratic legitimacy of Welsh institutions, and the political consequences of disregarding Welsh consent.

I would not describe the Senedd as institutionally precarious in everyday political terms. But its ultimate legal security is weaker than the word permanent might suggest.

7. The comparison with Scotland

The Scotland Act 1998 contains a similar permanence provision, inserted by the Scotland Act 2016.

It declares the Scottish Parliament and Government to be permanent parts of the UK’s constitutional arrangements and says they are not to be abolished except following a decision of the people of Scotland in a referendum.

It faces essentially the same parliamentary-sovereignty limitation. GOV.WALES +1

So this is not a peculiarity of Wales. It is a wider feature of the UK’s approach to devolution.

The UK has recognised the permanence of devolved institutions without fully converting that recognition into an entrenched federal constitutional settlement.

8. What this means for our wider examination

I think this exposes a particularly important distinction in the Welsh constitutional debate.

Question A: Does Wales have a permanent legislature?

Formally, yes. The legislation recognises the Senedd as permanent and commits to a referendum before abolition.

Question B: Is the Senedd constitutionally protected from Westminster?

Not absolutely. Westminster retains the legal authority to amend the legislation governing devolution.

Question C: Is Welsh self-government guaranteed a stable range of powers?

No. The current allocation of powers can be changed by legislation, subject to political conventions and constitutional considerations.

And this takes us back to your earlier observation about the correspondence.

If the UK Government treats the Senedd as a permanent national institution, but treats its requests for greater authority as something Westminster can simply accept or reject at its discretion, there is a potential tension between the language of permanence and the practical experience of autonomy.

The Welsh Government can say:

We are a permanent part of the UK’s constitutional arrangements. We have our own electoral mandate and legislative responsibilities. We are asking to develop the powers necessary to exercise those responsibilities.

The UK Government can respond:

The existing allocation of powers is a matter for the UK constitutional framework and Parliament.

Both statements can be legally compatible. But they express quite different understandings of what Welsh constitutional autonomy should mean.

My provisional conclusion

The Senedd is politically and institutionally established, statutorily recognised as permanent, but not legally entrenched against Westminster.

That is a rather unusual position for a national legislature.

It also gives us a useful next question:

Should Wales move towards a more entrenched constitutional settlement, in which the powers and permanence of the Senedd cannot be altered unilaterally by Westminster?

That would take us beyond asking for individual transfers of powers and towards asking what kind of constitutional relationship Wales should have with the rest of the UK.”.  

“What Westminster’s continuing legal sovereignty means in practice

The key point is that the UK Parliament remains the ultimate law-making authority for Wales, even though the Senedd has its own democratic mandate and substantial law-making powers.

That has practical consequences. It means Westminster retains powers that a federal central government would not ordinarily possess over a constituent state’s legislature.

But we need to be precise about what “Westminster” means here. It is the UK Parliament — the House of Commons, House of Lords and Crown acting through the legislative process — not simply the Prime Minister or the UK Government. A Prime Minister cannot personally change the Welsh constitution by writing a letter or issuing an instruction.

The Government can propose legislation and use its political authority to pursue it. Parliament makes the law.

1. Westminster can legislate for Wales, including on devolved matters

This is the most direct consequence.

Section 107(5) of the Government of Wales Act 2006 expressly preserves the UK Parliament’s power to make laws for Wales. Section 107(6) then recognises that Westminster will not normally legislate on devolved matters without Senedd consent. Legislation.gov.uk +1

In practice, that means:

* The Senedd can pass an Act on a devolved subject.
* Westminster can also pass an Act dealing with that subject.
* If the two Acts conflict, the legal position depends on the legislation and its interpretation, but Westminster can expressly override the Welsh provision.
* The Senedd cannot declare a valid Act of the UK Parliament invalid.

This is an overlapping legislative authority, not a completely separate division of sovereignty.

Example: suppose the Senedd passed legislation establishing a particular Welsh system of environmental regulation. Westminster could subsequently pass an Act changing that system or imposing a different UK-wide arrangement, even if the subject were devolved.

That would be constitutionally contentious. But the fact that the matter was devolved would not, by itself, make the Westminster Act legally invalid.

2. The Senedd has real powers — it is not merely advisory

It would be a mistake to interpret Westminster’s sovereignty as meaning that the Senedd’s laws are somehow provisional or optional.

The Senedd can pass primary legislation within its competence. Those Acts have legal force, can create rights and duties, establish public bodies, regulate services and bind people and organisations in Wales.

The Senedd’s legislative competence is legally defined. Broadly, an Act must relate to a devolved subject, must not breach the restrictions in the Government of Wales Act, and must comply with other applicable legal requirements.

The courts can determine whether a Senedd Act falls within those limits. If it does not, the relevant provisions can be held outside the Senedd’s competence. Legislation.gov.uk +2

So there are two directions of constitutional control:

* Westminster’s authority: it can legislate for Wales, including in devolved areas.
* The courts’ authority: they can police the limits of what the Senedd itself is legally allowed to legislate about.

The Senedd is therefore a genuine legislature, but not a sovereign one.

3. The Sewel Convention is a restraint, not a veto

This is probably the most politically important part of the arrangement.

The Sewel Convention says Westminster will not normally legislate on devolved matters without the consent of the relevant devolved legislature.

In Wales, that consent is generally sought through a Legislative Consent Motion.

Suppose Westminster introduces a bill that changes the law on a devolved Welsh matter. The Welsh Government can recommend that the Senedd withhold consent. The Senedd can debate the issue and refuse consent.

But that refusal does not legally prevent Westminster from passing the bill.

The Supreme Court confirmed this in the 2017 Miller judgment: Sewel is a constitutional convention, not a legally enforceable rule. The fact that it is recognised in the Government of Wales Act does not make it enforceable by the courts. House of Commons Library +2

There are therefore three possible outcomes:

* Westminster changes the bill in response to Welsh objections.
* Westminster removes the provisions affecting Wales.
* Westminster proceeds despite the Senedd withholding consent.

The third option is legally available. It is also likely to create a political and constitutional dispute.

This is an important distinction: the Senedd has a recognised constitutional voice, but not an absolute legal right to have that voice obeyed.

4. Westminster can change the devolution settlement itself

This goes beyond intervening in an individual policy area.

The UK Parliament can amend the Government of Wales Act 2006 and change the distribution of powers between the two governments.

In principle, it could:

* add or remove reserved matters;
* change the Senedd’s legislative competence;
* alter the responsibilities of Welsh Ministers;
* change the arrangements for Welsh elections;
* amend the financial framework;
* change the constitutional institutions themselves.

There are statutory and political commitments that matter, including the permanence provision in section A1. But under the orthodox doctrine of parliamentary sovereignty, a future Parliament can expressly amend or repeal earlier legislation. House of Commons Library +1

This is why devolution is sometimes described as constitutionally reversible.

That does not mean it can be reversed without consequence. It means that the current settlement is not legally entrenched against Westminster.

5. What does this mean for the four issues in Burnham’s letter?

It gives us a useful way to understand the underlying dispute.

Issue	Present constitutional position	What further devolution would change
Policing	Primarily a UK responsibility	Transfer some or all policing governance and accountability to Wales
Criminal justice	Much of the system remains UK-wide	Give Welsh institutions responsibility for additional justice functions
Crown Estate	Managed through UK-level arrangements	Transfer management and potentially revenue responsibilities to Wales
Rail	Important functions remain at UK level, alongside devolved Welsh responsibilities	Give Wales greater control over infrastructure, investment and management

The precise legal arrangements differ in each case. But the constitutional question is similar: does Westminster retain a particular responsibility because it believes that responsibility should remain UK-wide, or because the existing legislation has never transferred it?

Those are different justifications.

The fact that Westminster currently has the legal power does not establish that it should retain that power permanently.

6. Does this mean Westminster can do whatever it likes?

Legally, parliamentary sovereignty is extremely broad. Politically and constitutionally, however, it operates within a much wider environment.

There are several constraints and pressures:

* Elections: Westminster MPs are accountable to voters.
* Devolved democratic mandates: the Senedd has its own electoral legitimacy.
* Constitutional conventions: governments are expected to respect the principles of devolution.
* The courts: they interpret legislation, review executive action and enforce the statutory boundaries of devolved powers.
* International obligations: the UK may incur international responsibility if Parliament legislates contrary to an international commitment, even where the domestic legislation remains valid.
* Political legitimacy: a legally valid decision can still be seen as constitutionally unacceptable.

The House of Commons Library’s November 2025 briefing explains the orthodox doctrine while also discussing the debates about its practical and constitutional limits. House of Commons Library

The important thing is not to confuse legal authority with political legitimacy.

A government can have the legal authority to do something and still face a serious constitutional crisis if it uses that authority in a way that undermines the democratic settlement.

7. A hypothetical example

Imagine the following sequence:

* The Senedd passes a law establishing a new Welsh policing accountability framework, within its existing competence.
* The UK Government objects, arguing that policing should remain nationally coordinated.
* Westminster introduces a bill that changes the framework and removes the Welsh arrangements.
* The Senedd refuses legislative consent.
* Westminster passes the bill anyway.

Under the existing constitutional arrangements, the Westminster Act could still be legally valid.

But consider what has happened politically:

* The Welsh electorate elected a legislature with responsibility for devolved matters.
* That legislature made a law within its legal authority.
* The UK Parliament overrode that decision.
* The Welsh legislature objected, but could not legally prevent it.

The constitutional argument would then be about more than policing. It would be about the degree to which Welsh democratic decisions are protected from unilateral intervention by Westminster.

This is why the Sewel Convention matters even though it is not legally enforceable. It provides an important political restraint on the use of Westminster’s legal supremacy.

8. The fundamental distinction: sovereignty versus self-government

I think this is the distinction we need to carry forward.

Concept	Meaning
Parliamentary sovereignty	Westminster retains ultimate legal authority to make or unmake law
Devolution	Westminster has transferred legislative and executive powers to Welsh institutions
Democratic self-government	Welsh voters choose representatives who make decisions over devolved matters
Constitutional entrenchment	The allocation of powers cannot be changed unilaterally through ordinary legislation

Wales has the second and third. It does not have the fourth, and Westminster retains the first.

That is the constitutional structure in a nutshell.

9. The question this raises about the UK Government’s conduct

There is something important here for our examination of the Burnham–ap Iorwerth correspondence.

The UK Government’s legal authority to retain a reserved power is not the same thing as an argument that retaining it is constitutionally appropriate.

For each proposed transfer, we should ask:

* What legal authority does Westminster currently hold?
* What democratic responsibility does the Welsh Government currently exercise?
* Is there a practical reason for keeping the power at UK level?
* Could the benefits of UK-wide cooperation be maintained while transferring political accountability to Wales?
* If Westminster refuses, what is its constitutional justification beyond simply saying that the matter is reserved?

That fourth question is particularly important. Shared services, common standards and cross-border cooperation do not necessarily require shared political control.

And I think this brings us to the larger issue underneath your original concern.

The UK has created national democratic institutions in Wales, but it has not established a fully federal relationship in which those institutions have constitutionally protected spheres of authority.

The result is a system in which Welsh self-government depends partly on the continuing willingness of Westminster to respect the boundaries and conventions of devolution.

That is not the same as saying Wales has no meaningful autonomy. It means that the ultimate legal security of that autonomy remains asymmetrical.

The next question I would examine is whether this makes the UK constitution effectively unitary, despite its devolved institutions — or whether it has evolved into something closer to a quasi-federal system in practice. That distinction will help us understand whether the current arrangement is simply unfinished devolution or a structural constitutional contradiction.”  

“3. To what extent does Wales need its own legal jurisdiction to exercise meaningful autonomy?

This is a particularly important question because it exposes a gap in the Welsh constitutional settlement.

Wales already makes its own laws, but it does not have full control over the legal institutions that interpret, administer and enforce them.

That is the starting point. But there is a distinction we need to make before going further: a distinct body of Welsh law is not necessarily the same thing as a separate Welsh legal jurisdiction.

And, interestingly, the Welsh Government has recently been quite explicit about that distinction.

1. What Wales currently has

Wales and England share one legal jurisdiction.

That means they have a common overarching court system, judiciary and legal-professional framework, even though the laws applying in the two countries increasingly differ.

For example:

* Welsh housing law can differ from English housing law.
* Welsh education legislation can differ from English education legislation.
* Welsh environmental regulations can differ from English regulations.
* Welsh health and social care legislation can establish different legal duties.

These are not simply local variations in how the same law is administered. They can be genuinely different legal rules.

The Welsh Government itself explains that Welsh legislation and the case law interpreting it form part of the law of England and Wales, even where the legislation applies only in Wales. GOV.WALES +1

The Senedd has therefore developed a distinct body of Welsh law without Wales having a separate legal jurisdiction.

2. Three different things that are easily confused

I think we need three categories.

Already exists

A distinct body of Welsh law

Welsh legislation applies to Wales and can establish legal rules different from those in England.

A developing concept

Formal recognition of a Welsh legal jurisdiction

Wales would be formally recognised as having its own jurisdictional identity, reflecting its distinct legislation and legal development. This does not necessarily require entirely separate courts or a separate legal profession.

A more extensive institutional change

A separate Welsh court and justice system

Wales would have substantially autonomous arrangements for courts, judicial administration, justice policy and the institutions responsible for administering the law.

These distinctions matter because people sometimes use separate jurisdiction to mean all three things.

The Law Society of England and Wales, in a November 2025 position paper, specifically distinguishes a distinct Welsh jurisdiction from a fully separate one. It supports recognition of the former but opposes the latter, citing institutional costs and potential complications for cross-border legal work. The Law Society +1

3. Why does the current arrangement create difficulties?

The strongest argument is not simply about national identity or constitutional symbolism.

It concerns accountability and the ability to join up public policy.

Consider the relationship between these services:

A person’s route through public services

Housing and homelessness

Mental health and healthcare

Welsh Government responsibility

Policing and criminal justice

Prisons, probation and rehabilitation

Illustrative policy pathway, not a compulsory sequence for every individual.

The problem is that many of the services that influence offending, rehabilitation and prevention are devolved, while important justice institutions remain under UK control.

This can create:

* divided accountability;
* different policy priorities;
* difficulties sharing information;
* fragmented budgets;
* competing institutional objectives;
* difficulty evaluating outcomes across the whole system.

The Independent Commission on the Constitutional Future of Wales’s justice subgroup identified precisely this problem. It argued that the division between devolved public services and reserved justice institutions makes coherent policy more difficult. GOV.WALES +1

The commission recommended a staged programme of justice devolution, beginning with areas such as policing, probation and youth justice.

It envisaged a programme taking approximately ten years, rather than an overnight transfer of the entire system. GOV.WALES +1

That is an important distinction: the argument for justice devolution is not necessarily an argument for immediately creating a wholly separate legal system.

4. What would a Welsh jurisdiction actually achieve?

There are several potential benefits.

A. Clearer legal identity

As Welsh legislation grows, there is a practical need to make it easier for citizens, lawyers, businesses and public authorities to understand which law applies.

A formal Welsh jurisdiction could clarify that Wales is not simply a place where English law has some local amendments.

It has its own developing body of legislation, legal institutions and democratic law-making authority.

B. Greater judicial leadership

A Welsh jurisdiction could create stronger institutional recognition of Welsh legal expertise and priorities.

The Welsh Government has argued that Wales should benefit from stronger local judicial leadership, drawing comparisons with Scotland and Northern Ireland. GOV.WALES +1

C. Better alignment between legislation and enforcement

If Wales makes a law, it should be possible to develop a coherent system for administering and enforcing that law.

This does not mean every legal function must be controlled exclusively by Welsh institutions. But there is a legitimate question about why responsibility for making policy and responsibility for administering its consequences are divided between governments.

D. Greater democratic accountability

If the Welsh Government has responsibility for a justice policy, Welsh voters should be able to scrutinise the ministers and institutions responsible for implementing it.

That is difficult when the relevant services answer to UK ministers.

The Independent Commission argued that the case for justice devolution rests substantially on this accountability problem, rather than simply on the possibility of making criminal law different in Wales. GOV.WALES +1

5. Does a Welsh jurisdiction mean completely separate courts?

Not necessarily. And this is where a particularly interesting development occurred in the Senedd.

On 20 October 2026, the Counsel General, Elfyn Llwyd, was asked about the implications of establishing a distinct or separate Welsh legal jurisdiction.

He made a clear distinction between the two concepts.

He said the Welsh Government supports the formal establishment of a Welsh legal jurisdiction to provide greater clarity for citizens and legal practitioners as Welsh law develops.

Importantly, he said this would not in practice require:

* a completely separate judiciary;
* a completely separate court system;
* a separate legal profession;
* different professional qualifications;
* separate regulatory requirements.

He also said lawyers would continue to be able to practise across the border, provided they understood the relevant law. OpenSenedd

This is a significant clarification of what the current Welsh Government is proposing.

It suggests that formal recognition of a Welsh jurisdiction need not mean building an entirely new legal infrastructure.

There is still a distinction between that proposal and transferring all justice responsibilities to Wales. They are related but separate constitutional questions.

6. What are the arguments against it?

There are real practical considerations.

The UK Government’s traditional argument has also been that the shared jurisdiction provides efficiencies and established infrastructure.

That should be considered on its practical merits. But it does not establish that every aspect of justice must remain under UK political control.

7. What is the present Welsh Government actually proposing?

There is an important recent development.

The Counsel General’s October 2026 statement indicates support for formally establishing a Welsh legal jurisdiction while retaining shared professional and institutional arrangements.

The Welsh Government’s broader 2026 constitutional paper also argues for a discrete Welsh court system and stronger Welsh judicial leadership. So there are several strands of policy discussion, not one settled institutional blueprint.

8. The key question: what is the minimum necessary for meaningful autonomy?

I would break this into four levels.

Level	What Wales would control	What it would mean
1	Existing Welsh law-making	Current arrangement
2	More justice policy and administration	Greater ability to align justice with Welsh public services
3	Formal Welsh jurisdiction	Legal recognition of Wales’s distinct body of law
4	Extensive independent justice institutions	Greater control over courts, judiciary and justice administration

These are not necessarily an all-or-nothing package. Some could be developed together, others in stages.

My analytical distinction would be:

* A separate jurisdiction is not a strict prerequisite for Wales to exercise meaningful autonomy over health, education, housing, environment or many other devolved areas.
* Greater control over justice institutions becomes increasingly relevant if Wales wants coherent responsibility for policing, criminal justice and the enforcement of its own laws.
* Formal jurisdictional recognition addresses the constitutional and practical consequences of Wales having its own growing body of law.
* A wholly separate court system is a much larger institutional choice, and its necessity cannot simply be assumed.

9. How I would frame this in our Burnham examination

There is a question that goes right to the heart of the policing and criminal justice arguments.

Can Westminster reasonably argue that Wales needs to retain UK-level justice institutions for the sake of integration, while simultaneously expecting the Welsh Government to take responsibility for the social policies that are supposed to prevent crime and support rehabilitation?

The answer depends on the functions involved. Some are naturally cross-border or UK-wide; others may be more effectively coordinated at Welsh level.

But the existence of shared legal infrastructure does not, on its own, settle where political accountability should sit.

I would therefore keep three questions separate as we continue:

* Should Wales have greater control over policing and justice?
* Should Wales have formal recognition as a distinct legal jurisdiction?
* Should Wales eventually have a substantially separate court and judicial system?

They are connected, but they do not have to be answered identically.

My provisional finding: Wales can exercise meaningful autonomy without immediately establishing an entirely separate legal system. But as its legislative powers and body of Welsh law develop, the case for formal jurisdictional recognition becomes increasingly relevant. And if it wants full responsibility for justice policy, the question of who controls the institutions administering justice becomes much harder to avoid.

The next item in our agenda is fiscal autonomy: how much practical self-government can Wales exercise if it does not control most of the money it spends?”.

The answer is: Wales has substantial freedom to decide how to spend its allocated budget, but much less freedom to determine the overall size of that budget, raise the money it needs, or manage financial shocks independently.

That distinction is fundamental.

A government can have considerable policy autonomy without having fiscal autonomy. It can decide what to do with its money, but have limited control over how much money is available in the first place.

And the latest Welsh Government budget figures make the scale of that dependence quite clear.

2. What does the block grant actually allow Wales to do?

The block grant is not normally a collection of instructions from Westminster about how to run individual Welsh services.

Once funding has been allocated, the Welsh Government can make its own spending choices within the relevant budgetary and legal rules, subject to Senedd approval.

For example, it can decide:

* how much to allocate to the Welsh NHS;
* how to distribute funding between schools and further education;
* whether to prioritise social housing or other infrastructure;
* how much to spend on Welsh-language programmes;
* whether to direct more resources towards preventative health and social care.

It does not have to reproduce the UK Government’s spending priorities simply because the money originated in the UK settlement.

That is genuine self-government.

The distinction is that Wales has substantial discretion over allocation, but less discretion over the total resources available.

3. How does the Barnett formula affect that?

The Barnett formula is often misunderstood as a formula that calculates exactly how much money Wales deserves.

It doesn’t do that.

It principally calculates changes to the block grant when spending changes in comparable UK Government departments in England.

The calculation takes account of:

* The change in the relevant English departmental budget.
* The proportion of that department’s responsibilities that are devolved.
* Wales’s population relative to England.
* A needs-based factor for Wales.

The resulting adjustment is added to or deducted from the block grant. Wales also has a needs-based funding floor. House of Commons Library +1

This has two important consequences.

First: decisions made in Westminster about spending in England can affect the money available to the Welsh Government.

Second: the Welsh Government cannot independently redesign the whole funding settlement whenever it believes Welsh circumstances require a different approach.

There is a degree of predictability, because the formula operates mechanically rather than requiring the two governments to renegotiate the entire grant every year. But the Welsh Government remains exposed to decisions about UK departmental spending and the timing of UK spending reviews.

That is a form of indirect financial dependence, even when Westminster is not dictating Welsh spending priorities.

4. How much tax freedom does Wales have?

Wales does have tax-raising powers, but they are partial.

There are three particularly important categories.

Tax	Welsh Government’s powers
Welsh Rates of Income Tax	Can vary the Welsh rates applied to the basic, higher and additional income-tax bands
Land Transaction Tax	Controls the Welsh equivalent of stamp duty, including rates and bands
Landfill Disposals Tax	Controls the Welsh tax on landfill disposal

It also has responsibility for non-domestic rates, commonly called business rates.

The income-tax arrangement is particularly interesting. The UK Government reduces the relevant income-tax rates for Welsh taxpayers by 10 pence, and the Welsh Government sets its own Welsh rates on top.

It can therefore choose to maintain parity with England or set different rates. But it does not have the same freedom as a fully sovereign government to redesign the entire income-tax system, including all the tax bands and thresholds.

HMRC continues to collect income tax, including the Welsh rates. GOV.WALES +2

The practical result is that Wales can make some meaningful choices about taxation, but cannot independently redesign its whole revenue system.

5. What about borrowing?

This is arguably where the limits on Welsh fiscal autonomy become especially visible.

An independent national government can generally borrow in financial markets, subject to its creditworthiness, economic circumstances and any applicable fiscal rules.

The Welsh Government has statutory borrowing limits established through its relationship with Westminster.

The Institute for Fiscal Studies’ February 2026 analysis identifies two main forms of Welsh borrowing:

Type	Welsh position
Capital borrowing	For investment in infrastructure, buildings and other assets
Resource borrowing	Limited borrowing to manage adverse forecasting errors involving devolved taxes and related grant adjustments

The capital borrowing limit was £1 billion outstanding, with annual borrowing of £150 million in 2025–26. The annual and overall limits were scheduled to increase in subsequent years.

Resource borrowing has a separate limit of £200 million annually and £600 million outstanding. Institute for Fiscal Studies +1

There are also restrictions on the amount Wales can hold in reserves.

This matters because capital investment and day-to-day spending are different things.

Imagine Wales experiences a serious economic downturn:

* tax receipts fall;
* unemployment rises;
* demand for public services increases;
* the Welsh Government wants to support households and local businesses.

It cannot simply borrow unlimited sums to maintain public spending.

It has to work within its fiscal framework, and many of the financial decisions affecting the overall UK economy remain outside Welsh control.

6. What does this mean in practice?

I think there are four particularly important consequences.

A. Wales can choose different priorities, but not always fund them

The Welsh Government might decide that preventative healthcare, affordable housing or economic development should receive greater investment.

It can change spending priorities to some extent.

But if the overall budget is constrained, increasing spending in one area means reducing it elsewhere unless additional funding becomes available.

B. Wales is exposed to economic decisions made elsewhere

UK-wide taxation, monetary policy, interest rates, welfare policy and much of the wider economic framework are not controlled by the Welsh Government.

Those decisions can have substantial consequences for Welsh public finances.

C. Long-term planning is harder

Infrastructure projects, hospitals, transport networks and major economic-development programmes often require commitments over many years.

Restricted borrowing and reserve arrangements can make it harder to plan and manage those commitments independently.

D. Accountability becomes complicated

Welsh ministers are accountable to Welsh voters for public services, but some of the financial conditions under which they operate are determined by UK ministers and HM Treasury.

That creates a potential accountability gap:

The Welsh Government is responsible for delivering the service, but it does not control every financial lever affecting its ability to deliver it.

The Independent Commission on the Constitutional Future of Wales identified this as a significant weakness in the current arrangements. Its analysis noted that the block grant accounted for more than 80% of Welsh Government financing in 2022–23, and that restrictions on budget flexibility made it harder to respond to Welsh circumstances. GOV.WALES +1

7. Does this mean Wales cannot be genuinely autonomous?

No. It means we need to distinguish different kinds of autonomy.

Policy autonomy

Substantial

Wales can make its own choices in many major public-service areas.

Budget allocation autonomy

Substantial, with restrictions

Welsh ministers decide how to distribute much of their available funding.

Tax autonomy

Partial

Wales controls some taxes and income-tax rates, but not the full tax system.

Borrowing and fiscal autonomy

Limited

Borrowing, reserves and financial flexibility are subject to UK-set limits.

Macroeconomic autonomy

Very limited

Monetary policy, most major economic levers and the overall UK fiscal framework remain outside Welsh control.

This is why I would avoid describing Wales as either financially independent or financially powerless. Neither description is accurate.

8. What would greater fiscal autonomy actually look like?

There are several possible reforms that do not require Welsh independence.

Possible reform	What it would change
Greater control over income tax	More ability to shape the Welsh tax system
Additional devolved taxes	More independent revenue sources
Greater borrowing powers	More freedom to invest in long-term infrastructure
Larger reserves	Greater ability to manage unexpected financial pressures
More flexible budget rules	Greater ability to move resources between financial years and spending categories
A revised funding formula	Greater influence over how the UK settlement reflects Welsh needs
Greater control over natural-resource revenues	More direct connection between Welsh assets and Welsh public finances

The 2024 Independent Commission considered different constitutional options, including enhanced devolution, a federal UK and independence. It identified greater agency and accountability as potential benefits of more extensive constitutional change, while also recognising the financial and economic risks. GOV.WALES +1

An important point: greater fiscal autonomy does not necessarily mean keeping all the money raised in Wales.

A country can retain substantial fiscal autonomy while participating in a redistributive union. The question is how that redistribution is calculated, who controls the tax and spending decisions, and what financial risks each government carries.

That is a perfectly legitimate subject for negotiation within the United Kingdom.

9. Bringing this back to Burnham’s letter

I think this creates an additional dimension to the four issues we have been examining.

Take the Crown Estate.

If Wales has responsibility for economic development, energy policy and planning, but does not control the revenues and investment decisions associated with substantial Welsh natural resources, there is a question about whether its existing fiscal settlement properly supports its devolved responsibilities.

Take rail.

If Wales is expected to plan its economic development around transport infrastructure but does not control all the relevant investment decisions, its ability to deliver its own economic strategy is constrained.

Take policing and justice.

If the Welsh Government is responsible for substantial preventative public services but has limited control over justice expenditure, it may struggle to align its priorities with the institutions responsible for criminal justice.

These are not proof that every power must transfer to Wales. They are reasons to examine the relationship between responsibility, resources and authority.

The question I would put to both governments

If Wales is expected to take responsibility for its own national development, what degree of control over the financial resources necessary to deliver that development should accompany that responsibility?

The UK Government can reasonably argue that fiscal transfers, shared taxation and financial risk-sharing are advantages of the Union.

But it also needs to explain why those advantages require the present degree of central control.

And the Welsh Government needs to explain what additional fiscal powers it wants, how it would use them, and how it would manage the associated risks.

Where this leaves us

The current Welsh settlement permits meaningful policy autonomy, but it does not provide full fiscal autonomy.

That is not necessarily a contradiction: plenty of devolved and federal governments rely on transfers from a central government.

The more interesting question is whether Wales has sufficient financial flexibility to exercise the responsibilities it already possesses, before we even consider transferring additional ones.

I would add one further issue to our examination: the difference between fair funding and financial control. A government can receive a fair share of UK funding while still lacking the freedom to manage that money in ways that reflect its own long-term priorities.

That distinction is going to be important when we examine the argument over whether Wales should have greater control over the Crown Estate and its natural resources.”.  

“5. Are the current reservations coherent, or do they leave gaps between Welsh responsibilities and Welsh authority?

My provisional answer is: the settlement is legally workable, but it is not always coherent as a system of public accountability. Some reservations protect genuinely UK-wide functions. Others create boundaries where the Welsh Government is responsible for the outcome of a policy but does not control all the institutions or powers that materially determine it.

The key distinction is between two questions:

* Is it reasonable for a power to remain reserved to the UK Parliament or Government?
* Can Wales deliver and be held accountable for its devolved responsibilities when that power remains reserved?

A reservation can make sense in isolation and still create a practical problem when it interacts with devolved responsibilities. The test should be how the whole system works, not simply whether each power has a defensible constitutional label.

1. The clearest gap: justice and public safety

This is the strongest example because the institutional split is unusually visible.

The Welsh Government controls many of the services that shape whether people enter the criminal justice system, and what happens when they leave it:

* Schools, education and skills.
* Health and mental health services.
* Housing and homelessness support.
* Social services and local government.
* Important aspects of youth provision and prevention.

But the UK Government retains responsibility for policing, prosecution, courts, prisons and probation. The Criminal Justice Board for Wales’s own 2025–26 report describes these interdependencies explicitly. GOV.UK +1

That produces a real accountability problem. A Welsh Government can pursue prevention, rehabilitation and public-health approaches, but it cannot direct all the institutions whose decisions determine whether those approaches succeed.

For example, a person leaving prison may need housing, healthcare, employment support and supervision. Welsh institutions have important responsibilities for the first three, but probation and prisons remain UK-controlled. The two governments therefore need to coordinate a journey for which neither has complete authority.

The Independent Commission’s justice subgroup identified precisely this problem: overlapping accountability can create friction, and devolving policing, probation and youth justice could improve coordination. It proposed a staged process rather than an overnight transfer. GOV.WALES

My assessment: this is more than an untidy constitutional boundary. It is a structural mismatch between responsibility for social outcomes and authority over the institutions that materially affect them.

2. Rail: Welsh policy responsibility without full infrastructure control

Rail is another strong example, although the division is more complicated.

Wales has substantial responsibility for transport policy, bus services and some rail services. The Welsh Government also controls Transport for Wales and the Core Valley Lines. But the wider rail network and much of its infrastructure remain under UK-level control, principally through Network Rail.

The Senedd’s own constitutional evidence describes this as a divided arrangement: Welsh Ministers procure and manage important passenger services, while the UK Government remains responsible for Network Rail infrastructure planning and funding. GOV.WALES +1

That matters because a government can be judged on transport connectivity, economic development and access to jobs while lacking full control over the infrastructure investment needed to deliver them.

There is a legitimate UK-wide case for common railway standards, cross-border services and an integrated network. But those things do not logically require all infrastructure ownership, investment decisions and political accountability to remain centralised.

My assessment: the UK Government has a reasonable case for coordination, but coordination and control are not the same thing. This is a particularly good example of where the argument for retaining a reservation needs to be more specific.

3. Energy and the Crown Estate: responsibility for outcomes, limited control over assets and systems

Energy is a slightly different kind of mismatch.

Wales has devolved responsibilities for planning, environmental policy, climate targets and some renewable-energy consents. But major parts of energy policy—including electricity-market regulation, supply, transmission and strategic energy decisions—remain reserved. The division also varies by project size and technology. House of Commons +2

So Wales can set ambitions for renewable generation and influence where some projects are built, while lacking full authority over the market, grid and infrastructure arrangements that determine whether the energy can be transmitted, sold and used.

The Crown Estate adds another layer. Management of the Crown Estate is expressly reserved, while Welsh institutions have other relevant responsibilities, including planning and marine environmental functions. Legislation.gov.uk +1

That means the Welsh Government can be responsible for aspects of the development environment around natural resources without controlling the institution managing important assets or deciding how its commercial arrangements operate.

There are good reasons for national grid coordination and large-scale investment expertise. But the question of who manages an asset, who receives its revenue, who consents to development and who bears the environmental consequences should not be collapsed into one question.

My assessment: these are not necessarily incoherent reservations individually. But together they can leave Wales with responsibility for the local and environmental consequences of development without equivalent control over the strategic decisions or assets driving it.

4. Are there reservations that are coherent?

Yes. It would be a mistake to treat every reserved power as a defect.

There are strong arguments for UK-level responsibility over matters such as:

* Defence and national security.
* Foreign affairs and international relations.
* Immigration and border control.
* Currency and monetary policy.
* National intelligence and security services.

These functions involve external sovereignty, national security or systems whose operation depends on UK-wide arrangements. There is a credible case for common authority.

Even there, however, the UK Government should distinguish between retaining strategic authority and preventing Welsh institutions from having a meaningful role in decisions affecting Wales.

The question is not whether every function should be devolved. It is whether the boundary has been drawn at the right level.

5. There is also a technical problem: the boundaries are not always simple

The Welsh Government’s own Cabinet Handbook makes two important admissions.

First, the range of policy areas devolved to Wales remains significantly narrower than in Scotland and Northern Ireland, even though the reserved-powers model is now broadly similar.

Second, Welsh Ministers’ executive powers do not always match the Senedd’s legislative competence. Ministers can sometimes exercise powers in areas where the Senedd cannot pass primary legislation, including certain civil-contingencies functions. GOV.WALES

There are also restrictions on the Senedd imposing functions on reserved authorities, such as UK government departments or the Crown Prosecution Service, without UK ministerial consent.

That creates an additional complication: even where Wales wants to legislate around a problem in a devolved policy area, it may not be able to require the relevant UK institution to participate in the way it would like.

The result is a settlement that can be difficult to explain, administer and hold accountable.

6. My overall assessment

I would distinguish three categories:

Category	Examples	Assessment
Coherent reservations	Defence, foreign affairs, currency	Strong case for UK-level control
Shared-system reservations	Rail, energy markets, national infrastructure	UK coordination is justified; exclusive UK control needs a more specific case
Responsibility-authority gaps	Policing, probation, criminal justice	Strong case that the present division impedes coherent Welsh policy

The third category is the most constitutionally significant. The second is where there is considerable room for negotiation without necessarily changing the basic structure of the Union.

There is also a difference between a reservation being legally clear and being democratically satisfactory. Schedule 7A can tell us who has authority. It cannot, by itself, demonstrate that the allocation produces good government.

7. What would a coherent settlement look like?

I would apply five tests to every contested reservation:

* Responsibility: Which government is publicly accountable for the outcome?
* Authority: Does that government control the institutions and decisions needed to deliver it?
* Resources: Does it have the funding and revenue flexibility to discharge that responsibility?
* Coordination: If authority is shared, are there binding or dependable arrangements for joint decisions?
* Accountability: Can the public identify who made a decision, who can change it and who should answer when it fails?

A reservation that passes those tests may be entirely reasonable. One that repeatedly fails them deserves reconsideration.

My provisional conclusion: Wales does not simply have too few powers. It has a number of areas where its responsibilities, resources and authority do not line up. Justice is the clearest example; rail and energy illustrate the problem in a more shared-system form.

And this brings us back to the Burnham letter. The UK Government’s argument for retaining particular functions should not stop at saying that integration is valuable. It needs to explain why integration requires the UK Government to retain political control, rather than allowing Welsh democratic control within agreed UK-wide standards and cooperation.

That is a much harder question to answer—and a much more useful one to put to both governments.

The next distinction I would examine is whether shared administration and shared standards genuinely require shared political control. That is arguably the central constitutional assumption underlying the UK Government’s position on policing, rail and the Crown Estate.”.  

“6. The intergovernmental question: Are the existing mechanisms sufficient to protect Wales’s interests when the governments disagree?

The central issue is whether Wales has a reliable way to influence, challenge or resist a UK Government decision when cooperation breaks down.

There are two different situations:

* Disagreement within a shared policy area: the governments need to negotiate how a service, funding arrangement or cross-border system will work.
* Disagreement over constitutional authority: the UK Government wants to retain a power, or exercise a power, that the Welsh Government believes should be devolved or should require Welsh consent.

A forum for discussion can help with the first. It does not necessarily protect Wales in the second.

I would assess the arrangements against four practical questions: Can Wales get a dispute formally heard? Can it require the UK Government to explain its position? Can it prevent unilateral action? And, if agreement remains impossible, is there an independent process or remedy?

1. What mechanisms actually exist?

The 2022 Intergovernmental Relations Review established a three-tier structure:

Level	Mechanism	Purpose
Portfolio	Interministerial Groups (IMGs)	Deal with specific policy areas, such as transport, health or agriculture
Cross-government	Interministerial Standing Committee (IMSC) and Finance IMSC	Handle cross-cutting policy and financial disagreements
Heads of government	Prime Minister and Heads of Devolved Governments Council	Strategic oversight and final escalation stage

There is also an independent IGR Secretariat, staffed by officials from the governments, intended to support the machinery and facilitate dispute resolution. GOV.WALES +1

In principle, this is a sensible escalation structure: try to resolve the issue where the expertise sits, then raise it politically if necessary.

2. What happens if agreement cannot be reached?

The formal dispute process has several stages:

* The governments first attempt to resolve the disagreement through normal official and ministerial channels.
* Any government can refer a disagreement to the IGR Secretariat as a potential formal dispute. Another government cannot veto the decision to raise it.
* The Secretariat considers whether the issue meets the criteria for formal escalation, including whether it has been extensively considered at portfolio level and has implications beyond the individual policy area.
* Senior officials examine the issue and recommend possible solutions.
* Ministers consider it through the relevant interministerial committee.
* Independent advice or mediation may be used, subject to agreement between the governments.
* If unresolved, it can reach the Prime Minister and Heads of Devolved Governments Council.

The process is intended to produce a resolution, but it does not give an independent mediator or the Council the power to impose a binding settlement. GOV.UK +2

That distinction matters enormously.

Wales can formally raise a dispute, but it cannot necessarily obtain a decision in its favour, or compel the UK Government to change its position.

3. The fundamental weakness: these arrangements are not legally enforceable

The 2022 agreement expressly describes itself as a statement of political intent. It does not create new legal obligations, override existing legal relations or establish justiciable rights. GOV.UK

So there is a difference between:

* A government breaking a political commitment to consult or cooperate.
* A government acting unlawfully under the devolution legislation.

The second may be challenged through the courts, where the relevant legal conditions are met. The first is generally a matter of political accountability and intergovernmental negotiation.

That leaves a significant gap. If the UK Government fails to engage meaningfully, provides information late, or simply refuses a Welsh request, Wales cannot automatically turn that failure into a legally enforceable claim.

The Sewel Convention illustrates the wider problem: the convention that Westminster will not normally legislate on devolved matters without consent is politically important, but does not give the Senedd a legally enforceable veto.

4. What does the record tell us?

There are two particularly relevant pieces of evidence.

First, the Welsh Government’s March 2026 report.

Covering July 2024 to March 2026, it recorded a stronger overall pattern of formal intergovernmental engagement following the 2024 UK general election. But it also said that improvement was inconsistent, with some areas experiencing limited or uneven engagement. GOV.WALES

That is useful because it avoids two simplistic conclusions:

* The machinery is entirely dysfunctional.
* The existence of regular meetings means that the relationship is working adequately.

Both would be inaccurate.

Second, the Welsh Government’s Cabinet Handbook, published in February 2026.

It confirms that the formal structures are generally not reflected in law. It also states that no dispute had yet gone through the entire formal resolution process. GOV.WALES

That does not prove the procedure is ineffective: a mechanism may prevent disputes from escalating. But it means there is limited practical experience of the complete process being tested.

We should therefore distinguish the existence of a dispute procedure from evidence that it can resolve a serious constitutional disagreement.

5. Does Wales have equal standing within the machinery?

Formally, yes: the agreements say that the structures should serve all governments equally and fairly, and that each government should have an equal opportunity to influence the agenda.

But there is an important institutional asymmetry.

The Prime Minister chairs the Heads of Governments Council. The UK Government also retains the underlying parliamentary authority to legislate for Wales, including in devolved areas, subject to the political conventions already discussed.

The Welsh Government therefore enters negotiations with a government that has powers Wales does not possess.

This does not mean that the UK Government necessarily abuses that position. It means that procedural equality does not amount to equality of constitutional power.

A meeting in which both governments have an equal right to speak is not necessarily a meeting in which both have equal ability to determine the outcome.

6. What protection does the Senedd provide?

There is another important layer: the relationship between the Welsh Government and its own legislature.

The Inter-Institutional Relationship Agreement between the Senedd and Welsh Government requires reporting and information-sharing about intergovernmental relations. Welsh Ministers provide updates through plenary statements, committees and published correspondence. GOV.WALES +1

That gives the Senedd some ability to scrutinise:

* What Welsh Ministers have asked the UK Government to do.
* Whether they have obtained meaningful engagement.
* Whether Welsh interests have been represented.
* Whether Welsh Ministers have compromised or accepted a UK proposal.
* What they intend to do when negotiations fail.

But this is scrutiny of the Welsh Government, not a mechanism for compelling the UK Government to negotiate differently.

It can improve accountability within Wales without resolving the underlying constitutional imbalance.

7. What would stronger protection look like?

There are several possible reforms. They are not all-or-nothing.

Possible reform	What it would change
Statutory duty to cooperate	Give intergovernmental cooperation a legal foundation
Mandatory consultation and response periods	Make it harder to bypass Wales or provide inadequate notice
Independent mediation	Give disagreements an impartial forum
Independent arbitration in defined circumstances	Potentially provide a more authoritative outcome
Stronger legislative consent protection	Give the Senedd a more secure role when Westminster changes devolved powers
Published reasons for rejecting Welsh proposals	Make disagreements more transparent and politically accountable

The Welsh Government’s response to the Independent Commission on the Constitutional Future of Wales explicitly supported statutory underpinning for intergovernmental relations, including a duty of cooperation and parity of esteem. GOV.WALES

There is a reasonable counterargument: a highly legalised system could make government less flexible, slow down urgent decisions and create litigation over matters that should be resolved politically. Not every disagreement needs arbitration.

But that does not eliminate the case for enforceable minimum standards of consultation, information-sharing and good-faith engagement.

8. Applying this to the Burnham–ap Iorwerth correspondence

This is where the procedural question becomes particularly useful.

The existence of a bilateral meeting on 30 October and subsequent written correspondence does not, by itself, establish that the Welsh Government had a meaningful opportunity to influence the final UK position.

We would need to know:

* What proposals did the Welsh Government submit before the meeting?
* What commitments, if any, did the UK Government make during it?
* Were the four policy areas discussed through the relevant intergovernmental machinery, or mainly through a bilateral political relationship?
* Was there a joint record of the discussion?
* Did Welsh Ministers receive the UK Government’s objections in time to respond before the 20 November letter?
* Has either government formally referred any part of the disagreement to the IGR dispute process?

I would not assume that the letter exchange itself constitutes a formal dispute. That would need to be established from the governments’ records.

9. The central finding

The existing machinery provides Wales with routes to raise concerns, negotiate, escalate disagreements and obtain political scrutiny.

What it does not reliably provide is protection against a determined UK Government deciding to proceed without Welsh agreement.

That is the distinction between a system designed to facilitate cooperation and a system designed to protect the constitutional interests of its participants.

The current arrangements are largely based on mutual restraint, political goodwill and the expectation that governments will respect the spirit of devolution. They are less effective when the governments disagree about the limits of their own authority.

And that raises a question worth keeping separate from the individual policy disputes:

Can a voluntary, largely non-binding system of intergovernmental cooperation adequately protect a devolved nation’s democratic authority when the other government retains ultimate legal sovereignty?

That is not simply a question about whether ministers get on. It is a question about the constitutional security of devolution itself.”.
