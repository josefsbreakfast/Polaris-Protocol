“Executive summary: Burnham’s legal arguments against further devolution to Wales

The central issue is constitutional, not simply administrative: does Wales need to retain shared UK-wide institutions to make public services work effectively, or should it have its own democratic authority over matters increasingly affected by Welsh law and policy?

Burnham’s arguments, as reported, fall into four areas: policing, criminal justice, the Crown Estate and rail infrastructure.

One important qualification: these are principally arguments about institutional design, operational efficiency and public interest, rather than demonstrations that devolution would be legally impossible.

There is a significant distinction between saying that powers should not be transferred and saying that they cannot lawfully be transferred.

1. Policing: maintaining an integrated England–Wales system

Argument: operational integration

Burnham’s position

The England and Wales policing system should remain integrated because it:

* Enables interoperability between police forces.
* Facilitates cross-border investigations and operations.
* Gives Welsh forces access to national and specialist capabilities.
* Supports a coordinated response to serious threats and public protection.

His underlying argument is that separating governance could introduce institutional fragmentation and weaken operational effectiveness.

Legal and constitutional implications

Policing is currently not devolved to Wales in the way it is to Scotland. The relevant powers remain substantially within the UK Government’s constitutional and legislative framework.

However, there is no inherent legal requirement that Wales and England must have identical policing governance.

Scotland demonstrates that a separate policing structure can coexist with UK-wide security cooperation.

The relevant legal question is therefore whether operational integration requires common political control, or whether it can be maintained through statutory cooperation agreements, shared services and cross-border arrangements.

Executive assessment: Burnham establishes a legitimate operational concern, but does not establish that retaining Westminster control is legally necessary.

2. Criminal justice: the case for retaining a shared legal system

Argument: institutional coherence

Burnham’s position

The existing shared justice arrangements provide Wales with access to:

* Greater institutional resources.
* Specialist legal and judicial expertise.
* Shared administrative infrastructure.
* Greater resilience across the justice system.

He argues that effective partnership between the Welsh and UK Governments is preferable to transferring responsibility for the justice system.

Legal and constitutional implications

This is arguably the most substantial constitutional issue in the letter.

Wales has its own legislature and an increasingly distinct body of Welsh law in devolved areas, including housing, health, education and planning.

Yet the administration of justice remains substantially organised through England-and-Wales institutions.

This creates an increasingly complicated relationship between:

* The legislature making the law.
* The government implementing policy.
* The courts interpreting and applying the law.
* The institutions responsible for administering justice.

The case for a distinct Welsh legal jurisdiction is that legislative divergence creates a corresponding need for appropriate judicial, administrative and institutional capacity.

The counterargument is that a shared jurisdiction can accommodate different laws without requiring separate institutions.

Importantly, creating a separate Welsh jurisdiction would not necessarily mean establishing completely independent courts, prosecution services or prisons. Different models of shared infrastructure are possible.

Executive assessment: Burnham identifies the advantages of shared institutions but does not answer the central constitutional question: whether Wales has sufficient control over the institutions needed to implement and enforce its own laws.

The Commission on Justice in Wales, chaired by Lord Thomas, provides an important independent evidence base for examining this question. Law Gazette +1

3. The Crown Estate: ownership, control and economic benefit

Argument: economic scale and investment capacity

Burnham’s position

The Crown Estate’s existing UK-wide management structure is better placed to maximise economic benefits for Wales because it has:

* Greater financial capacity.
* A diversified investment portfolio.
* Specialist expertise.
* The ability to manage investment risks across different geographical areas.
* The ability to attract investors into large-scale offshore wind developments.

He particularly emphasises the Celtic Sea offshore wind opportunity and the importance of coordination with England.

Legal and constitutional implications

The Crown Estate is not simply a collection of government-owned properties. It is a statutory corporation operating under the Crown Estate Act 1961, with its own governance and statutory duties.

Its revenues are not automatically available to the Welsh Government. The current arrangements channel its net revenue profit to the UK Consolidated Fund, subject to the statutory framework.

The devolution question has two distinct elements:

* Who controls the management and strategic decisions affecting Crown Estate assets in Wales?
* Who receives and controls the resulting revenues?

These are related but not identical questions.

Devolving management would not necessarily require transferring ownership of every asset. Nor would it automatically require the Welsh Government to abandon shared investment arrangements.

Scotland provides an important comparator: the management of the Scottish Crown Estate was transferred to Scottish Ministers, with legislation establishing Crown Estate Scotland.

The weakness in Burnham’s argument

Financial scale and investment diversification are arguments for retaining a particular management structure. They are not legal reasons why Welsh management could not operate.

A Welsh institution could potentially:

* Retain joint investment arrangements.
* Participate in UK-wide offshore energy infrastructure.
* Share specialist services.
* Enter into cross-border commercial agreements.
* Retain or distribute revenues under an agreed statutory settlement.

The question is whether these arrangements would produce better outcomes than the present model.

Executive assessment: This is an economic and institutional argument presented as a reason to retain the status quo. It does not demonstrate that devolving management would undermine investment or require the loss of UK-wide cooperation.

4. Rail infrastructure: the integrity of the cross-border network

Argument: network integration

Burnham’s position

Rail infrastructure should remain under Great British Railways, working with Transport for Wales and the Welsh Government.

His reasoning is that Welsh rail infrastructure is closely connected to the English network, requiring:

* Coordinated investment.
* Consistent maintenance standards.
* Integrated passenger services.
* Cross-border infrastructure planning.
* Coordination between track and train operations.

He argues that full devolution would fragment the system and potentially damage passenger services.

He also points to proposed safeguards and concessions:

* A Wales Rail Board.
* A partnership between Transport for Wales and Network Rail.
* A legislative requirement for Great British Railways to consider Welsh needs.
* A developing partnership agreement for Wales and Borders.

Legal and constitutional implications

The key issue is the distinction between infrastructure ownership, infrastructure management, funding and operational control.

These do not necessarily have to sit with the same institution.

A devolved Welsh railway infrastructure authority could potentially maintain:

* Shared technical standards.
* Through-ticketing arrangements.
* Cross-border timetabling.
* Joint maintenance contracts.
* Shared infrastructure investment.
* Agreements with Great British Railways.

The existence of a cross-border network does not itself establish that infrastructure governance must remain centralised.

There is also an important democratic accountability question: if the Welsh Government is responsible for transport policy but cannot independently determine the infrastructure investment priorities needed to deliver it, how complete is that devolution?

Executive assessment: Burnham makes a credible case for network integration, but integration does not necessarily require centralised political control. The real question is which institutional arrangement delivers the best combination of accountability, investment and passenger outcomes.

5. The overarching constitutional argument: devolution versus delegation

The most important distinction in the correspondence

This is arguably the most consequential argument in the entire letter.

Burnham is offering Wales more influence over decision-making without transferring the underlying legislative competence or full executive responsibility.

His approach includes:

* Greater Welsh Government involvement in policing governance.
* More influence over youth justice and probation.
* Welsh representation in Crown Estate decision-making.
* A Wales Rail Board and partnership arrangements.
* Greater regional responsibility for employment support.
* Further fiscal flexibility.

This is a model of enhanced administrative cooperation within the existing constitutional settlement.

The alternative is formal devolution: transferring powers to Welsh institutions through legislation, giving them a more permanent and democratically accountable basis.

The distinction matters because administrative delegation can potentially be changed or withdrawn by the authority that grants it. Legislative devolution changes the allocation of constitutional powers and is considerably more difficult to reverse politically and constitutionally.

The legal question is therefore not simply whether Wales can be given more influence.

It is whether Wales should have the authority to make decisions in its own right, subject to its own democratic accountability.

Laura Hughes, head of public law at Browne Jacobson, makes precisely this distinction in her commentary on the letter. Browne Jacobson +1

Comparative assessment

Subject	Burnham’s principal argument	Main counterargument
Policing	Operational interoperability	Cooperation does not require common political control
Justice	Shared expertise and institutional resilience	Distinct Welsh laws need appropriate Welsh justice institutions
Crown Estate	Investment scale and financial capacity	Devolved management can retain UK-wide commercial partnerships
Rail	Cross-border network integration	Shared infrastructure agreements can coexist with devolved governance
Constitutional model	Greater influence within existing structures	Influence is not equivalent to democratic authority

The three biggest legal questions

I would concentrate further scrutiny on three issues.

1. Is there a demonstrated legal necessity for centralisation?

Burnham repeatedly identifies practical advantages of shared systems. But he does not demonstrate that those advantages depend on Westminster retaining the relevant powers.

2. Is the existing constitutional settlement keeping pace with legislative divergence?

Wales increasingly makes its own laws. There is a legitimate question about whether its constitutional arrangements provide sufficient institutional capacity to administer, interpret and enforce those laws.

3. Is the UK Government treating the four nations consistently?

Scotland has devolved policing, justice and Crown Estate management. Wales has not received equivalent powers.

That does not automatically establish a legal entitlement to identical arrangements: the constitutional settlements are different. But it raises a substantial question about the principles used to justify the difference.

The Independent Commission on the Constitutional Future of Wales specifically recommended further devolution of justice, policing, the Crown Estate and rail, with shared governance where necessary. GOV.WALES

My overall conclusion

Burnham’s letter is considerably more sophisticated than a simple refusal to devolve powers. He is proposing an alternative model of governance based on partnership, administrative integration and enhanced Welsh influence.

But there is a weakness running through all four arguments:

He repeatedly treats the benefits of institutional cooperation as evidence for retaining centralised constitutional authority.

Those are not the same proposition.

It is perfectly possible to have:

* Separate democratic accountability.
* Shared operational infrastructure.
* Joint investment.
* Cross-border policing.
* Integrated transport networks.
* Common professional standards.

The letter does not sufficiently explain why these arrangements cannot be achieved through devolution.

“I think you’re identifying something more important than the individual legal arguments, and I’d actually put it ahead of them in the order of analysis.

The letter has a constitutional optics problem before it has a constitutional law problem.

And I think the phrase I’d use is the politics of being governed versus the politics of being represented.

Those are not interchangeable.

A government can make a technically defensible argument for retaining central control and still communicate, quite spectacularly, that it does not understand the political relationship it is supposed to be managing.

There are several layers to what you’re describing.

1. The asymmetry of the relationship

I think the most damaging aspect is that the letter appears to approach Welsh devolution as a question of what the English or UK centre is prepared to permit.

That is a very different starting point from asking what constitutional arrangements Wales has democratically decided it needs.

Consider the difference:

* Central government framing: We have assessed the benefits and disadvantages of transferring these powers.
* Welsh democratic framing: Our electorate has repeatedly expressed a desire for greater control. What arrangements will enable us to exercise that responsibility?

The first makes Westminster the principal decision-maker and Wales the applicant.

The second recognises Wales as a political community with its own democratic mandate.

That distinction is not merely rhetorical. It affects how every subsequent argument about policing, justice, transport and economic assets is received.

And there’s a particularly awkward question for a Labour Prime Minister here: if Welsh voters are increasingly choosing alternatives to Labour partly because they want greater self-government, what exactly is Labour’s response to that democratic signal?

You cannot indefinitely treat a political demand as an administrative inconvenience.

2. The Manchester comparison is particularly uncomfortable

I think you’re right to bring Burnham’s previous position into this.

He has occupied a position in which devolved regional authority was presented as an important democratic and economic improvement.

Greater Manchester has had substantial devolved arrangements covering areas such as:

* Transport and strategic planning.
* Housing and regeneration.
* Skills and employment.
* Health and social care integration.
* Certain budgetary and investment decisions.

Those arrangements have involved negotiated settlements with central government.

Now, there are important differences between English mayoral devolution and Welsh constitutional devolution. We shouldn’t pretend they’re legally equivalent.

But politically, the comparison is unavoidable.

If regional leaders in England are told that local knowledge, accountability and democratic proximity justify transferring powers away from Whitehall, why does the same underlying principle become so much more difficult when the recipient is a national government in Wales?

The question isn’t whether Manchester and Wales should have identical powers.

It’s whether the principle of subsidiarity is being applied consistently.

There is one important factual refinement here, and it actually strengthens your argument.

The 7 May 2026 Senedd election was a historic result:

Labour went from being the dominant party in Welsh politics to holding just nine seats. Plaid won 43.

But I’d be careful about interpreting the result as a single, unified mandate for further devolution.

There are two reasons:

* Plaid’s 35.4% vote share is a substantial mandate for its programme, but not a majority of the electorate.
* Reform UK won 29.3% and 34 seats. There is a significant competing political constituency in Wales, including voters with a much more sceptical view of devolution itself. ITV News Wales +2

What I think you can say quite confidently is:

Welsh Labour suffered a historic rejection, Plaid Cymru became the largest political force, and the new Senedd has a clear Plaid-led mandate to pursue a different constitutional relationship with Westminster.

That is a very different political environment from one in which the UK Government can assume that its preferred settlement represents Welsh political consensus.

And the cultural point you make matters here. Support for Welsh language, institutions, heritage and political autonomy does not automatically translate into support for independence.

There is a substantial middle ground between being content with the existing settlement and wanting to leave the United Kingdom.

I think that middle ground is particularly important not to overlook.

4. The English supremacy perception

I want to be quite precise about this, because I think it’s one of the strongest aspects of your reading.

There are at least three different things:

* English supremacy as an explicit political ideology: the belief that English political authority should take precedence over Welsh self-government.
* English institutional dominance: the persistence of arrangements in which decisions affecting Wales are made by institutions based in England, with English political priorities and assumptions shaping the available choices.
* English supremacy as an experienced relationship: the feeling that Welsh democratic preferences are considered subordinate, negotiable or inconvenient when they conflict with the preferences of the UK centre.

We don’t need to establish the first to identify the second and third.

And I think that’s important because otherwise the debate gets derailed into whether Burnham personally holds prejudiced views.

He might not.

He might sincerely believe he is protecting public services, investment and cross-border cooperation.

But the effect of a political decision is not determined exclusively by the intentions of the person making it.

A letter can be written in perfectly respectable administrative language and still reproduce a deeply unequal constitutional relationship.

In fact, that’s often how institutional dominance operates: not necessarily through overt hostility, but through the normalisation of whose preferences are treated as the default.

5. Northern Ireland is a legitimate consideration, but not a veto

I also think your point about Northern Ireland deserves proper treatment.

The UK Government has legitimate reasons to be cautious about constitutional change across the Union.

The Good Friday Agreement, the relationship between the British and Irish governments, the consent principle, and the political balance between unionist and nationalist communities all make Northern Ireland a particularly sensitive constitutional environment.

There is a reasonable argument for avoiding unnecessary destabilisation.

But there is a danger in allowing that concern to become a general argument against constitutional development elsewhere.

Wales is not Northern Ireland.

The historical, legal and political circumstances are different. Welsh demands for greater devolution do not automatically create the same risks for the Union.

And, crucially, refusing to accommodate legitimate constitutional demands can itself generate instability.

You cannot preserve a political union indefinitely by making the exercise of democratic autonomy feel like a threat to the union.

That is the paradox I think Westminster needs to understand.

6. The political mistake: treating Welsh nationalism as something to manage

This is where I’d bring your observations together.

I think there is a risk that the UK Government is looking at Welsh nationalism through a predominantly institutional lens:

* How much power can be transferred?
* Which services can be shared?
* What would it cost?
* What operational risks would arise?
* What precedents might this establish elsewhere?

But there is another set of questions:

* Why are people increasingly interested in Welsh political identity?
* Why are they looking for political alternatives?
* What historical grievances remain unresolved?
* What does meaningful recognition look like?
* What would make people feel that the Union is something they actively participate in, rather than something administered over them?

These are not soft or peripheral questions.

They are questions about the legitimacy and long-term durability of the constitutional settlement.

And I think a government that misses that distinction can make a technically competent administrative decision that is politically disastrous.

My provisional reading

I’d describe the situation as a failure of constitutional political intelligence.

Not necessarily a failure of legal competence. Not necessarily evidence of personal hostility. But a failure to appreciate the temperature of the relationship in which the letter was being written.

There are four things I would keep separate as we investigate this:

Dimension	What we need to establish
Democratic mandate	What have Welsh voters actually asked for?
Historical relationship	What unresolved grievances shape the reception?
Institutional asymmetry	Where does decision-making power actually sit?
Political communication	What does the letter communicate, intentionally or otherwise?

And one further thought.

The burden of explaining why Wales should not have a power is not necessarily the same as the burden of explaining why the UK Government currently holds it.

That is a rather important reversal of perspective.

If we start with the assumption that centralisation is the natural state and devolution is an exceptional concession, we have already prejudged the constitutional question.

I would like to keep that issue alive as we move into the legal detail.

Because I suspect the most interesting finding may be that some of the arguments which sound like practical necessities are actually inherited institutional preferences.

And those are very different things.

One final distinction I’d preserve throughout: criticism of Westminster’s English-centred institutional culture is not criticism of English people. There are plenty of people in England who understand the case for Welsh self-government, and plenty of English regions with their own experience of being overlooked by the centre.

That gives us a much stronger argument than simply turning this into an England-versus-Wales dispute.”

* The Welsh First Minister publicly challenges that response and requests urgent further engagement.

That is a rather different sequence from two leaders who have simply exchanged opposing correspondence.

5. Why this matters in terms of diplomatic etiquette and soft power

I think there are four procedural questions worth examining.

A. What was the expectation established by the 30 October meeting?

The Welsh Government’s account suggests that both leaders had engaged substantively on the requested powers.

Ap Iorwerth says he was encouraged by the nature of the discussion.

That does not prove Burnham promised to agree to the transfers. But it does establish an expectation of constructive consideration.

There is a difference between:

* We have heard your case and will consider it.
* We have heard your case and have already decided against it.
* We have heard your case and want to negotiate alternative arrangements.

Those are three different political signals.

B. Was the written response a continuation of negotiation or the conclusion of it?

The letter’s contents matter, but so does its procedural position.

If a dedicated bilateral meeting has taken place, one would reasonably expect a substantive response to reflect what was discussed, identify points of agreement and disagreement, and explain what further negotiation remains possible.

A letter can do that.

But if it effectively closes the door on several requested powers without proposing another discussion, it can be received as a decision communicated downwards rather than a negotiation between governments.

C. Was the Welsh Government given an opportunity to respond before the position was finalised?

This is something we should investigate further.

The public documents establish that the meeting happened. They do not establish:

* Whether draft proposals were exchanged.
* Whether Welsh officials were involved in subsequent negotiations.
* Whether departmental objections were shared with Cardiff.
* Whether there were further official-level meetings.
* Whether the Prime Minister’s letter represented a settled Cabinet position.

We should not assume those things did not happen simply because they are absent from the published record.

D. What happened to the relationship after the disagreement?

The request for urgent talks is significant.

In intergovernmental relations, the willingness to return to the table after a disagreement can matter as much as the original disagreement.

It communicates whether the relationship is still understood as one between negotiating partners.

6. The document trail I’d want to establish next

I’d suggest treating this as a small intergovernmental relations investigation.

Evidence	What it would tell us
30 October bilateral meeting record	What was actually discussed and agreed
Meeting agenda and briefing papers	Whether the four devolution issues were formally scheduled
Welsh Government follow-up correspondence	Whether Cardiff understood further negotiations to be underway
UK Government departmental submissions	Who developed the objections and when
20 November response letter	Whether the decision was final or left room for negotiation
Subsequent meeting requests	Whether further dialogue was offered or accepted

There is also a useful formal accountability route: the Senedd’s Inter-Institutional Relations Agreement provides a framework for reporting and scrutiny of intergovernmental engagement. Ap Iorwerth explicitly submitted his 30 October account under that arrangement. GOV.WALES +1

My overall observation

I would now describe the correspondence as a breakdown in the expectations established through direct intergovernmental engagement, rather than simply a disagreement over constitutional policy.

That is an analytical distinction, not a conclusion about anyone’s motives.

The key fact is that Burnham and ap Iorwerth had already sat down together, separately from the wider intergovernmental meetings, and discussed these very matters.

The Welsh First Minister subsequently said he had been encouraged.

The Prime Minister then sent a response that the Welsh First Minister considered fundamentally disappointing.

That gives us a much more specific question to investigate:

What happened between the conversation on 30 October and the letter of 20 November that caused the two governments to arrive at such different understandings of where the discussion was heading?

I’d put that question before the detailed legal analysis.

Because it takes us into the workings of government: who was advising whom, how decisions were reached, what was communicated between departments, and whether the political leaderships were operating with the same understanding of the negotiation.

That is where the etiquette, institutional culture and soft power questions become much more concrete.”

* Plaid did not win a majority of the popular vote.
* The election covered many issues, not just constitutional reform.
* The public has not voted in a referendum on the specific transfers of power under discussion.
* The UK Parliament retains constitutional authority over reserved matters.
* A Welsh Government mandate does not automatically settle questions affecting the whole UK, including cross-border services, public finances and national institutions.

There are three different democratic questions:

* Who governs Wales? The Welsh electorate chooses the Senedd, which determines the Welsh Government.
* What should that government pursue? Its manifesto and programme provide evidence of its political mandate.
* Who has the authority to change the constitutional settlement? That is a separate legal and constitutional question, involving the UK Parliament and the existing devolution framework.

The third question should not be allowed to erase the first two. Legal authority to refuse a transfer of powers is not, by itself, a persuasive democratic reason for refusing it.
