# 🐼 Policing And Public Order  
**First created:** 2026-07-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-18  
*How police receive, classify, share, and operationalise information about Palestine-related protest—and how policing changes when risk itself becomes a contested product of the information environment.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Police do not encounter a protest as a blank slate.

Before an officer meets a marcher, steward, organiser, journalist, legal observer, or passer-by, information may already have passed through:

- media reporting;
- political statements;
- community briefings;
- intelligence systems;
- previous police operations;
- online monitoring;
- complaints;
- partner agencies;
- local authorities;
- universities;
- employers;
- private security;
- specialist policing networks;
- and other police forces.

That information may influence how police understand:

- the event;
- the people attending it;
- the likelihood of disorder;
- the severity of possible harm;
- the meaning of slogans or symbols;
- the relevance of terrorism legislation;
- and the level of intervention considered proportionate.

This node therefore examines policing as both:

- a public-order institution;
- and an information-processing institution.

It asks:

- What entered the police system as information?
- Who supplied it?
- How was its reliability assessed?
- How did it travel?
- Which legal and policy categories were applied?
- What assumptions entered the risk assessment?
- What action followed?
- And what happened when police action itself became new evidence in the public narrative?

The central chain is:

**information → evaluation → intelligence → risk classification → legal and policy framework → operational options → police action → new information**

Every stage can introduce:

- accuracy;
- caution;
- professional judgment;
- bias;
- misunderstanding;
- political pressure;
- or deliberate manipulation.

## 🧭 Who This Note Is For

This note is not written only for people who already believe that Palestine-related protest has been over-policed.

It is also for readers who ask:

- Why did police treat particular protests as risky?
- Why were some slogans or symbols interpreted as potential offences?
- Why did different forces act differently?
- Why did counterterrorism language enter ordinary protest?
- Why were arrests made when charges did not always follow?
- Why did police appear to respond to media or political controversy?
- Why do some protesters now treat policing as part of the original Palestine grievance?
- And what should police do when information about a protest may itself be manipulated?

The purpose is not to assume that every police intervention was unlawful.

Police may face genuine risks involving:

- violence;
- threats;
- harassment;
- antisemitism;
- Islamophobia;
- intimidation;
- criminal damage;
- incitement;
- proscribed organisations;
- public disorder;
- and danger to participants or the wider public.

The issue is how those risks are identified, evidenced, classified, and acted upon.

A democratic police service requires more than lawful powers.

It also requires that the information used to justify those powers is:

- sufficiently reliable;
- properly contextualised;
- open to correction;
- proportionately interpreted;
- and capable of later review.

## 🐼 Policing Is An Information System

Police action begins before the moment of enforcement.

Information may influence:

- whether an event receives specialist monitoring;
- whether organisers are contacted;
- how many officers are deployed;
- whether public-order specialists attend;
- which briefings officers receive;
- whether counterterrorism teams are consulted;
- what conduct is expected;
- and which people or groups are treated as likely sources of risk.

The police role is therefore not merely reactive.

Police:

- receive information;
- record it;
- assess it;
- compare it with existing holdings;
- share it;
- turn it into operational intelligence;
- and use it to select interventions.

This creates responsibility at every stage.

A lawful arrest may still rest on:

- incomplete context;
- a misunderstood slogan;
- duplicated reporting;
- unreliable intelligence;
- an outdated association;
- or a risk assessment shaped by public pressure rather than evidence.

Conversely, imperfect information does not mean police can wait for certainty before acting where serious harm may be imminent.

The difficult question is how institutions make defensible decisions under uncertainty without converting uncertainty into permanent suspicion.

## 📥 How Information Enters Policing

Police information may come from:

- officers;
- victims;
- witnesses;
- suspects;
- members of the public;
- event organisers;
- community representatives;
- local authorities;
- transport operators;
- universities;
- schools;
- employers;
- charities;
- faith organisations;
- private security firms;
- journalists;
- social-media platforms;
- open-source research;
- other forces;
- intelligence agencies;
- foreign law-enforcement partners;
- and government departments.

These sources do not have equal knowledge, independence, or reliability.

Information may be:

- observed directly;
- reported second-hand;
- inferred from association;
- copied from media reporting;
- supplied by a campaign group;
- extracted from an edited video;
- generated through automated monitoring;
- or repeated from another intelligence product.

A large number of reports does not necessarily mean that a claim has been independently corroborated.

Ten reports may all originate from:

- one video;
- one anonymous account;
- one political briefing;
- one misidentified person;
- or one inaccurate media report.

The number of times information appears can create an impression of certainty that exceeds the quality of its original source.

## 🧪 From Information To Intelligence

Raw information does not become reliable simply because it enters a police system.

It should be assessed for matters including:

- provenance;
- source reliability;
- directness of knowledge;
- accuracy;
- relevance;
- corroboration;
- age;
- context;
- handling restrictions;
- and the lawful purpose for retaining or sharing it.

The distinction between information and intelligence matters.

Information is what has been received.

Intelligence is information that has been:

- evaluated;
- contextualised;
- connected to a policing purpose;
- and interpreted as capable of supporting a decision.

That process is not neutral or mechanical.

It requires judgment.

Questions include:

- Is the source in a position to know?
- Is the source politically or personally interested?
- Has the information been edited?
- Is the surrounding context available?
- Does another record genuinely corroborate it?
- Could two reports share the same original source?
- Is the information current?
- Has it previously been corrected?
- Does it identify a person accurately?
- Does it distinguish lawful association from criminal involvement?

In an information-war environment, the evaluation stage becomes as important as the eventual legal decision.

“The police” are not one undifferentiated organisation.

Policing may involve:

- territorial forces;
- specialist public-order teams;
- regional structures;
- counterterrorism policing;
- national policing bodies;
- the National Police Chiefs’ Council;
- the College of Policing;
- the National Crime Agency;
- intelligence agencies;
- local resilience structures;
- transport police;
- local authorities;
- government departments;
- prosecutors;
- regulators;
- and international partners.

Information can travel through:

- intelligence reports;
- operational briefings;
- watchlists;
- tasking processes;
- referrals;
- liaison officers;
- joint operations;
- national assessments;
- regional coordination;
- and informal professional contact.

This can improve safety.

One force may know that:

- a person has previously used violence;
- a planned event has attracted credible threats;
- a group is attempting to provoke disorder;
- or a particular online claim is fabricated.

But sharing can also reproduce error.

An inaccurate or weakly sourced assessment may become more authoritative each time it is repeated through another system.

Once one force has recorded a person as:

- extremist;
- associated with terrorism;
- violent;
- high risk;
- vulnerable to radicalisation;
- or connected to disorder,

another force may receive the category without the full evidentiary history behind it.

The label travels more easily than the uncertainty.

## 🗂️ Local Knowledge And National Framing

Local police may have:

- relationships with organisers;
- knowledge of previous demonstrations;
- awareness of community tensions;
- familiarity with local venues;
- and a more detailed understanding of the people involved.

National structures may provide:

- broader threat intelligence;
- specialist expertise;
- cross-force comparison;
- legal guidance;
- and knowledge of coordinated activity.

Both forms of knowledge are necessary.

Both can also distort the other.

National framing may cause local officers to treat a familiar group as newly dangerous.

Local familiarity may cause a force to underestimate wider coordination or genuine escalation.

The relevant question is not whether local or national knowledge should dominate.

It is whether each claim can be:

- traced;
- explained;
- challenged;
- updated;
- and assessed at the correct level.

## ⚖️ Law, Policy, Ethics, And Discretion

Police action is shaped by more than statute.

It emerges through the interaction of:

- legislation;
- case law;
- human-rights obligations;
- operational policy;
- professional guidance;
- local priorities;
- national-security framing;
- political pressure;
- resources;
- officer discretion;
- intelligence quality;
- and perceived reputational risk.

An officer may possess a legal power without being required to use it.

The existence of a power does not establish that its use is:

- necessary;
- proportionate;
- suitable;
- or the least harmful available option.

A defensible police decision should consider:

1. the information available;
2. the threat, harm, and risk;
3. the applicable powers and policy;
4. the available operational options;
5. the likely consequences of action and inaction;
6. the ethical position;
7. and the need to review the decision as circumstances change.

This means scrutiny should not stop at:

**Did the police have a power?**

It should also ask:

- Why was that power selected?
- What alternatives were considered?
- What harm was being prevented?
- How likely was that harm?
- Was the severity of a possible outcome mistaken for evidence that it was likely?
- Was the decision recorded?
- Was it reconsidered when new information appeared?
- Did policy replace individual legal judgment?

## 📊 How Threat, Harm, And Risk Are Constructed

Risk is not a physical object waiting to be discovered.

It is an assessment.

Police may consider:

- likelihood;
- possible severity;
- vulnerability;
- imminence;
- available capability;
- intent;
- previous conduct;
- location;
- crowd density;
- community tension;
- symbolic targets;
- and consequences of failing to act.

These are legitimate considerations.

But risk can become inflated where:

- worst-case scenarios dominate;
- political embarrassment is treated as public harm;
- lawful disruption is confused with violence;
- slogans are assessed without context;
- association is treated as intent;
- hostile media coverage is treated as evidence;
- or uncertainty is consistently resolved against the protester.

A small likelihood of severe harm may justify preparation.

It does not automatically justify treating every participant as though that harm is already likely.

Risk assessment should distinguish:

- possibility from probability;
- capability from intent;
- rhetoric from action;
- association from participation;
- disruption from violence;
- and unpopular speech from unlawful speech.

## 🚨 Public Order And The Expanding Meaning Of Harm

Public-order policing requires police to balance:

- the right to protest;
- freedom of expression;
- freedom of assembly;
- public safety;
- access to transport and services;
- prevention of crime;
- and protection of other people’s rights.

Conflict often arises because the word **harm** may include very different things.

Potential harms can include:

- physical injury;
- threats;
- harassment;
- fear;
- damage to property;
- disruption;
- obstruction;
- distress;
- hostile environments;
- reputational damage;
- commercial loss;
- and political embarrassment.

These harms do not all carry the same legal or moral weight.

A protest may be:

- disruptive without being violent;
- distressing without being threatening;
- offensive without being criminal;
- unlawful without being dangerous;
- or politically confrontational without amounting to intimidation.

Where these categories are blurred, police may be expected to resolve political and moral disputes through enforcement.

That places officers in an impossible role.

It also allows government and other institutions to avoid clarifying what conduct society has actually decided to criminalise.

## ☄️ Public Order And Counterterrorism Mission Creep

Palestine-related protest may involve genuine questions concerning:

- proscribed organisations;
- encouragement of terrorism;
- terrorist publications;
- fundraising;
- incitement;
- and support for political violence.

But counterterrorism language can spread beyond those specific legal questions.

Ordinary protest may become framed through:

- extremism;
- radicalisation;
- security risk;
- hostile-state influence;
- ideological vulnerability;
- or terrorist sympathy.

This can change the entire operational environment.

Once a matter is treated as counterterrorism-related:

- information-sharing expands;
- secrecy increases;
- reputational consequences grow;
- specialist units become involved;
- ordinary associations may appear suspicious;
- and correction becomes harder.

A counterterrorism referral does not itself establish terrorist conduct.

An intelligence interest does not establish criminal guilt.

A security classification should not be reported as though it were a judicial finding.

The node should ask:

- What legal threshold was actually met?
- What was only suspected?
- Which power was used?
- What finding followed?
- And what records remained after the concern was not substantiated?

## 🗞️ Media Narratives As Operational Context

The media does not formally command police operations.

It can still shape the environment in which decisions are made.

Media narratives can affect:

- public concern;
- political pressure;
- community-tension assessments;
- expectations of disorder;
- force reputation;
- ministerial demands;
- and the perceived cost of under-reacting.

Repeated descriptions such as:

- hate march;
- extremist crowd;
- terrorist sympathiser;
- dangerous slogan;
- imported conflict;
- volatile protest;
- no-go area;
- or threat to community safety

may become part of the surrounding operational context before specific unlawful conduct is established.

This does not mean police simply follow newspapers.

The relationship is subtler.

Media reporting may:

1. amplify an allegation;
2. produce public complaints;
3. trigger political comment;
4. increase pressure on senior officers;
5. enter community-tension reporting;
6. influence pre-event planning;
7. and shape how ambiguous conduct is interpreted.

Police action can then generate a new headline that appears to confirm the original media claim.

## 📱 Open-Source Intelligence And Platform Pressure

Online material can be valuable.

It may reveal:

- credible threats;
- plans for violence;
- routes;
- mobilisation;
- weapons;
- targets;
- coordinated harassment;
- or attempts to provoke conflict.

But online information is unusually vulnerable to manipulation.

Potential problems include:

- edited clips;
- missing context;
- parody treated as literal;
- false accounts;
- impersonation;
- automated amplification;
- recycled images;
- mistranslation;
- false localisation;
- fabricated affiliations;
- coordinated reporting;
- synthetic audio;
- synthetic video;
- and strategic leaking.

Social-media popularity is not the same as evidential reliability.

A claim may trend because it is:

- shocking;
- politically useful;
- emotionally optimised;
- promoted by bots;
- or repeatedly shared by people drawing from one unverified source.

Police need capacity not only to collect open-source material, but to assess:

- authenticity;
- origin;
- chronology;
- account control;
- editing;
- translation;
- automation;
- and deliberate adversarial intent.

## 🌐 Policing In The Information-War Age

The information-war environment changes the police role.

Police are no longer asked only to decide whether conduct in front of them is unlawful.

They may also need to determine whether the information creating the apparent threat was:

- authentic;
- manipulated;
- coordinated;
- commercially promoted;
- politically planted;
- algorithmically amplified;
- or generated by an adversarial actor.

An information operation may seek to:

- make a peaceful group appear violent;
- provoke police overreaction;
- generate communal fear;
- discredit a force;
- create evidence of state repression;
- distract from another event;
- intensify antisemitism;
- intensify anti-Muslim racism;
- or manufacture the appearance of public consensus.

Police can become a target of the operation.

They can also become its delivery mechanism.

An adversary may not need to convince the whole public.

It may only need to get a distorted claim:

1. reported;
2. recorded;
3. shared;
4. attached to a person or organisation;
5. treated as credible;
6. and converted into police intervention.

Once police act, that intervention may be cited as apparent confirmation of the original allegation.

## 🪞 The Arrest-Confirms-The-Allegation Loop

Police action is frequently interpreted as evidence of guilt.

The reasoning may become:

- They were arrested, so they must have done something serious.
- Counterterrorism police were involved, so there must have been a terrorism concern.
- Conditions were imposed, so the protest must have been dangerous.
- Devices were seized, so investigators must have found a substantial risk.
- A person was interviewed, so the allegation must have had merit.

This is not how legal proof works.

Police may act on:

- suspicion;
- incomplete information;
- an urgent safeguarding concern;
- a need to secure evidence;
- or a precautionary assessment.

The matter may later end with:

- no further action;
- no charge;
- withdrawn conditions;
- an acquittal;
- a corrected record;
- or recognition that the original information was wrong.

But the public meaning of the arrest may remain.

This creates a confirmation loop:

1. an allegation produces police action;
2. police action is treated as evidence supporting the allegation;
3. that apparent evidence produces further reporting and intelligence;
4. the person or group becomes more strongly associated with risk;
5. and future police decisions begin from the contaminated record.

Correction often travels less effectively than suspicion.

## 🧾 Arrest Without Charge Still Produces Consequences

An arrest is not a neutral holding event.

Even where no charge follows, a person may experience:

- detention;
- searches;
- device seizure;
- disruption to medication or care;
- cancelled work;
- lost income;
- professional disclosure;
- family distress;
- media exposure;
- immigration consequences;
- bail restrictions;
- exclusion zones;
- reputational damage;
- and lasting fear of police contact.

Observers may also change their behaviour.

They may conclude that:

- a slogan is unsafe;
- a march is monitored;
- contact with a named person is risky;
- professional participation is dangerous;
- or lawful protest can produce consequences without conviction.

This may suppress visibility.

It does not necessarily change belief.

It may instead strengthen the view that ordinary democratic participation is no longer sufficient.

## 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Community Intelligence And Unequal Credibility

Police rely on communities for information.

Community knowledge can help identify:

- genuine threats;
- tensions;
- vulnerable locations;
- planned violence;
- targeted harassment;
- and attempts to provoke disorder.

But “the community” is not one voice.

Within Jewish, Palestinian, Muslim, Arab, Black, Christian, secular, student, labour, and local populations there may be:

- political disagreement;
- generational difference;
- unequal access;
- institutional competition;
- and varying relationships with police.

Police may hear most frequently from organisations that are:

- well funded;
- formally recognised;
- professionally staffed;
- politically connected;
- or already trusted by public institutions.

People with less access may include:

- migrants;
- precarious workers;
- young people;
- dissident community members;
- disabled people;
- undocumented people;
- and those with prior negative experiences of policing.

The most institutionally legible voice is not necessarily the most representative.

Police should ask:

- Who is speaking?
- Who do they claim to represent?
- Who disagrees?
- Who is absent?
- Whose fear is treated as authoritative?
- Whose fear is treated as political performance?
- And which communities bear the operational consequences?

## 🕯️ Antisemitism, Islamophobia, And Conflicting Risk Narratives

Police may receive competing claims that:

- Jewish people are being threatened;
- Palestinian speech is being suppressed;
- Muslim communities are being collectively suspected;
- protest slogans are antisemitic;
- accusations of antisemitism are being used strategically;
- political speech is being interpreted as terrorist support;
- or genuine hate speech is being minimised as activism.

These claims cannot be resolved through automatic alignment with one side.

Police must distinguish:

- Jewish people from Israeli government policy;
- criticism from collective blame;
- Palestinian identity from terrorism;
- religious speech from incitement;
- political anger from threat;
- and subjective offence from criminal conduct.

Genuine antisemitism should be identified and addressed.

So should:

- Islamophobia;
- anti-Palestinian racism;
- anti-Arab racism;
- threats;
- harassment;
- and collective suspicion.

The existence of bad-faith allegations does not mean all allegations are false.

The existence of real antisemitism does not make all Palestine-related speech antisemitic.

Evidence and context remain necessary.

## 🏢 Legal Form, Governance, And Accountability

Police forces should not be described simply as ordinary private limited companies.

In England and Wales, policing is divided between legally constituted office-holders and organisations with distinct functions.

Relevant actors may include:

- chief constables;
- Police and Crime Commissioners;
- mayoral policing bodies;
- police authorities in other UK jurisdictions;
- the Home Office;
- inspectorates;
- misconduct bodies;
- prosecutors;
- courts;
- and local scrutiny structures.

The legal form matters because it affects:

- employment;
- contracts;
- property;
- procurement;
- liability;
- litigation;
- data responsibility;
- operational control;
- and democratic accountability.

Operational independence does not mean absence of governance.

Political oversight does not mean that elected officials may direct individual investigations or arrests.

The node should distinguish:

- operational decision making;
- strategic priority setting;
- budgetary control;
- procurement;
- appointment and dismissal powers;
- complaints;
- inspection;
- and legal responsibility.

## 🏛️ Political Direction And Operational Independence

Police are expected to exercise operational judgment independently.

They still operate within a political environment.

Government and elected institutions may influence policing through:

- legislation;
- statutory guidance;
- budgets;
- national priorities;
- public statements;
- security classifications;
- appointments;
- performance expectations;
- and demands for visible enforcement.

Senior police leaders may also respond to:

- public confidence;
- reputational pressure;
- ministerial criticism;
- media campaigns;
- and fear of being blamed for failing to prevent disorder.

The existence of political pressure does not prove that an operational decision was politically directed.

The question is how pressure entered the environment.

Reporting should distinguish:

- a minister changing the law;
- a minister publicly calling for action;
- police interpreting the law;
- operational command selecting tactics;
- and an individual officer applying a power.

Without that separation, responsibility becomes either falsely concentrated or conveniently dispersed.

## 💼 Procurement And Technological Dependence

Modern policing increasingly depends on external technology.

This may include:

- communications systems;
- intelligence databases;
- cloud infrastructure;
- analytics;
- facial recognition;
- body-worn video;
- digital forensics;
- social-media monitoring;
- translation;
- identity resolution;
- automated triage;
- and risk-analysis tools.

Technology suppliers can influence what police are able to see and how information is organised.

Questions include:

- Who built the system?
- What assumptions are embedded in it?
- What training data was used?
- Which categories does it generate?
- Can officers understand how an output was produced?
- Can the data be corrected?
- What contracts govern access?
- Can suppliers reuse information?
- Are foreign jurisdictions involved?
- What happens when a system is discontinued?
- Who audits bias, error, and security?

A technical output should not be treated as objective simply because it was produced automatically.

Automation can scale:

- accuracy;
- consistency;
- or error.

## 🧠 Automated Risk And The Loss Of Context

Automated systems may assist with:

- prioritising reports;
- identifying links;
- detecting patterns;
- matching faces;
- translating text;
- ranking risk;
- or directing analyst attention.

But automated systems may struggle with:

- irony;
- political language;
- reclaimed slurs;
- religious references;
- multilingual context;
- historical slogans;
- satire;
- dialect;
- and rapidly changing events.

A model may detect association without understanding meaning.

It may amplify historical bias already present in police data.

If previous policing disproportionately targeted one community, a system trained on that data may interpret the resulting record as evidence that the community presents greater risk.

The system then reproduces the original policing pattern as apparently neutral prediction.

Human review is necessary.

Human review is not sufficient where officers are trained to over-trust technological output.

## 🧾 Recording, Retention, Sharing, And Correction

Police information may continue to affect a person long after the original event.

Records can influence:

- later stop and search;
- risk assessments;
- safeguarding decisions;
- licensing;
- employment checks;
- future protest planning;
- border encounters;
- inter-force intelligence;
- and how later allegations are interpreted.

Important questions include:

- Why was the information recorded?
- What legal basis supported retention?
- Was it intelligence, allegation, evidence, or finding?
- Was uncertainty preserved?
- Was the source grading retained?
- Who received it?
- When should it be reviewed?
- How can the person challenge it?
- What happens when no charge follows?
- Does correction travel to every recipient?

Deletion alone may not undo the effect where information has already been:

- copied;
- summarised;
- disclosed;
- relied upon;
- or converted into a new assessment.

Correction must follow the information chain.

## 🧬 Category Drift

A recurring risk is category drift.

A person may begin as:

- an attendee;
- a witness;
- a complainant;
- a legal observer;
- a journalist;
- a medic;
- a community contact;
- or a person mentioned by somebody else.

Through repeated recording, they may become described as:

- associated;
- involved;
- linked;
- known;
- of interest;
- concerning;
- or high risk.

Each word may appear cautious.

Together they can change how later officers perceive the person.

Category drift may occur without:

- a formal accusation;
- a charge;
- a hearing;
- or an opportunity to respond.

The absence of procedural challenge makes careful recording essential.

Police systems should distinguish clearly between:

- observed conduct;
- allegation;
- association;
- analyst inference;
- intelligence assessment;
- criminal finding;
- and information later shown to be inaccurate.

## ♿ Accessibility, Vulnerability, And Operational Reality

Public-order tactics do not affect everyone equally.

Police planning should account for:

- disability;
- mobility;
- sensory impairment;
- medication;
- pregnancy;
- age;
- trauma;
- communication needs;
- religious practice;
- access to toilets;
- weather;
- crowd density;
- and ability to leave an area.

A tactic that appears proportionate for a mobile adult may create serious risk for someone who:

- cannot stand for long periods;
- requires timed medication;
- cannot safely tolerate restraint;
- uses mobility equipment;
- has difficulty hearing instructions;
- cannot access transport independently;
- or becomes separated from a carer.

Vulnerability should not automatically be treated as incapacity or grounds for exclusion.

It should shape operational planning and the proportionality of intervention.

## 📰 Protest Policing Without The Protest Cause

Reporting often treats police activity as the central event.

Coverage may focus on:

- officer numbers;
- arrests;
- conditions;
- confrontation;
- road closures;
- slogans;
- and isolated incidents.

The reason for the protest becomes background.

This can create a circular narrative:

1. the protest is reported as a public-order problem;
2. police prepare for a public-order problem;
3. visible policing confirms that the event is dangerous;
4. confrontation becomes more likely;
5. and the original political cause receives even less attention.

The relevant reporting question is not only:

**What did police do?**

It is also:

**What unresolved issue brought people into contact with police in the first place?**

## 🌱 What Policing Produces

Policing does not only manage an event.

It can produce:

- new intelligence;
- new records;
- new political identities;
- new grievances;
- new litigation;
- new media narratives;
- new alliances;
- and new forms of distrust.

A person may arrive at a protest because of:

- civilian harm;
- international law;
- family connection;
- humanitarian concern;
- or arms exports.

They may remain involved because of:

- an arrest;
- treatment in custody;
- an inaccurate record;
- device seizure;
- police surveillance;
- a prosecution;

Policing can therefore become another root in the rhizome.

This does not mean every police action expands the movement.

Some interventions prevent harm.

Some increase safety.

Some protect protesters from attack.

The analytical question is:

- What did the intervention prevent?
- What did it create?
- And which consequences were anticipated?

## 🧭 Democratic Policing In The Information-War Age

Policing by consent requires more than public approval of every decision.

It requires a broadly credible belief that police powers are exercised through:

- law;
- evidence;
- proportionality;
- ethical judgment;
- correction;
- and public accountability.

That model is strained where the information environment is:

- fragmented;
- manipulated;
- automated;
- commercially controlled;
- politically polarised;
- or partly classified.

Police cannot solve this alone.

The problem also belongs to:

- legislators;
- government;
- technology companies;
- media organisations;
- prosecutors;
- courts;
- regulators;
- and the public.

But police remain the point at which information can become coercive state action.

That gives policing a distinctive responsibility.

Before information becomes:

- detention;
- search;
- surveillance;
- restriction;
- seizure;
- or force,

its provenance and meaning must be treated as part of the legal and ethical decision.

## 🗞️ Reporting Questions

### Information

- What information triggered police interest?
- Who supplied it?
- Was the source in a position to know?
- Was the material firsthand, indirect, inferred, or anonymous?
- Was the original source preserved?
- Did several reports share one origin?
- Was the information current and complete?

### Intelligence

- How was reliability assessed?
- What corroboration existed?
- Was uncertainty retained in the record?
- Was association distinguished from conduct?
- Was the information later corrected?
- Did the correction travel to every recipient?

### Risk

- What harm was anticipated?
- How likely was it?
- How severe was it?
- Was possibility confused with probability?
- Was reputational or political harm treated as public danger?
- Were vulnerability and accessibility considered?
- Was the risk reassessed as circumstances changed?

### Law And Policy

- Which legal power was used?
- What threshold applied?
- Was the decision necessary and proportionate?
- What alternatives existed?
- Did policy become a substitute for legal judgment?
- Was the rationale recorded?
- Could the decision be reviewed?

### Force-To-Force Sharing

- Which forces or agencies received the information?
- What category or label travelled with it?
- Did recipients receive the underlying evidence?
- Could local knowledge challenge national framing?
- Was an unproven assessment treated as an established fact?
- Who was responsible for correction?

### Public Order

- What conduct was violent?
- What conduct was disruptive but non-violent?
- What conduct was offensive but lawful?
- What conduct was alleged to amount to intimidation or incitement?
- Were those categories clearly distinguished?
- Did restrictions protect safety or primarily reduce visibility?

### Counterterrorism

- Why was counterterrorism involved?
- Was a referral treated publicly as proof?
- What legal threshold was actually met?
- Did the case produce a charge or finding?
- What information remained after the matter ended?
- Did counterterrorism framing alter later treatment?

### Media

- Did media reporting precede the police assessment?
- Was the reporting independently verified?
- Did political commentary increase pressure for enforcement?
- Did police action then appear to validate the original narrative?
- Was the final outcome reported as prominently as the arrest?

### Technology

- Which systems processed the information?
- Who supplied them?
- What categories or risk scores did they produce?
- Could officers explain the output?
- Was human review meaningful?
- Could the person challenge automated inference?
- Did procurement or technological dependence limit accountability?

### Information Warfare

- Could the information have been manipulated?
- Was synthetic or edited media considered?
- Did coordinated reporting create false corroboration?
- Did bot amplification affect perceptions of public concern?
- Was a foreign, commercial, factional, or extremist actor attempting to provoke police action?
- Did police intervention become part of the information operation?

### Consequences

- Was anyone arrested?
- Were charges brought?
- Were conditions imposed or withdrawn?
- Were devices seized?
- Was income or employment affected?
- Did the person receive a corrected record?
- Did the intervention deter harm, deepen distrust, or create a new grievance?

## 🧿 Editorial Guardrails

Do not describe all police forces, counterterrorism units, intelligence agencies, prosecutors, and government departments as one coordinated institution.

Do not assume that information-sharing proves common intent.

Do not treat an intelligence record as a finding of fact.

Do not treat an arrest as proof of guilt.

Do not treat a counterterrorism referral as proof of terrorism.

Do not treat the absence of charge as proof that police acted in bad faith.

Distinguish:

- information;
- intelligence;
- suspicion;
- risk assessment;
- arrest threshold;
- charging threshold;
- prosecution;
- and finding.

Do not describe police forces as ordinary private limited companies.

Explain the relevant legal and governance structure accurately.

Do not assume that political pressure directly caused a specific operational decision without evidence.

Trace:

- law;
- policy;
- public statements;
- command decisions;
- intelligence;
- and officer discretion separately.

Do not assume that media influence is direct command.

Examine how narrative becomes:

- complaints;
- community-tension reporting;
- political pressure;
- intelligence context;
- and operational expectation.

Do not romanticise every protester.

Genuine:

- antisemitism;
- Islamophobia;
- threats;
- harassment;
- intimidation;
- violence;
- criminal damage;
- incitement;
- and support for proscribed organisations

should be assessed seriously.

Do not collapse:

- disruption;
- offence;
- intimidation;
- criminality;
- and violence

into one category.

Do not treat technological output as inherently neutral.

Do not treat all error as algorithmic where human judgment made the decision.

Do not treat all error as individual where systems, policy, procurement, and organisational culture shaped the outcome.

Do not make Palestinians disappear beneath a discussion of British policing.

The relevant domestic process remains connected to:

- Palestinian life;

- continuing harm;
- government policy;
- public protest;
- and the reasons people entered the street.

## 🌿 Working Principle

Police do not merely respond to disorder.

They receive, grade, connect, retain, and operationalise information about people, movements, threats, and events.

In an information-war environment, democratic policing therefore depends not only on whether a power exists, but on the provenance, interpretation, proportionality, correction, and onward transmission of the information from which police risk is constructed.

A false or distorted allegation can become coercive state action if it travels through enough systems without its uncertainty travelling with it.

Policing by consent will therefore depend increasingly on whether police can remain lawful, intelligible, and correctable when the information used to define danger is itself politically contested, commercially mediated, algorithmically amplified, or deliberately manipulated.
