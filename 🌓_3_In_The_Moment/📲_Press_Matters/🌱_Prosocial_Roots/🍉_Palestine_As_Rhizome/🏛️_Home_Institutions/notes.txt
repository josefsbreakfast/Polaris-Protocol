🏛️_Home_Institutions/

Purpose

* Map the domestic institutions through which people in Britain are already materially, politically, professionally, and culturally connected to Palestine.
* Show that Palestine is not only a foreign-policy issue observed from a distance.
* Track decisions made at home concerning arms, policing, education, healthcare, employment, regulation, funding, public appointments, and political speech.
* Distinguish institutional responsibility from general public opinion.
* Identify who holds authority, what decisions were available, what evidence was considered, and what changed materially.
* Examine the difference between institutional rhetoric and institutional action.
* Track how attempts to contain Palestine-related speech or protest can create new legal, professional, labour, governance, and civil-liberties disputes.
* Keep Palestinian life, testimony, political agency, and material conditions central while analysing the domestic routes connected to them.
* Avoid treating every institutional dispute as equivalent.
* Avoid assuming that every allegation of inconsistency, discrimination, intimidation, antisemitism, Islamophobia, extremism, or professional misconduct is automatically either true or cynical.
* Examine evidence, context, process, power, and outcome.
* Support reporting that treats domestic institutional decisions as part of the substantive Palestine story.

Working questions for the cluster

* Which institution made the decision?
* Who held formal authority?
* Who exercised informal influence?
* What legal, professional, ethical, or governance duty was invoked?
* What evidence was considered?
* Was legal advice obtained or disclosed?
* What decision was available but not taken?
* Did public language match material policy?
* Were comparable causes treated differently?
* Who was consulted?
* Who was excluded?
* What relationships, funding streams, partnerships, or conflicts were relevant?
* What happened to the people directly affected?
* Did the decision reduce harm?
* Did it displace visibility?
* Did it create new grievances or constituencies?
* What remains unresolved?
* How does the decision connect people at home to events in Palestine?

🏛️_parliament_and_government.md

Purpose

* Examine how Palestine enters domestic politics through ministerial decisions, parliamentary activity, local government, foreign policy, defence policy, and public administration.
* Separate parliamentary rhetoric from executive responsibility and material action.
* Track how decisions made in Britain contribute to, restrain, or respond to events affecting Palestinians.

Possible sections

* Government responsibility for arms licensing, military cooperation, intelligence relationships, sanctions, humanitarian funding, diplomatic recognition, and international-law compliance.
* Parliamentary votes, statements, urgent questions, debates, written questions, committee work, and opposition scrutiny.
* The role of select committees, all-party groups, backbench groups, party caucuses, and parliamentary legal mechanisms.
* The relationship between public statements and material policy.
* Whether legal advice exists, whether it is disclosed, and how ministers describe it.
* How government separates domestic responsibility from events described as external.
* Local-government decisions involving procurement, pension investments, motions, grants, public buildings, twinning, and civic events.
* Cabinet, departmental, devolved, and local-government differences.
* The role of advisers, civil servants, special envoys, commissioners, and appointed representatives.
* Lobbying, meetings, donations, hospitality, advisory relationships, and undeclared influence.
* Party discipline and the compression of visible disagreement.
* Resignations, rebellions, abstentions, disciplinary action, and changes in language over time.
* Whether humanitarian concern is matched by policy change.
* Whether international rulings alter government conduct.
* What decisions remain active after rhetorical shifts.

Reporting questions

* What decision was available?
* Who had the power to make it?
* What evidence or legal advice was considered?
* What did Parliament know?
* What was withheld?
* What changed materially?
* What remained unchanged?
* Was responsibility displaced onto another institution?
* Were relationships or conflicts disclosed?
* Did local and national institutions act differently?

🐼_policing_and_public_order.md

Purpose

* Treat protest policing as part of the substantive Palestine story rather than merely as crowd management surrounding it.
* Examine how Palestine-related activity is translated into public-order, intelligence, safeguarding, extremism, and criminal-law frameworks.
* Track how policing responses can create new civil-liberties and legitimacy disputes.

Possible sections

* Protest conditions, route restrictions, dispersal powers, stop and search, arrests, bail conditions, charging decisions, and prosecutions.
* The distinction between preventing actual harm and managing political visibility.
* The interpretation of slogans, chants, symbols, clothing, banners, leaflets, signs, and social-media posts.
* Evidentiary thresholds concerning intimidation, harassment, extremism, terrorism, proscribed organisations, antisemitism, Islamophobia, and public disorder.
* Intelligence gathering, surveillance, facial recognition, protest databases, information sharing, and data retention.
* Differences between police forces and between comparable protests.
* Police relationships with ministers, local authorities, universities, event organisers, transport bodies, and private security.
* The role of counterterrorism frameworks in ordinary protest policing.
* Arrest as punishment even where no charge follows.
* Bail restrictions, device seizure, travel restrictions, exclusion zones, and reputational consequences.
* The position of legal observers, journalists, medics, stewards, children, disabled people, and first-time protesters.
* Accessibility, medication, mobility, transport, kettling, toilets, religious practice, and exposure to weather.
* The effect of policing on Palestinian, Muslim, Arab, Jewish, Black, migrant, disabled, and otherwise differently policed participants.
* Whether public-order reporting explains the cause of protest or allows police action to replace it as the story.
* How policing controversy creates new legal, political, professional, and media roots.

Reporting questions

* What specific conduct triggered intervention?
* Which power was used?
* Was the power used lawfully, necessarily, and proportionately?
* What evidence was recorded?
* What happened after arrest?
* Were charges brought?
* Were conditions later withdrawn?
* Was data retained?
* Were comparable events treated differently?
* Did the response reduce danger or widen the dispute?

🩺_healthcare_and_professional_ethics.md

Purpose

* Examine how Palestine enters healthcare through humanitarian concern, medical testimony, workplace expression, regulation, procurement, and professional ethics.
* Treat attacks on healthcare infrastructure and healthcare workers as professional concerns as well as political events.
* Explore the tension between claimed neutrality and duties to recognise mass harm.

Possible sections

* Testimony from Palestinian healthcare workers, patients, humanitarian organisations, and international medical personnel.
* Hospitals, ambulances, clinics, medical workers, patients, medicines, evacuation, and humanitarian access.
* NHS and healthcare-employer policies on badges, pins, clothing, statements, fundraising, vigils, workplace discussion, and public communication.
* Duties concerning patient safety, staff safety, equality, dignity, non-discrimination, confidentiality, and freedom of conscience.
* The difference between political neutrality and silence about harm to patients, staff, and healthcare systems.
* Professional regulation involving doctors, nurses, pharmacists, therapists, academics, students, and other regulated workers.
* Complaints, referrals, investigations, suspensions, disciplinary action, and fitness-to-practise proceedings.
* Institutional partnerships, procurement, research relationships, pensions, charitable activity, and investment.
* Moral injury among healthcare workers.
* Unequal treatment of different humanitarian causes.
* The treatment of Palestinian, Arab, Muslim, Jewish, migrant, and dissenting staff and patients.
* The danger of treating Palestinian identity or solidarity as inherently disruptive in clinical settings.
* Whether professional bodies respond consistently to evidence of attacks on healthcare.
* Whether patient-facing restrictions are evidence-led or reputational.
* The role of medical journals, royal colleges, unions, universities, regulators, and charities.

Reporting questions

* What professional duty is being invoked?
* Is the rule about patient care, workplace conduct, professional reputation, or political expression?
* Is it applied consistently?
* Who is said to be at risk?
* What evidence supports that claim?
* What effect does the policy have on staff and patients?
* Was the response proportionate?
* Was Palestinian medical testimony treated as credible evidence?

🗳️_parties_regulators_and_public_appointments.md

Purpose

* Examine the institutions that decide who may participate, who is disciplined, who is appointed, whose conduct is regulated, and which positions are treated as legitimate.
* Track how Palestine-related speech and affiliation affect political careers, professional standing, public appointments, candidate selection, and regulatory action.
* Map the boundary between legitimate standards and politically selective gatekeeping.

Possible sections

* Political-party membership, candidate selection, suspension, deselection, whip removal, disciplinary procedures, and internal complaints.
* Party rules concerning antisemitism, Islamophobia, racism, extremism, conduct, reputational harm, and social-media activity.
* The treatment of Jewish dissent, Palestinian advocacy, Muslim political participation, and anti-war organising.
* Independent complaint systems, disciplinary panels, legal review, and appeal processes.
* Professional regulators and standards bodies.
* Public appointments, commissioners, advisers, envoys, boards, panels, and review bodies.
* Appointment criteria, conflicts of interest, political vetting, due diligence, and declaration requirements.
* Lobbying, donations, campaign groups, think tanks, advocacy organisations, and informal networks.
* Whether regulators act consistently across political causes and professional groups.
* The relationship between government pressure and formally independent regulators.
* How allegations are reported before findings are reached.
* The use of suspension or investigation as an outcome in itself.
* Delays, secrecy, leaks, anonymous briefings, and procedural opacity.
* Whether political parties and regulators distinguish criticism of Israel, Zionism, government policy, Jewish people, and Jewish institutions.
* Whether allegations of antisemitism, Islamophobia, racism, intimidation, extremism, or foreign influence are evidenced and clearly defined.
* The consequences of public appointment decisions for trust, representation, and institutional legitimacy.
* How exclusion from one institution can create new independent political or professional networks.

Reporting questions

* Which rule or standard applied?
* Was the definition clear?
* Who initiated the complaint?
* Who investigated it?
* Was the process independent?
* Was evidence disclosed to the person affected?
* Was there a right of reply or appeal?
* Were comparable cases treated consistently?
* Did an investigation produce findings?
* Was suspension used before evidence was tested?
* What political or institutional relationships shaped the process?
* Who gained or lost access to public authority?

Cluster guardrails

* Do not treat all institutions as one coordinated system without evidence.
* Do not assume that similar outcomes prove a common cause.
* Distinguish formal decisions from informal pressure.
* Distinguish legal duties from political preferences.
* Distinguish genuine safeguarding from reputational management.
* Distinguish disagreement from misconduct.
* Distinguish criticism of institutions, states, governments, ideologies, and military action from hostility towards populations.
* Do not minimise antisemitism, Islamophobia, anti-Palestinian racism, racism, intimidation, or professional harm.
* Do not use allegations as substitutes for evidence.
* Track process as well as outcome.
* Track the consequences of investigations, suspensions, arrests, referrals, and disciplinary action even where no adverse finding follows.
* Identify the people with the least institutional power.
* Ask who carries the cost of caution.
* Ask who benefits when an institution delays, remains silent, or describes itself as neutral.
* Keep domestic institutional choices connected to their effects on Palestinians.
* Do not let the home-institution frame make Palestine disappear.
