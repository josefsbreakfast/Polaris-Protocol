# 🫒 Repetition Is Not Stagnation  
**First created:** 2026-07-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-18  
*Understanding repeated marches as changing acts of memory, political maintenance, public education, constituency building, and democratic pressure.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The marches can look repetitive.

The same streets may be used.

The same flags may be carried.

The same chants may be heard.

The same institutions may be addressed.

From a distance, this can create the impression that nothing has changed and that the movement is simply repeating itself.

But visual repetition is not political stagnation.

A march may recur because:

- the originating harm continues;
- a previous demand remains unanswered;
- new events have changed the field;
- new participants have entered;
- institutions have created new grievances;
- public memory requires maintenance;
- or the issue risks disappearing before meaningful resolution has occurred.

Two marches may look similar while responding to different:

- deaths;
- legal developments;
- political decisions;
- institutional actions;
- anniversaries;
- restrictions;
- or failures of implementation.

The proper question is not merely:

> Has this happened before?

It is:

> What is this repetition doing now?

---

## 🔁 What Repetition Does

Repeated mobilisation can perform several forms of political work at once.

It may:

- preserve public attention;
- prevent premature closure;
- demonstrate continuing constituency;
- sustain social networks;
- bring new participants into the movement;
- transmit knowledge;
- create spaces for grief;
- test democratic freedoms;
- apply pressure to institutions;
- and maintain the possibility of future action.

These functions are not always visible in conventional reporting.

Coverage tends to judge demonstrations through their immediate external effects:

- Was policy changed?
- Was there disruption?
- Were arrests made?
- Was attendance higher or lower?
- Did a politician respond?

Those questions matter.

But they do not capture all the work being performed.

Political movements also survive through maintenance.

They preserve the relationships, knowledge, memory, and public presence required for future change.

---

## 🕯️ Memory Against Premature Closure

Public attention moves quickly.

An unresolved event may disappear from headlines long before it disappears from the lives of those affected.

Repeated marches resist this gap.

They may preserve memory of:

- people killed;
- people injured;
- families displaced;
- people detained;
- homes destroyed;
- communities fragmented;
- journalists and aid workers lost;
- and ordinary lives made impossible.

This memory is not only retrospective.

It affects what can be demanded in the present.

When deaths and displacement are forgotten, it becomes easier to:

- withdraw attention;
- reduce accountability;
- describe continuing consequences as old news;
- and treat political closure as though it has already occurred.

Repetition can therefore function as refusal.

It refuses the assumption that the passage of time has resolved the issue.

---

## 🧍 Grief Does Not Follow The News Cycle

Grief does not become repetitive because it continues.

Nor does it become politically irrelevant when public attention declines.

People may return to marches because they need somewhere to:

- mourn;
- witness;
- name the dead;
- hold communal grief;
- recognise loss;
- and resist the conversion of human lives into anonymous totals.

This is particularly important where people believe official institutions have not provided adequate spaces for recognition.

A march can become:

- memorial;
- vigil;
- public testimony;
- political demand;
- and collective holding environment

at the same time.

This does not mean that grief should be romanticised or treated as beyond scrutiny.

It means that repeated participation may be carrying human functions that cannot be measured solely through immediate policy outcomes.

---

## 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Repetition Builds Constituency

Movements do not begin with their final constituency already assembled.

They build it.

Repeated marches allow people to:

- encounter the issue;
- meet organisers;
- recognise shared concern;
- find local groups;
- learn how to participate;
- and decide whether they wish to return.

A person may first attend as:

- an observer;
- a friend accompanying someone else;
- a member of a faith community;
- a professional responding to a particular event;
- a student;
- a trade unionist;
- or somebody moved by one image or testimony.

Over time, that person may become:

- a regular participant;
- organiser;
- fundraiser;
- researcher;
- legal observer;
- steward;
- speaker;
- or provider of practical support.

Repetition gives political concern somewhere to become durable participation.

Without recurrence, many people would encounter the issue only as passive spectators.

---

## 🌱 Each March Can Grow New Roots

Later marches may draw people through routes that did not exist at the beginning.

A new participant may arrive because of:

- a court decision;
- an arms-export review;
- a workplace sanction;
- a university encampment;
- a protest arrest;
- a media controversy;
- a professional dispute;
- or a local council decision.

The march may look similar from outside.

Its internal composition may have changed substantially.

New roots can alter:

- the demands;
- language;
- expertise;
- institutional targets;
- organisational capacity;
- and relationships within the movement.

Repetition therefore creates opportunities for the rhizome to widen.

It is not simply the same people performing the same response to the same event.

---

## 📚 Repetition Transmits Knowledge

Repeated events create a public learning environment.

People may encounter information about:

- Palestinian history;
- displacement;
- international law;
- detention;
- humanitarian access;
- arms relationships;
- institutional investment;
- protest rights;
- and media framing.

Knowledge travels through:

- speeches;
- leaflets;
- signs;
- teach-ins;
- conversations;
- reading groups;
- social media;
- cultural work;
- and contact with Palestinian participants and organisations.

This process can deepen public understanding.

It can also transmit:

- simplification;
- contested interpretations;
- factual errors;
- slogans detached from context;
- and emotionally powerful claims that require verification.

The educational function should not be assumed to be uniformly good.

It should be assessed.

But a movement that repeats is also a movement with repeated opportunities to:

- correct;
- refine;
- challenge;
- contextualise;
- and develop its own political knowledge.

---

## 🧰 Repetition Builds Practical Capacity

A large march requires infrastructure.

Repeated mobilisation may develop:

- stewarding;
- transport;
- accessibility planning;
- legal observation;
- first aid;
- communications;
- fundraising;
- safeguarding;
- speaker coordination;
- translation;
- media liaison;
- and community care.

These capacities can become available beyond the march itself.

They may later support:

- local campaigns;
- legal cases;
- workplace organising;
- humanitarian fundraising;
- public meetings;
- electoral pressure;
- or responses to repression.

This means repeated marches can increase organisational competence even where their immediate visible form remains familiar.

The public event may be the surface expression of a much larger learning process.

---

## 📡 Repetition Demonstrates Constituency

One march can be dismissed as a temporary reaction.

Repeated marches demonstrate that concern has survived beyond the first moment.

They may show:

- sustained participation;
- geographic spread;
- cross-community involvement;
- organisational durability;
- and the capacity to mobilise after media attention has declined.

This does not establish that the movement represents everyone.

Nor does turnout provide a simple measure of public opinion.

But recurrence can communicate that:

- a constituency exists;
- it remains willing to act;
- and institutions cannot assume that concern has disappeared.

Demonstration is both an act and a form of information.

It shows decision-makers who is still present.

---

## 🏛️ Repetition Applies Institutional Pressure

Institutions often respond slowly.

They may require:

- formal complaints;
- internal reviews;
- board decisions;
- legal advice;
- regulatory action;
- shareholder pressure;
- or repeated political scrutiny.

One demonstration may establish concern.

Repeated mobilisation may prevent an institution from treating that concern as temporary.

The target may change over time.

A movement may move from broad protest towards:

- a particular arms licence;
- a procurement relationship;
- a pension investment;
- a university partnership;
- a professional policy;
- a political donation;
- or a local authority decision.

The march remains recognisable while the pressure becomes more specific.

This is not necessarily stagnation.

It may be strategic narrowing.

---

## 🛡️ Repetition Tests Democratic Space

Each march also tests the conditions under which public disagreement is allowed to exist.

Repeated mobilisation reveals whether:

- protest remains lawful and accessible;
- restrictions are becoming broader;
- policing is proportionate;
- public-order rules are applied consistently;
- participants can express political identity without collective suspicion;
- and institutions preserve routes for democratic dissent.

The response may change from one march to the next.

This creates information about:

- state tolerance;
- institutional learning;
- enforcement patterns;
- risk classification;
- and public legitimacy.

A repeated march is therefore not only a message about Palestine.

It may also reveal how the society receiving the march manages persistent disagreement.

That domestic route should remain connected to the reason the protest exists.

---

## 🗞️ Why Repeated Marches Look Identical In Coverage

Reporting often selects the same elements each time:

- flags;
- crowds;
- police lines;
- traffic;
- arrests;
- controversial slogans;
- counter-protest;
- and brief comments from organisers.

This produces a visual loop.

Different marches are made to appear interchangeable because coverage repeatedly chooses the same features.

The loop may conceal changes in:

- motive;
- timing;
- constituency;
- demand;
- target;
- evidence;
- organisation;
- and institutional response.

A march triggered by a legal ruling may look visually similar to one triggered by renewed violence.

A march focused on arms exports may use some of the same slogans as one focused on humanitarian access.

A commemorative march may share the same route as an emergency protest.

The reporting needs to identify what distinguishes the event politically, not only what makes it visually recognisable.

---

## 🪧 The Same Slogan Can Perform Different Work

Slogans recur because they are:

- memorable;
- collective;
- rhythmically usable;
- emotionally legible;
- and capable of carrying a minimum shared demand.

But the meaning of a repeated slogan may shift with context.

It may function as:

- demand;
- mourning;
- warning;
- identity;
- refusal;
- or expression of continuity.

A slogan should not be treated as though its meaning is exhausted by previous coverage.

At the same time, repetition does not remove the need to examine:

- ambiguity;
- contested interpretation;
- historical context;
- and the effect on people hearing it.

The correct response is neither automatic condemnation nor automatic defence.

It is specific interpretation.

---

## 🫒 Repetition Can Normalise Solidarity

Repeated public presence can change what appears socially ordinary.

Early participation may feel:

- risky;
- marginal;
- unfamiliar;
- professionally dangerous;
- or socially isolating.

Over time, repetition may make solidarity more publicly legible.

People see:

- families;
- workers;
- students;
- faith groups;
- professionals;
- Jewish participants;
- disabled participants;
- and local communities

returning to the same field.

This can weaken the assumption that concern belongs only to one narrow ideological group.

Normalisation does not settle the underlying political argument.

It changes who feels able to enter it.

---

## 🪢 Repetition Maintains Relationships

Movements are not sustained only through agreement.

They are sustained through relationships.

Repeated events allow people to:

- maintain trust;
- repair disagreements;
- exchange information;
- coordinate future work;
- provide care;
- and recognise when participants are struggling or disappearing.

This relational work is often invisible.

It may be especially important where participants face:

- isolation;
- professional risk;
- grief;
- racism;
- antisemitism;
- Islamophobia;
- anti-Palestinian hostility;
- or fear of political surveillance.

A movement that cannot maintain relationships is unlikely to remain durable.

Repetition gives those relationships somewhere to be renewed.

---

## ⚠️ When Repetition Becomes Ritualised

Repetition is not automatically productive.

It can become ritualised in ways that reduce strategic value.

Warning signs may include:

- no clear account of current demands;
- no assessment of previous outcomes;
- declining accessibility;
- concentration of authority in a small group;
- dependence on spectacle;
- substitution of attendance for wider organising;
- repeated language that no longer communicates beyond the existing constituency;
- failure to respond to changed evidence;
- or treating criticism as betrayal.

A ritual can remain emotionally meaningful while becoming politically limited.

The task is not to abolish ritual.

It is to distinguish:

- remembrance;
- maintenance;
- education;
- community;
- strategic action;
- and habit.

Each may be legitimate.

They should not be confused with one another.

---

## 📉 Fatigue Is Also Information

Repeated mobilisation has costs.

Participants may experience:

- physical exhaustion;
- emotional depletion;
- financial strain;
- disability barriers;
- care conflicts;
- professional risk;
- legal fear;
- and grief saturation.

Organisers may struggle to sustain:

- safety;
- accessibility;
- communication;
- funding;
- and internal trust.

Declining participation may therefore reflect:

- exhaustion rather than resolution;
- repression rather than agreement;
- inaccessible organising rather than loss of concern;
- or movement into other forms of activity.

Fatigue should not be romanticised.

A movement that relies indefinitely on the same people absorbing the same burdens may become unsustainable.

Political maintenance also requires care.

---

## ♿ Repetition Must Remain Accessible

Regular mobilisation can silently narrow its constituency.

People may be excluded by:

- long routes;
- crowd density;
- lack of seating;
- inaccessible transport;
- poor information;
- sensory overload;
- absence of toilets;
- financial cost;
- policing risk;
- and lack of remote or local alternatives.

A movement may describe itself as broad while repeatedly designing participation around people with:

- physical stamina;
- flexible work;
- disposable income;
- low care burden;
- and tolerance of legal risk.

Repetition should increase organisational learning.

It should not reproduce the same barriers without reflection.

The question is not only who keeps returning.

It is also who would return if participation were made possible.

---

## 🧠 Strategic Adaptation

Repetition remains politically alive when it learns.

This may involve:

- changing the target;
- refining demands;
- varying tactics;
- improving evidence;
- developing local campaigns;
- supporting legal work;
- building professional networks;
- strengthening accessibility;
- changing communication;
- or recognising when another method would be more effective.

A movement does not prove its commitment by repeating one tactic forever.

Nor does changing tactic mean abandoning the cause.

Adaptation can be evidence of seriousness.

The relevant question is:

> What has the movement learned from the previous repetition?

---

## 🔄 Repetition And The Rhizome

In a rhizomatic movement, repetition does not occur only along one route.

Activity may recur through:

- marches;
- vigils;
- workplace actions;
- legal cases;
- university campaigns;
- cultural events;
- religious gatherings;
- fundraising;
- electoral pressure;
- and institutional review.

One visible form may decline while another grows.

A national march may become less frequent while local organising expands.

A campus encampment may close while legal and professional disputes continue.

A protest may become a pension campaign.

A vigil may become an archive.

A repeated political concern can change its form without disappearing.

This is movement, not stagnation.

## 🧺 Repetition Audit

Before describing a march or movement as repetitive, ask:

### Cause

- Is the originating harm still active?
- Has the underlying dispute been resolved?
- Has a temporary measure been mistaken for an endpoint?

### Timing

- What happened since the previous march?
- Is this event responding to a new development?
- Does it carry commemorative significance?

### Participation

- Who is attending now?
- Which participants are new?
- Who has withdrawn?
- Which barriers shape attendance?

### Demand

- Are the demands the same?
- Have they narrowed, widened, or become more specific?
- Is the institutional target different?

### Function

- Is the event protesting, mourning, educating, recruiting, maintaining networks, or applying pressure?
- Is it performing several of these functions at once?

### Learning

- What has changed in the movement’s organisation?
- Has the evidence improved?
- Have previous failures been assessed?
- Is the tactic adapting?

### Democratic Space

- Has the institutional or police response changed?
- What does the response reveal about access to lawful dissent?
- Has suppression created another route of mobilisation?

### Outcome

- What did the previous repetition accomplish?
- What remains unfinished?
- Would stopping make the issue disappear from public view before it is resolved?

---

## 🗞️ Reporting Questions

A report on a repeated march should ask:

- Why was this march called now?
- What has happened since the last one?
- Which demand is central today?
- Who is participating?
- Which new groups or institutions are involved?
- What function is the event performing?
- Is it emergency protest, commemoration, pressure, recruitment, or maintenance?
- How has the movement changed?
- What has it learned?
- Which barriers affect participation?
- What remains unresolved?
- and what would be lost if public repetition ended?

The story should not begin and end with the fact that there has been another march.

The recurrence is the starting point.

---

## ❓ Working Questions

When examining repeated mobilisation, ask:

- What does this repetition preserve?
- What does it change?
- What does it teach?
- Who does it bring into the movement?
- Which relationships does it maintain?
- What public memory would otherwise be lost?
- Is the same visual form carrying a different political demand?
- Has the constituency changed?
- Has the institutional target changed?
- Is the movement learning from previous events?
- Has ritual become detached from strategy?
- What care and accessibility burdens are being created?
- What does fatigue reveal?
- Which other forms of action are growing beyond the march?
- Is public repetition preventing premature closure?
- What would meaningful resolution make unnecessary?

---

## 🌿 Working Principle

Repetition is not evidence that nothing is happening.

It may preserve memory, build constituency, transmit knowledge, maintain relationships, test democratic space, and keep an unresolved issue publicly alive.

The relevant question is not whether the movement has repeated itself.

It is whether the repetition remains connected to changing conditions, meaningful political work, and a credible route towards resolution.
