# 🕸️ Connection Is Not Equivalence  
**First created:** 2026-07-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-17  
*Tracing relationships between Palestine and other histories, institutions, struggles, and forms of harm without collapsing their differences.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The rhizome model depends on connection.

It asks how Palestine becomes linked to:

- other political struggles;
- domestic institutions;
- professional duties;
- legal systems;
- histories of displacement;
- racial and religious identities;
- protest movements;
- policing;
- surveillance;
- arms production;
- media practice;
- and public arguments about rights, safety, and democratic legitimacy.

But connection is not equivalence.

Two struggles may share:

- an institution;
- a political technique;
- a legal question;
- a history;
- a financial relationship;
- a form of solidarity;
- or a recognisable pattern of public response

without becoming the same struggle.

The task is not to prevent comparison.

It is to make comparison precise enough to clarify responsibility rather than erase history.

---

## 🕸️ Why The Model Requires Connection

A rhizome is made legible by tracing its routes.

A route might show that:

- a university’s investments connect a campus protest to arms production;
- a government’s foreign policy connects domestic taxation to events in Palestine;
- a police response connects protest reporting to civil-liberties questions;
- a professional regulator connects Palestine to workplace speech;
- a legal ruling connects Palestinian rights to the credibility of international law;
- or a community recognises part of its own history in Palestinian displacement and builds solidarity from that recognition.

Without connection, the rhizome becomes only a metaphor.

The route must be shown.

But the existence of a route does not mean that everything at either end is interchangeable.

A university investment is not the same thing as a bombardment.

A protest arrest is not the same thing as military detention.

A history of persecution is not identical to another history of persecution.

The relationship can still be politically important.

Precision does not weaken connection.

It makes the connection usable.

## 🧭 Types Of Connection

Different connections make different claims.

Naming the type of connection prevents a general resemblance from being mistaken for total equivalence.

### Institutional Connection

The same institution participates in more than one part of the system.

This may include:

- governments;
- political parties;
- universities;
- banks;
- arms companies;
- charities;
- professional regulators;
- media organisations;
- courts;
- police forces;
- or cultural bodies.

An institutional connection asks:

> What decisions has this institution made, and how do those decisions link the two fields?

### Financial Connection

Money creates a direct route.

This may include:

- investment;
- procurement;
- weapons contracts;
- taxation;
- donations;
- insurance;
- banking;
- pensions;
- trade;
- lobbying;
- or charitable funding.

A financial connection asks:

> What material relationship exists, who benefits from it, and who bears its consequences?

### Legal Connection

The same legal principle, institution, definition, or accountability mechanism is being tested.

This may concern:

- international humanitarian law;
- human rights law;
- protest rights;
- freedom of expression;
- discrimination;
- charitable regulation;
- public procurement;
- professional duties;
- or criminal law.

A legal connection asks:

> Is the same rule being applied consistently, and what differences between the cases remain legally significant?

### Political Connection

A political actor has linked the issues through policy, rhetoric, alliances, or public decision-making.

This may include:

- arms-export policy;
- diplomatic recognition;
- sanctions;
- public-order legislation;
- party discipline;
- government appointments;
- or electoral campaigning.

A political connection asks:

> Who made the connection through power, rather than merely through interpretation?

### Historical Connection

One history materially shaped another, or both developed through connected institutions and political processes.

This may include:

- imperial governance;
- mandates;
- border-making;
- migration;
- nationalism;
- war;
- racial classification;
- international diplomacy;
- or the movement of legal and administrative practices between territories.

A historical connection asks:

> What can be demonstrated about transmission, influence, chronology, or shared institutional development?

Historical connection requires more than resemblance.

It requires evidence of relationship.

### Tactical Connection

Similar methods appear across different settings.

These may include:

- protest tactics;
- surveillance;
- censorship;
- policing;
- detention;
- propaganda;
- legal restriction;
- digital targeting;
- or community protection.

A tactical connection asks:

> Is the same method being used, adapted, or studied across settings?

Shared technique does not establish identical purpose, scale, or political context.

### Conceptual Connection

The same public or ethical question appears in different cases.

This may include:

- civilian protection;
- collective punishment;
- displacement;
- self-determination;
- state violence;
- political representation;
- or the limits of institutional neutrality.

A conceptual connection asks:

> What does placing these cases beside one another help us understand about the concept?

Conceptual comparison is often useful, but it is especially vulnerable to flattening.

### Relational Connection

Communities recognise connected stakes and act together.

This may arise through:

- shared organising;
- religious or ethical commitments;
- mutual aid;
- trade unions;
- student movements;
- anti-racist coalitions;
- diaspora relationships;
- or professional solidarity.

A relational connection asks:

> What relationship are the participants themselves building, and what do they believe it requires?

Solidarity can create a real connection without making experiences identical.

## ⚖️ Comparison Is Not Equivalence

Comparison means placing two or more things beside one another to examine a defined feature.

Equivalence makes a stronger claim.

It suggests that the things being compared are equal, interchangeable, or substantially the same in the relevant respect.

Most useful political comparisons do not require equivalence.

A comparison might show that:

- the same institution uses different standards;
- similar rhetoric legitimises exclusion;
- a legal principle is being applied inconsistently;
- communities recognise a related threat;
- a method of surveillance has travelled;
- or public grief is distributed unequally.

None of these claims requires the cases to be identical.

A responsible comparison should complete the sentence:

> These cases are being compared because they share **this specific feature**.

It should also complete a second sentence:

> They remain different in **these important respects**.

If neither sentence can be completed clearly, the comparison is probably doing rhetorical rather than analytical work.

---

## 🧱 Where Comparison Breaks Down

Comparison becomes destructive when it:

- erases chronology;
- ignores differences in power;
- collapses distinct legal statuses;
- treats different scales of harm as interchangeable;
- removes the people affected from their own history;
- imports categories without explaining them;
- turns suffering into a competition;
- claims ownership over another population’s experience;
- or uses one atrocity merely to intensify the emotional force of another argument.

A comparison may begin with a real connection and still be badly constructed.

For example, two populations may both have experienced displacement.

That does not mean:

- the causes were identical;
- the legal structures were identical;
- the forms of return available were identical;
- the political actors were interchangeable;
- or the histories can be narrated through one shared template.

“Both involve displacement” may be a valid starting point.

It is not a complete analysis.

---

## 🕯️ Historical Specificity

Historical specificity means allowing each history to retain:

- its own chronology;
- institutions;
- political actors;
- language;
- geography;
- legal structure;
- cultural meaning;
- internal disagreement;
- and forms of memory.

This does not require isolating histories from one another.

Histories meet.

Institutions travel.

Political movements learn from one another.

Governments transfer techniques.

Communities build solidarity through remembered experience.

But historical connection should be demonstrated rather than assumed.

A responsible historical comparison asks:

- Did one event influence the other?
- Did the same institution participate?
- Did people at the time make the connection?
- Did language, law, funding, or political technique travel between them?
- Are later communities drawing the comparison, and for what purpose?
- Which differences would be lost if the cases were merged?

Historical memory should open inquiry.

It should not close it by declaring the conclusion in advance.

---

## 🧳 The Danger Of Portable Histories

Some histories become politically portable.

They are repeatedly carried into new arguments as universal symbols of:

- evil;
- victimhood;
- resistance;
- innocence;
- betrayal;
- or moral emergency.

This portability can make public recognition easier.

It can also strip a history of its people and conditions.

A portable history may be used to say:

- “This is exactly the same.”
- “This cannot be serious because it does not look the same.”
- “Our history gives us unique authority over this question.”
- “Their history proves our preferred political conclusion.”
- or “Only the most extreme historical comparison can make the present harm legible.”

These uses place the remembered history above the present people being discussed.

The correct question is not whether a current event has reproduced an earlier catastrophe brick for brick.

The question is which specific mechanisms, warnings, responsibilities, and differences are relevant now.

History should sharpen perception.

It should not create an impossible threshold beneath which contemporary harm becomes tolerable.

## 🕍 Holocaust Comparison Requires Particular Care

The Holocaust is frequently invoked in arguments about Palestine and Israel.

It may appear through:

- Jewish historical memory;
- Palestinian accounts of European guilt and political consequence;
- warnings about dehumanisation;
- claims concerning genocide;
- accusations of Nazi equivalence;
- arguments about refugeehood;
- or demands that present events reproduce the Holocaust’s exact methods before extreme concern becomes legitimate.

These comparisons require precision.

The Holocaust was not one event occurring in one type of site through one method of killing.

It involved:

- exclusion;
- dispossession;
- forced movement;
- ghettos;
- starvation;
- shootings;
- forced labour;
- detention;
- deportation;
- concentration camps;
- killing centres;
- and mass murder across multiple territories and institutional systems.

Remembering this complexity does not establish that another event is equivalent to the Holocaust.

It does prevent the Holocaust from being reduced to a crude visual checklist.

The absence of gas chambers does not settle whether another population is experiencing grave mass harm.

Likewise, the presence of dehumanising language or forced displacement does not by itself establish complete historical equivalence.

The comparison must identify the exact mechanism or warning being discussed.

It must then preserve the differences.

---

## 🪞 Mirroring Can Conceal Asymmetry

Public debate often describes connected harms as mirror images.

This can make balance appear easier.

But not every relationship is symmetrical.

For example:

- antisemitism and anti-Palestinian racism may interact without operating identically;
- Jewish and Palestinian relationships to state power differ across countries and institutions;
- fear experienced by communities in Britain is not identical to material conditions in Gaza or the West Bank;
- protester misconduct and state repression are both assessable without carrying equal power or consequence;
- and competing national narratives do not necessarily possess equal institutional reach.

Symmetry should not be invented for the sake of appearing neutral.

Nor should asymmetry be used to imply that harm to the less powerful side is impossible.

The task is to identify:

- who holds which power;
- what action occurred;
- who was affected;
- which institution is responsible;
- and what scale and form the consequences took.

Fairness requires consistent standards.

It does not require every story to produce matching halves.

## 🪢 Solidarity Without Identity Collapse

Solidarity often begins with recognition:

> Something in your struggle connects to something in ours.

That recognition can support:

- mutual aid;
- political education;
- shared organising;
- public protection;
- and wider democratic participation.

But solidarity becomes unstable when recognition turns into appropriation.

This happens when a group claims:

- that the struggles are identical;
- that it can speak for the other population;
- that its own framework explains the whole conflict;
- that its participation should become the centre of the story;
- or that disagreement from those directly affected represents ingratitude or political failure.

Solidarity does not require identical histories.

It requires enough connection to create an obligation.

A stronger formulation is:

> Our experiences are not the same. We recognise a relationship, and that relationship gives us reasons to act.

Difference does not weaken solidarity.

It sets the conditions under which solidarity can remain respectful.

## 🗣️ Who Has Standing To Make The Comparison?

No community has exclusive ownership over every comparison involving its history.

But standing matters.

A comparison carries different weight depending on:

- who is making it;
- what relationship they have to the history;
- what expertise or testimony supports it;
- who bears the consequences;
- and whether affected communities have made or rejected the connection themselves.

Personal or inherited experience can provide insight.

It does not produce automatic authority over another population’s history.

Academic expertise can clarify structures.

It does not replace lived testimony.

Membership of one affected community may create a legitimate route into solidarity.

It does not confer the right to define the other community’s political demands.

The question is not simply:

> Is this person allowed to compare these things?

It is:

- What exactly are they claiming?
- What supports the comparison?
- Whose experience is being interpreted?
- Who is absent?
- What power does the speaker hold?
- What political use is being made of the analogy?
- and what happens if the comparison is accepted?

---

## 🧠 Analogy Is Not Evidence

Analogies can help readers recognise unfamiliar systems.

They can also create the illusion of proof.

Saying that one policy “resembles” another does not establish:

- common intent;
- common origin;
- identical legal status;
- equal scale;
- or the same likely outcome.

An analogy should direct attention towards evidence.

It should not replace evidence.

A useful sequence is:

1. identify the apparent similarity;
2. specify the mechanism being compared;
3. gather evidence for each case separately;
4. examine material or historical connections;
5. identify significant differences;
6. state the limited conclusion the comparison supports.

The comparison should be the beginning of the inquiry, not the entire argument.

## 🗞️ Reporting Comparisons Responsibly

Journalists often encounter comparisons through:

- protest signs;
- speeches;
- interviews;
- political rhetoric;
- legal submissions;
- campaign materials;
- academic analysis;
- and social media.

The comparison itself may be newsworthy.

But reporting that somebody made a comparison is not the same as assessing it.

A responsible report should ask:

- What was actually compared?
- Was the claim about intent, method, effect, law, language, or historical memory?
- Was it presented as equivalence or as warning?
- What evidence supports the stated connection?
- Which relevant differences were omitted?
- How do affected communities understand the analogy?
- Is the comparison common, contested, fringe, or deliberately inflammatory?
- Is a translated or abbreviated slogan being made to carry more meaning than it reasonably can?
- Does the comparison clarify the event, or has controversy over the comparison replaced the event itself?

Coverage should not reproduce an inflammatory analogy repeatedly while failing to explain the underlying issue.

Nor should it describe every uncomfortable historical comparison as inherently illegitimate.

The job is to identify what the comparison claims and whether that claim holds.

## 🏷️ Labels Must Not Smuggle In Equivalence

Labels can contain hidden comparisons.

Terms such as:

- terrorism;
- resistance;
- pogrom;
- genocide;
- apartheid;
- colonialism;
- fascism;
- extremism;
- riot;
- occupation;
- security;
- and self-defence

carry legal, historical, political, and emotional meanings.

Using one of these words may connect an event to a wider category.

That can be accurate.

It can also import conclusions that have not yet been demonstrated.

Reporting should distinguish:

- legal classification;
- scholarly interpretation;
- political description;
- movement language;
- institutional terminology;
- and rhetorical accusation.

Contested labels should be attributed and examined.

The strength of a comparison should not depend on allowing one emotionally powerful word to perform the whole analysis.

## 🌍 Categories Do Not Travel Unchanged

Political categories often move across borders.

A framework developed in one country may be applied to another without accounting for different:

- racial histories;
- legal systems;
- class structures;
- religious relationships;
- migration patterns;
- state institutions;
- or political vocabularies.

This is especially common when US categories dominate international media and online organising.

Terms may still be useful across borders.

But their travel should be explained.

Ask:

- Where did this category develop?
- What conditions did it originally describe?
- Which features are present here?
- Which features are absent?
- What local language or history is being displaced?
- Who benefits from importing the category?
- and how does the meaning change in transit?

A rhizome can cross borders.

The map should still record where the soil changes.

## 🧺 The Comparison Test

Before making or repeating a comparison, ask:

### 1. What is being compared?

Name the exact feature:

- an institution;
- law;
- tactic;
- form of rhetoric;
- pattern of harm;
- political response;
- historical process;
- or relationship of solidarity.

### 2. What kind of connection is present?

Is it:

- institutional;
- financial;
- legal;
- political;
- historical;
- tactical;
- conceptual;
- or relational?

### 3. What evidence establishes the route?

Identify:

- records;
- policies;
- contracts;
- testimony;
- historical sources;
- legal findings;
- public statements;
- or documented organisational relationships.

### 4. What does the comparison clarify?

State the limited analytical purpose.

### 5. What remains different?

Preserve differences in:

- chronology;
- geography;
- power;
- scale;
- law;
- political agency;
- institutional structure;
- and lived meaning.

### 6. Who is centred?

Ask whether the comparison helps explain Palestinian conditions or recentres the person making it.

### 7. Who has made or rejected the connection?

Include relevant Palestinian and other affected voices.

### 8. What political work is the comparison doing?

Is it being used to:

- warn;
- explain;
- mobilise;
- delegitimise;
- excuse;
- accuse;
- authenticate;
- or prevent recognition?

### 9. Could the point be made more precisely?

Replace total equivalence with the narrower relationship that can actually be demonstrated.

---

## 🚦Comparison Outcomes

After applying the test, a comparison may be:

### 🟢 Clarifying

The connection is evidenced, specific, proportionate, and preserves meaningful differences.

### 🟡 Limited

The comparison identifies a useful shared feature but requires strong qualification.

### 🟠 Distorting

A genuine connection exists, but the analogy exaggerates similarity or removes important context.

### 🔴 Collapsing

The comparison treats distinct histories or populations as interchangeable, substitutes analogy for evidence, or consumes the people it claims to illuminate.

This is not a scoring system for deciding which histories may be discussed together.

It is a discipline for deciding what the comparison can reasonably support.

---

## ❓ Working Questions

When tracing a connection, ask:

- What precise relationship is being claimed?
- Is the connection material, institutional, legal, historical, tactical, conceptual, or relational?
- What evidence shows that the route exists?
- Are two cases being compared or declared equivalent?
- Which differences remain essential?
- Is chronology being preserved?
- Is power being measured accurately?
- Are legal categories being used precisely?
- Has a portable history replaced the present people involved?
- Is suffering being turned into a competition?
- Does the analogy clarify a mechanism or merely add emotional force?
- Are categories travelling across borders without explanation?
- Who has standing, knowledge, or lived relationship to make the comparison?
- Whose testimony is missing?
- What political outcome does the comparison encourage?
- Could the same argument be made more precisely and with less historical collapse?

---

## 🌿 Working Principle

Connection identifies a route.

Equivalence erases the ground on either side of it.

The purpose of comparison is to make relationships, mechanisms, and responsibilities more visible—not to turn different peoples and histories into interchangeable evidence.
