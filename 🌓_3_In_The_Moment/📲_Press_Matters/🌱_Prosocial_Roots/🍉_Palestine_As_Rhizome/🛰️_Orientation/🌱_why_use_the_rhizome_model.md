# 🌱 Why Use The Rhizome Model  
**First created:** 2026-07-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-17  
*An analytical model for understanding why Palestine continues to generate multiple, interconnected routes into public, institutional, professional, and democratic life.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The rhizome model helps explain why Palestine remains a durable site of public mobilisation.

Conventional reporting often looks for a movement’s central trunk:

- one origin;
- one organisation;
- one leadership structure;
- one representative ideology;
- one typical participant;
- one set of demands;
- and one event after which public attention should naturally end.

Palestinian solidarity does not fit neatly into that structure.

People arrive through different routes. They remain involved for different reasons. New institutional responses create new disputes, and attempts to close one route may cause the issue to reappear through another.

The rhizome provides a better model for tracing this pattern.

It does not suggest that Palestine is vague, infinitely symbolic, or secretly responsible for every other political conflict.

It describes how an unresolved Palestinian struggle has developed many connected points of entry into public life.

---

## 🌿 What A Rhizome Is

A rhizome is a form of growth without one dominant central trunk.

It spreads through a network of roots, shoots, junctions, breaks, and new points of emergence.

A visible section may be cut back while the wider system remains alive.

Growth may reappear somewhere else because the connections beneath the surface have not been removed.

Applied carefully, the model helps explain how a political movement can be:

- decentralised without being meaningless;
- internally diverse without being entirely incoherent;
- repeatedly suppressed without disappearing;
- responsive to new events without abandoning its original concern;
- and connected to many institutions without becoming reducible to those institutions.

The point is not that every route is equally important.

The point is that the movement cannot be understood through one route alone.

---

## 🌳 Why The Tree Model Fails

A tree model assumes that political activity grows outward from a stable centre.

It encourages reporters and institutions to ask:

- Who is in charge?
- Which organisation represents the movement?
- What single ideology holds it together?
- Which demand would bring the activity to an end?
- Which leader can speak for everyone?
- Which branch can be removed to weaken the whole?

These questions are not always useless.

Movements do contain organisations, prominent figures, funding relationships, strategies, hierarchies, and internal struggles.

But the tree model becomes misleading when it assumes that the absence of one command structure means the absence of meaningful organisation.

It also encourages authorities to mistake visible containment for political resolution.

Closing an encampment does not resolve the reasons it was formed.

Banning one slogan does not settle the dispute beneath it.

Removing one organiser does not dissolve the professional, legal, humanitarian, religious, or political routes through which others became involved.

A movement may lose one branch while continuing to grow through the ground.

## 🕸️ Many Routes Into One Public Struggle

People reach Palestinian solidarity through different forms of connection.

Some routes begin with Palestine directly:

- Palestinian identity;
- family;
- displacement;
- land;
- imprisonment;
- bereavement;
- cultural continuity;
- political representation;
- or personal experience of occupation and war.

Other routes begin with obligations or institutions elsewhere:

- humanitarian medicine;
- civilian protection;
- international law;
- journalism;
- religious ethics;
- Jewish dissent;
- anti-racism;
- trade unionism;
- student politics;
- arms exports;
- public procurement;
- policing;
- protest rights;
- university governance;
- workplace speech;
- charitable regulation;
- media credibility;
- or democratic accountability.

These routes can converge at the same demonstration without becoming identical.

A doctor may arrive through medical ethics.

A student may arrive through university investment.

A Jewish activist may arrive through religious, historical, or anti-Zionist commitments.

A trade unionist may arrive through workplace organisation and arms production.

A Palestinian family may not experience any of these as an abstract political route at all. The issue may be immediate, inherited, domestic, and material.

The rhizome model allows these relationships to be mapped without pretending that every participant has the same stake.

---

## 🥁 One March, Many Entry Points

A march can contain people who disagree substantially about:

- political settlement;
- nationalism;
- Zionism;
- religion;
- armed resistance;
- sanctions;
- electoral strategy;
- protest tactics;
- movement language;
- and relations with existing institutions.

That disagreement does not automatically mean there is no shared centre.

The minimum shared centre may be narrower:

- opposition to continuing mass civilian harm;
- opposition to dispossession;
- demands for humanitarian access;
- demands for legal accountability;
- opposition to material support for destructive policy;
- recognition of Palestinian rights;
- or refusal to allow Palestine to disappear from public attention without a legitimate resolution.

The search for one perfectly representative protester therefore misunderstands the structure of the movement.

There may be no participant who represents every route.

That does not make the routes imaginary.

---

## 🔁 Persistence Without Central Command

Political persistence is often interpreted through an overly narrow choice.

Either a movement is centrally coordinated, or it is treated as spontaneous disorder.

Rhizomatic organisation sits between those descriptions.

A movement may persist through:

- formal organisations;
- informal networks;
- families;
- friendship groups;
- unions;
- faith communities;
- student societies;
- professional associations;
- campaign coalitions;
- social media;
- cultural work;
- legal cases;
- local organising;
- and repeated acts of individual participation.

These forms of activity can influence one another without receiving instructions from one command centre.

Language, tactics, evidence, images, arguments, and experiences travel through the network.

Some routes are organised carefully. Some are improvised. Some are inherited. Some emerge in response to state or institutional action.

The absence of central command should not be confused with the absence of structure.

---

## ✂️ What Happens When One Root Is Cut

Attempts to contain political activity may weaken one visible form while producing another.

A campus encampment may be removed.

The removal can then become:

- a legal dispute;
- an academic-freedom dispute;
- a student-discipline dispute;
- a donor-governance dispute;
- a labour dispute;
- a policing dispute;
- a media dispute;
- or evidence in a wider argument about institutional neutrality.

A protest arrest may reduce participation in one place.

It may also create:

- court proceedings;
- support campaigns;
- professional concern;
- new reporting;
- fundraising;
- scrutiny of police powers;
- and new participants who first encountered the issue through civil liberties rather than foreign policy.

This does not mean repression always strengthens a movement.

Repression can cause real fear, exhaustion, isolation, financial harm, and withdrawal.

It can destroy organisations and expose people to serious danger.

The rhizome model simply warns against assuming that removal and resolution are the same thing.

Containment may suppress one expression while creating another route into the conflict.

## 🧭 “Everything Routes Back To Palestine”

The phrase **“everything routes back to Palestine”** should be used with care.

It appears to function as contemporary movement vernacular rather than a formally attributable historic slogan.

Its value is not as a literal claim that every injustice originates in Palestine.

Used responsibly, it points to something more specific:

> Repeated examination of policing, law, media, universities, arms, procurement, protest, professional ethics, racism, antisemitism, Islamophobia, and democratic legitimacy frequently reveals a material or institutional route connecting the issue back to Palestine.

The phrase works when the route can be shown.

It fails when it becomes:

- conspiratorial;
- totalising;
- historically careless;
- a substitute for evidence;
- or a way of using Palestine as decorative proof that another issue matters.

The task is not to declare that everything is Palestine.

The task is to trace where the connection actually runs.

## 🗺️ Routes Must Be Material

A credible rhizome map should identify more than emotional or symbolic similarity.

A route may be:

### Institutional

The same government, university, company, regulator, charity, donor, broadcaster, or professional body is involved.

### Financial

Money, investment, procurement, insurance, donations, taxation, trade, or weapons contracts create a direct relationship.

### Legal

The same laws, rights, court decisions, definitions, or accountability systems are being tested.

### Political

A party, minister, public authority, or campaign has made a decision connecting domestic policy to Palestine.

### Professional

Doctors, journalists, lawyers, academics, artists, civil servants, or other workers encounter Palestine through the duties and restrictions of their profession.

### Historical

Past events, institutions, movements, or political decisions materially shaped the present relationship.

### Tactical

Similar methods of protest, policing, surveillance, censorship, or organising are used.

### Relational

Communities recognise connected stakes and build solidarity without claiming identical experience.

The strength of the model depends on showing which type of route is present.

A resemblance is not automatically a relationship.

---

## 🧩 Adaptation Is Not Necessarily Incoherence

Movements change their language, priorities, tactics, and visible constituencies over time.

This can be presented as evidence that the original concern was insincere.

Sometimes movements do drift.

Organisations may pursue their own interests. Opportunistic actors may attach themselves to a cause. Slogans may become less precise. Internal priorities may shift.

But change is not inherently proof of bad faith.

A movement responding to new events should be expected to change.

Since public mobilisation began, participants may have encountered:

- new deaths and displacement;
- new legal findings;
- new government decisions;
- new arms contracts;
- new restrictions;
- new arrests;
- new institutional sanctions;
- new reporting failures;
- and new forms of professional or civic involvement.

A movement that incorporated none of this would not be stable.

It would be inert.

The reporting question should therefore be:

> Is the movement abandoning its subject, or is it responding to the expanding consequences of an unresolved subject?

## 🗞️ What The Model Changes For Reporting

The rhizome model encourages reporting to move beyond isolated-event coverage.

Instead of asking only:

- How many people marched?
- Who organised it?
- Was anyone arrested?
- Which slogan caused controversy?
- Was the disruption proportionate?

Reporting can also ask:

- Through which routes did people become involved?
- Which unresolved events are sustaining participation?
- What new roots have developed since the previous march?
- Which institutions are now implicated?
- Which participants are repeatedly missing from coverage?
- What disagreements exist inside the movement?
- What remains its minimum shared centre?
- Has repression reduced harm, displaced activity, or generated another route?
- What would participants recognise as meaningful resolution?
- Why have existing democratic channels failed to absorb the dispute?

This produces a more accurate account of persistence.

It also reduces the temptation to treat repeated protest as inexplicable fixation.

---

## ⚠️ Limits Of The Model

The rhizome is an analytical tool, not a complete explanation.

It should not be used to claim that:

- all routes carry equal weight;
- all participants share equal responsibility;
- all organisations are decentralised;
- leadership and hierarchy do not exist;
- funding and coordination are irrelevant;
- every connected struggle is equivalent;
- every act undertaken in solidarity is justified;
- or Palestine exists primarily to illuminate the politics of other societies.

Networks can contain power.

They can contain exclusion, coercion, misinformation, antisemitism, racism, opportunism, and internal silencing.

Decentralisation can make accountability harder.

The language of organic growth should not romanticise conduct that requires scrutiny.

The model is useful because it expands what can be seen.

It does not remove the need to judge what is found.

---

## 🇵🇸 The Centre Must Hold

The rhizome describes the routes.

Palestine remains the centre of the inquiry.

The wider network matters because real decisions, institutions, and relationships connect people to Palestinian life and political conditions.

Those connections must not be allowed to make Palestinians disappear beneath:

- Western debates about free speech;
- institutional reputation;
- movement identity;
- professional anxiety;
- party politics;
- or the moral self-conception of solidarists.

The next orientation node, [🇵🇸 Palestine Remains Central](./🇵🇸_palestine_remains_central.md), establishes this boundary in full.

## ❓ Working Questions

When using the rhizome model, ask:

- What is the originating Palestinian issue?
- Which routes connect this issue to wider public life?
- Is each route institutional, legal, financial, professional, historical, tactical, or relational?
- Who entered the movement through each route?
- Which connections are material, and which are merely symbolic?
- What remains unresolved?
- Which new roots were created by institutional responses?
- Has one form of activity disappeared or moved elsewhere?
- Where does power sit inside the network?
- Which participants or experiences are being made invisible?
- Does the model clarify Palestinian reality or displace it?
- What evidence would show that a claimed route is real?
- What would meaningful political resolution change across the network?

---

## 🌿 Working Principle

The rhizome model does not mean that everything is the same.

It means that an unresolved struggle can travel through many connected systems, and cutting one visible route does not resolve the conditions that allowed the wider network to grow.
