# 🚀 Anti-War, Arms And Procurement Routes  
**First created:** 2026-07-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-18  
*Tracing the material systems through which war is authorised, supplied, financed, technologised, transported, and sustained — and the routes through which workers, institutions, communities, and governments can scrutinise or interrupt them.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

For some people, the route into Palestinian solidarity begins through opposition to war.

For others, it begins with a more specific material question:

- Who supplied the weapon?
- Which government authorised the export?
- Which company produced the component?
- Which institution purchased the technology?
- Which bank financed the transaction?
- Which insurer covered the risk?
- Which port or logistics company moved the goods?
- Which pension fund holds the shares?
- Which university developed the system?
- and which public authority possesses the power to stop or review the relationship?

War is often represented through leaders, soldiers, armed groups, battlefield decisions, and diplomatic statements.

But war also depends on infrastructure:

- contracts;
- factories;
- licences;
- software;
- research;
- cloud services;
- transport;
- maintenance;
- finance;
- insurance;
- fuel;
- data;
- and political authorisation.

This route follows those material relationships.

Its purpose is not to imply that every remote institutional connection creates equal responsibility.

It is to identify what the relationship is, how direct it is, what duty applies, who has control, what risk is foreseeable, and which intervention is realistically available.

> War is sustained not only through battlefield decisions, but through contracts, components, labour, data, finance, logistics, licensing, and institutions that can be mapped and scrutinised.

---

## 🕊️ Anti-War Traditions

Anti-war politics contains several different traditions.

These may include:

- pacifism;
- anti-militarism;
- conscientious objection;
- nuclear disarmament;
- internationalism;
- veteran dissent;
- feminist anti-war organising;
- religious peace traditions;
- labour opposition to arms production;
- and campaigns against particular wars or weapons.

These traditions do not all reach the same conclusions.

Some reject all military force.

Others accept force only for self-defence, civilian protection, collective security, or resistance to aggression.

Some focus on diplomacy, disarmament, arms control, war prevention, or the economic interests sustaining conflict.

The route should not treat “anti-war” as one settled ideology.

Its common concern is that organised violence should not become normal, profitable, politically convenient, or insulated from democratic scrutiny.

---

## ⚖️ Anti-War Does Not Mean Indifference To Security

People and states face genuine security threats.

These may include armed attack, terrorism, invasion, hostage-taking, rocket fire, mass atrocities, and violence against civilians.

An anti-war position should not require denial of these threats.

It should ask:

- Which response protects civilians?
- Which response escalates danger?
- What lawful alternatives exist?
- What political objective is being pursued?
- Is force likely to achieve it?
- What happens after military action?
- and who bears the long-term cost?

Security should be assessed through consequence rather than rhetoric.

A policy may be described as necessary for security while producing greater instability, radicalisation, regional escalation, civilian trauma, and repeated cycles of violence.

Anti-war scrutiny asks whether the claimed remedy is creating the conditions for the next emergency.

---

## 🛡️ Civilian Protection And Defensive Capacity

Opposition to war does not eliminate the question of defence.

Communities may require protection from armed attack, missiles, raids, terrorism, pogroms, and state or non-state violence.

The relevant distinction may be between systems designed primarily to protect, deter, intercept, evacuate, warn, rescue, or treat and systems designed to attack, occupy, destroy, displace, or exercise coercive control.

The distinction is not always clean.

Defensive systems can enable offensive policy.

Dual-use systems may support both.

A serious analysis should identify the system, function, doctrine, deployment, foreseeable use, and material effect.

“Defensive” should not operate as a label beyond scrutiny.

Neither should all protective capacity be treated as morally identical to offensive warfare.

---

## 🧱 The Permanent War Economy

War may be sustained by institutions whose economic stability depends on defence contracts, weapons sales, security procurement, military research, and continuing perceptions of threat.

This does not mean every person working in defence desires war.

Workers may depend on these industries for wages, pensions, skills, local economic stability, and community identity.

Governments may also consider defence production necessary for national security, strategic autonomy, alliance commitments, and industrial resilience.

The problem arises where political systems become structurally incentivised to expand threat, normalise military spending, protect contracts from scrutiny, and treat peaceful transition as economic loss.

Anti-war analysis should therefore include industrial policy, regional employment, retraining, alternative production, and a just transition for workers.

Disarmament without labour planning transfers the cost of peace onto workers who did not control the policy.

## 🚀 Arms, Components, And Export Control

Weapons systems are assembled through supply chains.

They may contain:

- airframes;
- engines;
- guidance systems;
- targeting equipment;
- communications;
- sensors;
- software;
- optical components;
- electronics;
- ammunition;
- explosives;
- and maintenance parts.

These may be produced across several countries by prime contractors, subsidiaries, specialist manufacturers, research institutions, and small suppliers.

The relevant relationship may involve a complete weapon, a component, a repair, an upgrade, technical support, a licence, or incorporation into a multinational programme.

Precision matters because different relationships create different legal tests, forms of control, policy options, and levels of responsibility.

---

## 📜 Export Licences

Governments may regulate military exports through licences.

Licensing systems may examine:

- the item;
- destination;
- end user;
- stated purpose;
- diversion risk;
- human-rights concerns;
- international-law risk;
- and national-security interests.

A licence may be individual, open, general, temporary, suspended, amended, or revoked.

Licensing decisions should be examined through dates.

Ask:

- When was the licence issued?
- What information was available then?
- Did the recipient’s conduct change?
- Was the risk assessment updated?
- What assurances were provided?
- What monitoring occurred?
- and which authority could suspend the transfer?

A licence is not a permanent declaration of safety.

Risk may change over time.

---

## 🔎 Risk, Knowledge, And Foreseeability

Arms-transfer scrutiny may not require proof that one exported component caused one identified death.

The relevant question may concern the foreseeable risk that exported goods could contribute to serious violations, unlawful attacks, repression, or diversion to another user.

This requires attention to past conduct, military doctrine, credible reporting, legal findings, internal assessments, public statements, and the reliability of assurances.

Risk is not certainty.

A government may be required to act before a final judicial determination if the available evidence crosses the relevant preventive threshold.

At the same time, risk should not be asserted through association alone.

The analysis should identify the evidence on which the assessment rests.

---

## 🧩 Components And Distributed Responsibility

A small component may be technically essential, easily replaceable, used across several platforms, or impossible to trace to one operation.

Its manufacturer may have limited visibility over final use.

The prime contractor or state purchaser may exercise much greater control.

This does not mean component supply is irrelevant.

It means responsibility should be mapped rather than presumed.

Ask:

- What does the component do?
- Is it incorporated into a weapon, vehicle, sensor, or communications system?
- Can the supplier control resale or end use?
- Is continuing maintenance required?
- Is the product unique?
- What did the supplier know?
- and what intervention is practically available?

A complex supply chain should not make responsibility disappear.

It should make analysis more exact.

---

## 📦 End Use And Diversion

An export may be approved for one user or purpose and later transferred, resold, integrated elsewhere, captured, or used outside the stated conditions.

End-use controls may include certificates, inspections, reporting, contractual restrictions, serial tracking, and suspension clauses.

These controls are only meaningful if compliance is checked, breaches have consequences, and political relationships do not override enforcement.

An end-user assurance is evidence.

It is not proof of future compliance.

---

## 🧨 Ammunition And Consumable Supply

Weapons platforms often attract public attention.

But continuing military operations may depend more directly on consumables such as ammunition, artillery shells, missiles, explosives, propellant, spare parts, and maintenance supplies.

These systems create recurring supply relationships.

A platform may already have been delivered.

Its continued operation may still depend on servicing, technical support, software updates, replenishment, and replacement components.

The material route should therefore examine sustainment as well as initial sale.

---

## 🔧 Maintenance, Repair, And Technical Support

Military capability depends on more than possession.

It may require maintenance, repair, calibration, engineering support, training, diagnostics, and software access.

A state or company may therefore retain leverage after delivery.

Ask:

- Who maintains the system?
- Where are repairs performed?
- Are technicians deployed?
- Are spare parts controlled?
- Does the system require proprietary software?
- Can support be suspended?
- and what would suspension affect?

Stopping maintenance may disable offensive capacity.

It may also affect defensive systems or personnel safety.

Consequences should be assessed specifically.

---

## 🧠 Training And Doctrine

Military support may include operational training, targeting practice, legal instruction, intelligence cooperation, logistics, and doctrinal exchange.

Training can improve precision, civilian protection, command discipline, and lawful conduct.

It can also increase a recipient’s overall military capacity.

A serious analysis should ask:

- What was taught?
- To whom?
- Under which agreement?
- What oversight exists?
- Were civilian-protection standards included?
- What evidence shows whether training affected conduct?
- and can cooperation be conditioned or withdrawn?

Training should not be treated as inherently benign or inherently complicit.

Its content and consequence matter.

---

## 🧾 Procurement And Public Contracts

Procurement is one of the clearest routes through which domestic institutions become connected to conflict.

Public bodies may purchase surveillance systems, security services, software, weapons, vehicles, communications, consultancy, data analysis, and infrastructure.

A procurement relationship should be mapped through buyer, supplier, contract, value, duration, service, subcontractors, performance requirements, termination provisions, and decision-making authority.

A political campaign that cannot identify the actual contract risks becoming symbolic.

An institution that refuses to disclose its relationship may prevent meaningful democratic scrutiny.

---

## 🏛️ Public Procurement Duties

Public authorities may need to consider legality, value for money, service continuity, competition, national security, equality, data protection, and human-rights or ethical risk.

These duties may conflict.

A supplier may provide a technically effective service while facing credible allegations concerning conduct elsewhere.

A public body should explain which criteria it used, what evidence it considered, whether exclusion was legally available, whether alternative suppliers existed, and how risk was mitigated.

Ethical procurement should not become improvised punishment.

It should be governed through transparent standards capable of consistent application.

---

## 🧱 Contract Lock-In

Institutions may become dependent on one supplier because of proprietary software, data formats, specialist training, infrastructure integration, long contract terms, or high transition costs.

This can reduce the practical effect of ethical review.

An institution may recognise serious risk but claim that exit is too expensive, technically impossible, operationally unsafe, or contractually prohibited.

These constraints may be real.

They should have been considered before dependence became entrenched.

Procurement scrutiny should therefore examine exit plans, interoperability, data portability, termination rights, and supplier concentration at the beginning of a contract, not only after controversy arises.

---

## 📑 Subcontracting And Opacity

A public contract may involve several layers of subcontracting.

The named supplier may rely on cloud providers, data brokers, hardware manufacturers, security companies, consultants, and offshore support.

Responsibility can become difficult to trace.

Ask:

- Which entity performs the relevant function?
- Where is data stored?
- Who employs the workers?
- Which subcontractors can access the system?
- What due diligence was performed?
- and can the buyer audit the chain?

Subcontracting distributes work.

It should not distribute accountability into invisibility.

---

## 💷 Investment, Pensions, Banking, And Insurance

War-related activity may be financed through commercial lending, bonds, share ownership, underwriting, insurance, asset management, and pension investment.

Financial relationships vary greatly.

A bank may lend directly, arrange a bond, process transactions, provide routine accounts, or hold shares through an investment fund.

These are not equivalent forms of involvement.

A credible financial map should identify the institution, service, client, value, date, purpose, and degree of control.

“Financing war” should not become a phrase detached from an actual financial mechanism.

---

## 🏦 Banks And Credit

Companies may depend on credit to build factories, acquire competitors, fulfil contracts, fund research, or manage cash flow.

Banks may provide loans, revolving credit, project finance, trade finance, and bond services.

A direct corporate loan may be more materially significant than passive share ownership.

Ask:

- What finance was provided?
- Was it restricted to a project?
- What due diligence occurred?
- What covenants exist?
- Could finance be withdrawn?
- and what consequences would follow?

---

## 🛡️ Insurance And Risk Transfer

Military and security activity may depend on insurance covering shipping, factories, cargo, liability, property, cyber risk, and political risk.

Insurance does not cause the underlying activity.

It can make activity financially possible by distributing potential loss.

Insurers may therefore possess leverage through underwriting standards, exclusions, premiums, disclosure requirements, and refusal to renew.

Campaigns should identify what is insured, by whom, against which risk, and whether withdrawal would affect the relevant activity.

---

## 📈 Share Ownership

Shares may be held by pension funds, asset managers, universities, charities, individuals, sovereign funds, and index funds.

Buying or selling shares on a secondary market does not necessarily transfer money directly to the company.

Ownership may still provide voting rights, access, influence, financial exposure, and institutional association.

Divestment may therefore function through risk reduction, public signalling, coalition pressure, and withdrawal of legitimacy.

Its effect should be described honestly.

---

## 🗳️ Shareholder Engagement

Investors may seek change through meetings, resolutions, voting, disclosure demands, board pressure, and collaborative engagement.

Engagement may be preferable where an investor possesses meaningful leverage.

It may also become indefinite delay.

Ask:

- What change was requested?
- What deadline applies?
- What evidence shows progress?
- What happens if the company refuses?
- and when does engagement become divestment?

A process without escalation may protect the investor’s reputation more than it changes corporate conduct.

---

## 💰 Profiting From Conflict

The fact that a company earns money from defence does not prove that it seeks unlawful war.

Defence production may serve legitimate security needs.

But conflict can create increased orders, rising share prices, emergency procurement, reduced scrutiny, and political pressure to expand production.

The relevant question is whether commercial incentives are shaping threat assessment, policy, lobbying, procurement, or the duration and scale of military commitment.

---

## 🛰️ Technology, Surveillance, And Dual Use

Modern military systems depend heavily on technology.

This may include cloud computing, artificial intelligence, biometric identification, satellite imagery, communications, mapping, cyber capability, data analytics, and automated decision support.

Many of these systems are dual-use.

The phrase “dual use” should begin the analysis rather than end it.

---

## ☁️ Cloud Infrastructure

Military and government systems may use commercial cloud services for storage, computing, communications, machine learning, administration, and data integration.

A cloud provider may supply general infrastructure without controlling individual operations.

It may also build specialised services for a particular customer.

Ask:

- What service is provided?
- Is it generic or customised?
- What data is processed?
- Who controls deployment?
- Can the provider audit use?
- What contractual restrictions exist?
- and can service be suspended?

Technical distance should not automatically erase responsibility.

Nor should providing general-purpose infrastructure be treated as identical to selecting a target.

---

## 🤖 Artificial Intelligence And Decision Support

Artificial intelligence may support image analysis, translation, logistics, intelligence triage, threat detection, target identification, and resource allocation.

Key questions include:

- What task does the system perform?
- What data trained it?
- What error rate exists?
- Who verifies the output?
- Can a human reject the recommendation?
- What time pressure applies?
- What audit trail remains?
- and how are civilian consequences assessed?

“Human in the loop” is not sufficient if the human has no meaningful time, information, or authority to disagree.

---

## 🎯 Targeting Systems

Targeting may involve intelligence collection, target development, legal review, weapon selection, collateral-harm estimation, authorisation, and post-strike assessment.

Technology may influence one or several stages.

The relevant question is how it affects accuracy, speed, scale, evidentiary quality, civilian-risk assessment, and human accountability.

Increasing precision may reduce harm per strike.

Increasing the number or speed of strikes may still increase total harm.

---

## 👁️ Surveillance And Population Control

Surveillance technology may include facial recognition, phone interception, location tracking, drones, biometric databases, cameras, and predictive analytics.

These systems may serve legitimate purposes involving security, missing persons, crime prevention, and border administration.

They can also support political control, collective monitoring, movement restriction, blackmail, detention, and discriminatory profiling.

A surveillance assessment should identify legal basis, target population, data collected, retention, access, error, oversight, and route to challenge a decision.

---

## 🧬 Biometrics

Biometric systems may process faces, fingerprints, irises, voice, gait, and other bodily characteristics.

Biometric data is difficult to replace if compromised.

In conflict or occupation, it may affect access to movement, work, aid, healthcare, detention review, and family reunification.

Biometric procurement should therefore be assessed through necessity, accuracy, population impact, data security, human review, and appeal.

---

## 🗺️ Mapping And Geospatial Data

Maps and satellite systems may support navigation, humanitarian response, damage assessment, evacuation, border control, surveillance, and targeting.

The same image may help locate civilians, document destruction, or plan military action.

A responsible analysis should identify who collected the data, who received it, resolution, delay, intended purpose, restrictions, and foreseeable secondary use.

---

## 🧑‍💻 Technology Workers And Conscience

Technology workers may possess specialised knowledge concerning system design, customer use, internal risk assessments, security flaws, and operational integration.

They may raise concerns through internal ethics processes, collective letters, whistleblowing, union activity, refusal, resignation, or public disclosure.

Workers should distinguish evidence, inference, rumour, and political objection.

Employers should provide credible routes to raise concerns without requiring workers to risk their careers as the first and only safety mechanism.

---

## 🛠️ Workers And Military Supply Chains

War-related supply chains depend on workers in factories, ports, warehouses, transport, software, engineering, administration, finance, and maintenance.

Workers may seek to inspect, delay, refuse, strike, disclose, or campaign against particular activity.

These actions may carry legal and employment consequences.

Workers should not be romanticised as possessing unlimited power over systems governed by executives and states.

They may still possess strategic knowledge and leverage.

---

## ⚓ Ports, Shipping, And Logistics

Military goods may move through ports, airports, roads, rail, freight companies, and private shipping networks.

Logistics can be a critical point of intervention.

But cargo identification may be difficult.

A serious claim should identify the shipment, carrier, route, destination, cargo, documentation, and legal authority.

Unverified claims can endanger workers or obstruct unrelated civilian goods.

---

## 🚛 Freight And Last-Mile Support

Military operations may depend on apparently ordinary services such as trucking, warehousing, fuel delivery, catering, communications, accommodation, and repair.

These providers may not see themselves as defence actors.

Their service may still contribute materially to military capacity.

Responsibility should be assessed through proximity, knowledge, control, substitutability, and the nature of the service.

---

## ⛽ Fuel And Energy

Military vehicles, aircraft, generators, hospitals, water systems, and civilian transport may all depend on fuel.

Restricting fuel can therefore affect both military capability and civilian survival.

Ask:

- What fuel is being supplied?
- To whom?
- Through which distribution system?
- Can military and civilian use be separated?
- Who controls allocation?
- What monitoring exists?
- and what humanitarian consequence follows from restriction?

A measure intended to limit military capacity may become collective punishment if civilian dependence is ignored.

---

## 🔄 Conversion And Just Transition

Communities dependent on defence employment may resist disarmament because they fear unemployment, regional decline, skill loss, and abandonment by government.

A just transition may require industrial investment, worker participation, retraining, wage protection, regional development, and conversion towards civilian production.

Peace policy requires economic planning.

---

## 🧑‍✈️ Veterans And Military Dissent

Veterans and serving personnel may enter anti-war routes through direct experience, concern for civilians, moral injury, knowledge of doctrine, or scepticism about political objectives.

Veteran testimony can provide important insight.

It should not be treated as automatically conclusive.

Military dissent may take forms including refusal, testimony, whistleblowing, protest, legal challenge, and support for peace movements.

---

## 🫀 Moral Injury

Moral injury may arise when a person participates in, witnesses, fails to prevent, or feels betrayed by authority over events that violate deeply held moral expectations.

It may affect soldiers, clinicians, aid workers, journalists, and civilians.

Anti-war work should not use traumatised veterans merely as political evidence.

Their testimony and recovery require care.

---

## 🏛️ Government And Democratic Authorisation

Military support may be governed through legislation, ministerial decisions, executive powers, export-control systems, classified agreements, alliance commitments, and parliamentary oversight.

A democratic accountability map should identify who authorised the policy, which information was available, whether parliament was informed, what secrecy applies, which review bodies exist, and how the public can challenge the decision.

National security may justify some confidentiality.

It should not become a blanket exemption from legal review, financial scrutiny, and democratic accountability.

---

## 🤝 Alliances And Dependency

States may supply or support military activity because of treaties, alliances, intelligence relationships, defence-industrial integration, diplomatic commitments, and strategic dependency.

These constraints matter.

They do not eliminate political choice.

Ask:

- Which commitment is binding?
- Which is customary?
- What consequences would withdrawal produce?
- Which dependencies were deliberately created?
- and can cooperation be conditioned rather than ended completely?

Strategic complexity should be mapped.

It should not be used to present policy as natural or unavoidable.

---

## 🕳️ Secrecy And Classified Relationships

Some military relationships cannot be fully disclosed.

Secrecy may protect operations, personnel, intelligence methods, vulnerabilities, and national security.

But secrecy also creates risk of unlawful conduct, misleading public statements, weak procurement, and political avoidance.

Where details cannot be public, accountability may require judicial review, parliamentary committees, inspectors, auditors, independent legal advice, and protected whistleblowing.

“Classified” describes access.

It does not establish legality.

---

## ⚖️ Legal And Ethical Risk

Military and procurement relationships may create several kinds of risk:

- legal risk;
- financial risk;
- operational risk;
- ethical risk;
- and democratic risk.

These risks overlap.

They should still be distinguished.

---

## 📣 Lobbying And Political Influence

Defence and technology companies may seek to influence procurement, export policy, regulation, military budgets, research funding, and public debate.

Influence may occur through meetings, trade bodies, donations, think tanks, former officials, advisory roles, sponsorship, and public campaigns.

Lobbying is not inherently corrupt.

The public should still be able to see who met whom, about what, with which financial interest, and what decision followed.

---

## 🚪 The Revolving Door

Officials may move between government, military service, defence companies, consultancies, and think tanks.

This movement can transfer useful expertise.

It can also create conflicts of interest, privileged access, procurement bias, and public distrust.

Safeguards may include cooling-off periods, disclosure, recusal, limits on lobbying, and independent approval.

---

## 📰 War Narratives And Procurement Legitimacy

Military spending is shaped by public narratives concerning threat, urgency, alliance, deterrence, technological competition, and national prestige.

Journalists should examine whether claims concerning capability gaps or emergency need come from actors with commercial interests, institutional incentives, or political agendas.

Procurement reporting should distinguish operational need, government assertion, industry lobbying, and independent assessment.

---

## 📸 Spectacle And Weapons Branding

Weapons systems may be marketed through dramatic footage, precision claims, successful interceptions, battlefield demonstrations, and descriptions of technological superiority.

Conflict can become a live showroom.

Terms such as “battle-tested” may convert civilian environments into commercial evidence.

Technology should not be assessed only through whether it worked as designed.

The purpose and effect of the design also matter.

---

## 🪤 The Precision Trap

Precision weapons may reduce harm relative to less accurate alternatives.

But “precision” can create excessive public confidence.

A precise weapon can still be used against an unlawful target, poor intelligence, a densely populated area, or an operation with excessive expected civilian harm.

Precision describes one technical characteristic.

It does not establish legality, necessity, proportionality, or good judgment.

---

## 🧨 The Deterrence Trap

Military capacity may be justified as deterrence.

Deterrence should be assessed through objective, target audience, credibility, proportionality, escalation risk, and evidence of actual preventive effect.

“Deterrence” should not become a general justification for unlimited force.

---

## 🕊️ Ceasefires And Material Enforcement

A ceasefire is not sustained through words alone.

It may require monitoring, agreed lines, communications, humanitarian access, release arrangements, verification, enforcement, and consequences for breach.

Arms and procurement routes remain relevant during ceasefire periods because resupply may continue, damaged systems may be repaired, forces may reposition, and parties may prepare for renewed fighting.

---

## 🧹 Demobilisation, Disarmament, And Reconstruction

Ending active conflict may require demobilisation, weapons collection, reintegration, security-sector reform, demining, unexploded-ordnance removal, and reconstruction.

Disarmament is politically difficult where people believe they will become defenceless.

Reconstruction may itself create procurement risks involving corruption, land, donor control, foreign contractors, and dependency.

The post-war economy should not reproduce the same unaccountable relationships that sustained the war.

---

## 🧹 Unexploded Ordnance And Long-Term Harm

Weapons continue causing harm after fighting stops.

Unexploded ordnance may affect homes, roads, farms, schools, and reconstruction sites.

Long-term obligations may include mapping, clearance, warning, victim assistance, rehabilitation, and responsibility for contaminated land.

The cost of a weapon is not exhausted at the moment of use.

---

## 🏗️ Reconstruction And Contractor Power

Reconstruction creates opportunities and risks.

Large contracts may determine who rebuilds, who profits, which standards apply, who owns infrastructure, and whether local workers and institutions gain capacity.

Procurement should prioritise transparency, local participation, accessibility, labour rights, anti-corruption, and durable public capacity.

---

## 🌱 From Protest To Material Intervention

Anti-war movements may act through marches, lobbying, litigation, divestment, worker organising, procurement review, shareholder action, research, boycotts, and public education.

Each intervention operates through a different mechanism.

A useful campaign should identify the target, relationship, decision-maker, requested change, legal route, likely consequence, and measure of success.

Symbolic action can be valuable.

It should not be confused with material interruption.

political decision
  ↓

  ↓
contract
  ↓

  ↓

  ↓

  ↓

  ↓

  ↓

Not every case follows every stage.

The map helps identify where knowledge, authority, leverage, and responsibility are located.

## ⚠️ Association Is Not Responsibility

A company may appear in a military supply chain without controlling the relevant operation.

An investor may hold shares without directing management.

A worker may contribute one task without knowing the final use.

A university may receive funding without producing an operational weapon.

These relationships should not be collapsed.

Responsibility may still arise through knowing contribution, direct control, foreseeable risk, failure of due diligence, or refusal to act after credible warning.

The answer is not to ignore association.

It is to establish what the association materially means.

---

## ⚠️ Purity Is Not A Procurement Strategy

Modern economies are deeply interconnected.

Institutions may be unable to eliminate every indirect relationship with defence, security, surveillance, or contested states.

A politics based on total purity may produce arbitrary targeting, symbolic exclusions, and incoherent standards.

The better goal is accountable material reduction.

Ask:

- Which relationships create the greatest risk?
- Which institution has real leverage?
- Which alternatives exist?
- What transition is feasible?
- and how will the decision be applied consistently?

---

## 🇵🇸 Palestinian Centrality

The arms and procurement route can easily become dominated by Western institutions, contracts, companies, legal processes, and movement strategy.

Palestinians may disappear from the analysis even though their lives provide its moral urgency.

The route should therefore return repeatedly to civilian consequence, Palestinian testimony, political demands, local expertise, reconstruction, and the right to shape future security arrangements.

The purpose of tracing a contract is not the intellectual satisfaction of discovering a connection.

It is to understand and alter the material conditions affecting people.

This route follows the systems through which political violence becomes materially executable.

## 🧭 Material Route Test

Before making an anti-war, arms, or procurement claim, ask:

### Relationship

- What exact relationship exists?
- Is it a weapon, component, service, licence, investment, contract, or general association?
- Is the connection direct or indirect?

### Product Or Service

- What does it do?
- Is it civilian, military, or dual-use?
- Is it customised for a particular operation or generally available?

### Authority

- Who controls production, export, purchase, deployment, or termination?
- Which institution has practical leverage?

### Knowledge

- What did each actor know?
- When did they know it?
- What credible warnings or assessments were available?

### Risk

- What harm was foreseeable?
- Which legal or ethical threshold applies?
- Did risk change over time?

### Supply Chain

- Which prime contractor, subsidiary, component supplier, financier, insurer, carrier, or maintenance provider is involved?
- Where does responsibility become strongest?

### Workers

- What control do workers possess?
- Are conscience, union, and whistleblowing routes available?
- Would intervention transfer unreasonable risk to workers?

### Procurement

- What contract exists?
- What value, duration, service, and termination terms apply?
- Are alternatives available?
- Is the institution locked in?

### Finance

- Is the relationship lending, underwriting, share ownership, insurance, or routine service?
- What leverage follows from it?

### Technology

- What data, algorithm, biometric, cloud, or surveillance function is provided?
- Is human oversight meaningful?
- Can use be audited?

### Consequence

- What effect would suspension, divestment, refusal, or termination have?
- Would it reduce harm?
- Could it undermine civilian protection or legitimate defensive capability?

### Palestinian Centrality

- Are Palestinian material conditions and political agency visible?
- Or has supply-chain mapping become detached from the people affected?

## ❓ Working Questions

When tracing anti-war, arms, and procurement routes, ask:

- What first brought attention to the material system?
- Which weapon, component, service, or contract is involved?
- Who authorised it?
- Which legal framework applies?
- What risk assessment was performed?
- When was it last reviewed?
- What did the supplier know?
- What end-use controls exist?
- Is continuing maintenance or software support required?
- Which workers and subcontractors are involved?
- Who financed and insured the activity?
- What does a pension or university actually hold?
- What leverage follows from ownership?
- Is the technology general-purpose or operationally integrated?
- How does the system affect targeting, surveillance, or movement?
- Are workers able to raise concerns safely?
- Does secrecy have independent oversight?
- What would suspension or termination change?
- Would civilian or defensive systems also be affected?
- What economic transition would workers require?
- Is the campaign seeking symbolic distance or material reduction?
- How will success be measured?
- Are standards being applied consistently across conflicts?
- and does the intervention make Palestinian civilians safer while preserving a credible route towards collective security?

## 🌿 Working Principle

War is not sustained by political declarations alone.

It depends on:

- budgets;
- licences;
- contracts;
- components;
- labour;
- data;
- finance;
- insurance;
- logistics;
- maintenance;
- and institutions willing to keep the system operating.

Tracing these relationships makes intervention possible.

But material scrutiny requires precision.

Association is not identical to control.

Investment is not identical to operational command.

A component is not identical to the completed weapon.

A worker is not identical to the executive or state making the decision.

The route is strongest when responsibility follows knowledge, authority, contribution, and foreseeable risk; when workers are protected rather than sacrificed; and when institutional action reduces material harm rather than merely performing moral distance.
