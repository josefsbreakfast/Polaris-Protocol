# 🌈 Feminist, Queer And Disability Routes  
**First created:** 2026-07-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-18  
*Tracing how bodily autonomy, gendered violence, queer life, disability, care, medical dependence, and interdependence create routes into Palestinian solidarity — while resisting selective liberation and the instrumental use of marginalised people.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

For some people, the route into Palestinian solidarity begins through questions of:

- bodily autonomy;
- gendered violence;
- reproductive healthcare;
- queer liberation;
- disability justice;
- medical access;
- care;
- dependence;
- family;
- and whose bodies are treated as disposable.

These routes may begin with recognition that war, displacement, occupation, detention, and humanitarian collapse are not experienced by every body in the same way.

People differ in their:

- mobility;
- health;
- age;
- gender;
- sexuality;
- reproductive status;
- access to care;
- family responsibilities;
- and dependence on medicine, equipment, infrastructure, or other people.

These differences affect:

- exposure to violence;
- ability to flee;
- access to aid;
- treatment by institutions;
- likelihood of survival;
- and the possibility of recovery.

Feminist, queer, and disability politics can therefore reveal forms of harm concealed by abstract language about:

- populations;
- military objectives;
- evacuation;
- aid;
- security;
- or civilian casualties.

But these routes also carry risks.

Marginalised people may be:

- used to justify violence;
- presented as proof of another society’s moral inferiority;
- reduced to symbols;
- spoken for by outside movements;
- or abandoned when their lives complicate a preferred political narrative.

The central guardrail is:

> Liberation is not universal when the rights of some women, queer people, disabled people, carers, children, or dependants are used to justify making others disposable.

## 🫀 Bodies Are Political Terrain

Political systems govern bodies through:

- movement;
- detention;
- borders;
- healthcare;
- reproduction;
- surveillance;
- identity documents;
- family status;
- and access to public space.

War intensifies this governance.

A person’s body may determine:

- whether they can flee;
- whether they can cross a checkpoint;
- whether they are searched;
- whether they receive medicine;
- whether they can carry a child;
- whether they can reach a toilet;
- whether they are presumed dangerous;
- and whether their death is publicly recognised.

Bodies are not only biological.

They are interpreted through social categories.

A young man may be presumed combatant.

A woman may be presented only as a victim or mother.

A disabled person may disappear from evacuation planning.

A queer person may be used as evidence that their whole society is undeserving of solidarity.

These interpretations distribute protection.

## 🌺 Feminist Routes Into Solidarity

Feminist routes may begin through concern about:

- violence against women and girls;
- reproductive healthcare;
- pregnancy and childbirth;
- sexual violence;
- detention;
- family separation;
- widowhood;
- care work;
- poverty;
- displacement;
- and political exclusion.

A feminist analysis asks:

- How is power distributed?
- Who performs unpaid survival labour?
- Who controls movement and reproduction?
- Who receives credibility?
- Who is protected?
- Whose vulnerability is instrumentalised?
- and whose political agency is ignored?

It should examine both:

- gendered exposure to harm;
- and women’s participation as political actors.

Palestinian women are not only:

- mothers;
- widows;
- patients;
- carers;
- or victims.

They are also:

- organisers;
- doctors;
- journalists;
- lawyers;
- teachers;
- workers;
- artists;
- political thinkers;
- and participants in competing political traditions.

Protection should not require passivity.

## 🧱 Gender Is Not A Decorative Lens

Gender analysis should not be added after the main political account as a separate human-interest section.

Gender shapes:

- work;
- movement;
- healthcare;
- detention;
- family responsibility;
- economic survival;
- access to public space;
- and exposure to violence.

For example:

- displacement may increase unpaid care work;
- destruction of sanitation may affect menstruation and pregnancy;
- detention may create gender-specific risks;
- death or injury of relatives may alter household income and responsibility;
- and restrictions on movement may change access to reproductive or specialist healthcare.

Gender is therefore part of how political structures operate.

It is not merely a category used to count different kinds of victims.

## 🤰 Pregnancy, Birth, And Reproductive Survival

Pregnancy continues during:

- bombardment;
- displacement;
- siege;
- infrastructure collapse;
- and acute shortages.

Safe reproductive healthcare may require:

- antenatal monitoring;
- skilled birth attendance;
- emergency obstetrics;
- blood products;
- surgery;
- neonatal care;
- pain relief;
- antibiotics;
- sanitation;
- nutrition;
- and reliable transport.

A pregnant person may be unable to:

- travel long distances;
- move quickly;
- sleep safely;
- carry supplies;
- or remain without toilets, clean water, and clinical supervision.

Reproductive harm may include:

- miscarriage;
- premature birth;
- untreated complications;
- infection;
- maternal injury;
- infant death;
- loss of fertility;
- and long-term trauma.

These are not secondary consequences.

They are part of civilian harm.

## 🌱 Reproductive Justice

Reproductive justice is broader than access to contraception or abortion.

It includes the ability to:

- have children;
- avoid having children;
- raise children safely;
- maintain family life;
- access healthcare;
- and live in social and environmental conditions that support survival.

Applied to Palestine, this raises questions about:

- maternity care;
- fertility treatment;
- family separation;
- destruction of homes;
- child loss;
- nutrition;
- environmental conditions;
- detention;
- movement;
- and the ability to raise children without repeated violence.

A framework that protects reproductive choice while ignoring whether families can keep their children alive is incomplete.

Reproductive freedom requires a livable world.

## 🩸 Menstruation, Sanitation, And Privacy

Menstrual needs are often treated as minor compared with food, water, or shelter.

They depend on all three.

People may require:

- menstrual products;
- clean water;
- private washing facilities;
- safe toilets;
- clean clothing;
- pain relief;
- disposal systems;
- and treatment for infection.

Displacement and overcrowding may remove privacy.

People may be unable to change products safely or regularly.

These conditions can produce:

- discomfort;
- shame;
- infection;
- exclusion from public activity;
- and increased dependence on others.

Humanitarian planning should include menstrual health as ordinary infrastructure rather than exceptional feminine need.

## 🧺 Care Work

Civilian survival depends on care.

Care may include:

- feeding;
- cleaning;
- comforting;
- finding medicine;
- carrying water;
- locating relatives;
- supporting disabled people;
- accompanying children;
- tending injuries;
- and maintaining family routines under impossible conditions.

Much of this work is:

- unpaid;
- feminised;
- invisible;
- physically demanding;
- and emotionally exhausting.

War destroys formal systems while increasing dependence on informal care.

Hospitals fail.

Schools close.

Transport disappears.

Families then absorb the work.

A feminist route asks who is carrying the survival system after institutions collapse.

---

## 🪢 Interdependence

Political culture often treats independence as the ideal human condition.

But everyone depends on:

- food systems;
- healthcare;
- infrastructure;
- family;
- workers;
- technology;
- and social trust.

Disability makes some forms of dependence more visible.

War makes everyone’s dependence harder to conceal.

People need:

- water;
- medicine;
- electricity;
- shelter;
- transport;
- communication;
- and other people.

Interdependence is not weakness.

It is a basic condition of human life.

A political system becomes cruel when it treats dependence as evidence that some lives are:

- burdensome;
- less valuable;
- or acceptable to abandon.

---

## 🧍‍♀️ Women Are Not A Moral Alibi

Women’s rights may be invoked to legitimise violence against the population to which those women belong.

The argument may take the form:

> This society mistreats women, therefore force used against it is morally progressive.

This reasoning fails.

A woman does not become safer because:

- her home is destroyed;
- her family is killed;
- her hospital closes;
- she is displaced;
- or her society is collectively punished.

Internal gender inequality is real.

External violence does not automatically liberate women from it.

Liberation imposed through mass harm is not liberation.

## 🗣️ Palestinian Feminist Agency

Palestinian feminist politics should not be treated as an imported Western lens.

Palestinian women and feminist organisers have developed analyses concerning:

- occupation;
- colonialism;
- nationalism;
- patriarchy;
- labour;
- imprisonment;
- family;
- class;
- religion;
- and political representation.

They may disagree about:

- strategy;
- secularism;
- statehood;
- resistance;
- religious tradition;
- and relationships with international feminist movements.

Outside feminist solidarity should engage Palestinian feminists as:

- thinkers;
- organisers;
- critics;
- and political actors.

It should not select only voices that reproduce an outside movement’s preferred analysis.

## 🧭 Gendered Nationalism

National movements may assign women symbolic roles as:

- mothers of the nation;
- cultural guardians;
- bearers of demographic continuity;
- victims requiring protection;
- or proof of national morality.

This can elevate women rhetorically while restricting their political autonomy.

State and non-state actors may both use:

- women’s bodies;
- fertility;
- clothing;
- sexuality;
- and family roles

to define national belonging.

A feminist route asks whether women can participate as full political subjects rather than vessels through which national identity is reproduced.

---

## 🚨 Sexual Violence

Sexual violence must be approached with:

- seriousness;
- evidentiary discipline;
- survivor-centred practice;
- and resistance to political instrumentalisation.

Reports may concern:

- rape;
- sexual assault;
- forced nudity;
- sexualised torture;
- threats;
- humiliation;
- invasive searches;
- and gendered abuse in detention or conflict.

Claims should be assessed through:

- testimony;
- medical evidence;
- independent investigation;
- patterns;
- and the conditions under which evidence could be collected.

Survivors should not be required to provide immediate public proof.

At the same time, allegations should not be inflated, denied automatically, or used to justify collective punishment.

Responsibility belongs to perpetrators and enabling structures.

It does not transfer to an entire population.

---

## 🧨 Sexual Violence As A Political Weapon

Sexual violence allegations can be used in several harmful ways.

They may be:

- denied because they implicate a favoured actor;
- exaggerated because they mobilise public support;
- racialised as evidence of inherent barbarism;
- stripped from survivor testimony;
- or used to justify violence against civilians.

A survivor-centred approach asks:

- What does the survivor need?
- What evidence can be preserved?
- What protection is required?
- Who controls the narrative?
- What legal process is available?
- and how can further harm be prevented?

The survivor should not become a national symbol owned by political institutions.

---

## 🧍 Gendered Assumptions About Civilian Status

Adult men are frequently treated as presumptively dangerous.

Casualty descriptions may distinguish:

- women;
- children;
- and everyone else.

This can imply that adult men are:

- combatants;
- less innocent;
- or less mournable.

Civilian status should not be inferred solely from:

- age;
- sex;
- clothing;
- location;
- or political environment.

Men may be:

- doctors;
- teachers;
- carers;
- journalists;
- fathers;
- workers;
- students;
- disabled people;
- or civilians performing ordinary survival tasks.

Gender analysis should oppose the disposability of men as well as the instrumentalisation of women.

## 🏳️‍🌈 Queer Routes Into Solidarity

Queer routes may begin through:

- opposition to state violence;
- concern for queer Palestinians;
- anti-colonial politics;
- resistance to policing and surveillance;
- experiences of stigma;
- or rejection of selective LGBT rights.

Queer solidarity asks whether liberation is being applied consistently.

It resists the idea that:

- queer rights belong only to certain states;
- queer Palestinians can be recognised only by leaving Palestinian society;
- or alleged homophobia makes an entire population less worthy of civilian protection.

Queer people exist within every population affected by war.

They may be:

- visible;
- private;
- closeted;
- politically active;
- religious;
- secular;
- supported;
- isolated;
- or connected through informal networks.

They do not disappear because public narratives fail to see them.

## 🌈 Queer Palestinian Life

Queer Palestinians are not one political constituency.

They may differ in:

- identity;
- language;
- visibility;
- religion;
- class;
- location;
- family relationship;
- politics;
- and preferred strategy.

Some may identify publicly.

Others may not use Western LGBT terminology.

Some may prioritise:

- national liberation;
- family;
- privacy;
- community reform;
- asylum;
- anti-occupation work;
- queer organising;
- or several of these together.

Outside movements should not demand that queer Palestinians choose between:

- Palestinian identity;
- queer identity;
- family;
- safety;
- and political belonging.

Their political lives are allowed to be complex.

## 🧼 Pinkwashing

Pinkwashing describes the use of LGBT rights or queer-friendly branding to improve the image of a:

- state;
- institution;
- military;
- company;
- or political project

while diverting attention from other harms.

The term should be used precisely.

The existence of real LGBT protections does not automatically make discussion of them propaganda.

Nor do LGBT protections erase:

- occupation;
- civilian harm;
- discrimination;
- or unequal political rights.

A pinkwashing analysis should identify:

- the claim being promoted;
- the institution promoting it;
- the audience;
- the political context;
- the omitted harms;
- and how queer identity is being used.

The goal is not to deny real advances.

It is to reject their use as moral immunity.

---

## 🧭 The Civilisational Comparison

Public narratives may contrast:

- a modern, liberal, LGBT-friendly society;
- with a supposedly backward, homophobic, or culturally primitive population.

This framing converts queer rights into a measure of civilisational worth.

It can imply that civilians in a less tolerant society deserve less protection.

That is unacceptable.

A person’s right not to be:

- killed;
- displaced;
- starved;
- detained arbitrarily;
- or deprived of healthcare

does not depend on their society having reached a particular position on LGBT law.

Human rights are not rewards issued to culturally approved populations.

---

## 🏠 Home, Family, And Queer Survival

Queer life is shaped by more than formal law.

Safety may depend on:

- family;
- housing;
- employment;
- privacy;
- healthcare;
- community;
- and the ability to control disclosure.

War and displacement can destroy these protective arrangements.

A queer person may lose:

- private communication;
- trusted friends;
- medication;
- housing;
- safe meeting places;
- and control over who knows their identity.

A communal shelter may create new risks.

So may detention, interrogation, or data exposure.

Queer humanitarian protection should consider privacy as a survival need.

---

## 🔐 Outing, Surveillance, And Coercion

Information about sexuality or gender identity can be weaponised.

Risks may include:

- outing;
- blackmail;
- coercion;
- family exposure;
- employment consequences;
- and pressure to collaborate.

Surveillance systems may collect or infer sensitive information through:

- phones;
- messages;
- location data;
- social networks;
- medical records;
- and digital platforms.

Queer people should not be forced to trade privacy for:

- movement;
- protection;
- asylum;
- or access to services.

Evidence concerning coercion or blackmail should be assessed carefully and reported without exposing individuals further.

---

## 🧳 Asylum And Escape Narratives

Queer Palestinians may seek asylum or relocation because of:

- family violence;
- social stigma;
- political persecution;
- occupation;
- war;
- or overlapping dangers.

Their experience should not be reduced to a simple rescue narrative in which:

- Palestinian society is the sole danger;
- another state is the uncomplicated saviour;
- and political conditions disappear.

Asylum systems may themselves be:

- hostile;
- invasive;
- disbelieving;
- and dependent on stereotyped performances of queer identity.

A person may need safety from more than one source.

Their need for asylum does not justify collective condemnation of the society they left.

## 🏳️‍⚧️ Trans And Gender-Diverse People

Trans and gender-diverse people may face particular risks involving:

- documentation;
- medication;
- body searches;
- detention;
- shelter allocation;
- healthcare;
- privacy;
- and exposure to violence.

Conflict can interrupt access to:

- hormones;
- specialist care;
- mental-health support;
- and safe social networks.

Identity documents that do not match appearance may increase danger at:

- checkpoints;
- borders;
- hospitals;
- aid distribution points;
- and security searches.

Humanitarian systems should not assume binary gender or require public disclosure to provide appropriate support.

## ♿ Disability As A Central Route

Disability should not be added as the final category after other analysis is complete.

It is central to:

- civilian protection;
- evacuation;
- healthcare;
- displacement;
- detention;
- infrastructure;
- communication;
- and long-term recovery.

Disabled people may have:

- mobility impairments;
- sensory impairments;
- intellectual disabilities;
- chronic illness;
- mental-health conditions;
- neurocognitive needs;
- fatigue;
- pain;
- or dependence on medical technology.

These conditions interact with political systems.

A restriction that appears equal on paper may be impossible for a disabled person to comply with.

---

## 🦽 Evacuation Is Not Universally Executable

An evacuation order assumes a person can:

- receive it;
- understand it;
- act quickly;
- travel;
- carry supplies;
- navigate the route;
- and survive at the destination.

Many people cannot.

A person may:

- be unable to walk;
- depend on oxygen;
- be bedridden;
- require multiple carers;
- be unable to hear warnings;
- be unable to see signage;
- experience cognitive confusion;
- or lack accessible transport.

The correct question is not only whether warning was given.

It is whether evacuation was materially possible for the people expected to move.

---

## 🔌 Infrastructure Dependence

Some disabled and chronically ill people depend on:

- electricity;
- refrigeration;
- clean water;
- charging;
- communications;
- oxygen;
- ventilation;
- feeding equipment;
- dialysis;
- and temperature control.

Infrastructure destruction can therefore become direct bodily harm.

A power cut may mean:

- a wheelchair cannot be charged;
- insulin cannot be stored;
- an oxygen concentrator stops;
- a communication device fails;
- or a person can no longer summon assistance.

Infrastructure is part of the body’s support system.

Destroying it does not affect all civilians equally.

---

## 💊 Medication And Continuity Of Care

Many conditions require consistent treatment.

Interruptions may cause:

- withdrawal;
- relapse;
- seizures;
- infection;
- uncontrolled pain;
- organ damage;
- psychiatric crisis;
- or death.

People may lose:

- prescriptions;
- records;
- specialist contact;
- dosage information;
- refrigeration;
- and access to pharmacies.

Humanitarian provision should account for long-term and non-emergency medicines.

A system focused only on acute trauma can abandon people whose survival depends on ordinary continuity.

## 🦿 Conflict Creates Disability

War creates new disability through:

- blast injury;
- burns;
- amputation;
- spinal injury;
- brain injury;
- sensory loss;
- infection;
- malnutrition;
- untreated disease;
- and psychological trauma.

Survival is therefore the beginning of a long-term obligation.

People may require:

- surgery;
- prosthetics;
- rehabilitation;
- pain management;
- accessible housing;
- mobility equipment;
- carers;
- education;
- employment support;
- and social inclusion.

A casualty count does not capture the future care system required by mass injury.

---

## 🏥 Rehabilitation Is Essential Infrastructure

Rehabilitation is sometimes treated as optional care delivered after the emergency.

It is part of survival.

Without rehabilitation, an injury may result in:

- avoidable pain;
- contracture;
- infection;
- permanent loss of function;
- dependence;
- pressure wounds;
- or social isolation.

Rehabilitation may include:

- physiotherapy;
- occupational therapy;
- prosthetics;
- orthotics;
- speech and language therapy;
- psychological support;
- pain treatment;
- and adaptation of homes and public spaces.

Reconstruction should not rebuild inaccessible systems.

---

## 🧠 Trauma, Distress, And Mental Health

Conflict produces:

- fear;
- grief;
- sleep disruption;
- dissociation;
- anxiety;
- depression;
- traumatic stress;
- and complicated bereavement.

These responses should not automatically be pathologised.

Many are understandable reactions to:

- danger;
- loss;
- displacement;
- and uncertainty.

People with existing mental-health conditions may lose:

- medication;
- clinicians;
- routines;
- privacy;
- support networks;
- and safe environments.

Mental-health provision should not replace political analysis.

Distress may be caused by conditions that require material and political change, not only individual treatment.

---

## 🧠 Intellectual And Cognitive Disability

People with intellectual or cognitive disabilities may face barriers to:

- understanding warnings;
- communicating needs;
- navigating displacement;
- giving consent;
- and accessing aid.

They may rely heavily on:

- family;
- familiar routines;
- known environments;
- and trusted carers.

Separation can be catastrophic.

Humanitarian systems should provide:

- simple communication;
- supported decision-making;
- identification of carers;
- safeguarding;
- and continuity of relationships.

A person’s inability to explain their needs does not make those needs less urgent.

---

## 👂 Communication Access

Warnings and aid information may be inaccessible to people who are:

- Deaf;
- hard of hearing;
- blind;
- visually impaired;
- non-speaking;
- cognitively impaired;
- or unable to read the language used.

Accessible communication may require:

- sign language;
- captions;
- audio;
- large print;
- tactile information;
- plain language;
- pictograms;
- and trusted interpersonal communication.

A message is not effective merely because it was broadcast.

It must be receivable and understandable by the people expected to act on it.

---

## 🧑‍🦯 Navigation And The Built Environment

Destruction changes familiar geography.

People may lose:

- tactile landmarks;
- accessible routes;
- lifts;
- ramps;
- street signs;
- lighting;
- and safe pavements.

Rubble creates particular risk for people with:

- mobility impairments;
- visual impairments;
- balance problems;
- fatigue;
- and pain.

Temporary shelters and aid sites may also be inaccessible.

Accessibility should be built into:

- routes;
- transport;
- distribution;
- sanitation;
- shelter;
- and reconstruction.

---

## 🧑‍🍼 Dependants And Carers

Disabled people are sometimes described only as dependants.

Carers are described only as helpers.

The relationship is more complex.

Disabled people may also be:

- parents;
- workers;
- organisers;
- professionals;
- and carers for others.

Carers may themselves be:

- disabled;
- elderly;
- injured;
- traumatised;
- or responsible for several people.

Humanitarian systems should not assume an endless supply of unpaid family care.

If a carer is killed, injured, detained, or displaced, the person relying on them may face immediate danger.

---

## 🏚️ Institutionalisation And Segregation

Where community care collapses, disabled people may be moved into:

- hospitals;
- institutions;
- collective shelters;
- or improvised facilities.

This can create risks involving:

- neglect;
- abuse;
- isolation;
- loss of autonomy;
- infection;
- and separation from family.

Emergency provision should not become permanent segregation.

Recovery should support disabled people to live within communities with:

- assistance;
- accessibility;
- autonomy;
- and family or chosen relationships.

---

## 🪦 Whose Death Is Counted?

Disability affects casualty recording.

A person may die because:

- dialysis stopped;
- medicine became unavailable;
- infection went untreated;
- food was inaccessible;
- evacuation was impossible;
- or a carer was lost.

These deaths may not be recorded as direct conflict deaths.

The distinction between direct and indirect harm is analytically useful.

It should not hide causation.

A full account should consider:

- excess mortality;
- interrupted healthcare;
- disease;
- disability-related risk;
- and delayed consequences.

---

## 🧬 Eugenic Logic And Disposable Bodies

Eugenic logic does not always appear as an explicit programme.

It may appear whenever institutions assume that some lives are:

- too dependent;
- too expensive;
- too complicated;
- unlikely to recover;
- or acceptable to deprioritise.

In conflict, triage may be unavoidable.

But scarcity decisions should not be shaped by unexamined assumptions that disability means:

- poor quality of life;
- reduced social value;
- or futility.

Disabled people should not have to prove exceptional productivity to merit survival.

---

## 🏥 Medical Neutrality And Selective Sympathy

Medical imagery often produces humanitarian sympathy.

But public attention may favour people whose injuries appear:

- visually legible;
- acute;
- and potentially curable.

People with chronic, psychiatric, cognitive, or invisible conditions may remain unseen.

Disability justice widens medical concern beyond dramatic injury.

It asks whether people can continue living with:

- ordinary medicine;
- stable care;
- accessible environments;
- and control over their own bodies.

---

## 🌍 Disability And Colonial Power

Colonial and military systems may both:

- create disability;
- classify bodies;
- regulate movement;
- and determine access to care.

Disability can be intensified by:

- poverty;
- inaccessible infrastructure;
- environmental damage;
- border controls;
- permit systems;
- and unequal healthcare investment.

The impairment may be bodily.

The disabling environment is political.

This does not mean every disability is caused by politics.

It means political conditions shape whether impairment becomes:

- exclusion;
- danger;
- dependence;
- or premature death.

---

## 🕊️ Disability Is Not A Metaphor

Disability language is often used metaphorically to describe institutions as:

- blind;
- deaf;
- crippled;
- paralysed;
- or insane.

This turns disabled bodies into shorthand for failure.

The political analysis usually does not need it.

Use specific language:

- institutionally indifferent;
- unable to act;
- deliberately unresponsive;
- structurally constrained;
- incoherent;
- or dysfunctional.

Disabled people should appear as political subjects, not metaphors for broken systems.

---

## 🏳️‍🌈 Queer And Disability Intersection

Queer disabled people may face overlapping barriers involving:

- healthcare;
- family recognition;
- chosen carers;
- privacy;
- shelter;
- medication;
- and legal identity.

A person’s recognised family may not include the partner or friend who actually provides care.

This can affect:

- access;
- medical decisions;
- evacuation;
- information;
- and reunification.

Humanitarian systems should recognise chosen family and trusted support relationships where possible.

## 🌺 Feminist And Disability Intersection

Feminist and disability routes meet through:

- reproductive justice;
- care work;
- medical autonomy;
- sterilisation;
- institutionalisation;
- sexual violence;
- poverty;
- and assumptions about dependence.

Disabled women may face increased risk of:

- abuse;
- neglect;
- inaccessible reproductive care;
- and exclusion from public decision-making.

They may also be treated as:

- asexual;
- incapable of parenting;
- or permanently dependent.

A feminist politics that ignores disabled women reproduces the hierarchy it claims to oppose.

## 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Chosen Family And Survival Networks

People survive through relationships that may not fit formal categories.

Chosen family may be especially important for:

- queer people;
- disabled people;
- estranged people;
- migrants;
- and those living outside conventional households.

Emergency systems often prioritise:

- spouses;
- parents;
- children;
- and legally recognised relatives.

This may exclude the person who actually:

- provides care;
- knows the medication;
- communicates on someone’s behalf;
- or offers the safest home.

Humanitarian and institutional systems should make room for trusted relationships beyond formal kinship.

---

## 🪧 Movement Accessibility

Solidarity movements can reproduce exclusion.

Participation may be organised around:

- marches;
- long meetings;
- crowded rooms;
- loud chants;
- inaccessible venues;
- police contact;
- and constant online engagement.

Disabled people may be praised symbolically while being practically excluded.

Accessible organising may require:

- remote participation;
- captions;
- sign-language interpretation;
- quiet spaces;
- seating;
- accessible toilets;
- transport;
- rest;
- clear schedules;
- and roles that do not depend on physical presence.

Movement accessibility is part of political equality.

---

## 💻 Digital Access And Online Organising

Online spaces can enable participation for people unable to attend physically.

They can also create barriers through:

- inaccessible images;
- missing captions;
- rapid information;
- harassment;
- sensory overload;
- and platform exclusion.

Accessible digital organising should include:

- alt text;
- captions;
- transcripts;
- clear formatting;
- content warnings where useful;
- and documents compatible with assistive technology.

Remote participation should include real decision-making power.

It should not become a spectator tier.

---

## 🧺 Care Within Movements

Movements often depend on invisible work such as:

- checking on distressed participants;
- arranging lifts;
- bringing food;
- translating;
- moderating conflict;
- providing first aid;
- supporting arrestees;
- and maintaining communication.

This labour is often performed by:

- women;
- queer people;
- disabled people;
- and people already carrying care responsibilities.

A movement that consumes carers without caring for them will become unstable.

Care should be:

- shared;
- resourced;
- bounded;
- and recognised as political work.

---

## 🧨 The Rescue Narrative

Marginalised people may be used to justify intervention by presenting them as waiting to be rescued by a more enlightened power.

The rescue narrative often contains:

- a backward society;
- a passive victim;
- a civilised rescuer;
- and violence presented as emancipation.

This structure can appear in claims about:

- women;
- queer people;
- religious minorities;
- and disabled people.

It removes the political agency of the people supposedly being rescued.

A serious solidarity route asks what affected people themselves:

- want;
- organise;
- fear;
- and propose.

---

## ⚠️ Selective Universality

Selective universality occurs when a principle is presented as universal but applied only to politically favoured people.

Examples include:

- defending queer rights while accepting queer civilian deaths;
- defending women’s freedom while destroying maternity care;
- praising disability inclusion while supporting inaccessible evacuation;
- condemning sexual violence selectively;
- or treating bodily autonomy as irrelevant under military necessity.

A principle is not universal because its language is universal.

It becomes universal through consistent application.

---

## 🧭 Liberation Cannot Be Conditional

Civilian protection should not depend on whether a person is:

- feminist;
- queer-friendly;
- secular;
- politically moderate;
- independent;
- productive;
- or socially approved.

People possess rights even where their views are:

- conservative;
- religious;
- patriarchal;
- prejudiced;
- or politically uncomfortable.

Those beliefs may deserve criticism and organised opposition.

They do not make a civilian killable.

Liberation politics loses its universality when it treats rights as rewards for ideological agreement.

---

## 🇵🇸 Palestinian Centrality

Feminist, queer, and disability routes should return continually to Palestinian life.

Palestinians should not appear only as:

- women needing rescue;
- queer people needing extraction;
- disabled casualties;
- or examples in an outside political argument.

They are people with:

- political analysis;
- internal disagreements;
- organisations;
- relationships;
- humour;
- culture;
- family;
- professional knowledge;
- and competing visions of liberation.

Outside movements should contribute:

- solidarity;
- resources;
- attention;
- protection;
- and institutional pressure.

They should not claim ownership over the people whose lives make the route meaningful.

## 🧭 Route Test

Before using feminist, queer, or disability framing, ask:

### Palestinian Centrality

- Are Palestinian people speaking and acting?
- Or are they being used as examples within an outside political argument?
- Are local organisations and analyses visible?

### Feminist Analysis

- Are women treated as political actors?
- Is gendered harm being described specifically?
- Are pregnancy, reproductive care, sanitation, care work, and economic survival included?
- Are women’s rights being used to justify collective violence?

### Sexual Violence

- Are allegations handled with seriousness and evidence?
- Are survivors protected?
- Is responsibility individualised?
- Is sexual violence being denied or instrumentalised?

### Queer Analysis

- Are queer Palestinians recognised as complex political subjects?
- Is privacy protected?
- Is LGBT progress being used as moral immunity?
- Is social conservatism being used to justify civilian harm?

### Disability

- Can warnings be received and acted upon?
- Is evacuation accessible?
- Are medicine, equipment, electricity, carers, and rehabilitation included?
- Are indirect disability-related deaths visible?
- Is disability being used metaphorically?

### Care

- Who is performing survival work?
- What happens if carers are lost?
- Are chosen families and trusted relationships recognised?
- Is unpaid labour being consumed without support?

### Universality

- Are rights applied consistently?
- Does protection depend on ideological approval?
- Are some bodies being treated as more disposable than others?

### Movement Practice

- Can disabled, queer, caring, and low-income participants take part?
- Are online and physical spaces accessible?
- Is care work shared?

---

## ❓ Working Questions

When tracing feminist, queer, and disability routes, ask:

- How do gender, sexuality, disability, and care alter exposure to harm?
- Are women visible beyond motherhood and victimhood?
- What happens to pregnancy and birth when healthcare collapses?
- Who performs unpaid survival work?
- How are sexual-violence claims being evidenced and communicated?
- Are survivors being centred or instrumentalised?
- Are adult men being treated as presumptive combatants?
- How are queer Palestinians describing their own priorities?
- Is queer identity being used to legitimise state power?
- What privacy risks arise through surveillance and displacement?
- Can trans and gender-diverse people access safe care and documentation?
- Which disabled people cannot comply with evacuation instructions?
- Which lives depend on electricity, medicine, equipment, or carers?
- How will newly disabled people receive long-term rehabilitation?
- Are chosen families recognised?
- Are movement spaces accessible?
- Is care work sustainable?
- Do universal principles remain universal when applied to Palestinians?
- Are marginalised people being asked to validate an outside political narrative?
- and does the route lead towards bodily autonomy, material survival, political agency, and freedom from disposable-life politics?

---

## 🌿 Working Principle

Feminist, queer, and disability politics reveal that violence is experienced through bodies, relationships, infrastructure, dependence, and unequal social value.

These routes should not be decorative additions to a general account of war.

They show:

- who can flee;
- who can access medicine;
- who performs survival work;
- who controls disclosure;
- whose family is recognised;
- whose suffering is believed;
- and whose life is treated as expendable.

Liberation is not universal when marginalised people are used to justify the abandonment of other marginalised people.

The route is strongest when it preserves Palestinian agency, treats disability and care as central, opposes sexual and gendered violence without instrumentalising survivors, and refuses to make civilian protection conditional on political or cultural approval.
