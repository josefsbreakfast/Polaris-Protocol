# 🧭 Movement Legibility  
**First created:** 2026-08-01 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-01  
*How to describe a plural, distributed, changing movement without mistaking its crowds, events, organisations, networks, speakers, demands, or incidents for one actor.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Large movements are not one thing.

They may include:

- organisations;
- coalitions;
- informal networks;
- recurring crowds;
- local groups;
- professional constituencies;
- faith communities;
- individual organisers;
- first-time participants;
- and unaffiliated people.

They may operate through:

- marches;
- vigils;
- encampments;
- workplace action;
- professional campaigns;
- legal cases;
- cultural events;
- fundraising;
- elections;
- local organising;
- online communication;
- and private support networks.

The same movement may appear differently from one week to the next.

One event may be highly centralised.

Another may be assembled through a coalition.

Another may attract a crowd larger and more politically diverse than the organisations that convened it.

A visible speaker may possess little organisational authority.

A less visible organiser may control logistics but not political messaging.

A slogan may be personal rather than agreed.

An incident may be serious without being representative.

A coalition may coordinate shared action without becoming one command structure.

Movement legibility therefore requires distinction.

Its governing proposition is:

> A movement becomes legible when its plurality is mapped rather than mistaken for either unity or chaos.

This cluster asks:

> What kind of actor, event, relationship, authority, or position is actually being described, and what evidence supports that description?

## 🧱 Core Distinctions

### Crowd Is Not Movement

A crowd is a group present at a particular time and place.

A movement is a wider political field that may persist across events, organisations, institutions, and forms of action.

### Event Is Not Organisation

An event may be convened by:

- one organisation;
- a coalition;
- an informal network;
- or several groups acting in parallel.

The event itself is not an organisation.

### Organisation Is Not Coalition

An organisation usually has some continuing structure, such as:

- membership;
- leadership;
- governance;
- branding;
- rules;
- or a declared programme.

A coalition coordinates autonomous participants around limited shared work.

### Coalition Is Not Command Structure

Coalition members may share an action without one body controlling every participant.

### Network Is Not Membership

A network describes relationships, communication, or recurring cooperation.

It does not automatically establish:

- command;
- funding;
- formal affiliation;
- ideological agreement;
- membership;
- or responsibility.

### Organiser Is Not Participant

An organiser may possess authority over:

- route;
- speakers;
- messaging;
- access;
- stewardship;
- safeguarding;
- or logistics.

A participant may simply attend.

### Speaker Is Not Constituency

A person speaking at an event does not automatically represent:

- every organiser;
- every participant;
- an entire movement;
- or everyone sharing their identity.

### Slogan Is Not Platform

A slogan may be:

- personal;
- organisational;
- improvised;
- disputed;
- inherited;
- or peripheral.

It should not be treated as the movement’s formal platform without evidence.

### Demand Is Not Ideology

People may support the same demand through different political analyses.

### Presence Is Not Endorsement

Attending an event does not establish endorsement of every actor, sign, slogan, speech, or incident present.

### Absence Is Not Opposition

Someone may miss an event because of:

- disability;
- care responsibilities;
- work;
- cost;
- fear;
- legal restrictions;
- exhaustion;
- illness;
- distance;
- or participation through another route.

### Visibility Is Not Authority

The most photographed or frequently quoted actor may not possess the greatest organisational authority.

### Decentralisation Is Not Disorder

A movement may distribute authority intentionally.

### Plurality Is Not Incoherence

Participants may disagree while retaining a clear shared demand.

## 🧭 Event, Actor, Relationship, Authority, Position

A useful movement description separates five layers.

### Event

What happened?

Possible events include:

- march;
- vigil;
- encampment;
- strike;
- meeting;
- legal action;
- fundraising campaign;
- workplace action;
- cultural event;
- direct action;
- or online mobilisation.

### Actor

Who participated?

Possible actor types include:

- individual;
- informal group;
- organisation;
- coalition;
- network;
- institution;
- constituency;
- or unaffiliated crowd.

### Relationship

How are the actors connected?

Possible relationships include:

- membership;
- affiliation;
- endorsement;
- co-sponsorship;
- logistical support;
- speaker invitation;
- recurring cooperation;
- information sharing;
- funding;
- or mere co-presence.

### Authority

What could the actor legitimately decide or represent?

Possible authority includes:

- personal speech;
- organisational mandate;
- event logistics;
- coalition communication;
- professional expertise;
- lived experience;
- elected leadership;
- or no demonstrated representative authority.

### Position

What claim, demand, tactic, or analysis is actually supported?

This should be evidenced through:

- formal statements;
- published policy;
- agreed demands;
- recorded speech;
- direct testimony;
- or observable conduct.

This five-layer model prevents one visible person, sign, or incident from being mistaken for the entire movement.

## 🪜 Movement Levels

Claims should be attached to the correct level.

### Participant Level

The individual person and their own conduct or views.

### Event Level

The actors, demands, incidents, and arrangements present at one event.

### Organisational Level

The formal position and conduct of a named body.

### Coalition Level

The limited shared commitments agreed among autonomous organisations or groups.

### Movement Level

The broader political field connecting multiple events, constituencies, organisations, networks, and routes of participation.

### Social-Field Level

The wider public conditions from which support, opposition, institutional response, and alternative participation emerge.

A claim established at one level should not automatically be transferred to another.

For example:

- one participant’s sign is not automatically an event demand;
- one event demand is not automatically an organisational platform;
- one organisation’s policy is not automatically the coalition position;
- one coalition’s action is not automatically the whole movement;
- and one visible movement is not the entire social field of concern.

## 🏷️ Controlled Relationship Language

Movement description should use relationship terms carefully.

### Member Of

Use where formal or reliably evidenced membership exists.

### Affiliated With

Use where an acknowledged formal relationship exists but membership may not.

### Organised By

Use where an actor exercised material responsibility for convening or managing the event.

### Co-Sponsored By

Use where named actors publicly supported, resourced, or formally shared responsibility for an event.

### Endorsed By

Use where a body formally expressed support without necessarily organising the action.

### Participated In

Use where attendance or involvement is established without implying control.

### Spoke At

Use where a person appeared as a speaker without assuming endorsement of every view they hold.

### Worked Alongside

Use where cooperation is shown but the exact formal relationship remains unclear.

### Funded By

Use where a material funding relationship is supported by evidence.

Funding alone does not establish control over every action.

### Connected To

Use cautiously and define the connection immediately.

### Associated With

This is often too vague to be useful.

Where used, it should be immediately explained.

### Represented By

Use only where a mandate, recognised office, or representative authority is established.

## 🎙️ Mandate And Representation

Movement speech may occur in several capacities.

### Personal Speech

A person expresses their own position.

### Lived-Experience Speech

A person describes experience connected to:

- identity;
- family;
- profession;
- religion;
- disability;
- displacement;
- or political participation.

### Professional Speech

A person speaks within an area of demonstrated expertise.

### Organisational Speech

A person communicates a formal position under a mandate from a named body.

### Coalition Speech

A person communicates an agreed coalition position within a defined scope.

### Population-Level Claim

A person claims to represent an entire:

- people;
- faith;
- nationality;
- diaspora;
- profession;
- or constituency.

These capacities should not be treated as interchangeable.

A Jewish participant may speak as a Jewish participant without representing Jews.

A Palestinian participant may speak from Palestinian experience without representing all Palestinians.

A clinician may speak from medical expertise without defining the movement’s entire political programme.

An organiser may describe agreed demands without controlling every participant.

A reporter, institution, or researcher should ask:

- In what capacity is this person speaking?
- What mandate exists?
- Who granted it?
- What is its scope?
- Is the speaker describing personal experience, formal policy, or a wider constituency?
- What remains outside their authority?

## 📣 Demands, Slogans And Platforms

Movement positions should be classified.

### Agreed Demand

A claim formally adopted by an organisation, coalition, or event.

### Campaign Objective

A stated outcome pursued through continuing action.

### Organisational Policy

A position formally attached to a named body.

### Coalition Position

A limited claim agreed among coalition members.

### Public Slogan

A compressed political phrase used in public mobilisation.

### Individual Sign

A message displayed by one participant.

### Chant

A repeated phrase audible at an event.

### Speech

A position expressed by a named speaker.

### Rhetorical Frame

Language used to interpret the issue rather than define a concrete demand.

### Long-Term Ideology

A wider theory of politics, statehood, liberation, religion, or social order.

These categories may overlap.

They should not be silently merged.

The analysis should ask:

- Is the demand written?
- Who adopted it?
- Is it part of the event description?
- Is it shared by every organiser?
- Is the slogan official or merely present?
- Is the position contested?
- Does the long-term ideology exceed the scope of the current action?

## 🧮 Participation, Scale And Composition

Movement size is difficult to establish.

Possible measures include:

- organiser estimate;
- police estimate;
- independent estimate;
- venue capacity;
- image-based estimate;
- registration;
- membership;
- online reach;
- repeat attendance;
- and unique participation.

These figures measure different things.

A march of 100,000 people does not necessarily establish 100,000 continuing members.

Ten repeated events do not automatically equal ten separate constituencies.

Online followers do not establish active participation.

Formal membership does not capture every supporter.

Low visible turnout does not prove declining concern.

Participation analysis should consider:

- accessibility;
- transport;
- weather;
- policing;
- legal restrictions;
- fear;
- work;
- care;
- disability;
- geography;
- cost;
- religious observance;
- illness;
- and competing forms of action.

A useful rule is:

> Count the event you can observe. Do not quietly convert it into a total movement population.

Composition claims require evidence.

Do not infer demographic identity only from:

- appearance;
- dress;
- name;
- location;
- political slogan;
- or organisational presence.

Where composition is uncertain, say so.

## 🕰️ Movement Change Over Time

Movements change.

They may also preserve:

- symbols;
- slogans;
- routes;
- recurring organisers;
- shared demands;
- and broad moral commitments.

Continuity and change can coexist.

A comparison across events should ask:

- Which organisations remained?
- Which entered?
- Which left?
- Did the shared demand change?
- Did the event type change?
- Did participation move into other institutions?
- Did policing or legal conditions alter?
- Did geographic spread change?
- Did professional, workplace, student, or faith participation become more visible?
- Did coalition governance change?
- Were previous participants removed through fear, injury, discipline, or exhaustion?
- Did one route decline while another expanded?
- What evidence supports continuity between the moments?

Movement change should not be inferred only from visual familiarity or unfamiliarity.

The same march route may host a changed movement.

A changed tactic may be used by many of the same participants.

## ⚠️ Incident Attribution And Representativeness

Movements may contain real:

- misconduct;
- discriminatory speech;
- intimidation;
- violence;
- harmful slogans;
- safeguarding failures;
- or irresponsible claims.

These should not be minimised.

They should also not be transferred beyond the evidence.

A reusable incident test is:

### 1. Identify The Incident

What exactly happened?

### 2. Identify The Actor

Who acted?

### 3. Establish Relationship

Were they:

- an organiser;
- steward;
- speaker;
- member of a named body;
- participant;
- or unidentified person?

### 4. Establish Authority

Did they possess a role that could make the conduct organisationally significant?

### 5. Assess Organiser Knowledge

Did organisers know, approve, ignore, challenge, or correct the conduct?

### 6. Assess Repetition

Was the incident:

- isolated;
- repeated;
- patterned;
- encouraged;
- or embedded?

### 7. Assess Formal Position

Did the conduct match or contradict published coalition or organisational policy?

### 8. Describe Scale Correctly

Attribute the conduct only as far as the evidence permits.

### 9. Record Response

What corrective, disciplinary, safeguarding, or public action followed?

One participant may be fully accountable for their own conduct without becoming representative of the entire movement.

Repeated tolerated conduct may become organisationally significant.

Both principles must remain available.

## 🧪 Confidence Labels

Claims about movements often vary in evidential strength.

### Confirmed

Supported by direct, reliable evidence.

### Strongly Supported

Several credible sources or records point in the same direction.

### Reported

Attributed to a named source but not independently verified.

### Inferred

Reasoned from evidence but not directly established.

### Unclear

Available information is insufficient or contradictory.

### Disputed

Credible actors provide materially different accounts.

These labels may apply to claims about:

- affiliation;
- authority;
- crowd size;
- representation;
- policy;
- funding;
- coordination;
- composition;
- and incidents.

A confidence label should not replace explanation.

State what the evidence is.

## 🛡️ Legibility Without Targeting

Movement mapping can create risk.

The aim is not to expose private individuals, informal contacts, or vulnerable participants.

Legibility should prioritise:

- public organisational roles;
- declared relationships;
- formal mandates;
- visible institutional responsibility;
- published demands;
- and information necessary for accountability.

Avoid unnecessary publication of:

- private contact details;
- informal relationship chains;
- personal locations;
- vulnerable identities;
- internal security arrangements;
- or speculative connections.

Do not convert open-source movement analysis into an operational targeting aid.

Where public-interest analysis can be completed at organisational or institutional level, avoid identifying private individuals.

## ❓ Working Questions

When describing a movement, ask:

- What event occurred?
- At what analytical level is the claim being made?
- Who were the actors?
- Was each actor an individual, organisation, coalition, network, institution, constituency, or unaffiliated participant?
- What relationship has been established?
- Is membership proven?
- Was the event organised, sponsored, endorsed, or merely attended?
- Who held logistical authority?
- Who held political authority?
- Who was authorised to speak?
- In what capacity did they speak?
- What demand was formally agreed?
- What slogan was merely present?
- Is a personal position being presented as a coalition platform?
- Is one organisation being presented as the whole movement?
- What participation measure is being used?
- Is turnout being converted into membership?
- Are disabled, precarious, fearful, geographically distant, or professionally exposed participants being erased?
- What changed over time?
- What persisted?
- What evidence connects the different moments?
- What incident occurred?
- Who was responsible?
- What authority did they hold?
- Was the conduct isolated, repeated, tolerated, or embedded?
- What response followed?
- How far can responsibility properly travel?
- What remains unknown?
- What confidence label fits the claim?
- Is the analysis exposing private people unnecessarily?

## 🧿 Editorial Guardrails

Do not call a crowd an organisation.

Do not treat a coalition as a command structure.

Do not treat network contact as formal affiliation.

Do not treat co-presence as endorsement.

Do not treat a speaker as representative without mandate.

Do not treat one sign or chant as a formal platform.

Do not treat one incident as movement-wide without evidence.

Do not dismiss repeated incidents as isolated without investigation.

Do not treat decentralisation as absence of responsibility.

Do not invent central leadership because observers expect it.

Do not present plurality as incoherence.

Do not present disagreement as fragmentation without evidence.

Do not convert turnout estimates into membership figures.

Do not assume visible attendance captures:

- disabled;
- precarious;
- fearful;
- geographically distant;
- or professionally exposed participants.

Do not use demographic labels without evidence.

Do not use vague terms such as “associated with” or “linked to” without defining the relationship.

Do not infer coordination from similar messaging alone.

Do not infer funding, control, or ideological agreement from contact alone.

Do not minimise serious:

- antisemitism;
- Islamophobia;
- anti-Palestinian racism;
- harassment;
- intimidation;
- violence;
- or safeguarding failure.

Do not use one serious incident to erase everyone else present.

Do not map relationships in ways that unnecessarily expose private individuals.

Movement legibility should improve public understanding and accountability.

It should not become a targeting tool.

## 🌿 Working Principle

Identify the level.

Name the actor.

Define the relationship.

Establish the mandate.

Separate demand from slogan.

Measure participation honestly.

Track change over time.

Attribute incidents only as far as the evidence travels.

The crowd is not one actor.

The event is not the movement.

The speaker is not the constituency.

The slogan is not the platform.

Plurality is not incoherence.

Legibility requires distinction, not compression.

## 🏮 Footer
