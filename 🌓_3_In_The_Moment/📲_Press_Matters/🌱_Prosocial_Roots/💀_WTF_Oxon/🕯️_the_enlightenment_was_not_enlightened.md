Editorial position: The European Enlightenment helped develop modern ideas of liberty, reason, scientific inquiry and universal human rights. Yet prominent Enlightenment thinkers also participated in constructing, defending or rationalising racial hierarchies, colonial domination and unequal conceptions of humanity.

The contradiction is not simply that eighteenth-century intellectuals failed to live up to modern standards. Some articulated principles of universal equality while simultaneously excluding particular populations from their practical application.

This file should examine that contradiction without treating the Enlightenment as a single intellectual movement or overlooking contemporary abolitionists and anti-colonial thinkers who challenged its exclusions.

Core research notes

* Universalism and exclusion: Investigate who counted as fully human, rational, autonomous or entitled to political rights in different Enlightenment traditions.
* The invention of scientific racial classification: Trace how European naturalists and philosophers developed systems for categorising humanity, often confusing geographic ancestry, physical appearance, culture and supposedly inherited moral or intellectual characteristics.
* Reason as a justification for domination: Examine how claims about civilisation, progress and rationality were used to legitimise colonial rule, enslavement and paternalistic governance.
* The relationship between classification and hierarchy: Distinguish descriptive attempts to categorise human variation from claims that populations could be ranked according to inherent worth or ability.
* Empire and knowledge production: Consider how colonial expeditions, museums, universities, trading networks and imperial administrations shaped the evidence available to European scholars.
* Counter-Enlightenment voices: Include enslaved people, abolitionists, women, Indigenous intellectuals and anti-colonial revolutionaries who challenged the restricted application of supposedly universal principles.

Historical figures and intellectual developments

1. Carl Linnaeus (1707–1778) — Taxonomy and human classification

* Classified humans within the natural world and divided humanity into geographic varieties.
* His descriptions mixed physical characteristics with stereotyped behavioural and temperamental attributes.
* Investigate how supposedly neutral taxonomic categories became vehicles for social judgements.
* Distinguish his eighteenth-century taxonomy from contemporary population genetics.

2. David Hume (1711–1776) — Empiricism and racial hierarchy

* His essay Of National Characters contains a notorious footnote asserting the supposed natural inferiority of Black people.
* Examine the contradiction between Hume’s empirical philosophy and his sweeping claims unsupported by adequate evidence.
* Compare contemporary objections, including those of James Beattie.
* Consider how philosophical authority can lend credibility to poorly evidenced assertions.

3. Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) — Universal morality and racial theories

* Developed influential accounts of universal moral obligation while also publishing hierarchical racial classifications.
* Investigate his arguments about inherited racial characteristics and geographic adaptation.
* Examine scholarly disagreement about whether his later writings substantially revised his earlier racial views.
* Ask how apparently universal philosophical frameworks coexisted with exclusions in their application.

4. Johann Friedrich Blumenbach (1752–1840) — The racial typology problem

* Developed a fivefold classification of humanity, including the influential term Caucasian.
* Emphasised humanity’s common origins and opposed claims that different races were separate species.
* Nevertheless, his classification helped establish enduring racial categories.
* Useful example of how a classificatory framework can have consequences beyond its author’s stated intentions.

5. Marquis de Condorcet (1743–1794) — Enlightenment opposition to slavery

* Opposed slavery and advocated expanding political and civil rights.
* His work demonstrates that opposition to racial oppression existed within Enlightenment intellectual traditions.
* Investigate the limitations and contradictions of his own universalism.
* Important counterexample to the claim that racial hierarchy was an uncontested product of its time.

Beyond European intellectual history

The file should not give European philosophers exclusive ownership of the Enlightenment’s intellectual and political history.

* Ottobah Cugoano: His 1787 Thoughts and Sentiments on the Evil of Slavery offered a forceful abolitionist critique grounded in moral equality and political rights.
* Olaudah Equiano: His 1789 autobiography challenged European accounts of African societies and provided influential testimony against slavery.
* Mary Wollstonecraft: Her arguments about women’s rational capacities exposed exclusions in Enlightenment claims about universal reason.
* The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804): Enslaved and formerly enslaved people asserted liberty and equality against a colonial order that had proclaimed universal rights while maintaining slavery.
* Toussaint Louverture: Examine how revolutionary demands for liberty challenged the boundaries of French republican universalism.

Historical development: from classification to eugenics

* Eighteenth century — Classification
    Natural history, racial typologies, theories of civilisation and inherited characteristics.
* Nineteenth century — Quantification
    Craniometry, anthropometry, racial anthropology and increasingly statistical accounts of human difference.
* Late nineteenth century — Eugenics
    Francis Galton’s programme for directing human reproduction, drawing on hereditarian assumptions and emerging statistical methods.
* Twentieth century — Institutional implementation
    Eugenic legislation, forced sterilisation, immigration restrictions and racialised state policies in multiple countries.
* Contemporary period — Scientific reassessment
    Population genomics, developmental biology, complex-trait genetics and renewed scrutiny of historical racial categories.

This is a history of connected intellectual developments, not an assertion that Enlightenment philosophy inevitably produced eugenics or that modern genetics is simply its continuation.

Questions to investigate

* When did European intellectuals begin treating geographic and cultural differences as evidence of inherited intellectual hierarchies?
* Which thinkers challenged those claims using the scientific and philosophical standards available at the time?
* How did slavery and colonial expansion influence the production of supposedly objective knowledge about human populations?
* How did universities, museums and learned societies legitimise racial classifications?
* What changed when racial typologies became associated with measurement, statistics and eventually genetics?
* Which historical assumptions have been overturned by contemporary genetics, and which questions remain genuinely unresolved?
* How should academic institutions distinguish legitimate scientific disagreement from the rehabilitation of claims already undermined by evidence?

Connection to WTF Oxon

The historical material supports three questions about Oxford’s contemporary responsibilities.

First, intellectual prestige is not a substitute for evidence. Historically, influential scholars could advance deeply flawed claims about race while retaining considerable academic authority.

Second, the freedom to challenge orthodoxy cuts both ways. Critics of racial hierarchy, including abolitionists and anti-colonial intellectuals, were themselves challenging powerful established ideas.

Third, institutions are responsible for the standards they apply. Hosting an academic event is not equivalent to endorsing its speaker, but institutions can still be asked how they distinguish open inquiry, scientific rigour and public legitimacy.

Suggested closing proposition

The Enlightenment’s most consequential contradiction was not simply that its thinkers made mistakes. It was that some developed universal principles of reason, liberty and human dignity while constructing intellectual frameworks that denied their equal application.

And there is an especially useful question to carry into the Cofnas analysis:

If the Enlightenment taught us to question received authority, why should the historical authority of racial classification itself be exempt from scrutiny?

The title is provocative, but the historical argument can be stronger than a blanket dismissal: the Enlightenment produced tools for challenging racial hierarchy alongside theories that helped sustain it. That contradiction is precisely what makes the period worth examining.
