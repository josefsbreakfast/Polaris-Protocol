Editorial position: The contemporary American campaign against diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) is not confined to universities. Its language, personnel, arguments and policy objectives overlap with broader efforts to redefine institutional merit, professional competence, national identity and military readiness.

The working hypothesis is that campus disputes over race, intelligence, sex and academic freedom can become part of a wider political process through which contested ideas acquire institutional authority and eventually influence public policy.

Your “college bro just asking questions → Pete Hegseth’s testosterone for troops” formulation is an excellent framing device.

But we should distinguish a documented transmission of ideas from an ideological resemblance. The question is whether we can demonstrate the connections, not simply observe that the rhetoric sounds familiar.

Central research questions

* How did American campus disputes about political correctness, free speech, affirmative action and DEI become organising issues for the political right?
* Which think tanks, advocacy organisations, media networks, donors and political figures helped translate those disputes into legislative and executive policy?
* How did claims about biological differences, meritocracy and institutional decline become incorporated into arguments about military effectiveness?
* Where do racial hereditarianism, opposition to DEI, anti-feminism and arguments about sex differences intersect — and where do they remain distinct?
* What evidence connects these American political developments to British universities, including the Oriel event?
* How do governments distinguish legitimate scientific criticism of institutional policy from ideologically motivated efforts to discredit entire academic disciplines?

The argument: from campus grievance to state power

There is already a documented connection between the rhetoric of anti-DEI campaigning and American military policy.

In January 2025, the Trump administration issued an executive order, Restoring America’s Fighting Force, asserting that DEI programmes undermined military merit, cohesion and readiness. It ordered the abolition of military DEI offices and reviews of teaching at military educational institutions. The White House

In September 2025, Pete Hegseth addressed senior military officers at Quantico, demanding stricter physical and grooming standards, including male-level physical standards for combat positions. ABC News

That gives us a concrete policy case study, not merely a comparison of personalities.

Campus grievance

DEI allegedly undermines merit and academic standards

Political mobilisation

Campaign organisations, media, think tanks and electoral politics

Government doctrine

Meritocracy, anti-DEI directives and restrictions on institutional programmes

Operational consequences

Military recruitment, standards, personnel policy, training and education

This is a proposed analytical pathway, not a proven chain of influence from Cofnas personally to Hegseth.

Notes for the American case study

* Academic freedom as political language: Examine how arguments about permitting controversial research become attached to wider campaigns against DEI, affirmative action and university governance.
* The changing meaning of merit: Distinguish individual competence and validated professional standards from assumptions that inclusion necessarily lowers standards.
* Sex differences and military readiness: Investigate the empirical basis for occupational physical requirements, including whether particular tests predict actual job performance and injury risk.
* Race and military opportunity: Examine whether abolishing DEI programmes changes recruitment, retention, promotion, reporting of discrimination or unit cohesion.
* The masculinity narrative: Study Hegseth’s rhetoric around toughness, appearance, discipline and the warrior ethos. His policy positions are not reducible to testosterone, but masculinity is a recognisable theme.
* The administrative mechanism: Trace executive orders through departmental memoranda, personnel rules, service academy curricula and implementation outcomes.
* The international transmission question: Determine whether British political organisations are importing American anti-DEI arguments, adapting them to British institutions or independently developing similar positions.

The Oxford connection

The Oxon Universities’ Reform Society advertised Cofnas’s event specifically around whether DEI undermines higher education. Its published programme also included a Westminster visit to four right-leaning policy organisations: the Adam Smith Institute, Centre for Policy Studies, CapX and TaxPayers’ Alliance. Oxon Universities’ Reform Society

That provides an identifiable interface between student political activity and policy networks. It does not establish that those organisations endorsed Cofnas or his racial hereditarian claims.

The event was also reported as being organised by a student society supporting Reform UK. Varsity

The research question is therefore more specific than whether American ideas have reached Oxford:

How are political arguments about race, merit and institutional decline being transmitted between universities, political organisations and policymaking networks across the Atlantic?

Further evidence to collect

* A timeline from the 2010s campus free-speech controversies through the Trump administrations’ anti-DEI measures.
* Documented relationships between advocacy groups, think tanks, elected officials and executive policymakers.
* The role of organisations such as the Heritage Foundation, Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression, and campus conservative networks — distinguishing their differing missions and policy positions.
* Hegseth’s military standards memoranda and subsequent implementation data.
* Independent research on physical occupational standards, sex differences, injury rates and military effectiveness.
* Evidence of UK political actors adopting American arguments, including speeches, policy papers, events and organisational relationships.
* Contradictory evidence: cases where opposition to particular DEI policies is empirically grounded and does not depend on racial hereditarianism or broader anti-academic politics.

Suggested closing proposition

The danger is not that a student asks whether diversity programmes work. That is a legitimate question. The danger arises when a political movement converts an untested assumption — that inclusion necessarily compromises competence — into a governing doctrine, and then uses that doctrine to reshape institutions without adequately examining the evidence.

And your Hegseth comparison gives the file its particularly pointed ending:

A question that begins in a university debating room can eventually become a personnel policy affecting an entire military. The evidential standards ought to become more demanding as the consequences grow, not less.

That’s the transatlantic thread worth pulling.
