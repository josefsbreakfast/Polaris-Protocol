# 🇺🇸 The War on the Academy
**First created:** 2026-11-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-11-18  
*The American anti-DEI campaign, disputes over academic freedom and the transmission of political arguments into British university governance.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The November 2026 Nathan Cofnas event at Oriel College provides a British case study in a wider political conflict over universities, diversity, equality and the authority to define legitimate knowledge. The Oxon Universities’ Reform Society advertised the event as **“Is DEI Undermining Higher Education?”** [1] The event's British setting matters: similar language does not, on its own, prove organisational coordination with American political campaigns.

**Editorial thesis:** a campaign that presents itself as defending intellectual freedom can simultaneously pursue state intervention in academic hiring, curricula, research priorities and institutional governance. The contradiction should be tested against actual policies, not assumed from rhetoric.

## 🧭 Distinguish the actors

- **Cofnas:** a philosopher whose public arguments about race, intelligence and DEI warrant examination on their own terms. His invitation does not establish a direct relationship with US government officials. [1][2]
- **Oxon Universities’ Reform Society:** organiser of the Oxford event. Similarities to American anti-DEI advocacy are grounds for comparative analysis, not proof of shared funding or control. [1]
- **Oriel College:** venue provider and decision-maker under the applicable British higher-education framework; its defence of lawful speech should not be confused with endorsement of the speaker. [3]
- **US executive branch and Department of Defense:** policymakers whose executive orders, directives and public speeches are primary evidence of state action. [4][5]
- **US universities, courts, professional associations and civil-liberties groups:** independent institutions and critics whose responses are not reducible to a single political position. [6][7]

## 🏛️ The US anti-DEI programme

In January 2025, the White House issued executive orders directed at diversity, equity and inclusion in federal government and the military. The order **“Restoring America's Fighting Force”** directed changes to Defense Department policies and framed DEI initiatives as incompatible with merit and military cohesion. [4]

**Questions for investigation:**

- Which measures applied to federal agencies, contractors, grant recipients or educational institutions, respectively?
- Which measures were implemented, revised, challenged or blocked by courts?
- Where did executive rhetoric translate into enforceable rules, and where did it remain political messaging?
- How did institutions respond in hiring, research administration, student support and disciplinary procedures?

Do not treat every university DEI programme as identical. They differ in legal basis, design, effects and evidence of effectiveness. Criticism of an individual programme is not automatically opposition to equality; nor does invoking merit establish that an intervention is evidence-based.

## 🪖 From campus rhetoric to military standards

Pete Hegseth's September 2025 speech to senior military officers at Quantico emphasised a “warrior ethos”, physical standards and changes to military culture. Its transcript is useful primary material for examining how the language of standards, masculinity and anti-DEI politics was presented to military leadership. [5]

**Analytical guardrails:**

- Distinguish combat-role physical standards from broader claims about the fitness of women to serve.
- Identify actual policy texts and implementation dates before describing a speech as a binding change.
- Separate empirical questions about operational performance from ideological claims about masculinity.
- Do not imply that Cofnas influenced Hegseth personally without documentary evidence.

The comparison is about **rhetorical and policy convergence**, not a demonstrated chain of personal influence.

## 🧪 The authority of science

The phrase *“just asking questions”* can describe legitimate scholarly scepticism. It can also be used rhetorically to shift the burden of proof while implying that unsupported hypotheses are being suppressed. The relevant test is the quality of evidence.

For racial hereditarian claims, distinguish:

- Genetic ancestry from socially defined race.
- Heritability within a sampled population from genetic explanations of between-group differences.
- Associations from causal mechanisms.
- Polygenic prediction within a population from portability across populations.
- Genotype from phenotype, including development, gene regulation and environmental effects.

The National Academies recommends careful, context-specific use of population descriptors in genetics and genomics; the National Human Genome Research Institute explains the history and harms of scientific racism and eugenics. [8][9] These are stronger foundations for scientific scrutiny than either deference to a provocative speaker or an attempt to prohibit discussion.

## ⚖️ Academic freedom and government power

There is a genuine tension between protecting scholars from ideological discrimination and using state authority to impose ideological conditions on universities. The tension requires concrete comparisons:

| Question | Evidence to seek |
|---|---|
| Does the measure protect individual expression? | Statutory text, university policy, court interpretation |
| Does it restrict institutional autonomy? | Funding conditions, accreditation rules, agency directives |
| Does it target conduct or viewpoint? | Operative definitions, enforcement criteria, exemptions |
| Is it proportionate? | Stated aim, empirical basis, available less restrictive measures |
| Who bears the practical burden? | Students, researchers, faculty, administrators, contractors |

US constitutional rules governing government action are not identical to the British statutory duties binding Oriel. The First Amendment constrains US state action; British university free-speech duties are governed by domestic higher-education law alongside applicable human-rights and equality obligations. [6][10]

## 🌍 Transatlantic transmission: a testable hypothesis

The Oxford event shows that anti-DEI themes circulate beyond the United States. It does **not** independently establish a coordinated US-to-UK operation. [1][3]

Potential evidence of actual transmission would include:

- Identifiable shared organisers, advisers or trustees.
- Public funding disclosures and grant records.
- Recorded speaking engagements, partnerships or correspondence.
- Direct citations of US policies in British institutional proposals.
- Reused campaign materials or documented strategic collaboration.

Keep three categories separate: **documented connection**, **shared ideological vocabulary** and **editorial inference**.

## 📣 Oriel as a case study

Oriel stated that it considered its equality duties, obtained legal advice and made security and welfare arrangements for the Cofnas event. It distinguished permission to use college premises from endorsement. [3] The legal issue is therefore not whether it claimed to consider those duties, but whether its process and the balance it struck were adequate.

The Oxford controversy also arose after Cofnas publicly made plagiarism allegations about the late Professor Jason Arday. Reporting on the opening of Arday's inquest in August 2026 makes the timing a distinct ethical question. **Do not infer that Cofnas caused Arday's death or that the inquest reached findings not established in the available record.** [11][12]

An institution may have good legal reasons to permit a controversial event and still face legitimate scrutiny over its timing, format and treatment of affected students.

## 🧿 Failure modes and research questions

- **False equivalence:** describing a student-society event and a US executive order as the same type of action.
- **Guilt by association:** treating ideological resemblance as proof of a coordinated network.
- **Science by slogan:** equating the freedom to advance a hypothesis with evidence that it is true.
- **Legal flattening:** treating US First Amendment doctrine as though it directly governs Oxford.
- **Selective scrutiny:** criticising constraints on one speaker while ignoring restrictions on other forms of political expression.
- **Retrospective certainty:** attributing later policy outcomes to earlier rhetoric without an evidenced causal chain.

**Further work:** build a dated evidence table of US executive actions, court decisions, military directives and identifiable UK responses; then separately test whether any named individuals or organisations actually link the cases.

## ⚖️ Closing proposition

**A university cannot defend intellectual independence by treating all challenges to its judgement as censorship. Nor can a government defend intellectual independence by deciding which ideas its institutions must endorse.**

The relevant question is who holds power, what they do with it, what evidence they use and which freedoms remain available to those who disagree.
