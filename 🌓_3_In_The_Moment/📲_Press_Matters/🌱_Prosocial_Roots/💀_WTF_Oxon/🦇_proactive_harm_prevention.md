Editorial position: Universities should not wait for demonstrable harm before considering whether their decisions create foreseeable risks. Proactive harm prevention means identifying those risks, consulting affected communities, evaluating proportionate alternatives and documenting institutional decisions.

The central argument is that protecting freedom of expression and preventing foreseeable harm are not necessarily competing objectives. Thoughtful governance can often accommodate both.

The file should examine what Oxford could reasonably have anticipated, which interventions were available, and whether existing procedures were sufficient.

1. From reactive safeguarding to proactive governance

* Reactive safeguarding: Responding after threats, harassment, discrimination or other harms have occurred.
* Proactive prevention: Identifying foreseeable risks and taking proportionate measures before an event or decision.
* Institutional duty of care: Universities owe legal duties in particular circumstances, but there is no unlimited general legal obligation to prevent all distress or offence.
* Foreseeability: Distinguish credible, evidence-based risks from speculation or disagreement with a speaker’s views.
* Proportionality: Interventions should address identifiable risks without unnecessarily restricting lawful expression.
* Accountability: Record the evidence, alternatives considered, reasons for decisions and arrangements for subsequent review.

Key proposition: A university should be able to explain not merely why an event was lawful, but why its chosen arrangements were reasonable in light of foreseeable consequences.

2. Risk categories to assess

Risk	Questions for institutional assessment
Physical safety	Are there credible threats, risks of violence, crowd-management issues or access concerns?
Harassment and discrimination	Could identifiable conduct cross legal or disciplinary thresholds? Are reporting routes clear?
Student welfare	Are there particular vulnerabilities, foreseeable distress or accessibility needs requiring reasonable adjustments?
Educational participation	Could arrangements materially interfere with students’ ability to attend classes, access accommodation or participate in college life?
Academic integrity	Is the event clearly presented as debate, political advocacy, research or institutional teaching?
Reputational and financial exposure	Have potential consequences for institutional trust and relationships been assessed without allowing donor preferences to dictate lawful speech?
Governance and procedural fairness	Were affected parties consulted, and were comparable events treated consistently?

3. The Oriel case: practical questions

* What risks were identified before the Cofnas event?
* Were Oriel’s JCR and MCR representatives consulted before decisions were made?
* Did the risk assessment address equality and educational participation, or primarily physical security?
* Was the event’s timing considered in relation to Professor Jason Arday’s death and the reported outstanding coroner’s proceedings? Verify the procedural status independently before presenting it as established fact.
* Did the college consider postponement, a moderated format, additional academic expertise or relocation?
* Were any proposed changes rejected on legal advice? If so, what was the relevant legal reasoning?
* Were students offered accessible, confidential routes to raise concerns?
* Did Oriel evaluate the effectiveness of its arrangements afterwards?

The point is not to assume that every alternative would have been lawful or practical. It is to determine whether the institution considered reasonable options and documented its reasoning.

4. A practical prevention protocol

* Identify the decision and applicable duties
    Establish who is responsible, what statutory obligations apply and where discretion exists.
* Assess foreseeable risks
    Consider physical safety, harassment, equality, accessibility and educational participation separately.
* Consult proportionately
    Seek relevant input from affected students, staff, organisers and appropriate specialists without creating an automatic veto.
* Consider less restrictive alternatives
    Assess changes to venue, timing, format, moderation, access arrangements and security.
* Record reasons and communicate
    Explain the decision, the competing rights considered and available safeguards, protecting genuinely confidential information.
* Review and learn
    Document incidents, complaints, implementation problems and lessons for future events.

5. UK legal and regulatory framework

Freedom of expression and academic freedom

* Article 10 ECHR protects freedom of expression, subject to lawful and proportionate restrictions.
* Article 11 protects freedom of assembly and association.
* Education (No. 2) Act 1986, section 43, establishes duties concerning lawful free speech in higher education.
* Higher Education (Freedom of Speech) Act 2023 and the Higher Education and Research Act 2017 create additional relevant duties and regulatory arrangements, with commencement and applicability requiring date-specific checking.
* Universities and colleges should not impose restrictions merely because lawful views are offensive, controversial or unpopular.

Equality and safeguarding

* Equality Act 2010, including section 149, requires relevant public authorities to have due regard to specified equality objectives.
* The public sector equality duty is principally a duty concerning the decision-making process, not an automatic obligation to achieve a particular outcome.
* Equality Act harassment and discrimination provisions may become relevant depending on the conduct, context and legal relationship.
* Universities have safeguarding and welfare responsibilities, but those responsibilities vary according to the circumstances and do not create a general right to avoid encountering disagreeable opinions.

Risk management and prevention

* The government’s trauma-informed practice guidance provides a useful non-statutory framework for recognising potential harm and avoiding unnecessary retraumatisation.
* The Office for Students’ guidance on freedom of speech explains the importance of proportionate restrictions and institutional procedures.
* Universities’ own codes of practice should provide the operational framework for event approval, risk assessment and complaints.

7. Proposed institutional improvements

* Integrated event assessments: Consider free speech, equality, safety, accessibility and educational participation together, rather than in isolated administrative processes.
* Documented proportionality analysis: Require decision-makers to explain why their chosen arrangements are appropriate and why less restrictive alternatives were accepted or rejected.
* Early consultation: Provide a defined process for relevant student and staff input, without creating a discretionary veto over lawful speech.
* Sensitive timing protocol: Consider bereavement, ongoing legal proceedings and other exceptional circumstances when scheduling events, while preventing indefinite postponement as a disguised prohibition.
* Clear responsibility: Identify which body owns the decision when a student society organises an event in college premises.
* Independent review: Provide a route to examine contested decisions without automatically suspending lawful events.
* Post-event evaluation: Review incidents, complaints, welfare impacts and procedural effectiveness.

Suggested closing proposition

Proactive harm prevention is not a demand that universities eliminate controversy. It is a demand that they exercise their existing powers intelligently, proportionately and transparently, rather than treating the existence of legal free-speech duties as the end of institutional decision-making.

And I would give this file one particularly pointed final question:

If Oriel could anticipate sufficient risk to change the venue and introduce security arrangements, what other foreseeable risks did it consider — and what evidence explains where it drew the line?

That is a question the college ought to be capable of answering without abandoning academic freedom.
