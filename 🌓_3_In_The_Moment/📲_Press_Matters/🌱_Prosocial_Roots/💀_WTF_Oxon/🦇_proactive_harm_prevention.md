# 🦇 Proactive Harm Prevention
**First created:** 2026-11-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-11-18  
*Preventative governance, proportionate risk assessment and institutional duties surrounding controversial university events.*

---

## 🧭 Orientation

**An institution need not wait for harm to occur before assessing whether its decisions are sensible.** Nor may it treat the possibility of offence as an automatic veto over lawful expression.

This node examines what proactive, evidence-led and proportionate prevention could mean for the decision to permit Nathan Cofnas to speak at Oriel College on 17 November 2026. The event was organised by the Oxon Universities' Reform Society. Oriel stated that it considered its statutory free-speech and equality duties, sought legal advice, consulted students and made security and welfare arrangements. [S1] Contemporary reporting records substantial protests outside the college. [S2–S3]

**Editorial question:** If Oriel could anticipate risks serious enough to alter arrangements, what other risks did it examine, what alternatives did it consider, and what evidence explains where it drew the line?

This is an inquiry into the *quality of institutional judgement*, not an assertion that Oriel broke the law or that every distressing event should be cancelled.

## ⚖️ Rights and duties: prevention has limits

- **Freedom of speech:** The higher-education framework imposes positive duties to secure lawful expression, including controversial expression. Restrictions must be justified within the applicable legal framework. [S4–S6]
- **Equality:** Section 149 of the Equality Act 2010 requires due regard to specified equality aims where the public sector equality duty applies. It does not prescribe automatic cancellation or any single outcome. [S7–S8]
- **Protest:** Peaceful protest is itself protected expression and assembly; this does not create an unlimited entitlement to obstruct another person's lawful speech. [S5–S6]
- **Safeguarding and welfare:** Trauma-informed principles can inform institutional practice, but the UK government's working definition is guidance rather than a freestanding statutory cancellation power. [S9]

The distinction is between **mitigating identifiable risks** and **suppressing a viewpoint because it is contested**. Time, place and manner changes can be proportionate; they can also become unjustified restrictions if deployed as indefinite postponement or a disguised veto. [S6]

## 🧿 Foreseeable-risk register

| Risk domain | Questions for an event assessment | Potential proportionate measures |
|---|---|---|
| Physical safety | Are entrances, accommodation, crowd movement and emergency access affected? | Venue adjustment, stewarding, accessible routes, coordination with security staff |
| Harassment and discrimination | Is there a specific, evidenced risk of unlawful conduct, rather than mere disagreement or offence? | Behaviour rules, reporting routes, intervention procedures |
| Welfare and distress | Are there particular circumstances requiring sensitive communication or support? | Consultation, opt-in support, clear information, post-event welfare access |
| Educational access | Could teaching, accommodation or ordinary use of college facilities be materially disrupted? | Timetabling, alternative routes, protection of essential access |
| Academic integrity | Are contested empirical claims presented as established findings? | Transparent framing, expert questioning, access to critical research |
| Governance and trust | Were alternatives and competing duties considered before the decision? | Documented reasons, responsible decision-makers, retrospective review |
| Reputational and financial exposure | Are claims about consequences supported by evidence? | Appropriate trustee oversight; no donor veto over lawful speech |

**The table is an analytical checklist, not a finding that each risk materialised at Oriel.** Regulatory guidance and the college's own account establish the need to examine the applicable duties and the arrangements actually made. [S1][S6–S8]

## 📣 Oriel: what is documented

Oriel's 16 November statement said it had received legal advice, considered Equality Act obligations, met student groups, worked with the University's Proctors' Office and arranged security and welfare support. It said the event's booking did not constitute endorsement of the speaker. It also declined to release the security risk assessment, citing sensitivity. [S1]

Student representatives questioned officials about event safety, legal obligations and risk assessment before the event. [S10] Contemporary reporting documented protests and changes to event arrangements. [S2–S3]

**What these sources do not establish:** the full content of the college's legal advice, the underlying risk register, the weight assigned to each concern or whether postponement was considered and rejected. The absence of public disclosure is not proof that no assessment occurred.

### 🕯️ The timing question

Professor Jason Arday died in August 2026. A coroner's inquest was opened and adjourned; the precise procedural position on the date of the November event requires checking against authoritative records. Cofnas had publicly made allegations concerning Arday, which should not be treated as proven findings. [S11–S12]

The ethical issue is whether the college considered the particular timing and circumstances with sufficient care. **There is no evidential basis here to attribute Arday's death to Cofnas.** The possibility of negotiated postponement merits scrutiny, but postponement itself would require justification under free-speech law. [S6]

## 🛠️ Six-stage preventative protocol

### 1. Define the decision and responsible body

Identify the organiser, premises provider, relevant college officers and any central university role. Record who can authorise or modify event conditions. Do not collapse Oriel, the University, independent societies and other colleges into one legal actor. [S1]

### 2. Identify applicable legal duties

Record free-speech obligations, equality requirements, protest rights, premises and safety responsibilities. Separate binding law from advisory good practice. [S4–S9]

### 3. Assess credible risks

Use specific evidence and distinguish severity, likelihood and uncertainty. Avoid equating discomfort with unlawful harassment; equally, avoid dismissing particularised concerns merely because speech is lawful. [S6–S9]

### 4. Consult and evaluate alternatives

Consult affected groups without granting any constituency an automatic veto. Consider venue, access, format, moderation, security, welfare provision and — where genuinely justified — a definite alternative date. Record the likely effects on the organiser's and speaker's rights. [S6][S10]

### 5. Decide and document reasons

Record what was considered, what was rejected and why. Protect genuinely sensitive operational information, but consider publishing a non-sensitive account of the decision-making rationale. [S1][S6]

### 6. Review outcomes

Examine incidents, complaints, access disruption, the adequacy of support and lessons for future events. Do not infer success merely because an event went ahead, or failure merely because it provoked protest.

## 🧩 What proportionate prevention is not

- Not a blanket rule against offensive or disputed views.
- Not an indefinite postponement dressed up as consultation.
- Not treating trauma-informed guidance as though it overrides statute.
- Not assuming all student objections are interchangeable or that every claimed risk is verified.
- Not transferring decision-making authority to donors, campaigners or the speaker.
- Not treating security arrangements as a complete substitute for equality analysis and institutional judgement.

**The purpose of prevention is to reduce avoidable harm while preserving lawful rights, not to make universities controversy-free.** [S6–S9]

## 🔎 Outstanding evidence requests

1. Can Oriel provide a redacted or non-operational account of its risk assessment?
2. Which risks were identified, by whom, and when?
3. What effect did consultation have on the final arrangements?
4. Was a definite postponement considered in light of the circumstances surrounding Professor Arday's death?
5. What alternatives to the chosen venue and format were examined?
6. What was the college's approach to academic moderation and the presentation of contested empirical claims?
7. Were complaints, safety incidents or access problems recorded after the event?
8. Will the governing body conduct or publish a post-event review?

These are **questions for verification**, not allegations of undisclosed misconduct.

## 🧾 Closing position

**The right to host a lawful event is not the same question as whether every aspect of its organisation was wise.**

Oriel has publicly described steps it took. Those steps matter. The next question is whether the college can explain how it assessed the risks beyond the security arrangements it disclosed, and why the chosen date and format were justified.

*Preventing avoidable harm is not the opposite of academic freedom. Done properly, it is part of the work required to sustain it.*
