# 🌱 Prosocial Roots  
**First created:** 2026-07-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-17  
*Reporting frameworks for social cohesion, legitimate disagreement, public trust, and responses that reduce rather than reproduce harm.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

**Prosocial Roots** looks at the conditions that help people remain part of a shared society, especially when trust is low, disagreement is intense, or harm has already occurred.

It is interested in the roots of both conflict and cooperation:

- what gives people a credible place in public life;
- what makes disagreement feel survivable;
- what allows institutions to retain trust;
- what pushes people towards isolation, hostility, or destructive action;
- and what kinds of public response reduce harm rather than compounding it.

The aim is not to manufacture consensus or ask harmed people to become endlessly understanding.

It is to examine how societies preserve enough recognition, connection, and legitimate agency for people to disagree without treating one another as disposable.

---

## 🗞️ Why This Belongs Under Press Matters

Journalism does more than describe social conflict after it happens.

Reporting can influence:

- which experiences are publicly recognised;
- whose fear is treated as credible;
- which disagreements remain discussable;
- whether whole communities are presented as threats;
- whether exceptional incidents become permanent identities;
- and whether public responses produce understanding, humiliation, solidarity, or escalation.

This does not mean journalism is responsible for every social outcome.

It means reporting forms part of the environment in which people understand one another, institutions establish legitimacy, and political choices become possible.

---

## 🌱 What Prosocial Means Here

Prosocial does not mean polite, moderate, obedient, or free from conflict.

A prosocial response strengthens the capacity of people and institutions to:

- recognise harm accurately;
- protect those at risk;
- preserve human dignity;
- resolve conflict through legitimate means;
- maintain truthful public memory;
- support meaningful participation;
- and prevent further harm without unnecessarily widening the field of enemies.

A response may be firm, angry, or coercive and still serve a prosocial purpose.

Equally, a response may sound compassionate while deepening exclusion, mistrust, or danger.

The question is not simply whether a response appears kind.

The question is what it helps grow.

## 🧭 Core Principles

### Recognition Is Not Endorsement

People can be recognised as members of society without their beliefs, demands, or conduct being approved.

Understanding why a position exists does not require accepting its proposed solution.

### Explanation Is Not Absolution

Understanding the conditions that produce harmful behaviour does not erase personal, political, or legal responsibility.

It makes prevention more realistic.

### Cohesion Is Not Consensus

Social cohesion does not require people to share one identity, culture, moral framework, or political conclusion.

It requires enough trust, restraint, and shared infrastructure for serious disagreement to remain inside social and democratic life.

### Safety And Belonging Are Connected

People need protection from violence, coercion, and abuse.

They also need credible routes to dignity, participation, recognition, and a future worth investing in.

Neither can safely replace the other.

### Public Trust Depends On Consistency

Institutions lose legitimacy when similar harms are described differently according to the identity, status, or political usefulness of those involved.

Consistency does not mean every case is identical. It means differences should be explained rather than assumed.

### Difficult Subjects Should Not Be Abandoned

When mainstream institutions refuse to acknowledge a real concern, radical actors may become the only people offering recognition.

Opening a subject for examination does not give every answer equal factual or moral weight.

### Responses Can Become Causes

A badly designed reaction may increase the conditions associated with future harm:

- humiliation;
- social expulsion;
- collective blame;
- status competition;
- institutional mistrust;
- conspiracy;
- or the belief that lawful participation is futile.

The response must therefore be studied as part of the system, not merely as its conclusion.

## 🧩 Scope

This folder can hold work relating to:

- political violence and radicalisation;
- democratic disagreement;
- public trust and institutional legitimacy;
- loneliness, exclusion, and social abandonment;
- intergenerational misunderstanding;
- race, class, religion, and national belonging;
- protest and public order;
- community safety;
- social repair after violence or scandal;
- moral injury and collective grief;
- media narratives that intensify conflict;
- routes out of extremist or conspiratorial movements;
- restorative and preventive practices;
- civic participation;
- youth agency and meaningful adulthood;
- shared public spaces;
- and the conditions under which diverse populations continue choosing to remain a society.

The branch should remain open to cohesion issues that do not initially look like security questions.

A breakdown in social trust may first appear through housing, healthcare, education, work, local media, family life, cultural conflict, or access to public institutions.

---

## ❓ Working Questions

Work in this folder should generally ask:

- What is the underlying social problem?
- Who experiences the current arrangement as protective?
- Who experiences it as excluding, humiliating, or dangerous?
- What harm must be acknowledged clearly?
- What legitimate routes for agency currently exist?
- Where have people stopped trusting formal routes?
- What response is being proposed?
- What behaviour will that response reward?
- What will it make easier or harder next time?
- Does it reduce the number of enemies, or merely rename them?
- Does it protect people without treating whole populations as suspect?
- What would meaningful repair require?
- What helps people remain invested in a shared future?

## 🧿 Editorial Approach

Nodes should be:

- curious without becoming naïve;
- compassionate without becoming exculpatory;
- firm about harm without relying on contempt;
- alert to structural conditions without erasing agency;
- and willing to distinguish legitimate disagreement from dehumanisation.

Do not assume that social conflict is produced by misunderstanding alone.

Material interests, power, impunity, prejudice, and deliberate exploitation remain real.

Prosocial analysis should not turn oppression into a communication problem or demand reconciliation from people who remain in danger.

Where repair is not yet possible, the work may instead identify:

- minimum safety conditions;
- factual boundaries;
- routes for accountability;
- protections against retaliation;
- and what must stop before trust can reasonably be requested.

## 🗺️ Routing Logic

Place a node in **🌱 Prosocial Roots** when its central concern is:

- belonging;
- cohesion;
- recognition;
- social repair;
- public trust;
- democratic participation;
- or the conditions that make constructive participation possible.

Related **Press Matters** branches may overlap, but their primary functions differ:

- **🌊 Playing Defence** focuses on media resilience, protective practice, and responses to pressure.
- **🏆 Good Sports** examines rules, fairness, competition, institutional conduct, and the cultures built around them.
- **👻 Ghosts Of Accountability** follows unresolved responsibility, institutional memory, and consequences that were deferred or denied.
- **💌 Visible Ink** makes submerged testimony, meaning, and power more publicly legible.
- **🌱 Prosocial Roots** asks what helps a diverse society remain connected, democratically functional, and capable of responding to harm without reproducing it.
