# 🛰️ What Is Being Grouped Together  
**First created:** 2026-07-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-17  
*Separating incidents, motives, ideologies, legal categories and political claims before repetition turns their association into apparent proof.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Political violence rarely enters public discussion as a cleanly defined subject.

A violent incident occurs. Facts emerge unevenly. Investigators use cautious or technical language. Political actors connect the incident to wider concerns. Journalists must explain events before every motive, relationship or classification has been established.

Different things begin to accumulate under one heading:

- attacks on politicians;
- attacks by people with political beliefs;
- terrorism offences;
- ideological movements;
- personal grievance;
- violent rhetoric;
- public disorder;
- sabotage;
- hate crime;
- online radicalisation;
- mental distress;
- and incidents whose motives remain unresolved.

Some of these things may genuinely be connected.

Others may share little beyond their appearance in the same speech, headline or briefing.

This node provides a way to separate them before the grouping itself begins to function as evidence.

---

## 🧩 The Grouping Problem

Public narratives often move through a recognisable sequence:

1. An incident occurs.
2. A political or ideological association is identified.
3. Earlier incidents are recalled.
4. A broad category is named.
5. The category is treated as an explanation.
6. A policy response is attached to it.

Each step may sound plausible on its own.

The problem appears when the links between the steps are assumed rather than demonstrated.

A person may have political views without acting because of them.

An attack may have a political target without belonging to an organised political campaign.

Several offenders may use similar language without knowing one another, sharing a strategy or responding to the same cause.

A legal category may combine several traditions because they are administratively convenient to manage together.

A series of isolated cases may indicate a wider social pattern without constituting a network.

The task is not to break every narrative apart reflexively.

It is to identify what kind of connection is actually being claimed.

## 🧺 What May Be In The Bundle

When a political-violence claim is made, the bundle may contain several different types of material.

### Incidents

These are the physical events:

- attempted killings;
- completed killings;
- assaults;
- arson;
- sabotage;
- criminal damage;
- threats;
- intimidation;
- disruption;
- public disorder;
- or preparatory conduct.

The method and consequence matter.

A fatal attack, a disrupted plot, threatening correspondence and damage to property may all be serious while presenting very different risks.

### Targets

The intended or actual target may be:

- a politician;
- a political party;
- a government building;
- an identity group;
- a workplace;
- infrastructure;
- machinery;
- private property;
- a public event;
- or an institution understood symbolically.

Target choice can provide evidence about motive.

It does not settle motive by itself.

### Motives

Possible motives include:

- ideological commitment;
- personal grievance;
- revenge;
- fixation;
- perceived betrayal;
- status-seeking;
- notoriety;
- prejudice;
- financial interest;
- mental distress;
- interpersonal conflict;
- or combinations of these.

Some motives are established through clear evidence.

Some are alleged.

Some remain unresolved.

### Beliefs

A perpetrator may express:

- left-wing beliefs;
- right-wing beliefs;
- nationalist beliefs;
- religious beliefs;
- anarchist beliefs;
- environmental beliefs;
- conspiratorial beliefs;
- or an unstable mixture drawn from several traditions.

Belief can be relevant without being the sole or immediate cause of violence.

### Organisations And Movements

A person may be:

- a formal member;
- an informal participant;
- a supporter;
- a donor;
- an online follower;
- an imitator;
- a hostile critic;
- or entirely unconnected to the movement with which they are publicly associated.

Affinity is not the same as membership.

Membership is not the same as direction.

Direction is not the same as a coordinated campaign.

### Legal And Administrative Categories

Officials may classify conduct as:

- terrorism;
- preparation of terrorism;
- extremism;
- hate crime;
- public-order offending;
- criminal damage;
- violent crime;
- harassment;
- or an ordinary offence with political context.

These classifications answer legal and administrative questions.

They do not always answer historical, sociological or preventive ones.

### Political Narratives

The incident may then be placed inside a wider claim about:

- rising left-wing violence;
- rising right-wing violence;
- anarchism;
- jihadism;
- foreign influence;
- protest movements;
- lone actors;
- online radicalisation;
- public disorder;
- declining social restraint;
- or a general crisis of democratic life.

The narrative may identify something real.

It may also exceed the evidence presently available.

## 🧭 The Connection Ladder

Not all connections are equally strong.

When several cases are placed together, identify the highest rung supported by evidence.

### 1. Proximity

The incidents occurred near one another in time or place.

This may explain why they are being discussed together, but it does not establish a shared cause.

### 2. Resemblance

The incidents share a visible feature:

- similar targets;
- similar methods;
- similar language;
- or similar political associations.

Resemblance can justify comparison. It does not establish coordination.

### 3. Shared Context

The incidents emerge from a common environment:

- the same political crisis;
- the same online ecosystem;
- the same local conflict;
- or the same widely circulated grievance.

A shared context may shape several people without creating an organisation.

### 4. Ideological Affinity

The offenders appear to share a political, religious or moral framework.

This strengthens the case for an ideological pattern but still does not prove contact, command or common strategy.

### 5. Social Or Organisational Link

The individuals share:

- a group;
- network;
- forum;
- organiser;
- funding route;
- communications channel;
- or recurring associates.

This may establish a meaningful network, although the nature of participation still matters.

### 6. Operational Coordination

There is evidence of:

- shared planning;
- direction;
- task allocation;
- material assistance;
- target selection;
- training;
- or common command.

This is substantially stronger than resemblance or affinity.

### 7. Campaign

Multiple acts form part of a continuing strategy intended to create a wider political effect.

A campaign usually involves some combination of:

- repeated action;
- shared purpose;
- recognisable methods;
- organisational continuity;
- strategic communication;
- and an intended audience beyond the immediate victim.

Public language should not move from the first rung to the seventh without showing the intervening evidence.

## 🎯 Political Target, Political Meaning And Political Motive

Three questions should be kept separate.

### Was The Target Political?

A politician, government office, party meeting or symbolic institution may clearly be a political target.

### Did The Incident Acquire Political Meaning?

An attack may reshape public debate, election campaigning, security policy or the behaviour of political representatives regardless of the perpetrator’s motive.

### Was The Perpetrator’s Motive Political?

This requires evidence about what the person was trying to achieve, express, punish or change.

These questions may all produce the same answer.

They may also diverge.

An attack with an unresolved or personalised motive can still have enormous political consequences.

Conversely, a politically motivated act may target property or infrastructure that initially appears non-political.

## 🏷️ Classification Is A Tool, Not A Complete Story

Classification is unavoidable.

Police, prosecutors, courts, intelligence agencies, researchers and journalists all need categories.

The danger begins when one category is asked to perform too many jobs.

A terrorism classification may determine:

- investigative powers;
- charging decisions;
- court procedure;
- sentencing;
- record-keeping;
- or institutional responsibility.

It may not explain:

- why this individual acted;
- why this target was selected;
- why violence became preferable to lawful action;
- whether others are likely to imitate the act;
- or which intervention would prevent recurrence.

Likewise, a broad administrative category may contain movements with different:

- histories;
- moral claims;
- targets;
- methods;
- casualty intentions;
- and relationships to the public.

Administrative grouping should not be mistaken for ideological unity.

## 🔗 When Repetition Becomes Apparent Evidence

Once incidents are repeatedly named together, audiences begin to remember the association even when the connective evidence is weak.

The pattern can become circular:

1. Cases are grouped because they appear similar.
2. The grouping produces a named threat.
3. Later cases are interpreted through that threat.
4. Their inclusion appears to confirm the original grouping.
5. The growing list is cited as proof of escalation.

This does not mean the pattern is necessarily false.

It means the method of construction must remain visible.

A threat category should not be validated solely by the fact that officials, politicians and journalists have repeatedly used it.

---

## 🪞 Why The Bundle Can Feel Socially Unreal

People do not encounter political-violence claims in isolation.

They compare them with the harms that shape their own lives:

- domestic abuse;
- sexual violence;
- child abuse;
- racial violence;
- workplace exploitation;
- street violence;
- inaccessible courts;
- untreated trauma;
- and institutional failures that continue for years.

When a small number of attacks on political figures produces immediate national attention, exceptional language and substantial security spending, the public may experience the response as disproportionate.

That reaction does not prove the political threat is imaginary.

It may instead reveal a second problem:

> The state can identify some harms as urgent without explaining why other severe and predictable harms remain routine.

Reporting should not force a false choice between political protection and public protection.

It should make the difference in attention, language and resources visible enough to be discussed.

## 🔭 Near And Distant Lenses

This node is a method rather than a single case study.

Its near lens should examine a contemporary political claim that groups several attacks or attempted attacks under one ideological heading.

Its distant lens should examine an earlier period in which distinct forms of violence were grouped into a broad public enemy category.

Possible distant lenses include:

- anarchist violence and the late nineteenth-century fear of a transnational anarchist conspiracy;
- Cold War treatment of labour militancy, civil disobedience and revolutionary violence as one communist threat;
- colonial authorities grouping reformers, nationalists, trade unionists and armed insurgents together;
- or post-attack security frameworks that merged religious conservatism, political Islam and violent jihadism.

The final choice should be made according to source quality and the specific contemporary claim being tested.

## 🌉 What Travels Between The Cases

Useful shared mechanisms may include:

- institutional pressure to classify quickly;
- the political usefulness of a named enemy;
- genuine violence sitting beside exaggerated association;
- administrative categories becoming public identities;
- movement boundaries being flattened;
- fear creating demand for simple explanations;
- and later incidents being interpreted through an earlier threat model.

These mechanisms can recur across very different political contexts.

---

## 🚧 What Does Not Travel

The comparison must not imply that different periods or movements are morally or operationally equivalent.

Differences may include:

- number of casualties;
- state capacity;
- access to weapons;
- degree of organisation;
- popular support;
- colonial or democratic context;
- availability of lawful participation;
- state repression;
- international sponsorship;
- and the intended scale of political transformation.

The point is to compare the construction of the bundle, not to declare the bundled actors identical.

## 🧾 A Sorting Table For Reporters

Before repeating a political-violence grouping, record the following for each incident:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Incident | What physically happened? |
| Date | When did it occur? |
| Target | Who or what was selected? |
| Consequence | Was anyone killed or injured? What damage occurred? |
| Legal status | Allegation, charge, conviction, acquittal or unresolved investigation? |
| Established motive | What has been demonstrated through reliable evidence? |
| Alleged motive | What has been claimed, and by whom? |
| Ideological material | What statements, texts, affiliations or actions are relevant? |
| Organisational link | Is there membership, contact, support or direction? |
| Operational link | Is there evidence of shared planning or assistance? |
| Official classification | How have authorities categorised it? |
| Political framing | How has the incident been described in public debate? |
| Evidential gaps | What remains unknown? |

Do not allow a shared entry in one field to imply shared entries in all the others.

Two cases may share a target type while having different motives.

They may share an ideology while having no organisational link.

They may share a legal category while operating through entirely different methods.

---

## ❓ Questions To Ask When A Bundle Appears

- Who created the grouping?
- What purpose does the category serve?
- Which specific incidents are included?
- Which incidents have been excluded?
- What feature is said to connect them?
- Is that feature ideological, operational, legal or merely visual?
- Are motives established or inferred?
- Is there evidence of contact or coordination?
- Are disrupted plans being counted alongside completed attacks?
- Are property offences being counted alongside lethal violence?
- Are convictions being counted alongside allegations?
- Does the category combine distinct political traditions?
- Has the definition changed over time?
- Is the grouping being used to justify a specific intervention?
- Would that intervention address the shared mechanism?
- What evidence would disprove or narrow the claim?

A serious threat assessment should be capable of becoming more precise as evidence improves.

A purely political category often expands instead.

---

## 🚫 What This Node Does Not Claim

This node does not claim that:

- separately planned incidents cannot reveal a real social trend;
- lone actors are harmless;
- ideological affinity is irrelevant;
- networks must be formally organised to create danger;
- broad categories have no administrative value;
- political actors should wait for perfect information before communicating;
- or every disputed classification is dishonest.

It argues that different kinds of connection should be described as different kinds of connection.

---

## 🌿 Working Principle

> A list of incidents is not yet a movement. A shared belief is not yet a network. A political target is not yet a political motive. The connections may be real, but they still have to be shown.
