# 🧬 Political Violence As A Human Problem

**First created:** 2026-07-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-23  
*Understanding why a human being comes to regard violence as politically meaningful is not a reward for violence. It is part of learning how to prevent it.*

<a id="top"></a>

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Political violence is a human problem for the deeply irritating reason that **human beings keep doing it**.

That sounds obvious. I do not think we consistently treat it as obvious.

There is a strong temptation, particularly after somebody has done something frightening or appalling, to put them immediately into a special category of human. Monster. Madman. Fanatic. Extremist. Terrorist. Evil. Brainwashed. Radicalised. Sometimes one or more of those words may describe something real. Sometimes they are legal classifications. Sometimes they are moral judgements. Sometimes they are attempts to describe ideology or behaviour.

None of them, by themselves, explains how the person got there.

If the question is prevention, **How could anybody do this?** is often the wrong question. Somebody did.

The more useful questions are:

> **How did this particular human being, in this particular information environment, come to understand violence as an available, permissible, necessary or desirable response?**

And then:

> **Why did most other people exposed to some of the same grievances, histories, identities, beliefs or circumstances not do it?**

Those are different questions from whether I agree with the person's grievance. They are different from whether the act was lawful. They are different from whether I think the act was morally justified. They are different from whether an organisation should be proscribed, whether somebody should be imprisoned, whether a target needs protection, or whether the state needs additional powers.

Throughout this pack I therefore keep pulling apart things which public discussion has a habit of shoving back together:

- explanation is not endorsement;
- understanding is not agreement;
- grievance is not justification;
- political belief is not political violence;
- structural influence is not the disappearance of agency;
- mental illness is not an explanation by default;
- a political target does not prove a political motive;
- state violence does not become legitimate merely because a state performed it;
- non-state violence does not become legitimate merely because somebody calls it resistance;
- legal classification, moral judgement, causal explanation and prevention are different jobs.

I am interested in political violence because I would like there to be less of it.

That requires treating it as something humans produce inside human systems.

## 🪞 Where I Am Writing From

I am not a neutral measuring instrument floating above this material.

Nobody is.

I am British and English. I come from the North East of England and from a working-class political environment in which Labour and socialist politics were ordinary parts of the landscape. I grew up around people for whom some positions that can sound unusually left-wing in contemporary Westminster discourse were not particularly exotic. Public healthcare is normal. Trade unions are normal. Strong public provision is normal. Tony Benn does not arrive in my head wearing a flashing neon sign reading **BEWARE: RADICAL OBJECT**.

That is information about my calibration, not proof that my calibration is correct.

My historical calibration matters too.

I grew up in the long shadow of the Troubles and during the peace process, rather than living the Troubles in Belfast or Derry. The Belfast/Good Friday Agreement was signed in 1998 and built a constitutional settlement around, among other things, the principle of consent and recognition of British and Irish identities in Northern Ireland. It did not make disagreement disappear. It helped construct institutions through which profound disagreement could continue without being settled by continuing political violence.

I do not claim experiential equivalence with people who lived that conflict.

I have, however, lived at several different distances from the systems around it.

There is **cultural proximity**: books, songs, television, news, political language and inherited historical narratives.

There is **institutional proximity**: growing up British within a state whose security and counter-terrorism architecture was partly shaped by political violence connected to Northern Ireland and then repeatedly reshaped by later threats.

There is **relational proximity**: people I love have, at different times, had professional responsibilities that brought them into proximity with political violence. I have sometimes been frightened for them because of it.

There is **social proximity**: as an adult, I have encountered people for whom Catholic–Protestant relationships and the question of where it felt possible to live were not historical material in a novel but part of their own lives. Those are their experiences, not mine, and I am deliberately not using them here as case studies.

There is **analytical proximity**: I spend a frankly unreasonable amount of time trying to understand political systems, security institutions, information environments and the people moving through them.

And there remains **experiential distance**.

That distinction matters.

> **Locate the observer. Do not reduce the observer to the location.**

I can be affected by a history without having lived somebody else's version of it. I can have useful information about a system without possessing all of the information available to somebody standing somewhere else inside it.

That is one of the reasons I want to know what they can see.

## 🧠 What My Position May Make Me Miss

When people talk about unconscious bias, there is sometimes a temptation to imagine an especially sinister little goblin living behind the eyes.

Often it is considerably more mundane.

It is where your cognition tends to go when you leave it unattended.

What sounds normal?

What sounds extreme?

What is government obviously for?

What counts as intolerable state interference?

What counts as basic social provision?

What kind of weapon looks frightening?

What kind looks respectable?

Which institutions receive the benefit of the doubt?

Which historical analogy arrives in your head first?

Which political figure sounds moderate before you have even examined the proposition?

My ruler has a calibration.

Growing up where I did means that universal healthcare, substantial public provision, redistribution, trade-union politics and scepticism about concentrated private economic power can feel intuitively ordinary to me. Jeremy Corbyn and Tony Benn therefore occupy a different place on my *felt* political spectrum than they do for somebody raised inside another political tradition.

The same thing works in reverse. Margaret Thatcher feels politically radical to me. Some of David Cameron's programme feels politically radical to me. Somebody formed inside Conservative politics may experience both very differently.

This node does not need to decide whose internal ruler is the One True Ruler.

It needs to notice that there are rulers.

> **The fact that something feels like common sense to me is not evidence that it is common sense.**

Some of my priors will survive examination. Some may become stronger once I examine the evidence. Some will turn out to have been based on incomplete information, emotional inheritance or assumptions I had never realised I was making.

That is not a reason to have no convictions.

It is a reason to know that I have a camera position.

## 🛡️ Unfortunately, I Cannot Call Myself A Pacifist

This would all be considerably tidier if I could.

I practise political nonviolence. I am strongly influenced by nonviolent political traditions. I do not use violence to pursue my political objectives. I am deeply interested in how societies maintain peace and how political movements can produce change without hurting people.

Unfortunately, I have been raised with rather too much Second World War and an extremely persistent **fuck off my fucking island, you bastards** reflex to describe myself honestly as a pacifist.

There it is.

I believe there are circumstances in which people may legitimately be defended by force.

That belief is not floating free of culture either. Family history, British historical memory, education and the stories through which I learnt the Second World War have supplied me with an extremely available example of violence understood as defensive necessity.

It would be remarkably convenient for me to analyse everybody else's inherited stories about when violence becomes legitimate while treating my own as simply common sense.

So:

**I have one too.**

That means I cannot solve political violence by writing:

> Violence is always wrong. Analysis complete.

I have already conceded the difficult bit. I think force can sometimes be justified.

Now I have paperwork.

When?

By whom?

Under whose authority?

Against whom?

For what purpose?

To what degree?

Under what law?

Who scrutinises it?

Who can object?

Who investigates misuse?

What happens when defensive force becomes punitive force?

What happens when deterrence becomes domination?

What happens when the state identifies the threat incorrectly?

How does an emergency end?

Those questions become more important, not less, if I think a state sometimes genuinely needs coercive capacity.

## 🪖 “Of Course A Country Has An Army”

This is one of those assumptions which becomes interesting as soon as somebody pokes it.

Of course a country has an army.

Does it?

There is nothing in the laws of physics requiring a political community to maintain an armed force. States have made radically different arrangements for defence, and even states which maintain militaries make political choices about their size, purpose, command, deployment, weapons and relationship to civilian government.

I happen to support maintaining effective armed forces capable of defending territory and people.

That is a political position.

It is so normal within my own information environment that it can easily disguise itself as furniture.

Once a political community deliberately maintains an institution capable of organised lethal violence, an enormous governance problem follows.

Who commands it? Who authorises deployment? What is a lawful order? What limits apply to the use of force? What protections apply to civilians? What happens when somebody inside the institution believes something unlawful is happening? How does democratic scrutiny work without making effective defence impossible? Under what circumstances may military capacity be used domestically? What separates an institution maintained for defence from an institution available for political domination?

Unfortunately, having admitted that some organised defensive violence may be legitimate, **there is now paperwork**.

The paperwork is not incidental.

Civilian control, law, scrutiny, doctrine, reporting systems, courts, oversight and rules of engagement are among the mechanisms through which societies try to stop possession of coercive capacity becoming its own justification for using it.

This is also why I am interested in the oddness of how completely military capacity has fused with our idea of statehood. The post-First World War settlement did not literally prohibit Germany from possessing any army; Versailles imposed severe restrictions upon German military capacity. But even the idea of externally restricting a state's military capacity feels to many people like restricting the state itself.

That reaction is worth examining.

Not because the answer must be **ARMIES BAD**.

Because things we stop perceiving as choices are exactly the things whose assumptions are easiest to leave unattended.

## 📚 I Deliberately Read People I Disagree With

I learnt this habit young.

When I was a teenager interviewing through rounds for military boarding school, one of the expectations around current affairs was that you read widely, including views you would not normally take yourself.

Whatever else I think about the institutions and environments involved, this was useful training.

I kept it.

One reason I deliberately read people I disagree with is that I know how many of my own political beliefs arrived as common sense before I ever had the opportunity to examine them as politics.

If I know that happened to me, why would I assume it did not happen to anybody else?

That does not mean everybody is partly right.

It does not mean truth lives magically halfway between two opinions.

It does not mean every claim deserves equal evidential weight.

It does not mean that listening to somebody creates an obligation to agree with them.

It means that if I do not know what information somebody is processing, I do not yet know why we have reached different conclusions.

Sometimes I understand somebody's model and discover that part of my own was wrong.

Sometimes I discover that we agree about a problem and disagree completely about the solution.

Sometimes we have different values.

Sometimes their evidence is terrible.

Sometimes their evidence is good and their inference is terrible.

Sometimes I understand the whole thing perfectly well and conclude:

**Nope. Still think you're wrong, mate.**

That is allowed.

Understanding somebody's model is not accepting their model.

## 🦆 Know Your Strengths

There is another reason I keep doing this.

I seem to find it relatively easy.

I am genuinely interested in why people think things. I can sit with somebody whose worldview differs substantially from mine and ask them how they got there. I might argue. I might not. Sometimes I would rather keep asking questions because the model itself is interesting.

I am aware that this costs other people considerably more.

That does not make me a superior human being. There are plenty of things other people seem able to do with the ease of water leaving a duck while I stand nearby wondering whether we could perhaps all go home.

Some people can apparently walk into a room containing twenty human beings and make all of them perform the necessary actions for an event to happen.

**Witchcraft.**

Human capacities are unevenly distributed.

Polaris comes back to this repeatedly: know your strengths.

If something socially useful comes unusually easily to you, **use the fucking thing**.

A functioning society does not require every person to calmly interview somebody whose ideology frightens, insults or dehumanises them. Nobody owes endless personal exposure to hostile political material.

Certain roles, however, create stronger obligations.

If you govern a plural society, legislate for it, research it, report on it, negotiate inside it, design security policy for it or build interventions intended to change people's behaviour, **I find these people unpleasant so I do not need to understand them** is a very poor operating model.

Somebody needs an accurate map.

## 🧭 Understanding Somebody's Model Is Not Accepting Their Model

So I want to know:

What have you experienced?

What history are you carrying?

What are you frightened of?

What do you believe has been done to you?

Which parts of that belief are demonstrably true?

Which are disputed?

Which are false?

Who do you trust?

What information have you never encountered?

What community makes this interpretation meaningful?

What do you think the state is for?

What do you think legitimate resistance looks like?

What looks like common sense from where you are standing?

And then — critically — **ask those questions of yourself as well**.

An accurate model can reveal genuine grievance and still reveal an indefensible proposed response.

It can reveal manipulated information.

It can reveal prejudice.

It can reveal rational self-interest.

It can reveal fear.

It can reveal historical memory.

It can reveal belonging.

It can reveal a conspiracy theory sitting next to a perfectly legitimate complaint sitting next to an extremely real material injury sitting next to somebody who has decided that all of this means they are allowed to hurt a stranger.

Humans are inconvenient like that.

The job is not to award the worldview a mark out of ten.

The job is to understand the processing system well enough to know what produced the output.

## 🧬 Political Violence Is Something Humans Do

This is the part where the inquiry becomes uncomfortable.

Human beings sometimes become willing to hurt other human beings for political purposes.

If we make them into a different species after the fact, we protect ourselves emotionally at the cost of understanding the mechanism.

**Monster** can be a moral judgement.

It is not much of a causal model.

If political violence is human behaviour, then the relevant field of inquiry becomes enormous.

We may need cognition.

Relationships.

History.

Political institutions.

Material conditions.

Identity.

Social networks.

Group dynamics.

Opportunity.

Capability.

Culture.

Moral reasoning.

Education.

Media.

Trauma.

Status.

Belonging.

Law.

Economics.

Security.

And, sometimes, psychiatry.

Humanising somebody does not exonerate them.

It merely refuses to pretend that the behaviour arrived from outside humanity.

That matters because prevention has to operate on humans.

## 🧲 Why This Belief? Why Violence?

These are not the same question.

Why does somebody believe that a grievance exists?

Why do they believe a particular group caused it?

Why do they believe an institution is illegitimate?

Why does an identity become politically central?

Why does a particular historical narrative become compelling?

Those questions may help explain a worldview.

They do not yet explain violence.

The second transition is:

> **Why did violence become an acceptable proposed solution?**

This distinction matters enormously because most people who are angry do not become politically violent.

Most people who experience injustice do not become politically violent.

Most people who hold radical political beliefs do not become politically violent.

Most people who share some ideological material with a violent actor do not perform the same act.

So we should be extremely suspicious of a model that quietly becomes:

```text
grievance
    ↓
radical belief
    ↓
violence
```

Human behaviour is not a fucking airport travelator.

A more useful provisional map might look something like:

```text
grievance
    ↓
interpretation
    ↓
identity / belonging
    ↓
moral permission
    ↓
violent imagination
    ↓
intent
    ↓
preparation
    ↓
capability
    ↓
target access
    ↓
act
```

But even that must not become **The Radicalisation Conveyor Belt™**.

People stop.

People reverse.

People disengage.

People skip stages.

People fantasise and never act.

People prepare and then withdraw.

People change communities.

People encounter new information.

People lose access.

People acquire somebody they love more than they love the cause.

People decide prison actually sounds shit.

People remain furious for sixty years and never deliberately hurt anybody.

The interesting analytical problem is not merely the presence of grievance.

It is the transition.

## 🧮 Most Angry People Do Not Become Violent

This denominator should haunt the entire pack.

Suppose a political grievance is widespread.

Suppose distrust of an institution is widespread.

Suppose an extremist text has a large readership.

Suppose thousands of people share an identity.

Suppose millions of people are angry.

If a tiny number become violent, the widespread characteristic cannot by itself explain the rare behaviour.

That does not make the widespread characteristic irrelevant.

It means we need another question.

Why this person?

Why this moment?

Why this method?

Why this target?

Why did the inhibition fail here?

Why did a violent solution become morally imaginable?

Why did imagination become intent?

Why did intent acquire capability?

Why did capability reach the target?

That is where political science has to meet criminology, psychology, sociology, history, security studies and everything else we can drag into the room.

## 🪫 Why Accept This Risk To Yourself?

There is another part of political violence that I think we sometimes discuss badly.

Some acts expose the perpetrator to an extraordinary foreseeable risk of death, serious injury, imprisonment, retaliation, loss of livelihood, family rupture or permanent social exclusion.

That does not mean the perpetrator is suicidal.

It does mean **self-preservation is a legitimate question**.

Did they expect to survive?

Did they plan an escape?

Did they expect imprisonment?

Did they talk about death?

Was sacrifice part of the political or religious narrative?

Was martyrdom relevant?

Was there evidence of hopelessness?

Was there prior suicidal ideation or self-harm?

Could they imagine a future for themselves outside the violent act?

Those are empirical questions.

They should not be answered by staring at an attack and saying:

> Well, you'd have to be mad.

No.

**Recklessness is not suicidality.**

**Willingness to risk death is not necessarily a desire to die.**

A suicidal person can retain political agency.

A political actor can also be mentally ill.

A person can be traumatised, ideologically committed, socially embedded, politically aggrieved and clinically unwell at the same fucking time.

Human beings have rudely declined to organise themselves into one-variable research categories.

---

## 🧠 Mental Health Without “They're All Mad”

This is therefore an area where I want both psychiatry **and restraint**.

Research has not established a simple relationship in which terrorism or radicalisation can be explained by psychiatric disorder. Systematic reviews have found substantial methodological limitations and inconsistent prevalence estimates, while some evidence suggests particular subgroups — including some lone actors — may show higher rates of certain disorders.

That is exactly the kind of evidence that should make us ask better questions rather than adopt a slogan.

Mental illness may be:

- causal;
- contributory;
- incidental;
- misattributed;
- undiagnosed;
- absent.

We should not retrospectively diagnose people because their beliefs seem bizarre to us.

We should not infer psychosis from ideological extremity.

We should not infer suicidality from risk-taking.

We should not decide that somebody lacked agency merely because they had a psychiatric diagnosis.

But neither should we create a political taboo in which mental health may never be investigated because doing so might "depoliticise" the offender.

If there is evidence, investigate it.

If there is not, do not invent it.

## 🧑‍🔬 Bring More Disciplines Into The Room

Political violence is too complicated to belong entirely to one profession.

| Discipline | One useful question |
|---|---|
| Political science | What institutions, conflicts and distributions of power matter? |
| History | What happened before this event that participants believe matters now? |
| Sociology | What groups, identities, networks and social structures matter? |
| Criminology | What distinguishes offending from belief or grievance? |
| Forensic psychology | What individual pathways, decisions and behavioural patterns matter? |
| Psychiatry | Where clinically evidenced mental illness is relevant, what role did it play? |
| Anthropology | How do meaning, ritual, identity and cultural interpretation operate? |
| Economics | What material incentives, exclusions and resource conflicts matter? |
| Education | How are histories, identities and civic possibilities transmitted? |
| Public health | What population-level interventions reduce harm? |
| Security studies | How does violent intent acquire capability and reach a target? |

No single discipline gets to declare itself **The Explanation™**.

And no table makes the disciplines independent.

Economic conditions alter social relationships. Historical narratives affect political identities. Institutions affect material opportunity. Mental health is experienced inside culture. Security responses change political information environments.

That mess is the point.

## ⚖️ Agency Survives Explanation

There is a strange fear that if we explain somebody's behaviour too well, responsibility will evaporate.

I do not think it does.

Somebody can have been genuinely harmed.

Marginalised.

Discriminated against.

Traumatised.

Manipulated.

Mentally ill.

Politically excluded.

Economically precarious.

Fed propaganda.

Embedded in a violent social network.

And still make decisions.

Structural causation and human agency are not mutually exclusive.

A legitimate grievance does not automatically legitimise the response chosen to address it.

I can understand every fucking thing that happened to somebody and still eventually arrive at:

> **Mate. What exactly were you planning to achieve by blowing this shit up?**

That is not a failure of empathy.

It is taking the person's agency seriously enough to ask about the decision.

## ♻️ Everyone Is Generating Information

This is where political violence becomes very obviously an Embodied Information Ecology problem.

A violent actor has a model of the state.

The state has a model of the violent actor.

Communities have models of both.

Journalists report those models.

Political leaders interpret the reporting.

Security services interpret behaviour.

People interpret security responses.

Everybody is generating information.

A crude feedback loop might look like:

```text
grievance
    ↓
violent action
    ↓
state response
    ↓
experienced protection / repression / injustice
    ↓
changed grievance narrative
    ↓
solidarity / disengagement / recruitment / fear
    ↓
changed likelihood of further violence
```

That is not a law.

A state response can remove capability.

It can protect targets.

It can reassure frightened people.

It can deter somebody.

It can create an exit route.

It can disrupt a network.

It can also validate an adversary's propaganda, widen identification with a grievance, undermine institutional legitimacy or create new recruitment material.

The response becomes part of the next information environment.

This is why I am so interested in feedback.

**Everyone is generating information. Pay fucking attention to it.**

## 🎮 Do Not Make Yourself Easy To Play

If somebody understands your response system better than you understand theirs, you have a problem.

Suppose an actor knows that action X reliably produces state response Y.

Response Y is now part of the actor's strategic environment.

Was provoking repression part of the objective?

Was polarisation useful?

Was publicity the objective?

Was martyrdom useful?

Would an indiscriminate crackdown make recruitment easier?

Was the immediate target actually the intended audience?

Was the purpose to destroy something, or to make the state reveal something about itself?

These are not arguments against responding to violence.

Sometimes capability has to be interrupted immediately.

Sometimes people need physical protection.

Sometimes somebody needs arresting.

The point is that **reaction is not the same thing as strategy**.

Understanding an adversary's model is not softness.

It is part of not allowing somebody else to operate your system for you.

That affects how I write.

## 📖 Voltaire Never Said The Voltaire Quote

This is an excellent little information problem.

Voltaire is useful here not because I need an eighteenth-century Frenchman to settle twenty-first-century politics for me.

He is useful as a **forerunner of modern civil libertarianism**: somebody whose writing and public interventions repeatedly engaged questions of intellectual liberty, religious intolerance, censorship and coercive authority.

He also had skin in the game.

Voltaire's life included imprisonment, exile, censorship and conflicts with powerful institutions. That does not make every opinion he held correct. It does mean that his concern with coercive power was not merely a parlour exercise conducted under conditions in which nothing could happen to him.

And then we get the famous Voltaire quote.

Except Voltaire did not write it.

The line usually rendered as **"I disapprove of what you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it"** came from Evelyn Beatrice Hall's *The Friends of Voltaire* as a memorable description of the attitude she attributed to him.

This is fantastic.

Not only because a woman managed to write one of the most successful summaries of somebody else's intellectual attitude and then have her own name fall off it.

Although:

**well done, everybody. On brand.**

It is also a tiny worked example of information transmission:

```text
Voltaire's writing and conduct
        ↓
Hall interprets Voltaire
        ↓
Hall produces an extremely effective compression
        ↓
the interpreter disappears
        ↓
the compression is attributed to its subject
        ↓
context disappears
        ↓
the quotation becomes a portable political object
        ↓
new audiences give the object new meanings
```

We have somehow managed to lose both the woman **and** the argument.

Excellent work.

The useful question is not **Would Voltaire vote for my preferred political party?**

Please leave the dead Frenchman alone.

The useful historical problem is the one his life and work repeatedly force us to confront:

> **What happens when institutions possessing coercive power decide that being right gives them permission to physically dominate the people they consider wrong?**

That is also why invoking "Voltaire" cannot mean **I am allowed to say whatever I want and everybody else has to shut the fuck up**.

Everybody else has freedom of expression too.

Criticism is not automatically censorship.

Disagreement is not automatically persecution.

Loss of a guaranteed platform is not identical to imprisonment.

Social consequences are not automatically state coercion.

At the same time, a society that cares about civil liberty has to remain alert to actual coercive suppression even when the person being suppressed is unpopular.

That is the difficult bit.

Voltaire is interesting because the difficult bit was the point.

## 🕊️ Peace Is Work

I am deeply suspicious of language that treats peace as what naturally happens when everybody stops being difficult.

Peace is not an empty space.

It is work.

People can disagree about territory, sovereignty, religion, history, economics, identity and justice for generations.

The question is whether a society has enough functioning mechanisms for those disagreements not to require continual violence.

The Belfast/Good Friday Agreement is useful here precisely because it did not require everybody in Northern Ireland to suddenly acquire the same constitutional preference. Its settlement recognised competing identities and rested Northern Ireland's constitutional status on consent.

That is more interesting than **everybody agreed**.

They did not.

Peace can require institutions capable of containing disagreement without erasing it.

It can require:

- legitimate routes for political change;
- credible institutions;
- participation;
- toleration;
- material security;
- trusted dispute resolution;
- limits upon state coercion;
- effective protection from violence;
- ways to challenge government;
- ways to lose a political argument without becoming politically nonexistent;
- relationships capable of surviving disagreement;
- historical memory;
- recognition;
- repair.

> **Peace is one of the things human beings construct so that disagreement does not continually become violence.**

And constructed things require maintenance.

## 🌱 Why Study The Perpetrator At All?

Because I would like fewer perpetrators.

That requires knowing more than what label applies after somebody has already acted.

Why did violent intent form?

What converted belief into action?

What inhibited other people who shared some of the same grievance?

What enabled preparation?

What supplied capability?

What allowed target access?

What intervention might have altered the trajectory?

Which interventions made it worse?

I find it useful to separate two broad problems.

### Threat production

Why does somebody become willing to commit political violence?

### Threat penetration

How does somebody who possesses that intent acquire the capability and access required to harm the target?

These are connected.

They are not identical.

A security system can become very good at protecting a building while doing nothing about the production of violent intent.

A social intervention can reduce recruitment while doing very little against an already operational actor.

Surveillance may reveal capability without resolving grievance.

Physical security may stop target penetration without changing the information environment producing the threat.

Different problems require different interventions.

If we call all of them **terrorism problem**, we make it much harder to know what the fuck we are trying to fix.

## 🧯 Understanding Is Part Of Prevention

This is the proposition underneath the entire node.

> **Understanding is not a reward we give to people whose behaviour we approve of. It is a prerequisite for changing systems whose outcomes we want to change.**

Understanding does not absolve.

It does not forgive.

It does not make every grievance true.

It does not make every actor equivalent.

It does not eliminate criminal responsibility.

It does not make violence inevitable.

It does not require adopting the perpetrator's worldview.

It gives us more information.

And if we are serious about prevention, more accurate information about how the behaviour is produced is useful.

That includes information about violent actors.

It includes information about states.

It includes information about communities.

It includes information about institutions.

It includes information about historical memory.

It includes information about us.

I do not think the useful final question is:

> **How could anybody do this?**

Somebody did.

The question I want to keep asking is:

> **Given that humans keep fucking doing this, what do we have to keep doing so that we don't?**

That is where political violence belongs in **Prosocial Roots**.

Not because the violence is prosocial.

Because preventing it is.

🧬 **Human behaviour** — political violence remains human behaviour even when it is horrifying.

🪞 **Observer position** — investigators also process information through histories, identities and priors.

♻️ **Embodied Information Ecology** — grievance, violence, security responses and political narratives alter one another through feedback.

⚖️ **Agency** — causal explanation and personal responsibility can coexist.

🕊️ **Maintained peace** — peace is a social and institutional achievement, not merely the absence of an explosion today.
