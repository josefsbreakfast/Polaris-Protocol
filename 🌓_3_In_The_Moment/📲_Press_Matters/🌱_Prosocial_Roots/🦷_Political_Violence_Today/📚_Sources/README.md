Drafting Instructions: 🦷 Political Violence Today

Location

📲_Press_Matters/
└── 🌱_Prosocial_Roots/
    └── 🦷_Political_Violence_Today/

This is a Press Matters pack for reporters, editors and public-interest writers. It is not primarily a counterterrorism manual, ideological critique or argument for a particular political settlement.

Its purpose is to help journalists describe political violence accurately, understand why current threat language can feel disconnected from public experience, and test whether proposed security responses fit the actual mechanism of harm.

⸻

Controlling propositions

The pack should remain anchored to the following:

Political violence must be taken seriously enough to describe accurately.

Do not ignore a small threat, inflate it into the wrong threat, or build an expensive security response around an ideological label when the mechanism of harm remains unclear.

Explanation is not absolution. Responsibility belongs to the perpetrator; prevention requires understanding the conditions that repeatedly produce willing perpetrators.

Similarity is not culpability. Refusing to recognise any similarity between violent actors and the communities from which they emerge makes prevention harder.

A political target does not automatically establish a coherent political motive, movement or terrorist campaign.

Opening a subject for discussion does not mean giving every position equal factual or moral weight.

⸻

Editorial posture

Write from a position of clarification without mind-reading.

Do not assume that a politician, official, campaigner or journalist is:

* lying;
* cynical;
* frightened;
* opportunistic;
* strategically manipulating language;
* or sincerely inhabiting a different threat reality.

Any of those may be true, and several may coexist. The pack should ordinarily leave that question open unless there is direct evidence.

Focus instead on:

* what is being claimed;
* what is known;
* what remains unresolved;
* what categories are being combined;
* what evidence connects the cases;
* what intervention is proposed;
* and whether the intervention fits the mechanism of harm.

The tone should not be prosecutorial. It should be exact, curious and useful.

⸻

What the pack is not doing

The pack does not claim that:

* left-wing, anarchist or single-issue violence does not exist;
* low-frequency threats can safely be ignored;
* political actors are necessarily dishonest;
* mental illness automatically explains political violence;
* sympathy for a cause excuses dangerous conduct;
* all ideologies generate violence in the same way;
* political representatives should not receive protection;
* surveillance and preventive intelligence have achieved nothing;
* every difficult opinion deserves equal evidential weight;
* video games or media exposure directly cause firearm violence;
* young men are unintelligent or inherently violent;
* understanding an offender removes moral or legal responsibility;
* or political violence can be reduced to one demographic or political tendency.

Include these limits explicitly where readers could otherwise infer them.

Expected audience

Primary readers include:

* reporters covering politics, crime, security or protest;
* editors working under deadline;
* researchers trying to understand inconsistent public statistics;
* journalists encountering unstable ideological labels;
* commentators writing across UK and US political contexts;
* and readers who recognise that current rhetoric does not neatly match their experience of daily violence.

Assume readers are intelligent but may not be specialists in terrorism law, criminology, Islamic terminology, firearms regulation or security science.

Define technical terms clearly. Do not condescend.

⸻

The Two-Lens Method

Most substantive nodes should contain two contrasting examples.

Near case

Use a contemporary or recent case that explains why the issue matters now.

Distant case

Use a case that differs meaningfully through:

* time;
* country;
* ideology;
* community;
* social class;
* method;
* target;
* degree of organisation;
* or level of harm.

The purpose is not to claim equivalence. It is to expose the underlying mechanism.

Each comparison should identify:

What travels

The analytical feature shared by the cases.

What does not travel

The differences that make direct equivalence misleading.

Prevention question

What a serious prevention strategy would have to consider in both cases.

Use examples from as diverse a range of countries, communities and political traditions as the evidence permits. Political violence must be presented as a recurring human possibility, not the pathology of one alien group.

⸻

Evidential standards

Clearly distinguish:

* confirmed fact;
* prosecution allegation;
* intelligence assessment;
* political attribution;
* media inference;
* unresolved motive;
* historical interpretation;
* and the project’s own analytical inference.

Do not convert:

* an arrest into a conviction;
* a political target into a proven political motive;
* an individual belief into organisational membership;
* online rhetoric into operational direction;
* a residual statistical category into a precise ideological count;
* or one incident into evidence of a coordinated campaign.

Use exact dates where they matter.

For live cases, state what was publicly known at the relevant date and mark later developments separately.

Primary sources should carry the greatest weight:

* court documents;
* official investigation findings;
* police statements;
* government statistics;
* parliamentary reports;
* inspectorate reports;
* legislation;
* and original speeches or interviews.

High-quality reporting can supply chronology and context but should not silently replace primary evidence.

⸻

Handling statistics

Explain why political-violence figures are often difficult to locate or compare.

Common problems include:

* LASIT being a composite category;
* cases being placed under “Other”;
* prison populations being stocks rather than annual flows;
* preparatory offences being mixed with completed attacks;
* hidden intelligence investigations;
* disrupted plots remaining partly secret;
* sabotage being prosecuted as ordinary crime;
* legal definitions varying across countries;
* and survey methodologies measuring different populations or offences.

Where precise numbers are unavailable, use a defensible upper bound.

For example:

Even if every person in the broadest residual category were incorrectly counted as LASIT, the result would remain small. The true figure must be lower.

Never present that upper bound as the actual number.

Always keep raw counts, rates, years, geography, definitions and methodological cautions together.

⸻

Core analytical distinctions

The pack should repeatedly preserve these distinctions.

Political target versus political motive

A politician may be selected because of ideology, symbolic importance, personal fixation, grievance, notoriety-seeking, perceived betrayal or mixed motives.

Ideology versus operational profile

Knowing that someone holds left-wing, right-wing, nationalist, religious or anarchist views does not automatically tell us:

* their target;
* their capability;
* their method;
* their organisation;
* their casualty intention;
* or the intervention likely to work.

Threat production versus threat penetration

Ask separately:

1. Why did the person develop violent intent?
2. Why could they obtain the necessary capability and reach the target?

A rhetorical campaign against an ideology may do little to correct protective-security failures.

Violence against people versus violence against systems

Ask what the offender believes the enemy to be:

* an individual;
* an identity group;
* a political class;
* a government;
* an institution;
* infrastructure;
* machinery;
* property;
* or an entire system.

This conception affects target choice and casualty patterns.

Cause versus method

A cause may attract public sympathy while its methods remain unlawful, coercive, reckless or lethal.

Emotional legitimacy versus legal legitimacy

An intervention may feel compassionate or brave without being lawful, proportionate, safe or practically beneficial.

Explanation versus absolution

Causal analysis must not erase agency or responsibility.

Recognition versus endorsement

Acknowledging that a constituency exists and has a concern does not require agreeing with its preferred answer.

Discussion versus equal weighting

A democratic press may open a subject while still concluding that one argument is factually false, historically ignorant or morally unacceptable.

⸻

How violence becomes meaningful

Do not explain political violence through ideology alone.

Ask both:

Why this belief?

and:

Why did this person convert belief, grievance or fantasy into physical harm?

Potential contributing conditions may include:

* blocked adulthood;
* economic abandonment;
* humiliation;
* social isolation;
* weak belonging;
* loss of status;
* peer permission;
* excitement;
* online reinforcement;
* attraction to sacrifice;
* a search for significance;
* belief that lawful participation is futile;
* or the absence of anything constructive in which to invest.

Two broad pathways may overlap:

Overwhelming conviction

The stakes become absolute, compromise becomes betrayal and violence appears morally purified by the cause.

Collapsed investment

The person lacks a credible future, social role, community or legitimate route to agency. An ideology supplies a role, enemy and promise of significance.

Do not turn these into universal formulas. They are analytical routes, not diagnoses.

⸻

Democratic and prosocial framing

The deeper subject is how people with radically different experiences, fears and beliefs continue to recognise one another as members of the same society.

The pack should support:

* factual correction without social expulsion;
* firm boundaries without contempt;
* curiosity about difficult constituencies;
* consistency across politically convenient and inconvenient victims;
* and prevention responses that do not create more alienation than they resolve.

A functioning democracy does not require every commentator to present every side sympathetically.

It does require enough people and institutions to:

* preserve accurate history;
* understand affected constituencies;
* discuss unresolved policy questions;
* reject dehumanisation;
* and prevent difficult subjects from being monopolised by radical actors.

Useful line:

Democracy is not agreement. It is the discipline of examining the whole subject with people whose conclusions differ from ours while continuing to recognise one another as members of the same society.

⸻

Animal liberation material

Use animal liberation as a case study in contested public morality, not as comic relief or an invitation to excuse unlawful conduct.

Hold the following together:

* genuine animal suffering;
* scientific and medical benefit;
* unnecessary or duplicative experimentation;
* replacement and reduction policies;
* failed legal welfare routes;
* public sympathy for rescue;
* risks to staff and emergency responders;
* ecological and infection risks;
* animal trauma;
* and the need for proper aftercare.

Key point:

Extraction is not synonymous with rescue. A rescue includes what happens afterwards.

Animal-removal cases should consider:

* veterinary triage;
* species-appropriate handling;
* quiet transport;
* quarantine;
* bonded animals;
* behavioural stress;
* stabilisation;
* and long-term placement.

The public’s mixed view is not necessarily incoherent. People may distinguish cause, method, target, proportionality and consequence.

⸻

Weapon-culture material

Keep this cluster proportionate. It is illustrative, not the centre of the entire pack.

Dunblane

Treat Rupert Lowe’s description as a case in:

* factual compression;
* selective curiosity;
* public memory;
* responsibility when reopening a safety settlement;
* and the difference between “one incident” and “one murder.”

Do not frame the discussion as requiring commentators to treat handgun liberalisation sympathetically.

The point is that an advocate for major policy change should understand the history, casualties and competing interests that produced the existing rule.

Knives and firearms

Examine British cultural scripts:

* the racialised, urbanised knife;
* the respectable rural rifle or shotgun;
* and the handgun as an unstable category between these social worlds.

Do not erase real legal differences. Examine how race, class, geography and presumed respectability shape who is imagined as threatening or legitimate.

Young people and media

Keep the claim narrow:

Younger people developed their understanding of firearms in a different media environment from many adults now making policy or commentary.

Do not claim that games or American media directly cause violence.

Possible effects worth asking about include:

* familiarity;
* glamour;
* technical vocabulary;
* tactical aesthetics;
* status;
* rebellion;
* and ideas of competence or adulthood.

Adults should not project their pre-internet media childhood onto younger cohorts.

Ask teenagers and young adults what they have actually seen and what firearms signify to them.

Young men should be treated as an audience, not merely a risk category. Intelligence does not eliminate adolescent sensitivity to status, novelty, peer performance or forbidden-object glamour.

A punitive response can accidentally turn a safety boundary into a status challenge.

⸻

Security, surveillance and spending

Acknowledge that:

* intelligence work has probably prevented serious attacks;
* CCTV can help reconstruct offences;
* digital evidence can expose preparation and networks;
* and the public will not know every disrupted plot.

Then test the limits:

* surveillance may record rather than prevent harm;
* more data can create overload;
* predictive systems can reproduce political bias;
* commercial contracts can reward permanent threat inflation;
* and some forms of violence remain chronically underfunded despite extensive surveillance capacity.

Useful line:

A society can become more observable without becoming proportionately safer.

For each proposal, ask:

* What threat is claimed?
* What mechanism produces it?
* What intervention is proposed?
* How should that intervention interrupt the mechanism?
* What evidence supports that theory?
* What are the civil-liberty and financial costs?
* What other services may lose resources?
* How will success be measured?
* What happens when the ideological classification is wrong?

Security provision should follow demonstrated risk, not merely political status.

Equal entitlement to safety does not require identical protection for every office-holder.

⸻

Language instructions

Use terminology carefully.

LASIT

Always expand Left-Wing, Anarchist and Single-Issue Terrorism on first use.

Explain that it combines distinct traditions and activities.

Islamism, jihadism and jihad

Do not use these interchangeably.

* Islamism is a broad family of political projects relating Islam and governance.
* Jihadism usually refers in contemporary security language to militant revolutionary currents legitimising violence through a particular interpretation of jihad.
* Jihad has much wider religious meanings involving striving or struggle and is not synonymous with terrorism.

Avoid claiming that jihad has always or only had a peaceful meaning. Its historical meanings are broader and have included armed struggle.

Terrorism and ordinary crime

Do not assume every politically meaningful act is legally terrorism.

Mark the difference between:

* criminal damage;
* sabotage;
* intimidation;
* public disorder;
* assault;
* hate crime;
* preparation of terrorism;
* and completed terrorist attacks.

False friends

When an Arabic, Hebrew, religious or ideological term has acquired a narrowed political meaning in English, explain the gap rather than treating the borrowed shorthand as the whole meaning.

Reporter-facing outputs

The final cluster should provide directly usable tools.

Incident template

For each case record:

* date;
* location;
* target;
* perpetrator or alleged perpetrator;
* act;
* legal status;
* survival or death;
* established motive;
* alleged motive;
* ideological evidence;
* organisational links;
* capability and access;
* unresolved questions;
* official classification;
* subsequent political framing;
* and source quality.

Questions before repeating a label

Ask:

* Who supplied the label?
* Is it legal, analytical or rhetorical?
* What evidence establishes motive?
* Is the person connected to an organisation?
* Is there evidence of direction or merely affinity?
* What other cases are being grouped with it?
* What operational feature do the cases share?
* What remains unknown?
* Does the proposed response address that shared feature?

Threat-response matrix

Compare:

* claimed threat;
* causal mechanism;
* proposed response;
* plausible benefit;
* foreseeable harm;
* civil-liberty cost;
* financial cost;
* missing evidence;
* and success measure.

Claims-this-pack-does-not-make note

Maintain this as a standing inoculation against careless or bad-faith readings.

⸻

Source-list requirements

The reading list should deliberately include different perspectives.

Look for:

* security-service and protective-security accounts;
* criminology and terrorism studies;
* sociology of movements and radicalisation;
* histories of revolutionary, nationalist, religious and far-right violence;
* community and survivor perspectives;
* public-health approaches to violence prevention;
* civil-liberties criticism;
* animal-liberation and animal-research ethics;
* firearms policy;
* youth media studies;
* and writing from both elite-target and working/community perspectives.

Do not flatten these books into “right-wing” and “left-wing” truth.

Instead note:

* the author’s institutional location;
* likely audience;
* definition of violence;
* theory of causation;
* treatment of the state;
* treatment of insurgents or activists;
* and blind spots created by class, geography or political position.

⸻

Two-example diversity rule

Across the pack, avoid repeatedly using the same handful of US and UK examples.

Where evidence permits, vary examples across:

* Europe;
* the Americas;
* the Middle East;
* Africa;
* South Asia;
* East Asia;
* colonial and anti-colonial settings;
* religious and secular movements;
* state and non-state violence;
* elite and working-class actors;
* violence against people;
* violence against property;
* and failed, disrupted or nonfatal activity.

Do not use diversity as decoration. Select cases because they expose the mechanism under discussion.

⸻

Final quality check for every node

Before treating a node as complete, ask:

1. Is the central distinction clear?
2. Does the node avoid mind-reading?
3. Are facts separated from allegations and inference?
4. Are key terms defined?
5. Is there at least one useful example?
6. Would a second contrasting example improve the analysis?
7. Are the limits of comparison stated?
8. Does the node explain without excusing?
9. Does it identify a prevention or policy implication?
10. Does it give reporters useful questions?
11. Are numbers attached to definitions, years and geography?
12. Does the response proposed actually fit the mechanism of harm?
13. Does the node avoid treating one political community as uniquely human or uniquely pathological?
14. Does it preserve democratic disagreement without tolerating dehumanisation?
15. Are important evidential gaps visible rather than quietly filled by assumption?

⸻

Desired overall effect

A reporter using this pack should finish with a clearer ability to say:

* what happened;
* what did not happen;
* what investigators know;
* what politicians are adding;
* what the available numbers can support;
* how different forms of political violence operate;
* why people may convert grievance into harm;
* which interventions plausibly reduce risk;
* and how to report the subject without minimising danger, inflating it or pushing whole constituencies outside the shared social world.

The pack should make political violence less mystified, not less serious.
