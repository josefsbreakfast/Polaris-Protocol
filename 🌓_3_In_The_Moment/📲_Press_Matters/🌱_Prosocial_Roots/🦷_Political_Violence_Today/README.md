# 🦷 Political Violence Today
**First created:** 2026-07-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-16  
*Why some people become violent while others do not; why some violence is recognised as political while other violence is not; and what democratic societies might repair to make violence less likely.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

In theory, liberal democracy is meant to offer alternatives to violence. We have elections, courts, petitions, protest, journalism, trade unions, community organisations and all the other machinery through which people are supposed to be able to challenge power, seek redress and change the conditions under which they live.

In practice, some people still resort to violence. Others, facing grievances every bit as serious, do not. Some forms of violence are instantly treated as threats to democracy; others are so ordinary in public discussion that their relationship to power barely gets a shrug.

This cluster is interested in all three problems.

**The point is not “do violence”. The point is: can we improve the systems we have so that people do not resort to violence?** And can we recognise and prevent the violence people are already experiencing, including the kinds we have learnt to treat as background noise?

Political violence is not one thing. The attack on a politician, a bomb placed at a family home, the intimidation of women in public life, the sabotage of infrastructure and violence deployed in the name of an apparently sympathetic cause may differ radically in intent, organisation, risk, effects and legal classification. Their differences matter. So do the ways they are selectively grouped together.

This is an evidence-first, prosocial investigation: classification without flattening; explanation without absolution; proportionality without indifference; and prevention rather than a permanent competition over whose enemies are the most frightening.

## 1. 🪞 An Acknowledged Authorial Bias

The author is more interested in **why some people do and others do not** than in producing an inventory of dangerous identities.

Why does one person respond to injustice by organising with neighbours, another by taking a case to court, another by withdrawing, and another by hurting somebody? What makes violence seem meaningful or effective to the person choosing it? What makes restraint, solidarity and nonviolent confrontation possible for someone else?

The author is also interested in **why some violence is recognised as political and some is not**, even where the latter appears connected to political beliefs, social hierarchy or the enforcement of unequal power.

That is an openly stated research orientation, not permission to predetermine the answer. The evidence may establish a political motive, an interpersonal motive, mixed motives or no sufficiently evidenced motive at all. It may also reveal that the social consequences of an act extend beyond what its perpetrator consciously intended.

A legal category, a perpetrator's self-description and a social analysis are different things. The purpose of this cluster is to keep them distinguishable long enough to learn something useful.

**The author's preference is for making nonviolence more viable, not for making violence sound romantic.**

## 2. 🏛️ The Democratic Promise — And Its Limits

A democracy cannot promise that every demand will be granted. Nor can it make disagreement disappear. Its more modest and more important promise is that people can disagree, organise, seek remedies and pursue change without having to harm each other.

That promise depends on more than the existence of a procedure on paper.

| Democratic route | What it should make possible | What is worth examining when it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Elections | Participation in political representation and change | Exclusion, unequal influence, lack of credible choice |
| Courts and legal remedies | Enforceable rights and redress | Expense, delay, access, enforcement and institutional accountability |
| Petitions and representation | Public concerns reaching decision-makers | Whether consideration is meaningful or merely procedural |
| Peaceful protest | Visible collective dissent | Suppression, selective tolerance or persistent non-response |
| Trade unions and organising | Collective bargaining and material influence | Retaliation, weak leverage or barriers to membership |
| Journalism and testimony | Scrutiny, disclosure and public knowledge | Silencing, distortion and unequal attention |
| Community organisation | Mutual support and practical agency | Resource scarcity, exclusion and lost trust |

A route that produces an unwelcome decision has not necessarily failed. Equally, a route that technically exists but cannot be accessed, enforced or trusted may be much less useful than its formal description suggests.

The research question is not whether dissatisfaction licenses violence. It does not. It is whether institutions can respond better to grievances and harms **before** violence becomes thinkable, attractive or feasible.

### 🗳️ The suffrage problem

British women's suffrage provides an uncomfortable historical example. Suffragists pursued constitutional and nonviolent campaigning; some suffragettes, particularly within the Women's Social and Political Union, also used militant methods including property destruction, arson and bomb attacks. Women gained parliamentary voting rights on a limited basis in 1918, and equal voting rights with men in 1928.

It is not possible simply to declare that militant violence was necessary for those reforms. Historians dispute its effects, and the counterfactual cannot be proven. Nor should the many nonviolent campaigners disappear from the story.

But the contrast is worth examining: actions condemned as dangerous militancy at the time can later sit inside a broadly celebrated national story about democratic progress. **How does a democracy remember coercive movements once it has accepted some of their demands?** And what might earlier, credible political inclusion have changed?

This is a question about institutions and historical interpretation, not a recommendation to repeat militant tactics.

## 3. 🧠 Why Some People Become Violent

Grievance is not destiny. Poverty is not destiny. Isolation, political conviction, humiliation, anger and the experience of injustice do not automatically produce violence. Millions of people encounter some combination of these conditions without attacking anybody.

So we need more than a list of background circumstances. We need to understand the transitions.

| Question | What it asks us to investigate |
|---|---|
| Why this belief or grievance? | Experience, interpretation, ideology, identity and perceived injustice |
| Why this response? | The shift from belief or anger to coercion, restraint, organising or withdrawal |
| Why now? | Triggering events, social permission, opportunity, capability and timing |
| Why this target? | Who or what the person imagines as the enemy, and why |
| Why did others not act violently? | Protective relationships, commitments, credible alternatives and different interpretations |

Ideology may matter enormously. It may also explain much less than a confident headline suggests. A person can espouse an ideology without belonging to an organisation; belong to a movement without being directed by it; or attack a political target for reasons that do not amount to a coherent political programme.

Personal grievance and ideology may overlap. The task is to establish the relationship rather than pick one label for convenience.

**Understanding a perpetrator's pathway is not the same as excusing their decision.** Responsibility for harm and investigation of its causes are compatible.

## 4. 🪢 Why Other People Do Not

If prevention is the goal, nonviolence cannot remain the unexplained remainder after all the exciting violence has been catalogued.

People confronted with injustice may organise, negotiate, litigate, strike, protest, practise civil disobedience, support each other, document abuses, create art, build institutions or refuse to harm people even when they are furious. These routes differ in legality, confrontation, effectiveness and risk. They are not all interchangeable, and nonviolence is not the same as passivity.

What sustains those choices? Do people believe they can make a difference? Do they have trustworthy relationships, organisational resources, material security or credible access to redress? Are there ways to disagree without losing all social membership? What happens when people try peaceful routes and repeatedly meet indifference?

None of these questions implies that the absence of a satisfactory route makes violence inevitable. Most people who face institutional failure still do not become violent. **That is precisely why their choices deserve study.**

The cluster therefore treats prosocial organisation, legitimate disagreement and effective remedies as prevention subjects in their own right, not sentimental additions to a security briefing.

## 5. 🫀 Why Some Violence Is Not Seen as Political

Violence can be politically motivated, politically consequential, or politically interpreted. Those are three distinct claims.

A perpetrator may explicitly intend to influence government, intimidate a political community or enforce an ideological programme. An act may also constrain people's participation or reinforce a social hierarchy even when its perpetrator does not think of themselves as conducting politics. Finally, politicians, journalists and institutions may give an incident a political meaning that the evidence about motive does not support.

| Dimension | The question | What not to assume |
|---|---|---|
| Individual intention | What was the perpetrator trying to achieve? | That target or method proves motive |
| Social and political effect | Whose autonomy, safety or participation is constrained? | That an effect proves conscious ideological intent |
| Institutional classification | How do law, policing and reporting describe the act? | That a legal label exhausts its social meaning |
| Public narrative | What does the event come to represent? | That repetition turns an allegation into a fact |

### 🪞 Violence against women

This distinction becomes particularly revealing when we look at violence against women.

An attack on a politician may be immediately discussed as a possible threat to democratic participation. Yet violence that restricts a woman's movement, employment, relationships, bodily autonomy, economic independence or participation in public life may be treated as a private matter, an isolated domestic dispute or an ordinary crime with no political dimensions worth discussing.

The criminal offences are real and need appropriate investigation and enforcement. But **the fact that violence occurs in a home does not place its consequences outside society**.

Some misogynistic attacks involve explicit political ideology. Other perpetrators use coercion and entitlement without articulating any political theory. In both cases, there may be legitimate questions about gendered power, institutional protection and the distribution of freedom. Those questions do not require us to label every domestic abuser a terrorist, or to claim every individual offence had a political motive.

They do require us to ask why the violence affecting political elites so readily enters a national conversation about democracy while violence limiting women's ordinary freedoms can attract a collective shrug.

**Who gets to have their safety treated as a democratic concern?**

## 6. 🧩 Classification Is a Tool, Not an Explanation

The pack distinguishes events, methods, targets, motives, organisational links, legal definitions and subsequent political uses. None should quietly stand in for the others.

| Dimension | What needs establishing |
|---|---|
| Event | What physically happened, when and where? |
| Target | Who or what was attacked or threatened? |
| Method | What was used, and what risks did it create? |
| Harm | What happened to people, communities, property and institutions? |
| Motive | What does the available evidence show about intended purpose? |
| Ideology | Which beliefs are evidenced, rather than merely attributed? |
| Organisation | Was there direction, coordination, affiliation or independent action? |
| Law | Which jurisdiction and statutory tests apply? |
| Narrative | Who characterised the event, when, and to what end? |
| Prevention | Which causal mechanism could a proposed response change? |

Political significance does not automatically mean terrorism. A political target does not automatically mean political motive. A bomb does not identify the group that planted it. A slogan does not establish operational coordination. And an ordinary-crime classification does not settle every question about wider social power.

The cluster also refuses mind-reading. We can evaluate public claims and their effects without claiming to know whether a politician is sincerely frightened, cynically exploiting a crisis, or some mixture of the two. We can investigate motives without inventing certainty for a suspect or defendant.

## 7. 📊 Numbers, Proportionality and the Violence We Normalise

Rare violence can have grave consequences. An assassination, bombing or serious threat against democratic institutions may warrant urgent protection even when incident counts are small.

But frequency, lethality, severity, political significance and preventability are not interchangeable measures. Nor are police-recorded offences, victimisation surveys, arrests, convictions, disrupted plots, completed attacks and intelligence assessments.

| Measure | Useful for | Cannot establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Recorded offences | Trends within a defined counting system | Every unreported incident or its motive |
| Victimisation surveys | Experiences among sampled populations | Every rare offence or security event |
| Completed attacks | Documented acts within a stated definition | The universe of violent intent |
| Disrupted plots | Investigative or preventive activity | That every plot would have become an attack |
| Arrests | Exercise of specified police powers | Guilt or confirmed ideology |
| Convictions | Proven offences under applicable legal tests | Every act of violence or every underlying belief |
| Security assessments | Professional evaluation of threats | A publicly reproducible incident census |

Always ask about dates, denominators, geography, definitions, revisions and missing information. Do not add incompatible categories to manufacture a larger threat. Do not turn small numbers into zero risk. Do not use a rare spectacular event to erase routine violence.

The author is especially interested in the asymmetry between urgent protection for political elites and the much slower recognition of violence affecting women, children and marginalised groups. The answer is not to remove protection from politicians. It is to ask why so much other preventable harm remains acceptable as background conditions.

**Equal concern for safety does not require identical security arrangements. It requires taking the wider distribution of harm seriously.**

## 8. 🎯 Targets, Methods and Foreseeable Harm

An attack on a person, a community, an institution or a machine may have very different intended purposes. It may also have consequences far beyond the intended target.

A campaign against animal experimentation, for example, raises questions about animal suffering, scientific necessity, lawful welfare routes and the moral status of particular methods. Sympathy for animals does not settle whether intimidation, arson or dangerous sabotage is justified. Nor does criminality settle whether the underlying welfare concerns are legitimate.

Similarly, an attack intended to disrupt infrastructure may endanger workers or bystanders. Damage described as “only property” can create foreseeable risks to life. A political label alone does not predict operational capability or casualty risk.

Two questions should travel together: **What does the offender think the enemy is?** And **who actually bears the danger?**

This is why the pack keeps targets, methods and effects separate from the offender's ideology and from public approval of the cause.

## 9. 📰 How Violence Becomes a Story

Political violence has at least two timelines: what investigators learn about the incident, and what the public is told to think about it.

A familiar sequence can run from an alarming event, through uncertain early attribution, to repeated labels, institutional responses and demands for additional powers or spending. Sometimes the initial account proves correct. Sometimes it does not. The existence of a response is not, on its own, proof that every claim used to justify it was sound.

The pack therefore distinguishes:

- **The evidential timeline:** incident, investigation, charging, proceedings, findings, corrections and unresolved questions.
- **The narrative timeline:** statements, headlines, comparisons, political arguments, proposed interventions and changes in public framing.

It also examines how threat language travels across borders and institutions, and why elite descriptions of danger can feel detached from what people experience in daily life.

### ☘️ Northern Ireland has Wi-Fi

The [Demystifying NI Reporting](./☘️_Demystifying_NI_Reporting/README.md) cluster provides a contemporary test. Northern Ireland's history matters, and people have good reasons to take threats seriously. But a frightening incident in Belfast does not arrive with its motive already attached by history.

Northern Ireland also has smartphones, social networks, contemporary political movements, organised crime, interpersonal disputes and the same international information environment as everywhere else. Older and newer mechanisms may overlap; they may also have nothing to do with a particular incident.

The task is to investigate what happened, not to summon the Troubles as an automatic explanation every time something explodes.

History is context. It is not a substitute for evidence.

## 10. 📹 Security Responses Are Not the Same as Prevention

Policing, intelligence, CCTV, protective security and prosecution can all serve important purposes. But they act on different parts of a problem, and the ability to observe harm is not identical to preventing it.

| Problem | What we are trying to change | Possible response types |
|---|---|---|
| Violent intent develops | Conditions and processes that make violence attractive | Credible participation, relationships, early intervention, community work |
| A threat becomes operational | Capability, opportunity and access to a target | Investigation, protective security, disruption |
| Violence is missed or normalised | Recognition, reporting and institutional accountability | Accessible services, safeguarding, enforcement, independent scrutiny |
| Harm has occurred | Victim safety, accountability and recovery | Emergency protection, justice, support, repair |
| A public response is proposed | Whether intervention matches the evidenced mechanism | Evaluation, proportionality, rights safeguards and audit |

No single intervention solves every problem. A barrier may stop an attacker reaching a target without changing their beliefs. Better access to justice may address grievances without neutralising an imminent threat. A conviction may establish responsibility without dismantling the conditions in which a violent network operates.

The question for spending is not merely *how serious does this sound?* It is **what problem is this money actually solving, by what mechanism, and how would we know whether it worked?**

## 11. 🏛️ Democratic Disagreement Without Dehumanisation

A democracy is not a place where everyone agrees, or where every position must receive equal evidential or moral weight. People can disagree sharply about religion, constitutional arrangements, economic policy, migration, animal welfare, state power and almost everything else.

The minimum requirement is not compulsory centrism or polite silence. It is continued social membership: opponents remain people with rights, and disagreement does not grant permission to intimidate or harm them.

Recognising a grievance is not endorsing every proposed solution. Discussing a contested belief is not validating false claims. Examining the social conditions of violence is not transferring culpability from an offender to a whole community.

When legitimate concerns have no credible place in public discussion, more extreme actors may offer recognition. That is a mechanism worth examining, not an excuse to accept their prescriptions. A functioning democracy should be able to hear difficult questions, reject dehumanisation and maintain firm boundaries against violence at the same time.

**The goal is not a society without conflict. It is a society capable of having conflict without making people disposable.**

## 13. 🧾 Evidence, Sources and Revision

This is a live research cluster, not a finished verdict on every case it mentions. The following distinctions apply throughout:

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Established | Supported by evidence sufficient for the specific claim |
| Officially reported | Attributed to a named authority, not treated as infallible |
| Alleged | Asserted but not established |
| Contested | Material evidence or interpretation is disputed |
| Inferred | An analytical reading whose premises should be visible |
| Unknown | Evidence is insufficient |
| Superseded | An earlier account has been corrected or overtaken |

Where possible, link directly to legislation, judgments, official datasets, original statements, contemporaneous reporting and scholarship. Give dates and jurisdictions. Preserve the difference between what was known at the time and what emerged later. Record definitions alongside figures. Do not quietly convert an allegation into a fact through repetition.

The cluster's [Sources](./📚_Sources/README.md) and [Reporter Tools](./🗞️_Reporter_Tools/README.md) directories are intended to make those checks repeatable. Their contents should be assessed individually rather than assumed complete because a folder exists.

A research bias can determine which questions we pursue. It must not decide the answers in advance.

## 14. 🌱 Make Nonviolence Work Better

The destination of this research is not a more elaborate taxonomy of dangerous people. It is a better account of what makes people safe, what makes violence possible, and what can be changed.

We should want to know why people with serious grievances sometimes choose violence. We should be at least as curious about those who do not. We should examine why certain harms are treated as attacks on democracy while others, particularly violence restricting women's freedom, are allowed to fade into the ordinary scenery.

We should also resist a comforting but useless choice between declaring violence inexplicable and declaring it inevitable. Neither helps build institutions that work.

Democracy cannot promise everybody the outcome they want. It can aim to make participation real, rights enforceable, disagreement survivable, and protection available beyond the people whose safety already commands national attention.

**The aim is not to make a better argument for violence. It is to make the alternatives better — and to make fewer people vulnerable to harm.**
