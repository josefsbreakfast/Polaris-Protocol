# 🕳️ The Leverage Economy

**First created:** 2025-11-16 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-11  
*Why accountability becomes expensive when capability, access, status, knowledge, relationships, or institutional survival concentrate around particular people.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Power is not only authority.

A person can have little formal authority and enormous practical leverage.

Another can sit at the top of an organisational chart while remaining dependent on people below them for information, specialist capability, relationships, delivery, or institutional continuity.

That matters when something goes wrong.

If challenging, suspending, removing, investigating, or exposing an actor carries substantial costs for other people or for the institution itself, accountability becomes harder to execute.

```text
dependency
    +
concentrated capability
    +
high replacement cost
    +
institutional exposure
        ↓
accountability becomes expensive
```

This does not mean accountability becomes optional.

It means the system has created an **economy of leverage** around the actor.

> **The cost of holding somebody accountable is partly a property of the system built around them.**

---

## 🕳️ What leverage means here

Leverage is the capacity to alter what other actors can safely or cheaply do.

It can arise from:

- formal power;
- scarce expertise;
- money;
- prestige;
- political relationships;
- access;
- organisational memory;
- operational capability;
- public popularity;
- donor or client relationships;
- control of information;
- control of opportunities;
- social networks;
- mutual exposure;
- practical indispensability.

Not all leverage is improper.

Institutions depend on capable people. Specialists are valuable. Trust matters. Relationships matter.

The governance problem appears when the system has no affordable route for challenging a person on whom it depends.

> **Leverage becomes dangerous when ordinary accountability threatens functions the institution believes it cannot lose.**

---

## 🧱 Predation and leverage are related but not identical

`🧱 Predation Logic` asks what environmental conditions make exploitation easier.

This node asks what happens when intervention becomes costly.

The sequence can be:

```text
access
   ↓
importance
   ↓
dependency
   ↓
greater access
   ↓
greater importance
   ↓
higher cost of challenge
```

A harmful actor can deliberately cultivate that position.

But the same structure can arise without manipulation.

An institution may simply fail to distribute knowledge, train successors, document relationships, or separate critical functions.

Therefore:

> **Do not infer predatory intent from structural leverage.**

Instead ask whether the architecture has made accountability unnecessarily expensive.

---

## 🧲 Indispensability is concentration risk

An institution may say:

> We cannot lose him.

That statement should trigger a second question:

> **Why not?**

Possible answers include:

- only this person understands the system;
- only this person has the client relationship;
- only this person can operate the equipment;
- only this person holds the political relationship;
- only this person has the passwords or access;
- only this person remembers how the process works;
- only this person can keep the team together;
- removing them would expose previous failures;
- replacing them would be financially costly;
- nobody else has been trained.

Some of those facts may be unavoidable in the short term.

Persistently, they represent **concentration risk**.

```text
critical function
      ↓
one person
      ↓
person becomes difficult to challenge
      ↓
institution tolerates greater risk
      ↓
dependency deepens
```

A resilient system should not require anybody to be unaccountable in order to remain functional.

## 🏝️ Pitcairn — the leverage problem made visible

Pitcairn offers an unusually stark worked example.

During the 2004 sexual-offence proceedings, the island's tiny population and extreme isolation meant that accountability for several men was discussed alongside practical questions about whether essential community functions could continue.

Contemporary reporting included concern about the island's longboat, which was essential to connecting Pitcairn with visiting ships. Some defendants were among the limited number of people able to perform important physical and operational work.

That produced an extraordinary version of a common governance problem:

```text
accountability requires removal

BUT

removal threatens essential capability
```

The existence of the second problem did not erase the first.

It revealed how concentrated capability could become leverage even where the underlying criminal process still had to proceed.

The response ultimately had to account for Pitcairn's unusual operational conditions while allowing convictions, governance changes, and imprisonment to occur.

The transferable lesson is not that ordinary institutions are miniature Pitcairns.

It is:

> **When accountability appears capable of breaking the system, examine why the system was allowed to depend so heavily on the people being held accountable.**

Sometimes the immediate dependency is real.

That makes redundancy and succession part of safeguarding.

## 🪢 Leverage can be relational

A person need not control a formal resource.

They may sit inside a network of relationships that other actors do not want to disrupt.

Examples include:

- donors;
- clients;
- ministers;
- journalists;
- regulators;
- celebrities;
- community leaders;
- professional networks;
- contractors;
- security relationships;
- institutional partners.

The actor becomes valuable partly because they connect the institution to other valuable actors.

This can create **network leverage**.

```text
institution
    ↓
key intermediary
   ↙ ↓ ↘
A  B  C  D
```

Removing the intermediary appears to threaten all four relationships at once.

That can produce unusual tolerance for behaviour that would be easier to challenge in a less connected person.

---

## 🗃️ Information itself can be leverage

Institutional memory is often concentrated informally.

Someone knows:

- why an old decision was made;
- where records are;
- how a legacy system works;
- which unofficial route gets something done;
- who needs to be called;
- what happened during an earlier crisis;
- which relationships are fragile;
- what has never been formally documented.

This knowledge can make the person extremely difficult to replace.

It can also make an institution frightened of conflict with them.

The repair is not suspicion.

It is documentation.

```text
knowledge held by person
        ↓
document + distribute + train
        ↓
knowledge held by institution
```

Institutional memory should belong to the institution.

---

## 🔐 Secrets and mutual exposure

The strongest form of leverage may arise when actors know things about one another.

That can include legitimate confidential knowledge.

It can also include:

- embarrassing decisions;
- misconduct;
- informal arrangements;
- conflicts of interest;
- procedural shortcuts;
- earlier failures;
- politically damaging information.

This can create mutual dependency.

```text
A can expose B
B can expose A
      ↓
both incur cost from rupture
      ↓
stability becomes valuable
```

Do not assume such an arrangement exists merely because actors protect one another.

But where evidence shows mutual exposure, it can explain why accountability becomes collective rather than individual.

The question changes from:

> Why protect this person?

to:

> **What else becomes vulnerable if this person is challenged?**

---

## 💸 Accountability has a price

Institutions make trade-offs constantly.

Holding somebody accountable may impose:

### Operational cost

Loss of staff, capability, relationships, or continuity.

### Financial cost

Litigation, replacement, investigation, compensation, lost business.

### Reputational cost

Admission that earlier assurances or decisions were wrong.

### Political cost

Exposure of allies, policies, appointments, or leadership judgement.

### Informational cost

Disclosure of records or relationships previously kept private.

### Governance cost

Recognition that the problem is systemic rather than individual.

### Emotional and social cost

Conflict inside teams, communities, families, or professional networks.

None of those costs automatically outweighs the need to act.

But pretending they do not exist makes institutional behaviour harder to understand.

> **The correct governance response is not to deny the price of accountability. It is to stop allowing that price to become prohibitive.**

---

## ⚖️ The wrong comparison

Institutions can unconsciously compare:

```text
COST OF ACTING NOW

versus

COST OF DOING NOTHING TODAY
```

That often favours delay.

The better comparison is:

```text
COST OF ACTING NOW

versus

EXPECTED COST OF
continued exposure
+ future harm
+ accumulated liability
+ later revelation
+ deeper dependency
```

Non-action can look cheap because its costs arrive later and are distributed across other people.

That is one reason survivors and junior staff can end up carrying risks that never appear on the institution's balance sheet.

---

## 🫥 Externalised costs create false affordability

Suppose keeping a harmful actor in place causes:

- juniors to avoid shifts;
- survivors to leave;
- colleagues to supervise informally;
- managers to rearrange work;
- families to change behaviour;
- other staff to absorb emotional labour.

The institution may record little of this as a cost of retaining the actor.

The costs have been **externalised**.

```text
institution retains valuable actor
          ↓
others modify behaviour
          ↓
institution preserves capability
          ↓
safety cost is paid elsewhere
```

This is where the leverage economy meets `🕸️ Harm Containment`.

The actor remains apparently affordable because somebody else is paying.

---

## 🪜 Status can increase the evidential price of action

High-status people may attract greater procedural caution.

Some caution is appropriate: serious allegations can have serious consequences.

The problem arises if status changes the practical evidential threshold so dramatically that intervention becomes unavailable until harm is overwhelming.

This can happen through:

- repeated demands for more corroboration;
- fear of litigation;
- deference;
- reputational concern;
- political sensitivity;
- assumptions about credibility;
- reluctance to impose interim restrictions.

The question is not whether important people deserve due process.

They do.

It is:

> **Does the system require materially more risk to accumulate before using proportionate safeguarding controls against a high-leverage actor?**

That can be tested.

---

## 🔁 Leverage reproduces itself

Leverage can be self-reinforcing.

```text
actor becomes valuable
      ↓
receives more access
      ↓
builds more relationships
      ↓
accumulates more knowledge
      ↓
institution becomes more dependent
      ↓
challenging actor becomes harder
      ↓
actor remains in position
      ↓
becomes still more valuable
```

This is why waiting can make accountability more difficult even if no new misconduct occurs.

Dependency itself deepens.

A system should therefore detect excessive concentration before it becomes a safeguarding crisis.

---

## 🧭 Formal power and practical leverage can diverge

The actor map from `🧭 Who Knew What and Who Could Act?` should include both.

For each relevant actor ask:

```text
FORMAL AUTHORITY
What can they officially decide?

PRACTICAL LEVERAGE
What becomes difficult if they resist,
leave, disclose, or are removed?
```

The answers can be radically different.

A technical specialist may have little formal authority but control essential infrastructure.

A celebrity may hold no management role but generate enormous revenue.

A longstanding administrator may possess more institutional memory than senior leadership.

A contractor may control a system the organisation cannot quickly replace.

These are governance facts.

---

## 🥱 Leverage and concealment

Leverage can help explain why people who did not cause the underlying harm participate in defensive behaviour.

They may fear:

- operational collapse;
- loss of funding;
- litigation;
- exposure of earlier decisions;
- political consequences;
- loss of institutional status;
- damage to colleagues;
- disruption of essential services.

This does not make concealment acceptable.

It helps distinguish:

```text
protecting the underlying misconduct

from

protecting dependencies built around
the person accused of misconduct
```

The two can become indistinguishable in effect.

That is why `🥱 How Cover-Ups Actually Happen` examines the actual decisions rather than assuming one motive.

## 🫧 The BLEURGH transition

The leverage economy explains why the objective function can shift.

At first:

> Is somebody being harmed?

Then the institution discovers the consequences of intervention:

> If we act, we may lose staff, money, capability, relationships, reputation, or political stability.

The institutional problem has expanded.

If consequence management begins to dominate the original safeguarding objective, the system enters `🫧 The BLEURGH Principle`.

```text
harm identified
      ↓
cost of correction calculated
      ↓
institution begins managing
cost of correction
      ↓
risk that harm correction
becomes secondary
```

Leverage does not guarantee BLEURGH.

It supplies the pressure that can produce it.

---

## 🛠️ Designing leverage out of the system

The answer is not to make everybody replaceable in a dehumanising sense.

It is to make **critical functions resilient**.

Useful controls include:

- succession planning;
- role redundancy;
- documented processes;
- shared institutional memory;
- cross-training;
- separation of duties;
- independent oversight;
- conflict-of-interest rules;
- distributed access;
- auditable decision-making;
- supplier diversification;
- secure credential management;
- rotation where appropriate;
- external reporting routes;
- contingency planning for suspension or removal.

The design test is simple:

> **Can the institution investigate or remove an important person without losing the ability to perform its essential functions?**

If not, accountability has a single point of failure.

---

## 🧯 Temporary continuity is not permanent immunity

Sometimes immediate removal genuinely would create unacceptable operational risk.

That does not produce a permanent exemption.

A proportionate response can include:

```text
immediate safeguarding
      +
temporary continuity controls
      +
rapid transfer of capability
      +
independent oversight
      +
clear exit date
      ↓
accountability without collapse
```

The important element is the **exit date**.

If the institution repeatedly says:

> We cannot act yet because we still depend on them.

while doing nothing to reduce the dependency, the temporary constraint has become a governance choice.

## 🧪 A leverage audit

For a significant actor or role, ask:

### Capability

- What essential functions depend on this person?
- Who else can perform them?
- How long would replacement take?

### Knowledge

- What do they know that is not documented?
- Where is institutional memory stored?
- Are successors trained?

### Access

- What systems, people, resources, or opportunities do they control?
- Is access separable from the individual?

### Relationships

- Which external or internal relationships depend unusually on them?
- Could those relationships survive transition?

### Exposure

- Would challenging this person expose earlier institutional decisions?
- Are decision-makers reviewing their own conduct?

### Safety

- Does the person's importance change safeguarding thresholds?
- Who currently absorbs the risk created by keeping them in place?

### Resilience

- Is there a credible plan for suspension, removal, illness, resignation, or conflict?
- Has that plan ever been tested?

The audit should be useful before allegations arise.

That is the point.

## ♻️ Crosshatch — Re: Recommendations

`🌸 Digitally Women/♻️ Re: Recommendations` can test whether reforms actually reduce the cost of acting.

Relevant recommendations may concern:

- independent oversight;
- professional standards;
- vetting;
- governance separation;
- external reporting;
- whistleblowing;
- succession;
- supervision;
- conflicts of interest;
- information-sharing;
- audit.

Ask:

- Did responsibility become less dependent on one person?
- Did specialist knowledge become institutional knowledge?
- Were conflicts of interest reduced?
- Could safeguarding action occur without permission from interested actors?
- Was independent escalation genuinely usable?
- Did staffing or budget pressure later recreate the dependency?
- Did reforms survive leadership turnover?
- Did the next comparable case show that a high-status actor could be challenged earlier?
- Did accountability become operationally cheaper?

A recommendation can succeed formally while leverage migrates.

For example:

```text
remove dependency on Person A
          ↓
same dependency moves to Role B
          ↓
formal reform succeeded
          ↓
systemic vulnerability survives
```

That is **downstream migration**, not necessarily non-implementation.
