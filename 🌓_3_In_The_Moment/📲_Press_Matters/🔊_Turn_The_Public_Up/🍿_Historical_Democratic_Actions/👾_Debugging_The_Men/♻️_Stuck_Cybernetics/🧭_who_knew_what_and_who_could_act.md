# 🧭 Who Knew What and Who Could Act?

**First created:** 2026-09-11 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-11  
*Reconstructing institutional knowledge, authority, responsibility, capability, and action without turning hindsight into mind-reading.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

After institutional failure, one question appears almost immediately:

> **Who knew?**

It is necessary.

It is also incomplete.

Knowing something is not the same as having authority to act on it. Having authority is not the same as being responsible for acting. Formal responsibility does not guarantee practical capability. And possessing all four does not establish what somebody actually did.

A more useful reconstruction separates five questions:

```text
KNOWLEDGE
What information did this actor possess?

AUTHORITY
What decisions could this actor make?

RESPONSIBILITY
What were they expected or required to do?

CAPABILITY
What could they practically cause to happen?

ACTION
What did they actually do?
```

The point is not to make institutional analysis more forgiving.

It is to make attribution more exact.

> **“They must have known” is weaker than showing what information reached whom, when, through which route, and what that person could then have done.**

This node is a method for doing that reconstruction.

---

## 🧭 Start with the map, not the personality

Institutional scandals invite behavioural interpretation.

A person looks evasive.

A statement sounds defensive.

A minister appears uncomfortable.

A manager changes wording.

An organisation issues an oddly narrow denial.

Those observations can generate useful questions. They are poor substitutes for an institutional map.

Start instead with:

- organisational structure;
- reporting lines;
- statutory duties;
- delegated powers;
- committee terms of reference;
- case ownership;
- referral routes;
- dates;
- records;
- correspondence;
- minutes;
- decision logs;
- escalation requirements;
- operational capability.

Then ask what behaviour means within that structure.

```text
weak method:
demeanour → inferred knowledge → inferred culpability

stronger method:
record → information route → authority → duty → decision → consequence
```

The second method can still produce a severe conclusion.

It simply shows its workings.

---

## 📦 1. Knowledge — what was actually available?

Knowledge should be reconstructed at the smallest defensible level.

Distinguish:

- direct observation;
- first-hand disclosure;
- formal complaint;
- safeguarding referral;
- intelligence;
- allegation;
- rumour;
- press reporting;
- legal advice;
- audit finding;
- review recommendation;
- briefing;
- correspondence;
- information available elsewhere in the organisation but not shown to the actor.

Do not silently collapse these into one category.

A useful ledger is:

| Date | Actor | Information available | Source | Confidence | Evidence of receipt |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | A | Fragment 1 | disclosure | high | signed record |
| T2 | B | Fragments 1 + 2 | briefing | high | minutes |
| T3 | C | possible concern | informal report | medium | witness account |

The purpose is not bureaucratic fussiness.

It prevents hindsight from turning:

> The institution knew.

into:

> Every senior person knew everything.

Those propositions are not equivalent.

---

## 👁️ Notice is not omniscience

An actor can be **on notice** without possessing a complete account.

For example, they may know:

- that complaints exist;
- that a safeguarding concern has been raised;
- that litigation is underway;
- that another agency is investigating;
- that a review identified a systemic weakness.

That may itself create duties.

But the analysis should still specify what the notice consisted of.

Likewise, somebody can lack detailed knowledge while being responsible for maintaining a system capable of detecting the problem.

That creates another important distinction:

```text
case knowledge
≠
system responsibility
```

A chief executive need not personally read every complaint to bear responsibility for whether complaints can be escalated safely.

A minister need not know every operational fact to be accountable for a policy architecture within their remit.

A safeguarding lead may have stronger case-specific duties than either.

Different forms of responsibility coexist.

---

## 🔑 2. Authority — what could they decide?

Authority is the legitimate power attached to a role.

It may include power to:

- suspend;
- investigate;
- commission review;
- refer;
- disclose;
- allocate resources;
- change policy;
- impose safeguarding restrictions;
- discipline;
- hire or dismiss;
- escalate;
- convene;
- order audit;
- seek legal advice;
- notify another body.

Authority can be:

- statutory;
- delegated;
- contractual;
- professional;
- managerial;
- political;
- informal but operationally real.

Do not infer authority merely from seniority.

A very senior person may lack lawful power over a particular decision.

A comparatively junior safeguarding professional may possess a specific duty or referral power that a senior executive does not.

The question is:

> **What decision was actually available to this actor at this time?**

---

## 🪧 3. Responsibility — who was supposed to act?

Authority asks *could they?*

Responsibility asks *were they supposed to?*

Responsibility may arise from:

- statute;
- regulation;
- professional standards;
- safeguarding policy;
- job description;
- delegation;
- case allocation;
- organisational procedure;
- explicit instruction;
- assumed ownership after referral.

This distinction catches a common institutional failure.

Several people may possess enough authority to intervene, while each reasonably believes somebody else owns the problem.

```text
many actors could act
        +
no clear actor owns action
        =
responsibility diffusion
```

That is why:

> **“Somebody could have done something” is not yet an accountability map.**

The stronger question is:

> **Who had the duty to decide whether something should be done?**

---

## 🛠️ 4. Capability — could the decision actually be executed?

Formal authority can exist without practical capability.

A team may have responsibility but lack:

- staff;
- budget;
- specialist expertise;
- data access;
- legal access;
- jurisdiction;
- technical systems;
- cooperation from another agency;
- physical capacity;
- political support;
- safe reporting routes.

This does not automatically excuse failure.

It identifies a different failure.

If an institution assigns responsibility without capability, the architecture itself may be defective.

```text
responsibility without authority
= impotence

authority without information
= blindness

information without responsibility
= orphaned knowledge

responsibility without capability
= paper safeguard
```

These combinations are often more informative than a generic statement that “the system failed”.

---

## ⚙️ 5. Action — what happened next?

Once knowledge, authority, responsibility, and capability are mapped, examine behaviour.

Possible actions include:

- escalation;
- referral;
- investigation;
- safeguarding restriction;
- documentation;
- consultation;
- delay;
- deferral;
- closure;
- reassignment;
- informal containment;
- no action;
- destruction or loss of information;
- narrowing of scope;
- challenge to another actor's decision.

For each action, ask:

1. What information was available?
2. What options existed?
3. What constraints were documented?
4. What reason was given?
5. Who approved it?
6. What happened downstream?
7. Was the outcome reviewed?

This moves analysis from motive speculation to decision reconstruction.

---

## 🧩 The four things rarely arrive together

Institutional failure often occurs because the system distributes its necessary components.

```text
Actor A
has KNOWLEDGE

Actor B
has AUTHORITY

Actor C
has RESPONSIBILITY

Actor D
has CAPABILITY
```

No single actor possesses enough of the set.

This is where the node directly crosshatches with `🔗 Chains of Silence`.

The information may have travelled.

The system may still have failed to create an actionable configuration.

The key diagnostic becomes:

> **Where, if anywhere, did knowledge, authority, responsibility, and capability meet?**

If they never met, ask why.

If they did meet, ask what happened next.

That is the fork between **architecture failure** and **decision failure**.

---

## 🔀 Architecture failure versus decision failure

This distinction should be made explicitly.

### Architecture failure

The system never assembled the ingredients required for action.

Examples:

- information never reached the decision-maker;
- nobody had clear ownership;
- jurisdiction was fragmented;
- records could not be joined;
- the responsible team lacked capability;
- incompatible thresholds caused information to fall between processes.

### Decision failure

The ingredients assembled, but the resulting decision was inadequate.

Examples:

- credible risk was consciously minimised;
- escalation was declined;
- safeguarding was subordinated to another concern;
- scope was deliberately narrowed;
- action was postponed despite known risk;
- reputation or continuity was given greater weight than protection.

The evidence may show both.

But distinguishing them matters because the repair is different.

```text
architecture failure
→ redesign transmission / ownership / capability

decision failure
→ examine judgement / incentives / accountability
```

## 🪜 Build the ladder of escalation

For a significant warning, reconstruct the route it was supposed to travel.

```text
reporter
   ↓
first recipient
   ↓
case owner
   ↓
specialist / safeguarding function
   ↓
decision authority
   ↓
external referral if required
   ↓
oversight / review
```

Then place the actual route beside it.

```text
EXPECTED                    ACTUAL

reporter                     reporter
   ↓                            ↓
manager                      manager
   ↓                            ↓
safeguarding                 local workaround
   ↓                            ↓
decision-maker               [stops]
```

The point where the two routes diverge is often more useful than a general claim of institutional failure.

---

## 🗓️ Time matters

Knowledge is not static.

An actor can make a defensible decision at T1 and an indefensible one at T4 because new information has accumulated.

Therefore reconstruct chronologically.

```text
T1: isolated concern
T2: second similar concern
T3: external warning
T4: review identifies systemic weakness
T5: another comparable incident
```

At each stage ask:

> **What should a reasonable institutional actor have understood now that they could not reasonably have understood earlier?**

This avoids both errors:

- judging early decisions using later information;
- pretending later actors remained entitled to the uncertainty that existed at the beginning.

Accumulation changes the evidential environment.

---

## 📈 The threshold can move as signals accumulate

A single weak signal may justify monitoring.

Several weak but independent signals may justify escalation.

A formal finding may justify stronger intervention.

The institutional mistake is sometimes to assess every new fragment as though it were still the first.

```text
signal 1 → insufficient alone
signal 2 → insufficient alone
signal 3 → insufficient alone

but

signal 1 + signal 2 + signal 3
→ potentially significant pattern
```

This is why cross-case visibility matters.

It is also why `🧩 Everyone Had a Piece` is so important to the `♻️ Re: Recommendations` crosshatch.

---

## 🏷️ Titles are not functions

Institutional reconstruction should resist assuming what somebody did from their title.

“Director”, “minister”, “chief”, “safeguarding lead”, “case officer”, “chair”, or “commissioner” can conceal substantial variation in actual powers.

For each actor, identify:

```text
TITLE
what they were called

FUNCTION
what they actually did

POWER
what they could authorise

DUTY
what they were required to do

DEPENDENCIES
whose cooperation they needed
```

This becomes particularly important across central government, policing, regulators, contractors, charities, health services, and multi-agency safeguarding arrangements.

---

## 🧱 Institutional actors are not a hive mind

Avoid sentences such as:

> The government knew.

> The police knew.

> The BBC knew.

> The NHS knew.

Sometimes those are useful shorthand.

For serious analysis, unpack them.

Which:

- department;
- force;
- team;
- office;
- committee;
- executive;
- minister;
- civil servant;
- clinician;
- investigator;
- contractor;
- regulator?

And what did *that* actor know?

Large institutions can contain mutually inconsistent understandings at the same time.

That is not proof that nobody is responsible.

It is why responsibility has to be traced.

---

## 🗣️ Denials need grammatical analysis

Institutional statements can be extremely narrow.

For example:

> We have found no evidence that senior leadership was informed of X.

That does **not** necessarily answer:

- whether junior staff knew;
- whether records existed;
- whether another department knew;
- whether leadership knew a broader or differently described concern;
- whether the institution had constructive notice;
- whether somebody should have escalated it.

Likewise:

> No complaint was upheld.

does not necessarily mean:

> No complaint existed.

And:

> We have no record.

does not necessarily mean:

> The event did not happen.

Read the proposition actually being denied.

Do not silently widen it.

This is especially useful when comparing statements across time.

---

## 🧾 Absence of evidence needs handling carefully

A missing record can mean many things:

- the event did not occur;
- it occurred but was not recorded;
- the record was retained elsewhere;
- the record was lost;
- retention expired;
- systems changed;
- the search was too narrow;
- terminology changed;
- the relevant organisation was different;
- information was deliberately removed.

Do not jump directly to the most dramatic explanation.

But do not treat absence from one search as proof of absence from reality either.

Ask:

> **What record would we expect this process to generate if it operated normally?**

That converts missing evidence into a testable records question.

---

## 🥱 When reconstruction starts pointing toward concealment

This node should stop before it becomes the cover-up node.

But it can identify the questions that justify moving there.

For example:

- Did somebody prevent information reaching an authorised decision-maker?
- Was scope narrowed after the significance of the information was understood?
- Were records altered, withheld, destroyed, or selectively disclosed?
- Were repeated warnings deliberately separated?
- Did actors give explanations inconsistent with contemporaneous records?
- Did institutional protection become an explicit decision criterion?
- Did responsibility repeatedly move in ways that prevented resolution?

At that point the question changes from:

> **How was the institution arranged?**

to:

> **What did actors do with the arrangement once the problem became visible?**

That belongs to `🥱 How Cover-Ups Actually Happen`.

---

## 🕳️ Add dependency to the actor map

Authority diagrams alone can miss leverage.

For important actors, also ask:

- Who depended on them?
- What functions depended on them?
- What relationships did they control?
- What information did only they possess?
- What would their removal disrupt?
- Who would incur the cost of challenging them?

That is where the institutional map meets `🕳️ The Leverage Economy`.

An actor may have little formal authority and enormous practical leverage.

Another may have immense formal authority but little operational freedom.

Both matter.

## 🧪 A reusable reconstruction worksheet

For each actor:

```text
ACTOR:
ROLE:
DATE / PERIOD:

KNOWLEDGE
- What information is evidenced?
- What was its source?
- How certain is receipt?

AUTHORITY
- What decisions could they lawfully or organisationally make?

RESPONSIBILITY
- What were they required or expected to do?

CAPABILITY
- What resources, access, jurisdiction, or cooperation were available?

ACTION
- What did they do?
- What did they not do?
- What reason was recorded?

DEPENDENCY / LEVERAGE
- What made action easier or harder?

DOWNSTREAM EFFECT
- Where did the information or decision go next?

FEEDBACK
- Did the reporter / referring actor learn what happened?

EVIDENCE GAPS
- What remains unknown?
```

Then repeat for the next actor.

The aim is to reconstruct a **network**, not find a single villain-shaped box.

## ♻️ Crosshatch — Re: Recommendations

`🌸 Digitally Women/♻️ Re: Recommendations` asks whether reforms converted vague institutional responsibility into operational ownership.

For recommendations concerning accountability, escalation, multi-agency working, safeguarding, or oversight, test:

- Was a named owner created?
- Did that owner have authority?
- Did they have capability?
- Did information reliably reach them?
- Were deputies or succession arrangements defined?
- Could responsibility survive restructuring?
- Was action auditable?
- Did external bodies know who owned the issue?
- Did reporters know where responsibility sat?
- Did later cases still produce responsibility diffusion?

A recommendation saying:

> Agencies should work together.

is weaker than an architecture specifying:

```text
who receives
who owns
who decides
who acts
who checks
who reports back
```

The empirical question is whether reform made that chain real.

## 🚀 Crosshatch — British responses to CSE

The historical CSE timeline supplies repeated opportunities to apply this method.

Rather than asking only:

> Why did Britain keep failing?

the actor map asks, case by case:

- what information existed;
- which organisations held it;
- which thresholds applied;
- who could act;
- who was expected to act;
- whether capability existed;
- where responsibility diffused;
- what later reviews changed.

This prevents the historical spine from becoming one undifferentiated story of “institutional failure”.

Different episodes may fail at different points.

That is exactly what the larger `♻️ Stuck Cybernetics` architecture is designed to distinguish.
