# 🚀 Timelining British Responses to CSE

**First created:** 2026-09-10 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-10  
*A longitudinal map of British responses to child sexual abuse and exploitation: what became knowable, what changed, what persisted, and what later cases tested.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

British responses to child sexual abuse and exploitation are often discussed scandal by scandal.

That makes the history harder to read.

A scandal becomes public. An inquiry follows. Recommendations are made. Policy changes. Attention moves. Years later, another case exposes a recognisably similar failure and the public is told, once again, that lessons must be learned.

That sequence can create two equally misleading stories:

> Britain learned nothing.

Or:

> Britain fixed the problem and later failures were unrelated exceptions.

Neither is a good enough account.

This timeline asks a more difficult set of questions:

> **What happened? What became knowable? What response followed? What actually changed? What remained unresolved? What did the next case test?**

This is **not a timeline of Britain doing nothing**.

It is a timeline of institutional learning under pressure: genuine reform, partial implementation, uneven capability, recurring failure, political contest, data weakness, public distrust, and the repeated problem of making corrective knowledge survive contact with the next case.

The term **CSE** is used here in its ordinary safeguarding sense of *child sexual exploitation*. It is not synonymous with one offender profile, one community, or the politically loaded shorthand of “grooming gangs”.

The timeline necessarily reaches into wider child sexual abuse, institutional safeguarding, rape investigation, policing, vetting, and elite-status questions because the corrective systems overlap.

---

## 🧭 How to read the timeline

For each major period, the useful sequence is:

```text
WHAT HAPPENED?
      ↓
WHAT BECAME KNOWABLE?
      ↓
WHAT RESPONSE FOLLOWED?
      ↓
WHAT CHANGED?
      ↓
WHAT REMAINED UNRESOLVED?
      ↓
WHAT DID THE NEXT CASE TEST?
```

Where a failure appears again, recurrence should not automatically be classified as “non-action”.

Possible explanations include:

- non-implementation;
- partial implementation;
- uneven or local implementation;
- integration failure;
- downstream migration;
- status-contingent failure;
- recommendation decay;
- a genuinely new failure;
- or an unclear mechanism requiring further evidence.

The point is to distinguish **the existence of reform** from **the durability of reform**.

## 🏝️ 1999–2006 — Pitcairn as an overseas-territory prelude

Pitcairn is not a mainland British CSE case and should not be flattened into one.

It is useful here because it makes an unusually difficult systems problem visible at tiny scale.

Widespread sexual abuse on Pitcairn came under investigation around the turn of the millennium. In 2004, seven resident men faced 55 charges relating to sexual offences against children and young adults; six were convicted on at least some charges. Further proceedings involved men living overseas.

The trials also generated a constitutional challenge. Defence arguments disputed British sovereignty and the applicability of British-derived criminal law. The resulting litigation eventually reached the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council.

At the same time, accountability became entangled with the practical continuation of an exceptionally small and isolated community.

Contemporaneous reporting by Kathy Marks records fears among some islanders that imprisoning a substantial share of the able-bodied male population could undermine the settlement itself. Men performed work associated with roads, boats, and the island's physical connection to the outside world. Some residents described the prosecutions as a British attempt to jail the island's men and effectively “close” Pitcairn.

The community response also exposed how kinship, normalisation, power, and survival could become entangled. At the September 2004 meeting at Big Fence, some women defended the island's existing sexual norms and rejected the interpretation placed on events by investigators and prosecutors. Other women were reported as uncomfortable or silent, and some complainants had withdrawn allegations amid family pressure.

The point is not to speak over Pitcairn Islanders about their own history.

Nor does this node claim that every relationship involved in the settlement's earlier history was coercive. The historical record cannot support that proposition. It is nevertheless reasonable to acknowledge that extreme isolation, dependency, concentrated power, and gender imbalance can resemble environments familiar from other histories of sexual coercion.

For survivors reading the history, that structural resemblance may itself be uncomfortable. Possibility should not be converted into fact; neither does evidential restraint require pretending the resemblance is invisible.

### 🌱 What actually changed

- British jurisdiction was tested through the courts rather than simply assumed away.
- Trials proceeded despite the practical difficulties of conducting criminal justice in a micro-community.
- Sentencing and imprisonment had to be adapted to Pitcairn's unusual circumstances.
- Governance changed after convictions, including the removal of Steve Christian from the mayoralty.
- The existing equilibrium was not treated as sufficient reason to abandon accountability.

### ♻️ What matters later

Pitcairn gives this timeline an early systems proposition:

> **Sometimes accountability really does threaten the existing operating configuration. That does not establish that the existing configuration should be preserved.**

If a community or institution cannot hold a person accountable without threatening an essential function, the problem is not only the allegation or offender.

It is also the architecture that made accountability so expensive.

## 🧱 Before Savile — Britain did not discover child sexual abuse in 2012

The mainland story does not begin with Jimmy Savile.

By the early 2010s, localised and group-based child sexual exploitation had already produced major criminal cases and parliamentary scrutiny.

The House of Commons Home Affairs Committee later recorded that Operation Central in Rotherham led to five convictions in 2010 and Operation Span in Rochdale to nine convictions in 2012. Its inquiry examined children's social care, criminal justice, health, education, multi-agency safeguarding, race, and the treatment of victims.

The significance was not merely that offenders existed.

It was that children could encounter multiple agencies while the meaning of what was happening to them remained badly classified.

Victims were sometimes understood through behavioural frames — troublesome, promiscuous, drinking, taking drugs, making choices — rather than through the exploitation being done to them.

That classification problem matters.

> **A safeguarding system cannot reliably transmit a warning it does not recognise as a warning.**

---

## 🔥 2012–2013 — Rochdale, Rotherham, and localised grooming enter national scrutiny

The 2012 Rochdale convictions intensified national attention to localised grooming and group-based CSE.

The Home Affairs Committee's 2013 report examined both Rochdale and Rotherham. It concluded that senior leadership in both councils had failed in its duty of care to exploited girls, while also noting that both authorities had subsequently recognised the nature and extent of the problem and made improvements.

This distinction matters.

The historical record is not simply:

```text
failure
→ nobody responds
```

It is already:

```text
failure
→ public exposure
→ scrutiny
→ local improvement
→ unresolved questions about whether the correction is sufficient
```

The Committee also examined race directly. That issue would become progressively more politically dominant over the following decade, sometimes clarifying real local patterns and sometimes collapsing a wider safeguarding problem into a single racialised model.

### 🌱 What actually changed

- Localised grooming became a major subject of parliamentary scrutiny.
- Agency responses to exploited children were publicly examined.
- Rochdale and Rotherham altered aspects of their CSE response.
- Multi-agency working, victim recognition, and information sharing became explicit reform questions.

### ♻️ What appears again later

- poor recognition of victimisation;
- fragmented information;
- leadership failure;
- uncertainty about perpetrator data;
- disagreement over the role of ethnicity;
- the gap between local improvement and national assurance.

---

## 📺 2012–2016 — Savile turns a predator story into an institutional story

The posthumous revelations concerning Jimmy Savile created a different kind of legitimacy shock.

The question was no longer simply how one prolific offender had behaved.

It became:

> **How had a high-status man obtained and retained access across multiple institutions while warnings, allegations, behaviours, and opportunities failed to become effective safeguarding action?**

The NHS response alone eventually produced **44 published investigation reports**: 28 in June 2014 and 16 in February 2015.

Kate Lampard and Ed Marsden's 2015 lessons-learned work drew across those investigations and made **14 recommendations** concerning areas including access, volunteering, safeguarding, complaints, and governance.

Government accepted 13 of the 14 recommendations in principle. NHS trusts and foundation trusts were asked to review their own practice against the recommendations and report back on plans and progress.

The BBC separately commissioned the Dame Janet Smith Review into the culture and practices of the BBC during the years in which Savile and Stuart Hall worked there. The report was published in February 2016 and was followed by further independent audit work on contemporary safeguarding, complaints, and whistleblowing arrangements.

### 🌱 What actually changed

- Dozens of institutional investigations were completed.
- Access and volunteering arrangements were reviewed.
- Safeguarding and complaints architecture was examined.
- Executive responsibility became part of the corrective discussion.
- The BBC subjected contemporary practice to further independent audit.
- Post-Savile reform generated explicit institutional learning rather than merely public condemnation.

### ♻️ What appears again later

Status, reputation, fragmented warnings, informal access, inconsistent practice, weak escalation, and the difficulty of converting dispersed information into a coherent risk picture remain recognisable themes elsewhere.

The correct conclusion is therefore not:

> Savile happened and nothing changed.

It is:

> **Post-Savile Britain accumulated substantial corrective knowledge. The later question is how universally and durably that knowledge became institutional capability.**

---

## 🩻 2014–2015 — Rotherham forces the question of institutional culture

Professor Alexis Jay's 2014 independent inquiry examined CSE in Rotherham between 1997 and 2013 and estimated that at least 1,400 children had been sexually exploited during that period.

The report triggered further intervention.

Louise Casey's subsequent inspection examined Rotherham Council's governance, children's services, and related functions. Her report was published in February 2015 and found widespread failings across the council's culture and services. Commissioners were subsequently appointed to take over executive functions and begin a rapid improvement programme.

Rotherham Council itself accepted the Jay report, apologised to victims and families, committed to implementing the recommendations, established support and improvement structures, and acknowledged the need for cultural change.

Again:

> **There was action.**

The question is what kind of action, how far it reached, and whether the same underlying failure mechanisms survived elsewhere.

---

## 🛠️ 2015–2017 — national government attempts to turn scandal into system reform

In March 2015 the Home Office published *Tackling Child Sexual Exploitation*, explicitly responding to failures identified in the Jay and Casey reviews.

The programme addressed:

- accountability and leadership;
- cultures of inaction and denial;
- joint working and information sharing;
- protection of children at risk;
- law-enforcement activity;
- support for victims and survivors.

A 2017 progress report then documented government action since the 2015 programme.

This period matters because it makes a later claim of simple non-action increasingly difficult to sustain.

By the middle of the decade, the British state was not merely aware that CSE existed.

It had begun explicitly describing recurring mechanisms:

```text
failure to recognise exploitation
        ↓
poor information sharing
        ↓
weak leadership / accountability
        ↓
victims dismissed or ignored
        ↓
offenders retain opportunity
```

The problem increasingly becomes one of **implementation, integration, and maintenance**.

---

## 🏛️ 2014–2022 — IICSA becomes an attempt to build national institutional memory

In July 2014 the Home Secretary announced an inquiry into whether public bodies and other non-state institutions had taken seriously their duty of care to protect children from sexual abuse.

In March 2015 the inquiry was placed on a statutory footing with powers to compel evidence.

The Independent Inquiry into Child Sexual Abuse became a seven-year attempt to aggregate knowledge across institutions and settings rather than treating each scandal as an isolated aberration.

Its final report was published on **20 October 2022**.

Across its work, IICSA made **107 recommendations to 33 specified state and non-state institutions**, alongside recommendations affecting other organisations and settings. Its final report contained **20 concluding recommendations**, six of them reiterated from earlier reports.

IICSA's findings are particularly important for this timeline because they describe the information problem directly.

The Inquiry encountered cases in which children disclosed abuse, or institutions held information indicating abuse, but relevant authorities were not informed. It identified circumstances in which perpetrators retained access to children despite information being held by senior people. It also identified reputation, institutional protection, weak procedures, uncertainty, and poor reporting as recurring problems.

### 📚 What Britain formally knew by October 2022

By the time IICSA reported, the institutional record supported several propositions that could no longer sensibly be treated as novel discoveries:

- children do not always disclose abuse in a simple or immediately legible form;
- behaviour associated with exploitation can itself be misread as evidence against the child;
- warnings can exist in fragments across people and institutions;
- records do not protect children merely by existing;
- information sharing and reporting routes matter;
- somebody must own the whole risk;
- status and reputation can distort institutional response;
- institutional embarrassment can compete with safeguarding;
- safeguarding requires action even where cultural or social norms have normalised harmful behaviour;
- support, records, redress, and therapeutic provision matter after abuse;
- online environments alter the opportunity structure;
- formal policy is not the same thing as reliable implementation.

This is a major threshold in the timeline.

> **2022 is not the year Britain begins understanding institutional child sexual abuse. It is the year a large body of accumulated learning is formally assembled and returned to the state as recommendations.**

## 📉 2020–2022 — the pandemic disrupts the measuring instruments

The pandemic created a separate but important measurement problem.

Face-to-face Crime Survey for England and Wales interviewing was suspended and replaced temporarily by the Telephone-operated Crime Survey for England and Wales.

For ethical, confidentiality, and safeguarding reasons, sensitive questions on sexual assault, partner abuse, and childhood abuse were omitted from the telephone survey.

Face-to-face interviewing resumed in October 2021 and the questions returned, but early post-resumption estimates were based on reduced periods of data collection and required caution.

This should not be exaggerated into:

> rape stopped being recorded.

Police records, services, and other sources continued to exist.

The narrower and more important point is:

> **An important survey-based measurement instrument for sexual and domestic violence suffered a discontinuity at precisely the point when social conditions and reporting behaviour were themselves being disrupted.**

That matters later because political claims about prevalence, trend, and offender characteristics are being made against an evidence environment that was already imperfect and then became temporarily harder to compare.

## 🧮 2020–2021 — the ethnicity evidence problem is formally acknowledged

In December 2020 the Home Office published its evidence paper on group-based CSE.

Its conclusions were awkward in exactly the way good evidence sometimes is.

Research on offender ethnicity was limited and often based on poor-quality data. Some studies indicated over-representation of Asian and Black offenders in particular group-based CSE samples; many of the same studies nevertheless identified White offenders as the largest group. The evidence did not support a conclusion that any one community or culture was uniquely predisposed to this offending.

The accompanying literature review stressed incomplete ethnicity recording, small or partial samples, selection effects, and the difficulty of generalising from high-profile cases.

This creates a problem that will still be visible in 2026:

```text
real local offending patterns
        +
incomplete national data
        +
previous institutional failure
        +
high public distrust
        ↓
large space for competing narratives
```

The responsible position is not to erase ethnicity.

Nor is it to turn evidence from particular places and offender networks into a universal national profile.

## 🌹 2021–2024 — Everard turns the safeguarding question back onto policing

Sarah Everard was abducted, raped, and murdered in March 2021 by serving Metropolitan Police officer Wayne Couzens.

This is not a CSE case.

It belongs in the timeline because it radically altered the institutional environment in which British sexual-violence and safeguarding reform was taking place.

The question became unavoidable:

> **What happens when the institution expected to protect women contains people who themselves present a sexual-violence risk?**

The Metropolitan Police commissioned Baroness Louise Casey to review its culture and standards. The review began in 2022 and reported in March 2023.

Casey identified institutional misogyny, racism, and homophobia and described failures in misconduct, complaints, management, and organisational culture.

The Angiolini Inquiry separately examined how Couzens was able to become and remain a police officer. Part 1, published in February 2024, identified red flags and missed opportunities across his career and made 16 recommendations. Government subsequently accepted the government-led recommendations.

The inquiry later expanded: Part 2 addressed recurrence, policing culture, and women's safety, while Part 3 was established around former officer David Carrick.

### 🌱 What actually changed

- Police vetting, recruitment, and misconduct received sustained national scrutiny.
- VAWG was elevated within national policing priorities.
- The Casey and Angiolini processes produced specific recommendations.
- Government and policing began further reform of vetting and standards.
- The problem was framed as systemic capability, not merely one criminal officer.

### ♻️ What appears again later

The recurring questions are familiar:

- Did information exist?
- Who possessed it?
- Who could act?
- Were warning signs integrated?
- Did the safeguard operate consistently across forces?
- What happened when status, hierarchy, or institutional confidence entered the decision?

## 🧪 2021–2026 — Operation Soteria provides a useful control case

Operation Soteria is important because this timeline should not become a museum of failure.

The programme was designed to transform rape investigation and prosecution, shifting attention toward suspect behaviour and away from disproportionate scrutiny of victims.

It began through pathfinder work and expanded before a National Operating Model was rolled out across police forces and rape prosecutors in England and Wales in 2023.

By March 2024, government described all police forces and prosecutors as being months into implementation.

This gives the timeline a useful distinction:

> **Formal adoption is an event; embedding is a process.**

A reform can be real and still require:

- training;
- supervision;
- capability;
- quality assurance;
- local adaptation;
- maintenance;
- and later testing against actual cases.

That distinction will matter repeatedly in `♻️ Re: Recommendations`.

---

## 📜 2022–2026 — IICSA recommendations move from inquiry into implementation

IICSA's final report did not end the story.

One of its major recommendations was a statutory requirement for certain people to report child sexual abuse.

Government consulted on mandatory reporting in 2023–24.

In January 2025 the Home Secretary announced that mandatory reporting would be introduced through Crime and Policing legislation.

The **Crime and Policing Act 2026** subsequently implemented key IICSA-related measures, including the statutory reporting framework.

This is a useful example of why timelines need to distinguish:

```text
recommendation
      ↓
government response
      ↓
consultation
      ↓
legislation
      ↓
implementation
      ↓
future test
```

A recommendation can therefore be neither “ignored” nor “finished”.

It can be **in transit through the system**.

The next analytical question is whether the resulting safeguard works, reaches the relevant settings, survives pressure, and changes the next case.

## 🐉 2023–2026 — CSE returns as a legitimacy and political conflict

By the mid-2020s, group-based CSE had returned to intense national political debate.

That debate did not emerge into an empty environment.

It arrived after:

- Rochdale;
- Rotherham;
- years of survivor testimony;
- parliamentary scrutiny;
- IICSA;
- repeated government programmes;
- longstanding arguments about ethnicity;
- and a public already primed to ask whether institutions were withholding uncomfortable information.

The national audit led by Baroness Casey is therefore especially important.

It found that national demographic data on perpetrators remained poor. Ethnicity was not recorded for roughly two-thirds of perpetrators in the national data examined, preventing a reliable national assessment.

At the same time, better-quality local police data in Greater Manchester, South Yorkshire, and West Yorkshire showed disproportionate numbers of Asian-ethnicity suspects in group-based CSE in those areas. The audit also pointed to significant numbers of Asian-ethnicity perpetrators in local reviews and high-profile prosecutions and concluded that this warranted further examination.

Those findings need to remain together.

> **Poor national data do not establish that ethnicity is irrelevant. Local disproportionality does not establish a definitive national ethnic profile.**

Removing either half produces a worse analysis.

The audit also makes the cybernetic problem painfully visible:

> **Britain had been arguing about this question for years while still failing to collect sufficiently complete national data to answer it well.**

That is not merely a communications problem.

It is a feedback problem.

### 🧨 Why this matters for public trust

When institutions have previously failed children, weak data do not create a neutral vacuum.

They create space in which:

- genuine local concerns;
- survivor anger;
- political opportunism;
- racial prejudice;
- institutional defensiveness;
- community fear;
- and reasonable demands for better evidence

can all become entangled.

An institution that says “the evidence does not support that conclusion” may be statistically correct and still fail to persuade a public asking:

> **Why, after all these reviews, is the evidence still this incomplete?**

That is the bridge into the rest of `🐉_Societal_Responses`.

## 🕯️ 2025–2026 — elite sexual-abuse associations meet an already jaded public

The later Epstein-related political shocks do not constitute another CSE case.

They matter because they arrive after decades in which British institutions have repeatedly promised that status, access, reputation, and safeguarding failures will be treated differently.

Peter Mandelson was withdrawn as British Ambassador to the United States on **11 September 2025** after newly published emails showed that the depth and extent of his relationship with Jeffrey Epstein was materially different from what had been known at the time of his appointment.

The controversy subsequently became an institutional-process question as well as a political one.

In 2026 Parliament compelled production of appointment-related material through a Humble Address. The Foreign Affairs Committee examined the appointment and vetting process.

On **20 April 2026**, the Prime Minister told the Commons that he had learned on 14 April that UK Security Vetting had recommended in January 2025 that Mandelson should not receive Developed Vetting, but that Foreign Office officials had nevertheless granted it. He said this information had not been passed to ministers or the then Cabinet Secretary and announced further review and process changes.

The Foreign Affairs Committee's July 2026 report subsequently examined the appointment in detail.

The institutional question does not require mind-reading:

```text
specialist risk information exists
        ↓
adverse recommendation is made
        ↓
another institutional decision is reached
        ↓
information about that divergence does not reach
all later decision-makers / reviewers
        ↓
new information emerges
        ↓
public scandal
        ↓
process is reconstructed and altered
```

Ask:

> **Where was the information? Where was the authority? Where was responsibility? What allowed those things not to coincide?**

That is a Stuck Cybernetics question.

For the public, however, it lands in a different register.

After Savile, Rotherham, IICSA, Everard, Carrick, and repeated “lessons learned”, another story involving status, sexual-abuse associations, vetting, and incomplete institutional knowledge does not arrive as an isolated event.

It arrives inside an accumulated legitimacy history.

That does not make every allegation true.

It changes the **threshold of belief**.

## 🧠 The two tracks have now become inseparable

Across the timeline, two histories increasingly interact.

| Safeguarding / institutional track | Public / political track |
| --- | --- |
| recognise exploitation | who is believed? |
| improve reporting | who was allowed to stay silent? |
| share information | what are institutions not telling us? |
| collect better data | whose narrative fills the data gap? |
| strengthen vetting | does status change the threshold? |
| implement recommendations | why are we hearing the same recommendation again? |
| investigate recurrence | why should the next reassurance be trusted? |

This is why `🐉_Societal_Responses` and `♻️_Stuck_Cybernetics` need to remain separate but connected.

The first asks what repeated institutional experience does to public interpretation.

The second asks why corrective information can fail to become durable system behaviour.

## 🌱 What Britain actually changed

Across this timeline, Britain built or altered substantial parts of its safeguarding architecture.

Among other things:

- local CSE practice changed after major prosecutions and reviews;
- parliamentary scrutiny made multi-agency failure a national issue;
- Rotherham underwent external intervention and governance reform;
- the NHS completed 44 Savile-related investigations;
- Lampard produced national lessons and recommendations;
- the BBC reviewed historical culture and contemporary safeguarding arrangements;
- IICSA created an unprecedented national body of institutional evidence;
- government developed national CSE programmes;
- group-based CSE evidence was formally reviewed;
- rape investigation was redesigned through Operation Soteria;
- police culture, vetting, and misconduct underwent major review;
- Angiolini recommendations entered implementation;
- IICSA recommendations moved through consultation and legislation;
- mandatory reporting entered statute;
- national CSE data weaknesses were themselves subjected to renewed audit;
- security-vetting processes were altered after the Mandelson appointment controversy.

This is not nothing.

It is a large amount of state, institutional, professional, and survivor labour.

---

## ♻️ What keeps recurring

The recurrence is not identical every time.

But the same family of problems remains visible:

- children or survivors misclassified rather than protected;
- fragmented information;
- records without synthesis;
- unclear ownership;
- weak handoffs;
- institutional variation;
- poor national data;
- status and reputation affecting decision environments;
- reluctance to impose disruptive accountability;
- safeguards that exist formally but are not uniformly embedded;
- recommendations requiring repeated restatement;
- crisis response outrunning long-term maintenance;
- public reassurance losing value because previous reassurance did not close the loop.

The correct historical question is therefore not:

> **Why did Britain never learn?**

It is:

> **Why did known learning fail to become sufficiently durable, universal, integrated, or resilient to stop recognisably similar failures recurring in altered forms?**

## 🧩 Recurrence classification

When later nodes use this timeline, recurrence should be classified rather than rhetorically flattened.

### Non-implementation

The recommended action was not meaningfully carried out.

### Partial implementation

Some elements were delivered, but the relevant protective function remained incomplete.

### Uneven implementation

Capability existed in some institutions, forces, teams, or areas but not others.

### Integration failure

Multiple safeguards or datasets existed but did not connect into a usable whole.

### Downstream migration

An upstream problem improved but failure moved to another stage of the process.

### Status-contingent failure

A safeguard operated ordinarily but became less reliable around powerful, prestigious, or institutionally valuable people.

### Recommendation decay

A reform initially worked but weakened through turnover, restructuring, funding, technology change, training drift, or loss of ownership.

### New failure

The later case exposed a mechanism not adequately captured by earlier reform.

### Unclear

The recurrence is visible, but the evidence does not yet justify assigning the mechanism.

This taxonomy matters because:

> **“The problem happened again” is an observation. It is not yet an explanation.**

---

## 🧿 Where Britain stands in September 2026

Britain has substantially more safeguarding architecture, survivor-informed knowledge, specialist capability, statutory machinery, review evidence, and institutional memory than it possessed at the beginning of this timeline.

It also continues to encounter:

- implementation gaps;
- fragmented information;
- incomplete data;
- local variation;
- status effects;
- maintenance problems;
- political capture of unresolved grievances;
- and distrust generated by previous institutional failure.

Both things can be true.

Indeed, the entire point of this timeline is that they **are** true at the same time.

The public can reasonably observe repeated scandal without concluding that every institution is engaged in one continuous conspiracy.

Institutions can point to genuine reform without concluding that recurrence is therefore inexplicable or merely unfortunate.

The useful question sits between those positions:

> **Did the safeguard survive the next case?**

And if not:

> **Where did the corrective signal get stuck?**
