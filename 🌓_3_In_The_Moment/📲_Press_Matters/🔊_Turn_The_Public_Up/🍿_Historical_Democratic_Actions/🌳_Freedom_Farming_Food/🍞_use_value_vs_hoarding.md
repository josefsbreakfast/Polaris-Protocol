# 🍞 Use-Value vs Hoarding  
**First created:** 2025-12-22 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-03  
*Why the public distinguishes between wealth that feeds people and wealth that merely accumulates.*  

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

Not all wealth is judged equally.

Across cultures and political traditions, people make a persistent moral distinction between:
- **use-value** — assets actively employed to sustain life and community, and  
- **hoarding** — assets held primarily to extract rent, preserve status, or accumulate power.

This distinction operates intuitively, not ideologically.  
It shapes how people respond to taxation, regulation, and protest — often before they can explain why.

This node explains why farmers, despite high paper asset values, are rarely read as “the rich,” and why attempts to treat them as such repeatedly misfire.

---

## 🧿 What “use-value” means in practice  

Use-value is not about modesty or virtue.  
It is about **function**.

Assets with high use-value:
- are visibly working,
- are necessary to others’ survival,
- degrade if unused,
- and lose value if detached from labour.

A working farm fits this category perfectly:
- land is producing food,
- machinery is required to operate,
- animals require daily care,
- and interruption harms more than the owner.

This is materially legible to the public.

---

## 🏦 What hoarding looks like  

Hoarded wealth behaves differently.

It is typically:
- abstracted from daily life,
- insulated from risk,
- scalable without labour,
- and liquidatable without social consequence.

Examples include:
- speculative land banking,
- financial instruments,
- absentee ownership structures,
- and extractive rent models.

When people say “tax the rich,” this is usually what they mean — even if they lack the technical vocabulary.

---

## 🧠 Why the distinction matters politically  

Public tolerance for redistribution is high when:
- wealth is perceived as idle,
- accumulation is detached from contribution,
- and extraction does not threaten survival systems.

Tolerance collapses when:
- productive assets are destabilised,
- continuity is broken,
- or necessary work is punished as if it were hoarding.

This is why farming consistently confounds class rhetoric built around liquid capital.

---

## 🌾 Farming as a category error  

A farm can be worth millions and still:
- generate modest income,
- require constant labour,
- and be impossible to sell without collapse.

From the outside, this looks like privilege.  
From the inside, it is **obligation embedded in assets**.

The public recognises this intuitively — which is why policy framing that ignores it struggles to gain consent.

---

## ⚖️ Why “wealth is wealth” fails here  

Arguments that all assets should be treated identically rely on a fiction:
that liquidity, function, and social consequence are interchangeable.

They are not.

When policy flattens these differences:
- it appears blind to reality,
- it triggers moral resistance,
- and it produces outcomes opposite to those intended.

Uniform treatment can be inequitable when function diverges.

---

## 🧩 The deeper pattern  

This distinction between use-value and hoarding predates capitalism.

It appears in:
- religious prohibitions on usury,
- commons governance rules,
- food riot norms,
- and modern moral economy analysis.

People tolerate hierarchy.  
They do not tolerate **extraction from survival systems**.
