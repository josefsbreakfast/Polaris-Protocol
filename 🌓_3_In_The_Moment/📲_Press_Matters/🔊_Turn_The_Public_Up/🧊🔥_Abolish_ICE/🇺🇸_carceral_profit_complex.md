# 🇺🇸 Carceral Profit Complex
**First created:** 2026-09-09 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-15  
*Who gets paid when the state confines people — and what happens when detention becomes an industry?*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The phrase *carceral profit complex* is not a statutory category or the name of one corporation. It is a way of asking a material question: **where does public money go when the state decides to confine people, and how do the resulting contracts, buildings, jobs and political relationships influence what becomes administratively possible?**

In the United States, immigration detention is civil confinement, not ordinarily a criminal sentence. That distinction matters in law. It does not make confinement imaginary: people still lose freedom of movement, contact with families and control over ordinary decisions. The detention system buys buildings, meals, medical services, transport, security, telecommunications, software and staffing. Public agencies make decisions; public and private contractors implement many of them; people detained inside the system live with the consequences.

This node examines that machinery without making the lazy leap from *a company earns revenue* to *a company caused every detention decision*. Profit, state power and policy are related, but their relationships have to be demonstrated. A budget allocation is not a contractor payment; a contract ceiling is not revenue received; revenue is not profit; an inspection failure is not automatically proof of corruption. We can be critical without making our evidence do acrobatics.

The guiding question is not simply whether detention costs money. Everything the state does costs money. It is whether **the incentives built around confinement can favour maintaining or expanding it even when cheaper, safer or more effective arrangements are available**.

## 🧱 1. The prison is not the whole system

The carceral economy reaches beyond privately operated prisons. Its participants include public prisons and jails, county sheriffs, federal agencies, construction firms, transport providers, healthcare contractors, catering services, technology suppliers, bond and finance structures, communications vendors, insurers and professional services. Some sell beds. Others sell the infrastructure required to keep those beds occupied and operational.

A county jail holding immigration detainees under an intergovernmental agreement may receive federal payments while remaining publicly operated. A privately owned facility may operate under a direct federal contract or through a state or local intermediary. A federal agency may own a building but contract out services. The ownership of the walls, the employer of the guards, the purchaser of the services and the authority to detain may be four different entities.

This fragmentation is important. It can make responsibility harder to locate, especially when complaints concern healthcare, staffing, restraint, solitary confinement, transport or access to lawyers. The state cannot outsource its legal obligations merely by outsourcing operations. Equally, a private operator's responsibilities must be identified from the actual agreement, standards and governing law, not assumed from a logo on a gate.

### What counts as profit?

- **Appropriations:** money Congress authorises or provides to an agency. Not automatically spent.
- **Obligations:** binding commitments of public funds, including contracts. Not necessarily cash already paid.
- **Contract ceilings:** maximum potential value, sometimes across options or years. Not realised revenue.
- **Payments and revenue:** money actually paid or recognised by a provider, subject to accounting definitions.
- **Operating profit and net profit:** what remains after relevant expenses, financing and taxes; different measures answer different questions.
- **Public costs:** staff, facilities, litigation, oversight and health consequences may remain public liabilities even where a contractor operates the facility.

Without these distinctions, a dramatic billion-dollar headline can tell us almost nothing about the actual flow of money.

## 🕰️ 2. The longer American inheritance

The American relationship between confinement, labour and economic advantage is older than ICE and older than the contemporary private-prison industry. Slavery, post-emancipation Black Codes, convict leasing, prison labour and twentieth-century mass incarceration form part of that history. They are not identical institutions. Their legal categories, targets, labour arrangements and historical settings differ. The point is to investigate the recurring possibility that state coercion can create material opportunities for other actors.

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery and involuntary servitude *except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted*. That exception belongs in any serious history of American punishment and labour. It does **not** mean that every contemporary immigration detainee is lawfully available for forced labour: immigration detention is generally civil, and its work programmes raise separate statutory and legal questions.

Contemporary private imprisonment grew through specific twentieth-century contracting decisions, alongside broader punitive policy choices and federal, state and local incarceration. Immigration detention expanded through its own legislative and administrative pathways. The mechanisms overlap; the histories should not be flattened into a single uninterrupted business plan.

For the racial and colonial genealogy, see [⛓️ Historical Parallels](⛓️_historical_parallels.md). This node's job is narrower: to follow the money, the contract and the feedback loop.

## 🛂 3. How ICE buys detention

ICE's Enforcement and Removal Operations uses several routes to acquire detention space. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) identifies direct contracts, intergovernmental service agreements with state or local entities, and arrangements that join existing US Marshals Service contracts. Facilities may be public, private or mixed in ownership and operation.

A particularly consequential contract feature is a **guaranteed minimum**: a payment commitment associated with a specified number of beds or level of service, regardless of whether every bed is occupied. Such arrangements may be defended as necessary to maintain staffing and readiness. They can also make unused capacity expensive and weaken the apparent savings from reducing detention. The correct test is the actual contract clause and payment formula, not the assumption that every facility has a bed quota.

GAO's 2021 investigation identified weaknesses in detention-space acquisition, strategic planning and contract oversight. In a recommendation-status update available in June 2026, GAO said ICE had described plans to phase out guaranteed minimums in favour of monthly facility-operating charges in new agreements, but had not yet provided documentation demonstrating implementation of the proposed approach. A new charging formula is not automatically an escape from fixed-cost incentives: one still needs to examine what is paid when occupancy falls. [S1]

**Mechanism to test:** Does the payment structure reward keeping capacity available, filling capacity, providing safe care, or some combination? These are not the same thing.

## 💰 4. Who receives the money?

The GEO Group and CoreCivic are prominent publicly traded detention and correctional-services companies. Other providers include Management and Training Corporation, LaSalle Corrections and firms specialising in particular facilities or services. Their businesses and contract portfolios differ; not all their income comes from ICE, and not all immigration detention is privately operated.

The Prison Policy Initiative's 2026 analysis estimated that private prison and detention companies received approximately **$2.4 billion in ICE-related revenue during 2025**, within approximately **$5.5 billion in combined revenue from ICE and the criminal legal system** for the firms it examined. Its estimates allocated about $1.21 billion of ICE-related revenue to GEO Group and $742 million to CoreCivic. These are the organisation's estimates, based on its stated methodology, not a government census of every contractor and subcontractor. The report expressly acknowledges uncertainty and gaps involving newer facilities. [S2]

Those numbers establish the scale of the market. They do not establish net profit, the share earned at a particular site, or the effect of any one company's lobbying on a specific policy decision. To establish those, we would need annual reports, segment disclosures, contract records, campaign-finance filings, lobbying disclosures and a dated chain of decisions.

The supply chain also matters. A company operating a detention centre can purchase healthcare, food, telecommunications, surveillance and transport from other companies. Some services may be sold directly to detained people or their families. The public contract is therefore only one channel through which confinement generates commercial activity.

## 📈 5. A budget is a political decision; a bed is a material commitment

In July 2025, Public Law 119-21 provided approximately **$45 billion for immigration detention capacity** within a larger package of ICE funding through fiscal year 2029, according to GAO's June 2026 report. The scale is consequential, but the appropriation must not be misreported as money already transferred to prison companies. [S3]

GAO reported that ICE's average daily detained population rose from **39,314 on 20 January 2025 to 67,204 on 1 April 2026**, while authorised detention facilities increased from **134 to 239** over the same period. Those are ICE administrative counts cited by GAO, not a count of everyone detained at any point in the period. [S3]

Once the government invests in a detention facility, the expenditure can create pressure to use it. Buildings require maintenance; staff need contracts; transport routes and medical provision are organised around an anticipated population. That is a plausible form of institutional path dependence. It does **not** prove that every official deliberately seeks to fill beds to enrich a contractor. The stronger argument is structural: capacity decisions change the menu of options that administrators see as feasible, and sunk costs can make alternatives look inconvenient even when confinement itself is expensive.

## 🏗️ 6. The 2025–2026 expansion: a very expensive experiment

The most direct contemporary evidence of planning failures comes from GAO, not from an inferred conspiracy.

In September 2026, GAO reported that ICE had pursued **six detention-expansion initiatives** since January 2025 without the necessary comprehensive analysis and planning. One initiative involved buying **11 warehouses** for roughly **$1.07 billion** with the intention of converting them into detention facilities. ICE subsequently intended to sell **seven** of those warehouses; GAO identified more than **$20 million in nonrecoverable expenditure** on the sites slated for sale. GAO also found that ICE had not established consistent goals for the size and characteristics of detention capacity or adequately assessed the relative risks and costs of different facility types. [S4]

This is a particularly useful case because it separates intention from outcome. An expansion programme may be justified by officials as necessary for enforcement operations. GAO's finding is that major procurement and planning decisions were made without the analysis needed to demonstrate value for money. Waste does not, by itself, establish personal enrichment or criminal misconduct. It does establish a failure of public financial control.

### Camp East Montana

GAO's June 2026 investigation of Camp East Montana, opened in August 2025, found that the contract required ICE to pay the full cost of meals and operational services for **5,000 people**, even when the population was far lower. At the end of February 2026, the facility held approximately **1,600** people. GAO identified resulting waste and failures to meet key detention standards, creating risks for detained people and staff. [S3]

That is the economic mechanism in unusually legible form: **a contract can generate expenditure against planned capacity rather than actual need**. Whether a payment term is commercially reasonable in some circumstances is a separate question from whether it was properly designed and monitored here.

## 🧾 7. Procurement failure is not a footnote

A September 2026 DHS inspector-general report examined ICE detention-space contracts and agreements worth nearly **$10 billion** across fiscal years 2020–2023. The inspector general found that ICE did not consistently manage those arrangements in accordance with federal and departmental requirements and lacked reliable data to forecast detention needs and allocate resources. [S5]

The dates matter: the contracts reviewed belong to **2020–2023**, while the report was issued in **2026**. Its findings cannot simply be attributed to one administration. They show how procurement and oversight problems can persist across political transitions.

GAO had already identified weaknesses in acquisition and contract monitoring in 2021. Repeated findings should prompt a harder question than *who issued the latest press release?* Why did the institutional arrangements remain vulnerable after earlier warnings?

An audit trail for any facility should establish the award date, procuring agency, operator, intermediary if any, contract vehicle, payment structure, performance requirements, inspection history, modifications and actual expenditure. If one of those fields is unavailable, mark it unknown. Do not fill the gap with suspicion and call it accounting.

## 🩺 8. The cost of inadequate care

Contracts may set standards for healthcare, sanitation, food, staffing, grievances and access to legal processes. ICE describes a multilevel inspection and compliance system, including detention standards and reviews by internal and external oversight bodies. That is the agency's account of its oversight design. [S6]

Independent review has repeatedly found weaknesses. GAO's May 2025 report examined inspection programmes and called for clearer performance goals and measures. It recorded a fiscal-year 2024 average daily detained population of more than **37,000** across more than **100** facilities. [S7]

A facility can pass an inspection and still experience serious incidents; conversely, a recorded deficiency does not prove that every detainee suffered the same harm. Inspection schedules, announced versus unannounced visits, complaint access, medical records, language provision, retaliation concerns and the ability to enforce corrective action all affect what oversight can actually discover.

Poor care can also shift costs elsewhere: emergency treatment, legal claims, outside investigations, family support and long-term health consequences. Those costs are not necessarily captured in a facility's per-diem rate. A supposedly cheap detention bed may therefore be expensive when viewed from the perspective of the person detained, their family and the wider public system.

## 🧹 9. Labour, commissaries and communication

Detention economies are not limited to government payments. The cost of communicating with family, purchasing additional necessities or accessing legal support can be borne by detained people and those supporting them. Contracts may assign revenue, commissions or fees differently across facilities; each arrangement needs documentary verification.

ICE's Voluntary Work Program has historically involved payments as low as **$1 per day**, with litigation over whether particular arrangements violate labour laws or amount to coercive work. A nominally voluntary programme must be assessed against the actual conditions of confinement, including what happens when someone declines to participate. Do not conflate civil immigration detention labour rules with the Thirteenth Amendment's criminal-punishment exception, and do not assume one court's ruling governs every jurisdiction.

The research question is straightforward: **who performs the work, who would otherwise have to be paid to do it, who receives the economic benefit, and what meaningful choice does the detained person have?** A wage figure without the working conditions and legal framework is an incomplete story.

## 🗳️ 10. Lobbying, donations and influence: show the chain

Detention providers may lobby legislators and executive agencies, participate in trade associations, donate through lawful political channels and make public arguments about the services they provide. Those activities are relevant to understanding the policy environment. They do not automatically establish bribery, a quid pro quo or control of an administration.

A responsible investigation should compare **dated** campaign-finance and lobbying records with contract awards, policy proposals, meetings, executive actions and procurement decisions. It should ask whether an actor requested a specific change, whether officials adopted it, whether that change created a measurable benefit and what alternative explanations exist. Mere chronological proximity is not causation.

This is also why private-sector profit cannot be treated as the sole explanation for detention. Elected officials may pursue enforcement policies for ideological, electoral, security or administrative reasons; public-sector agencies may defend their budgets; local governments may value employment or contract revenue. The incentives can reinforce one another without requiring a single central author.

## 🧒 11. Children are not a separate economy

Children's detention and care bring additional layers of law, responsibility and contracting. CBP short-term custody, ICE family detention and HHS Office of Refugee Resettlement care for unaccompanied children are not interchangeable categories. Nor is every ORR shelter a prison or every child-welfare contractor an immigration-detention operator.

But children still move through funded institutions. The public pays for processing, transport, accommodation, health provision, case management, sponsor screening and follow-up. When agencies have incentives to process quickly, fill facilities, prolong custody or prioritise enforcement data, those incentives can collide with safeguarding duties. The effects have to be established from placement and welfare outcomes, not inferred from contract value alone.

Our companion node [🍎 Kids in Cages](🍎_kids_in_cages.md) examines the history of family separation, Flores, sponsor screening and trafficking claims. Its concept of **protective displacement** is useful here: a genuine fear about children's exploitation can be redirected towards an intervention whose success is measured by arrests or detention rather than by whether children are safer.

This does not mean that every investigation of a sponsor is illegitimate. It means that the protective claim should be tested against protective outcomes.

## ♻️ 12. The feedback loop: when capacity becomes the answer

The carceral profit complex is most useful as a **feedback hypothesis**, not a cartoon in which a prison company presses a button and the president sends more people to jail.

One possible loop runs as follows:

1. Political or administrative demand increases detention capacity.
2. Public funding supports buildings, staffing, contracts and suppliers.
3. The resulting infrastructure creates fixed costs and organised constituencies.
4. Officials assess new problems using the infrastructure already available.
5. Detention appears administratively straightforward; less institutionalised alternatives require fresh design.
6. The system measures its activity through beds, transfers, arrests and removals rather than through the harms prevented.

This is not inevitable. Procurement rules, judicial oversight, independent inspection, legislative control, public disclosure and effective alternatives can interrupt the loop. Nor does a contractor's income prove that its incentives determined policy. The value of the model is that it tells us **what evidence to look for**.

### The missing feedback signal

A detention system can report how many beds it purchased and how many people it held. Those numbers do not, on their own, tell us whether people attended proceedings, whether children were safer, whether families were reunited, whether rights were respected, or whether public money achieved the stated purpose.

If the system cannot measure those outcomes, it may become very good at expanding its own operations while remaining poor at demonstrating that the expansion was necessary.

## 🛟 13. What would an alternative have to demonstrate?

An alternative to detention is not automatically humane because it lacks bars. Electronic ankle monitoring, intrusive reporting requirements and commercial surveillance can reproduce coercion outside a facility. Community-based case management, legal advice, reliable notice of hearings, family support and safe accommodation operate through different mechanisms and should be evaluated separately.

Useful comparisons need to control for the populations served and the objectives of the programme. A person assessed as posing a serious, individualised flight or safety risk is not interchangeable with a family awaiting an asylum hearing. Nor should the cost of detention be compared with a support programme without accounting for staffing, compliance, appeals, health outcomes and the wider costs of family disruption.

The basic evaluation questions are: Did people receive fair notice and meaningful access to counsel? Did they attend proceedings? Were children safe? Was confinement necessary in individual cases? What did the full intervention cost? Who could complain, and what happened when they did?

The policy choice is not between doing nothing and buying another warehouse. There are multiple possible systems; evidence should decide which objectives each actually serves.

## 🔍 14. A practical audit trail for Polaris

For each major contract or facility, build a dated record with these fields:

| Field | Evidence to collect | Common error to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Facility and operator | ICE facility list, contract, ownership records | Assuming operator owns the building |
| Contract route | Direct award, IGSA, USMS rider, subcontract | Treating every agreement as a direct ICE contract |
| Financial amount | Obligations, payments, options, revenue | Calling a contract ceiling profit |
| Payment incentive | Guaranteed minimum, fixed charge, per-diem, services | Assuming every bed is paid for identically |
| Population | Daily average, capacity, admissions, length of stay | Mixing snapshots with cumulative totals |
| Oversight | Standards, inspection findings, complaints, remedies | Treating an inspection as proof of compliance |
| Political activity | Dated lobbying and finance disclosures | Inferring corruption from a donation alone |
| Human outcomes | Health, family contact, counsel, release, deaths | Replacing welfare outcomes with activity counts |

A good investigation can produce a finding of **unknown**. That is preferable to teaching the archive a false certainty.

## 🕯️ 15. Accountability is not a synonym for abolition

The title of this folder expresses a political position. The research still needs to distinguish the available policy choices and the evidence for their effects. One may argue for ending immigration detention, sharply limiting it, replacing private operators, improving public procurement, or strengthening legal safeguards. Those proposals are not identical, and the available evidence does not make them interchangeable.

Ending private contracting alone would not necessarily end detention: public facilities can confine people and fail standards. Conversely, reforming a contract may reduce a particular abuse without resolving the underlying question of whether someone needed to be detained. Financial accountability and liberty are related but distinct tests.

The strongest question is therefore not *which company is the villain?* It is **why a particular person was confined, what the state hoped to achieve, what the confinement actually accomplished, and who benefited or suffered along the way**.

### Further evidence required before naming individual beneficiaries

A future facility-by-facility financial map should collect USAspending obligations, FPDS contract documents, SEC annual filings, lobbying disclosures, FEC campaign-finance data, county agreements, facility inspection reports and litigation records. Specific allegations of unlawful conduct require their own evidence and legal status. This node does not claim that a contractor's profit, a politician's donation or an audit deficiency proves a criminal conspiracy.
