# 💉 The Shitty History of Migration Healthcare
**First created:** 2026-10-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-17  
*On plague ships, vaccination scars, racialised borders, medical exploitation, consent, and the extraordinary difficulty of protecting people without treating them as the disease.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

We have vaccines. We have antibiotics. We have hospitals, laboratories, international health regulations, disease surveillance, medical screening, and an almost erotic attachment to paperwork.

We eradicated smallpox. We know that treating tuberculosis matters. We know that people who are frightened of doctors, hospitals or government officials may delay seeking care. We know that the pathogens which cause infectious diseases are spectacularly uninterested in the nationality of their hosts.

And then we construct immigration systems in which some people may be afraid to seek medical help because the institution providing it might also expose them to immigration enforcement.

**Bro. Do you even care about your border you fuss about so much?**

This is not an argument that states have no legitimate interest in managing migration, or that communicable diseases are imaginary. It is an argument for examining whether the methods deployed in the name of protection actually protect anybody. It is also an examination of what happens when a state takes custody of a person and acquires power over their health, liberty, body, reproductive autonomy and ability to complain.

*Migration healthcare* means two things here: healthcare **for people who migrate**, and the medical machinery developed to **manage migration**. Sometimes those purposes reinforce one another. Sometimes they collide. Sometimes one becomes the excuse for doing something quite different.

The questions are: **What kind of safety are we seeking? What is the reality? Who is protected? Who is exposed? And how do we do this without becoming monstrous?**

---

## 1. 💉 We Used to Die of This Shit

Infectious disease shaped family life, trade, childbirth, labour, war, migration and the rhythms of entire cities long before anyone knew what a virus was. Smallpox killed and disfigured; measles could devastate children; polio could paralyse; tuberculosis was a persistent killer. Disease prevention was not an abstract luxury. It changed whether people got to grow up.

Inoculation did not begin with one heroic European gentleman having a clever idea. Variolation—deliberately exposing a person to smallpox material in the hope of preventing a worse natural infection—had histories in Asia and Africa. In early eighteenth-century Boston, the enslaved African man Onesimus conveyed knowledge of inoculation to Cotton Mather. Lady Mary Wortley Montagu observed Ottoman practices and advocated their use in Britain. Edward Jenner's 1796 cowpox work helped establish vaccination against smallpox, but he entered a much longer story of circulating knowledge. None of this means variolation was safe by modern standards: it carried real risks, including causing smallpox and spreading infection.

Vaccination and the public-health infrastructure around it became extraordinary achievements. The World Health Organization declared smallpox eradicated in 1980. This was not a victory for one nationality. It depended on coordinated work across political borders, including during the Cold War.

Vaccination also has an uncomfortable history: compulsory laws, uneven access, class conflict, distrust, coercive administration and political resistance. The proper response to those problems is neither to pretend vaccines do not work nor to treat every exercise of medical authority as automatically justified. The medicine and the governance must both be examined.

**Preventing people from dying is, generally speaking, useful.** A functioning society benefits from healthy children, workers, carers, neighbours and strangers. It would be peculiar to forget this the moment somebody produces a foreign passport—or no passport at all.

## 2. 🦠 Unfortunately, Diseases Travel

Trade routes carried people, goods, ideas, animals and pathogens. Ports and market towns could connect distant outbreaks. Medieval plague history is an obvious illustration, although its details deserve care.

The 1346 siege of Caffa appears in a famous account by Gabriele de' Mussi describing plague-infected corpses being hurled into the city. Historians dispute aspects of that account and how much the siege contributed to plague's arrival in Europe. It would be dishonest to present one dramatic episode as the settled explanation for the Black Death. What is not seriously in doubt is that plague travelled through interconnected human and ecological systems. Ragusa, now Dubrovnik, introduced an early quarantine regime in 1377; subsequent port-health systems developed elaborate practices for managing arriving ships and travellers.

Imagine being responsible for a port when a ship arrives from a place suffering an epidemic. You might reasonably want to know whether anybody is sick. You might isolate exposed travellers for an evidence-based period. You might arrange treatment and communicate with other ports. **A real threat can justify a proportionate response.**

But it does not justify treating the passengers as morally contaminated. A pathogen does not require its host's permission, and a sick traveller is not thereby a villain.

## 3. 🐀 When Fear Becomes a Population Category

The Black Death also generated grotesque accusations against Jewish communities, including well-poisoning libels, persecution and massacres. The pathogen was real; the antisemitic explanation was false. The fear did not make the accusation true.

Across other times and places, disease has been mapped onto racial, religious, colonial and class categories. Authorities may confuse overcrowding and poor sanitation with inherent properties of the people forced to live under those conditions. Once a population has been labelled dirty or dangerous, an institution can begin to treat its own scrutiny as proof that the population required scrutiny in the first place.

This is a recurring mechanism, not a claim that one uninterrupted medieval folk memory explains modern immigration policy. Historical continuity must be demonstrated, not assumed.

The analytical distinction matters: **risk factors can be epidemiologically relevant; race and nationality are not diagnoses.** Where particular diseases are more prevalent in particular places, good medicine asks about exposure, living conditions, vaccination, symptoms and treatment. Bad administration mistakes the category of person for the disease.

## 4. 🛂 Papers, Please. Also, Show Me Your Arm.

Passports and travel passes long predate modern vaccination. But nineteenth- and twentieth-century states expanded identity documentation, port inspections, migration registration, medical certification and international disease-control rules. A vaccination scar might be visible evidence of an earlier procedure; it was not a complete medical record and certainly not a guarantee of immunity.

Modern international health law retains legitimate tools for cross-border disease management. The WHO's International Health Regulations provide a framework for responding to public-health risks, while some travel circumstances involve recognised vaccination certificates, notably for yellow fever.

Notice how many different questions an official might be asking:

- **Who are you?** Identity.
- **Are you permitted to enter?** Immigration status.
- **Have you been exposed to a particular infection?** Epidemiology.
- **Are you infectious now?** Clinical assessment and testing.
- **Do you need treatment?** Healthcare.
- **What information may be shared, and with whom?** Confidentiality and law.

These are not interchangeable. A government may need answers to several of them, but that does not mean every agency needs unrestricted access to every answer.

**Having somebody's papers is not the same thing as knowing whether they have tuberculosis.**

## 5. 🇨🇳 The Disease Was Real. The Racism Was Optional.

Chinese migration to nineteenth-century California was shaped by gold mining, labour demand, railroad construction and increasingly hostile politics. Anti-Chinese movements turned racial exclusion into law. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 restricted Chinese labour immigration; the Geary Act of 1892 intensified registration and documentation requirements.

Then plague appeared in San Francisco around 1900. This was a genuine public-health problem. It also became the setting for discriminatory interventions against Chinatown. The litigation in *Wong Wai v. Williamson* challenged racially targeted inoculation measures. The case is important precisely because the presence of a real epidemic did not remove the requirement for equal and rational treatment. The outbreak continued into the early twentieth century; Angel Island Immigration Station opened in 1910, as part of a wider exclusionary migration regime.

There are several different questions to keep apart: Was plague present? Yes. Did authorities have grounds to act? Yes. Were all measures proportionate, effective and equally applied? No such conclusion follows from the first two answers.

And there is an administrative trap here. If admitting that a disease exists threatens a city's commerce, officials may have incentives to minimise or dispute it. If an ethnic neighbourhood is politically vulnerable, officials may instead concentrate restrictions there. Neither response is a substitute for good epidemiology.

**An emergency is not a scientific endorsement of every intervention someone proposes during it.**

## 6. 🌍 When Europeans Brought the Diseases

The familiar story of outsiders carrying infection into a settled population becomes rather less comfortable when applied to European expansion into the Americas. European contact brought infections including smallpox into populations with different histories of exposure. Epidemics combined with warfare, displacement, enslavement, dispossession and disruption of food systems to produce catastrophic losses among Indigenous peoples.

The precise agent responsible for the 1616–1619 epidemic along parts of the New England coast remains debated. That uncertainty should not be turned into doubt about the wider, well-established epidemiological and colonial history.

A history of migration and disease that casts migrants as an inherently dangerous category is not history. It is selective storytelling. Disease can travel in every direction; the capacity to survive and respond is shaped by material conditions and political power.

## 7. 🧪 The Shitty History of Medical Progress

Medical knowledge has been built through remarkable cooperation, but some of the institutions claiming to advance medicine have treated certain people as expendable research material. The details matter, because these cases are not interchangeable.

**Enslaved Black women and gynaecological experimentation.** J. Marion Sims performed experimental operations on enslaved women, among them Anarcha, Lucy and Betsey. The conditions of slavery fundamentally compromised the possibility of freely given consent. This was not a vaccine trial. It belongs here because it shows what happens when medical authority and ownership-like control over another person's body overlap.

**Colonial medical campaigns.** European colonial administrations conducted campaigns against diseases including sleeping sickness. Some treatments involved dangerous arsenical drugs. Their histories contain both efforts to treat serious disease and profound questions about coercion, experimentation, unequal risk and who controlled decisions. We should not flatten all colonial medicine into one event or one motive.

**Tuskegee, 1932–1972.** The US Public Health Service observed Black men with syphilis while misleading participants and withholding adequate treatment, including after penicillin became an effective standard treatment. The researchers did **not** deliberately infect the men; this was **not** a vaccination experiment. Those corrections make the actual wrongdoing clearer, not smaller. The study did not need to happen in that form, and its continuation after effective treatment became available is particularly damning. Its exposure, legal settlement and eventual presidential apology form part of the accountability history.

**Kano, Nigeria, 1996.** During a meningitis epidemic, Pfizer conducted a trial involving trovafloxacin (Trovan) and a comparator treatment. The trial generated disputes about consent, research ethics, injuries, regulatory authorisation and responsibility, followed by litigation and settlements. The event, the allegations, the company's responses and the legal outcomes must be distinguished rather than collapsed into one confident accusation.

**🎬 *The Constant Gardener*.** The novel and 2005 film are fiction, not a dossier proving a particular conspiracy. Their institutional dynamics are nevertheless worth examining: pharmaceutical research, desperate patients, diplomatic interests, unequal bargaining power, people trying to help, and the difficulty of challenging powerful organisations. The question is why the story's abuses are recognisable enough to feel plausible.

Medical progress is not discredited by acknowledging exploitation. It is made more trustworthy by establishing the rules that prevent exploitation from being repeated.

## 8. ⚖️ Whose Body? Whose Permission?

America's history of medical coercion against racialised women needs more than a passing mention. It is a central part of this node's inquiry into **custody, consent and the ability to refuse**.

Compulsory sterilisation programmes were justified under eugenic theories and disproportionately harmed some marginalised communities. The phrase *Mississippi appendectomy* became associated with involuntary sterilisation of Black women, sometimes disguised as other surgery. Puerto Rican sterilisation histories raise questions about informed consent, economic pressure and the politics of population management; specific programmes and consent practices must be examined separately. Investigations in the 1970s documented serious consent failures involving sterilisation of Native American women through Indian Health Service facilities.

In 2020, women detained at Georgia's Irwin County Detention Center alleged abusive and non-consensual gynaecological treatment. A subsequent bipartisan US Senate investigation reported evidence that women appeared to have undergone excessive, invasive and often unnecessary procedures, and examined failures of oversight. That finding must not be inflated into a claim that every publicly circulated allegation was proved. It is serious enough on its own.

Consent is not merely a signature at the bottom of a form. It requires comprehensible information, appropriate interpretation, clinical explanation, freedom from threats, and a meaningful possibility of saying **no**. A person who cannot choose their clinician, leave the institution, or complain without fearing consequences is in a structurally vulnerable position. That does not make every medical intervention in custody unlawful. It makes independent safeguards indispensable.

A person does not lose ownership of their body because an immigration officer has custody of their paperwork.

## 9. 🩺 Healthcare Is Also a Public-Health Intervention

This is the bit that makes the administrative contradictions particularly difficult to understand.

Tuberculosis control depends on identifying disease and latent infection, providing appropriate treatment, and supporting completion of care. Measles control depends heavily on vaccination and outbreak response. Sexual-health services depend on confidential testing and treatment. Maternal and child healthcare involves prevention, safeguarding and continuity of care. The exact interventions differ; the general requirement for people to be able to access them does not disappear.

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention already publishes guidance for post-arrival refugee screening, including TB evaluation, immunisation, medical history, interpretation and connection to ongoing care. Its clinical guidance emphasises building trust and clarifying that confidential refugee medical visits are for health, not immigration regulation. That is not an open-borders manifesto. **It is the American public-health system explaining how public health works.**

Not every migrant has the same eligibility for care, and screening rules differ across immigration categories. A country-of-origin risk factor can be relevant to screening without justifying stigma. Likewise, access to healthcare does not mean every clinical intervention is mandatory, or that infectious disease can be managed without any proportionate restrictions.

But if somebody avoids diagnosis because they fear detention, loses access to medicine during a transfer, or cannot afford treatment, the consequences may reach beyond that one person. Public-health protection relies on participation, continuity and timely information.

**If you make people frightened to tell you they are ill, what exactly have you achieved?**

## 10. 🇪🇺 We Have Already Invented Cross-Border Cooperation

States have long coordinated disease surveillance and quarantine rules. The WHO's International Health Regulations are one example. European reciprocal healthcare arrangements are another: EHIC and the UK's GHIC support defined access to medically necessary state healthcare during eligible temporary stays. They are not universal free-care passes, not immigration permission and not substitutes for all travel insurance.

The point is not that these systems solve every migration problem. It is that governments already know how to distinguish mobility, eligibility, medical need, payment arrangements and cross-border administrative cooperation. None of this requires pretending that borders are imaginary.

It requires deciding what problem the border is supposed to solve, and designing the administrative interface accordingly.

## 11. 🧊🔥 ICE: What Safety Is the System Producing?

Immigration detention is a profound transfer of control. The detained person may not be free to choose a doctor, leave an unsafe room, preserve evidence, find a solicitor, obtain independent interpretation or reach family. The state and its contractors therefore carry substantial responsibilities for safety, healthcare and accountability.

The US Government Accountability Office reported in May 2025 that DHS had not established adequate goals and measures for assessing the effectiveness of several detention-facility inspection programmes. This is not proof that every facility is unsafe. It is a documented problem with the machinery meant to tell the public whether standards are being met.

### 🚨 Rape, sexual assault and the right to safety in custody

A person in immigration custody must be protected against rape, sexual assault, sexual coercion and exploitation, whether the perpetrator is another detainee, an employee or a contractor. Sexual-abuse prevention standards and the Prison Rape Elimination Act framework matter here, but a policy on paper is not the same as an accessible remedy.

The practical questions are brutally simple. Can someone report abuse confidentially? Can they report a staff member without being left dependent on that same staff member? Can they access independent medical care, a forensic examination, emergency contraception, STI prevention and treatment, and appropriate psychological support? Can they preserve evidence? Can they reach legal counsel? Will an allegation be independently investigated? Can a transfer or removal interrupt that investigation? Are complainants protected against retaliation?

Claims about individual incidents must be separated into allegations, investigative findings, civil settlements and criminal convictions. The absence of complaints is **not** evidence of the absence of abuse when people may be frightened to complain.

**The right not to be raped is not a reward for possessing the correct immigration documents.**

### 🩻 Reproductive autonomy and medical coercion

The Irwin County findings make the custody problem concrete. A woman dependent on an institution for medical access can be exposed to invasive treatment without meaningful choice. Proper clinical consent requires qualified interpreters, clear explanations, access to records, the ability to refuse non-emergency procedures and independent review. A signed form cannot by itself answer whether consent was voluntary.

### 🧯 The state's responsibility does not end at the cell door

Detention creates foreseeable needs: medication, specialist care, disability access, mental-health support, infection control, nutrition, safe accommodation, sexual-assault prevention, complaint mechanisms and independent oversight. The state cannot justify taking away liberty and then disclaim responsibility for the dependency it has created.

## 12. 💰 The Border Is Expensive, Actually

Enforcement costs money. So do detention beds, contractors, transportation, medical provision, litigation, administration and removal operations. Preventative care costs money too. So does delayed diagnosis, emergency treatment, avoidable transmission and preventable deterioration.

A serious public-expenditure argument needs actual budgets, defined populations, time horizons and comparisons with alternatives. Migrants' fiscal contributions vary with employment, age, legal status, location and the level of government being examined. Some undocumented migrants pay taxes; this is not the same as claiming every migrant produces the same net fiscal effect.

A useful evaluation would compare the outcomes of detention-heavy enforcement with faster case processing, legal representation, community-based case management, work authorisation where lawful, healthcare access and targeted action against exploitation. The relevant measures are not simply arrests or removals. They include cost, lawful case resolution, public-health outcomes, safety, rights compliance and administrative reliability.

**An arrest count measures arrests. It does not, by itself, measure safety.**

## 13. 📖 Success Is Not the Same Thing as Legitimacy

Thomas Hobbes's *Leviathan* contains a discussion of how successful wrongdoing can acquire the language of virtue. The precise wording and context should be checked against an authoritative edition before direct quotation. The broader distinction is useful: an institution's success at exercising power does not establish that the power was exercised rightly.

Governments may have legitimate reasons to regulate entry, adjudicate asylum claims and remove people following lawful processes. Those purposes do not establish that every detention practice is necessary, proportionate or effective. Election results do not demonstrate that every voter endorses every administrative method. People vote in coalitions, with overlapping and sometimes conflicting priorities: wages, housing, services, identity, security, trust and disillusionment.

So the government should be able to explain: **What problem are you solving? What evidence identifies it? Why this intervention? What alternatives were assessed? How much will it cost? How will you know it worked?**

The same discipline applies when states invoke human rights in foreign policy. Abuses by another government can be criticised on their merits; alleged or documented abuses at home still demand scrutiny. Different violations should not be treated as legally identical, and human-rights concerns do not automatically authorise military action. Principles become less credible when they are presented as universal abroad but negotiable in custody at home.

## 14. ✝️ The Stranger, the Sick and the Prisoner

There is also an awkward theological problem for political actors who explicitly invoke Christian morality while defending degrading treatment of migrants.

In Matthew 25:35–36, the Gospel brings together care for **the stranger**, **the sick** and **the prisoner**. In Luke 10:25–37, the Good Samaritan turns the supposedly suspect outsider into the example of neighbourly responsibility. Christian traditions differ about the role of government, immigration restrictions and the relation between personal charity and state policy. These passages do not supply a complete immigration statute.

But they make it difficult to argue that the dignity of the person in custody is irrelevant. The prisoner does not cease to be a neighbour. The stranger does not become less human at a checkpoint. A state can debate lawful entry without treating access to medicine, freedom from sexual violence or basic bodily autonomy as discretionary favours.

**I also just don't think your Jesus believes in treating human beings this badly.**

That is a moral challenge, not a claim to speak for every Christian. It asks people who appeal to those teachings to explain how their proposed methods fit the obligations they themselves recognise.

## 15. ♻️ The Body in the Border

Here is the cybernetic problem, stripped of its patriotic packaging.

**Possible coercive loop:**

```text
Fear of immigration enforcement
            ↓
Avoidance of healthcare or reporting
            ↓
Less timely information and treatment
            ↓
Potentially worse health or safety outcomes
            ↓
More political demand for visible control
            ↓
More fear of institutional contact
```

**Possible cooperative loop:**

```text
Safe access to care and reporting
            ↓
Earlier contact with institutions
            ↓
Better diagnosis, evidence and intervention
            ↓
Potentially better outcomes and accountability
            ↓
Greater practical trust and participation
```

These are mechanisms to test against evidence, not universal predictions. They vary by policy design, population and context. But they expose a central information problem: **compliance is not cooperation**. People may comply with coercive orders while withholding the information institutions need to keep them and others safe.

Disease surveillance and surveillance of people are not identical. Clinicians may need exposure histories, vaccination records and test results. Immigration officers may need lawful identity and case-status information. Sharing every medical fact with every enforcement agency is neither an inevitable nor an automatically desirable consequence. Purpose limitation, medical confidentiality, independent oversight and carefully defined exceptions protect both individuals and the quality of public-health information.

Sexual violence makes the problem starker. A detention centre can report few complaints because abuse is rare—or because reporting is dangerous. Without independent routes for disclosure and investigation, its own numbers may be incapable of distinguishing those explanations.

The body is not a spreadsheet entry. It is where the consequences of these administrative decisions actually arrive.

## 16. 🪿 Do You Even Care About Your Border?

We began with vaccination because it is one of the clearest examples of what coordinated human protection can accomplish. We moved through quarantine because there are genuine dangers in infectious disease. We examined racialised exclusion because real threats can be managed through discriminatory methods. We examined medical exploitation because protecting health requires consent and accountability. And we arrived at detention because the state cannot claim the authority to control a human being while treating that person's safety as an optional expense.

The choice is not between having borders and having compassion. Nor is it between taking infectious disease seriously and respecting migrants' rights. Those are false administrative binaries.

If a government says it is protecting public health, ask how its policy improves diagnosis, treatment and prevention. If it says detention protects the public, ask how it protects people **inside** detention. If it invokes the rule of law, ask how a person in custody can challenge a violation. If it invokes morality, ask whose dignity counts.

A government should be able to protect the population without confusing the pathogen with the person, the document with the diagnosis, the arrest with the outcome, or the power to detain with the right to abuse.

**What kind of safety are we seeking? What is the reality? And how do we do this without becoming monstrous?**

Because, genuinely: **it don't make no sense to me.**
