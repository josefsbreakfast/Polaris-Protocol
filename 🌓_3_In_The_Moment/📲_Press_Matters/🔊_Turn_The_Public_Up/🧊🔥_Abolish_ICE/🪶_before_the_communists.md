# 🪶 Before the Communists
**First created:** 2026-10-14 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-15  
*The United States was deciding who belonged, who could move, and who could be expelled long before the Cold War supplied another vocabulary for suspicion.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

It is tempting to begin a history of American immigration enforcement with the Cold War, the creation of Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), or the latest president to make deportation a political spectacle. All three starting points are too late.

Equal citizenship is the starting principle, not a reward for satisfying a shifting test of national belonging. The machinery of exclusion did not arrive fully assembled. It accumulated: land seizure, racial classifications, citizenship rules, labour controls, exclusion statutes, border inspections, registries, detention, deportation procedures, and courts willing—at critical moments—to grant unusually broad deference to the political branches. Different institutions operated these systems, and their legal foundations were not interchangeable. But together they established an enduring administrative question: **who has the authority to decide that another person does not belong?**

This node follows the earlier development of that question. It does not claim that colonial dispossession, slavery, immigration exclusion and wartime incarceration are identical. It asks what each contributed to the American repertoire of classifying, controlling and removing people—and what resistance exposed about those arrangements.

## 🗝️ 1. Citizenship Is Not a Favour: Enslavement and Equal Belonging

Before examining the territorial formation of the United States, there is a foundational fact about its present population: **millions of Americans descend from Africans who were forcibly transported through the transatlantic slave trade and enslaved in North America**. Their ancestors did not arrive as voluntary immigrants seeking admission. They were brought under coercion into a system that treated human beings as property. Their descendants' claim to equal citizenship is not conditional on an immigration story, an ancestry test or anybody else's preferred account of who belongs.

- The slave trade, hereditary slavery and subsequent struggles over emancipation and citizenship are central to American history, not exceptions to a supposedly separate history of national belonging.
- **The Fourteenth Amendment (1868)** recognised birthright citizenship for people born or naturalised in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction. Its Citizenship Clause repudiated the citizenship holding in *Dred Scott*; the Fifteenth Amendment (1870) prohibited denial of voting rights on account of race, colour or previous condition of servitude.
- Formal constitutional recognition did not automatically secure equal participation. Black Codes, racial terror, segregation, poll taxes, literacy tests and discriminatory registration practices obstructed rights in different places and periods. The **Voting Rights Act of 1965** addressed important parts of that machinery, though disputes about access and enforcement have continued.
- **Ancestry in slavery is not a lawful reason to withhold the rights of a citizen.** Nor should citizens have to continually demonstrate that they are sufficiently acceptable, assimilated or historically 'deserving' to exercise equal rights.

### Racism hidden in plain sight: the administrative barrier

A system need not announce a racial exclusion to create racially unequal access. It can impose requirements that sound routine in isolation, then distribute their costs unevenly.

- **Direct exclusion:** a rule openly differentiates by race or ancestry.
- **Facially neutral requirements with unequal effects:** identification, proof of residence, records, fees, travel, waiting times or repeated appointments may be harder for some groups to satisfy because of unequal access to resources or records.
- **Discretionary scrutiny:** additional questioning, suspicion or demands for proof may be imposed unevenly by officials.
- **Cumulative barriers:** several individually modest requirements can become a serious obstacle when combined.
- **Institutional feedback:** people encountering more checks produce more administrative records and more opportunities for alleged irregularities to be discovered, which can then be used to justify further scrutiny.

These are **mechanisms to investigate**, not proof that every identification rule, health inspection or administrative check is discriminatory. Historical examples require evidence about the rule's design, enforcement and consequences; present-day claims require contemporary evidence as well. US constitutional equal-protection claims and statutory disparate-impact claims also operate under different legal standards: a disproportionate effect alone does not automatically prove an unlawful constitutional violation.

The historical comparison is nonetheless important. The same repertoire of **extra examinations, additional papers, official discretion and repeated proof of eligibility** appears in the histories of slavery, segregation, immigration control and voting-rights restriction, although the laws, populations and purposes differ. A state may recognise someone's rights on paper while making those rights increasingly difficult to exercise in practice.

### The boundary of belonging keeps moving

- American racial and religious hierarchies have never been fixed. Groups widely classified as white today—including some European immigrant communities—were once subjected to intense nativist suspicion, even where they were legally eligible for citizenship.
- The revived **1920s Ku Klux Klan** promoted a narrow white Protestant conception of national belonging and attacked Black Americans, Catholics, Jews and immigrants. Its ideology should not be mistaken for the legal definition of citizenship or for the views of all Americans at the time.
- Modern political proposals should be assessed through their **specific wording, implementation and effects**, rather than assuming that every proponent privately shares the same historical ideology. The warning sign is the potential for formally neutral barriers to reproduce unequal exclusion.
- The relevant question is not merely *who is declared eligible?* It is also **who can actually exercise the right without disproportionate obstruction?**

**Mechanism to retain:** equal citizenship is not secured solely by removing an explicit racial prohibition. It also depends on whether the procedures for exercising rights are genuinely accessible and fairly administered.

## 🪶 2. Before There Was a Border to Police

The United States began as a settler state on lands already inhabited and governed by Indigenous nations. The familiar immigration story—people arriving at a country's border and requesting permission to enter—cannot describe that beginning.

- European settlement involved competing imperial claims, warfare, treaties, land purchases under unequal conditions, and forced displacement.
- Indigenous nations possessed political institutions, territorial relationships and their own understandings of movement and belonging. They were not simply populations waiting to be absorbed into a future United States.
- The Indian Removal Act of **1830** authorised the federal government to negotiate removal treaties; its implementation and related coercion drove devastating expulsions, including the Cherokee Trail of Tears.
- Federal control over territory developed alongside arguments about which populations could remain, under whose jurisdiction, and on what terms.
- Indigenous removal is **not** an immigration-deportation programme. Its importance here is the earlier use of law, administration and force to remake the population of a territory.

The first uncomfortable point: a state can describe itself as managing legitimate entry while leaving the violence of its own territorial formation outside the frame.

## ⛓️ 3. Slavery, Movement and the Lawful Person

Chattel slavery built another set of controls around movement, identity and documentation.

- Enslaved people were subjected to surveillance, pass requirements, patrols, capture and forced transportation.
- The **Fugitive Slave Act of 1850** strengthened federal machinery for returning people claimed as fugitives from slavery, including through commissioners and penalties for interference.
- Free Black people could also be endangered by false identification and the denial of meaningful procedural protections.
- The **Dred Scott** decision of **1857** notoriously denied that Black people descended from enslaved Africans could claim national citizenship under the Court's interpretation.
- The **Thirteenth Amendment (1865)** abolished slavery and involuntary servitude except as punishment for crime following conviction; the exception later mattered to convict labour systems.
- The **Fourteenth Amendment (1868)** established constitutional birthright citizenship for people born or naturalised in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction, repudiating Dred Scott's citizenship holding.

These systems cannot be collapsed into immigration law. Yet they reveal how much can turn on official recognition of personhood, status, papers and the ability to move without being seized.

**Mechanism to retain:** the state's classification of a person can determine whether movement is understood as freedom, trespass, flight or grounds for confinement.

## 📜 4. 1790: Citizenship Was Restricted from the Beginning

The **Naturalization Act of 1790** offered naturalisation to a restricted category of applicants described as **“free white persons”**, subject to residency and other conditions.

- The law concerned eligibility to become a citizen, not a universal prohibition on entry.
- It made race an explicit feature of the early federal citizenship system.
- Subsequent changes altered residence requirements and administration, but racial restrictions persisted for generations.
- The **Naturalization Act of 1870** extended naturalisation eligibility to people of African nativity or African descent, while leaving most Asian immigrants excluded.
- The **Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952** finally removed the remaining racial bars to naturalisation, while retaining a discriminatory national-origins quota structure.

A person might live, work, pay taxes, raise children and still be legally excluded from the political membership available to neighbours. **Residence and belonging were deliberately kept apart.**

## 🏛️ 5. 1798: The Alien as a Political Threat

The **Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798**, enacted during heightened tensions with France, predate both modern immigration enforcement and communism as an American political category.

- The **Alien Friends Act** authorised the president, during peacetime, to order certain non-citizens deemed dangerous to depart; it expired in 1800.
- The **Alien Enemies Act** created powers concerning nationals of hostile foreign states during declared war, invasion or predatory incursion; unlike the Alien Friends Act, it endured.
- The **Naturalization Act of 1798** increased the residence requirement for naturalisation to fourteen years; this was later reduced.
- The **Sedition Act** criminalised certain criticism of the federal government, generating major constitutional and political opposition.

The distinction between these statutes matters. They were not one undifferentiated deportation power. Together, however, they show an early connection between foreignness, political suspicion and executive authority.

**Research question:** when officials describe an exceptional threat, what new powers become administratively ordinary after the emergency has passed?

## 🧳 6. Nineteenth-Century Migration: Labour Wanted, People Debated

Industrial expansion, railways, mining and agriculture relied on migrant labour while anti-immigrant politics grew alongside that reliance.

- Irish migration, particularly during and after the Great Famine, met anti-Catholic and nativist hostility as well as opportunities for settlement and political organisation.
- German and other European migration prompted recurring disputes about language, religion, political radicalism and labour competition.
- Chinese workers contributed substantially to western industries and railway construction, while encountering exclusionary violence and discriminatory law.
- Mexican and other cross-border labour mobility developed across a border whose political location changed dramatically following the **1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo**.
- Migrant workers were often described as economically necessary and socially unwelcome at the same time.

That contradiction is not a minor inconsistency. It is a recurring political arrangement: **a labour market can invite a person's work while withholding security from the worker.**

## 🚫 7. 1875: The Page Act and Gendered Exclusion

The **Page Act of 1875** is a crucial predecessor to the better-known Chinese Exclusion Act.

- The statute targeted certain contract labour arrangements and the entry of women for prostitution or other activities described in moralising language.
- Enforcement disproportionately burdened Chinese women, including women seeking lawful migration and family reunion.
- Officials scrutinised women's morality, relationships and documentation.
- A formally framed concern about exploitation or sexual morality could become a mechanism of racialised exclusion.
- Restricting women also affected the formation and continuity of immigrant families and communities.

**Mechanism:** exclusion can be accomplished not only by announcing that an ethnic group is unwelcome, but by making particular categories of family life difficult to establish.

## 🀄 8. 1882: Chinese Exclusion Becomes Federal Policy

The **Chinese Exclusion Act**, signed on **6 May 1882**, suspended the immigration of Chinese labourers for ten years and sharply restricted access to naturalisation.

- The law singled out an ethnic labour category in federal immigration legislation.
- Exceptions existed for some non-labourers, but applicants faced demanding documentary requirements and scrutiny.
- The **Geary Act of 1892** extended exclusion and imposed certificate-of-residence requirements; inability to produce documentation could expose residents to deportation.
- Exclusion was extended again and eventually made permanent before repeal in **1943**.
- The 1943 repeal did not create unrestricted entry: a small national quota remained.

The state did not merely say **no** at the border. It developed a continuing system for asking people already present to **prove** their right to remain.

### The paperwork problem

- Certificates and case files created records of identity, employment, kinship and travel.
- Officials could challenge the credibility of those records and the testimony supporting them.
- People responded through litigation, community organising, documentation and, in some cases, evasive strategies generated by the exclusions themselves.
- The archive contains the government's questions, but those questions are not neutral descriptions of the people being interrogated.

**Important distinction:** a record of suspicion is evidence that suspicion occurred; it is not automatically evidence that the suspicion was justified.

## ⚖️ 9. Courts, Sovereignty and the Power to Exclude

Late nineteenth-century litigation helped define immigration as an area in which the federal government claimed extraordinary authority.

- **Chae Chan Ping v. United States (1889)**, often called the *Chinese Exclusion Case*, upheld a federal exclusion measure against a Chinese labourer who had previously been authorised to return.
- **Fong Yue Ting v. United States (1893)** upheld deportation provisions under the Geary Act and treated deportation as a civil measure rather than criminal punishment.
- **United States v. Wong Kim Ark (1898)** affirmed birthright citizenship for a US-born man of Chinese parentage whose parents were domiciled in the United States, subject to the case's legal conditions.
- These decisions do not tell a single story of courts always endorsing exclusion. They show contestation over the boundary between sovereign power and constitutional protection.

The distinction between **civil removal** and **criminal punishment** became particularly consequential. A process may carry devastating consequences while not being classified as criminal prosecution, with corresponding differences in procedural safeguards.

## 🗺️ 10. The Mexican Border Was Not Always This Border

The United States–Mexico border developed through conquest, treaty, commerce, settlement and enforcement—not as an unchanging geographical fact.

- The **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848)** transferred vast territories to the United States after the Mexican–American War and included protections relating to inhabitants' property and citizenship choices.
- The **Gadsden Purchase (1853–54)** further altered the boundary.
- Indigenous communities, families, traders and workers had networks that predated or crossed the new line.
- Later enforcement transformed familiar patterns of mobility into questions of permission and status.
- Mexican labour was repeatedly sought by employers even as restrictionist politics developed.

The question is not whether borders can be administered. It is how a state narrates movement as inherently suspect while treating the political history of the boundary itself as settled and invisible.

## 🏥 11. Screening, Public Charge and the Administrative Body

Federal immigration controls expanded through inspection and categories of admissibility.

- The general **Immigration Act of 1882** excluded several categories, including people considered likely to become a public charge.
- The **Immigration Act of 1891** expanded federal administration and exclusion grounds.
- **Ellis Island** opened as a federal immigration station in **1892**; **Angel Island** operated as a major West Coast immigration station from **1910**.
- Health, finances, family relationships and perceived moral character became subjects of official examination.
- Inspection systems differed by location and population; Ellis Island and Angel Island should not be presented as interchangeable experiences.
- Medical inspections were not simply a neutral search for illness. Inspectors examined travellers for legally specified diseases and disabilities, while racial prejudice, class assumptions and unequal access to medical evidence could shape who received additional scrutiny, detention or exclusion.
- Public-health restrictions sometimes addressed genuine infectious-disease risks; the historical question is how disease categories were defined, applied and combined with assumptions about particular populations.
- The **1891 and 1903 immigration laws**, among other measures, expanded disease-related grounds of exclusion. The precise categories and inspection procedures changed over time.
- The documentary apparatus also expanded: passenger manifests, identity certificates, visas, photographs, residence records and later passport requirements made movement increasingly legible to officials.
- **Passports were not invented for American immigration exclusion**: passports and comparable travel documents have much older international histories. But the modern requirement to present standardised passports and visas became increasingly embedded in twentieth-century US border administration, notably around the First World War and through the **1918 passport-control legislation** and **1924 immigration reforms**.
- Requirements were not experienced evenly. Chinese exclusion certificates, for example, created a particularly intrusive documentary regime for a population already targeted by law.

The body became evidence. So did the wallet, the marriage, the accent, the surname, the answers to a question—and whether an official believed them.

## 🧱 12. Building the Bureaucracy

The machinery of enforcement developed before ICE existed and before the federal government had a single modern immigration department.

- Federal immigration administration expanded in the **1890s**.
- The **Bureau of Immigration and Naturalization** was established in **1906**, alongside more standardised naturalisation procedures.
- The **Immigration Act of 1917** introduced a literacy test and expanded exclusion, including an Asiatic barred zone with specified exceptions.
- The **US Border Patrol** was established in **1924**, within the Department of Labor's immigration service.
- The **Immigration and Naturalization Service (INS)** was formed in **1933** through the consolidation of immigration and naturalisation functions.
- ICE itself would not be established until **2003**.

**Do not retroactively call earlier agencies ICE.** Institutional ancestry matters precisely because names, legal authorities, departmental homes and operating practices changed.

## 📰 13. Nativism, Race Science and the Quota State

The early twentieth century saw restrictionist politics draw on eugenics, racial hierarchy, nationalism and claims about assimilation.

- The **Emergency Quota Act of 1921** introduced numerical national-origins limits for many immigrants.
- The **Immigration Act of 1924 (Johnson–Reed Act)** tightened quotas using a system designed to favour older northern and western European immigration patterns.
- The 1924 law also excluded many people deemed ineligible for citizenship, with major effects on Asian immigration.
- The national-origins system did not apply identically to every part of the world; Western Hemisphere migration was treated differently under the 1924 quota provisions.
- Policymakers and advocates employed statistical and pseudo-scientific claims to argue that some populations were more desirable than others.

The apparent neutrality of a percentage does not explain why its denominator, historical baseline or eligible categories were selected.

**Mechanism:** discriminatory political choices can be hidden in the architecture of a calculation.

### Who counted as white?

- The meaning of **whiteness** was historically contested, and its boundaries shifted across law, politics and everyday life. It should not be read backwards from today's US census classifications.
- Federal naturalisation law made eligibility depend on being a **“free white person”** for much of the nineteenth century, but courts and officials disagreed about which populations fitted that category.
- Irish Catholics, southern and eastern Europeans, Jews and other groups now often described as white encountered forms of nativism, religious prejudice, racial theorising and suspicion that complicate any simple account of an unchanging white majority.
- **Important distinction:** prejudice against these groups did not always mean that they were formally barred from naturalisation as non-white. Legal racial eligibility, social acceptance and the racial hierarchies advanced by nativist movements were related but different questions.
- The **Ku Klux Klan's 1920s revival** is a useful case study: its white Protestant nationalism targeted Black Americans, Catholics, Jews, immigrants and others. Its boundaries of acceptable national belonging were considerably narrower than a present-day checkbox labelled “white”.
- Restrictionist arguments often invoked supposedly hereditary national or racial characteristics, treating some European populations as less assimilable or less desirable than others. Such hierarchies helped shape the 1920s quota debates.
- The broader mechanism was not merely sorting people into two permanent racial categories. It was the state's and society's capacity to **redefine which people were trustworthy, assimilable or entitled to belong**, sometimes even within populations later grouped together as white.
- Investigate the precise historical terminology used by lawmakers, courts, eugenicists and the Klan rather than treating their definitions as consistent or scientifically meaningful.

## 🕍 14. Jewish Refugees and the Closed Door

For Jewish communities, the history of American exclusion cannot be told solely through later Cold War politics.

- Jewish immigrants from eastern and southern Europe were among the populations disadvantaged by the national-origins quota system.
- Antisemitism interacted with nativism, class prejudice and fears about political radicalism.
- As Nazi persecution intensified in the 1930s, restrictive quotas, administrative obstacles, economic conditions and political opposition limited refuge in the United States.
- The **MS *St. Louis*** refugee voyage of **1939** became a particularly visible example of failed refuge, though its history should be told accurately rather than reduced to a slogan.
- The US government and public were not uniform: advocates, organisations and officials argued for rescue and admissions while others resisted them.

A state may condemn persecution abroad while preserving domestic rules that prevent people escaping it. The moral contradiction deserves attention without erasing the specific legal and political decisions involved.

## 🚨 15. The First Red Scare: Communism Arrives Late to an Older System

The **First Red Scare (1919–20)** intensified the treatment of radical political affiliation as an immigration and security concern.

- Wartime repression and postwar unrest formed the background.
- The **1918 Immigration Act** expanded deportability for certain anarchist and radical affiliations.
- The **Palmer Raids** involved mass arrests and searches directed at suspected radicals, with serious abuses and contested legal procedures.
- **Emma Goldman** and **Alexander Berkman** were among those deported in **1919**.
- Officials used immigration status as one means of acting against political movements.
- Labour militancy, anarchism, socialism and communism were distinct traditions, even where officials and newspapers blurred them together.

The title of this node is deliberately chronological, not a claim that anticommunism had no role before the Cold War. **Political radicalism became another reason to deploy an exclusionary apparatus whose foundations were already in place.**

## 🪖 16. 1942: Citizenship Did Not Prevent Mass Incarceration

Following Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor, **Executive Order 9066**, signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on **19 February 1942**, authorised military exclusion zones. Its implementation produced the forced removal and incarceration of approximately **120,000 people of Japanese ancestry**, most of them US citizens.

- The order did not itself name Japanese Americans, but military implementation targeted them.
- Families were uprooted and confined in guarded facilities; many lost property, livelihoods and community ties.
- The programme was carried out through wartime military and civilian agencies, **not ICE**.
- Wartime controls also affected some German and Italian nationals, but the scope and implementation were not equivalent to the mass incarceration of Japanese Americans.
- **Korematsu v. United States (1944)** upheld a wartime exclusion conviction; it remains a landmark warning about judicial deference and racialised national-security claims.
- The **Civil Liberties Act of 1988** formally apologised and provided redress to eligible surviving Japanese American internees.

This was not immigration detention in the ordinary sense: many imprisoned people were citizens. That is exactly why it belongs in the wider history of state classification and confinement.

**The government can recognise citizenship in principle while constructing an exception that strips citizens of practical liberty.**

## 🧭 17. What the Earlier History Actually Establishes

The record does **not** establish one uninterrupted institution, a single conspiracy, or an unchanged ideology running from 1790 to ICE. It establishes a series of recurring administrative possibilities:

1. **Classification:** define populations by race, origin, citizenship, labour, health or perceived threat.
2. **Documentation:** require people to prove a status that officials can challenge.
3. **Discretion:** give officers or executives room to decide who is dangerous, admissible or credible.
4. **Civil process:** impose severe consequences through systems not classified as criminal punishment.
5. **Emergency:** invoke war, disorder, disease or economic danger to expand state power.
6. **Economic dependence:** seek labour while withholding durable security from workers.
7. **Judicial deference:** narrow opportunities for review by treating exclusion as an exceptional sovereign function.
8. **Public narrative:** describe targeted populations as a problem requiring administrative management.
9. **Resistance:** challenge those classifications through courts, organising, journalism, mutual aid and the preservation of testimony.
10. **Repair:** recognise that later apologies or statutory reforms do not automatically undo accumulated losses.

These mechanisms recur in different combinations. Their presence is an invitation to investigate **specific powers and practices**, not permission to claim that every historical episode was identical.

## 🧊 18. The Handover to the Cold War

By the time post-1945 national-security politics hardened, the United States already possessed:

- Federal immigration and naturalisation agencies.
- A border enforcement workforce.
- Categories of inadmissibility and deportability.
- Documentary inspection and registration practices.
- National-origins quotas.
- Judicial doctrines granting substantial federal authority over admission and removal.
- Precedents for associating political ideas with deportability.
- Experience of large-scale wartime confinement under a security rationale.

The Cold War did not invent these capacities. It reorganised and extended parts of them under new political pressures. The next node should examine exactly **which statutes, agencies, categories and practices changed**, rather than assuming that a new ideology created the whole apparatus from nothing.

## 🔬 Research and Verification Notes

- Distinguish the rights of citizens descended from enslaved people from the separate legal position of non-citizens subject to immigration controls; compare mechanisms without equating the populations or legal claims.
- Investigate contemporary allegations of indirect racial exclusion against the precise policy text, implementation data and relevant US legal standards; do not infer concealed intent from unequal outcomes alone.
- Keep **settler dispossession**, **slavery**, **immigration exclusion**, **deportation** and **wartime incarceration** legally distinct even while comparing mechanisms.
- Trace which court decisions remain operative, which have been limited or repudiated, and which are cited today; historical importance is not the same as current controlling law.
- Investigate Indigenous perspectives and treaty histories rather than relying exclusively on federal administrative archives.
- Research Mexican and Indigenous cross-border mobility beyond an exclusively Washington-centred account.
- Add migrant-authored testimony and community histories alongside statutes and government records.
- Verify precise statutory wording and dates before quoting historical legislation.
- Check immigration medical-inspection records and distinguish medically justified measures from discriminatory enforcement.
- Trace passport, visa and identity-certificate history separately; avoid attributing the invention of passports to US exclusion law.
- Compare legal naturalisation eligibility with nativist and Klan conceptions of whiteness; these were not identical.
- Avoid suggesting that US exclusion was uniquely American; comparisons with British imperial and European systems belong in `⛓️_historical_parallels.md`.
- The principal question for later nodes is **continuity of mechanisms**, not presumed continuity of motive.
