# 🧶 Getting Hands Dirty

*Working notes — creative protest, collective participation, historical memory, and the responsibilities of joining movements already in progress.*

## 🛰️ Orientation

- Examine how ordinary people participate in public protest through:
  - Knitting.
  - Sewing.
  - Embroidery.
  - Banner-making.
  - Placards.
  - Costumes.
  - Music.
  - Performance.
  - Humour.
  - Community gatherings.
  - Shared creative projects.
- Investigate the relationship between making things together and building political communities.
- Use contemporary examples including:
  - No Kings demonstrations in the United States.
  - Minnesota's red-hat knitting initiatives.
  - Other creative responses to immigration enforcement.
- Connect contemporary protest traditions to their historical predecessors.
- Examine how creative participation can:
  - Make political engagement accessible.
  - Reduce social isolation.
  - Communicate complex ideas.
  - Establish shared identities.
  - Encourage further participation.
  - Provide practical resources.
- Recognise the value of participation without requiring every creative activity to produce an immediate legislative outcome.
- Equally, distinguish:
  - Feeling involved.
  - Being visible.
  - Providing useful assistance.
  - Supporting existing organisers.
  - Producing institutional change.
- Investigate tensions between newly mobilised participants and communities that have experienced discriminatory enforcement for many years.
- Examine the importance of listening when affected people express discomfort with particular symbols, practices or representations.
- Central question:
  - How can creative public protest become part of sustained collective action without displacing the people who were already doing the work?

- Investigate the historical relationship between textile production and political organising.
- Examine how sewing, knitting and embroidery have provided opportunities for collective activity.
- Consider the historical association between textile work and women's domestic labour.
- Examine how activities dismissed as decorative or domestic have communicated political demands.
- Investigate:
  - Suffrage banners.
  - Labour movement banners.
  - Trade union insignia.
  - Abolitionist textiles.
  - Political quilts.
  - Anti-war textile projects.
  - Memorial embroidery.
  - Community banner-making.
- Examine how textile objects preserve information about their makers.
- Consider the relationship between handmade objects and collective memory.
- Investigate the labour involved in making political objects.
- Ask whose work is recognised and whose remains anonymous.

### Craft as communication

- Examine how visual symbols communicate across linguistic barriers.
- Investigate the importance of:
  - Colour.
  - Repetition.
  - Familiar objects.
  - Shared patterns.
  - Recognisable silhouettes.
  - Humour.
- Examine how handmade objects differ from professionally produced campaign materials.
- Investigate whether making an object encourages a different relationship with a political issue than purchasing one.
- Avoid assuming handmade objects are inherently more authentic or politically meaningful.
- Central question:
  - What can a handmade object communicate about participation, labour and belonging?

## 🪞 8. When Someone Says They're Uncomfortable

- Examine the responsibilities of organisers when affected people raise concerns.
- Distinguish:
  - Discomfort.
  - Disagreement.
  - Exclusion.
  - Misrepresentation.
  - Material harm.
- Recognise that discomfort can reveal information organisers have overlooked.
- Examine concerns involving:
  - Cultural appropriation.
  - Racial stereotypes.
  - Historical imagery.
  - Religious symbolism.
  - Political slogans.
  - Public identification.
  - Unwanted representation.
  - Trivialisation of trauma.
- Investigate whether organisers have accessible ways to receive feedback.
- Examine how concerns are evaluated.
- Avoid requiring an individual to prove that an entire community agrees with them.
- Equally, avoid treating one person's response as representative of everyone sharing their identity.
- Consider the difference between:
  - Listening seriously.
  - Asking clarifying questions.
  - Reviewing the evidence.
  - Changing a practice.
  - Explaining a decision.
- Examine whether concerns can be resolved without public confrontation.
- Recognise that some disagreements cannot be eliminated.
- Central question:
  - Can a movement treat discomfort as information worth investigating rather than automatically interpreting it as an attack?

## 🕸️ 13. The Cybernetics of Creative Collective Action

- Examine creative protest as a system of information exchange.
- Identify:
  - Participants.
  - Organisers.
  - Existing community networks.
  - Affected populations.
  - Shared symbols.
  - Resources.
  - Communication channels.
  - Feedback.
  - Decision-making.
- Examine how participation generates new social connections.
- Investigate whether those connections persist.
- Consider how movements identify:
  - Emerging needs.
  - Organisational problems.
  - Misunderstood symbols.
  - Excluded participants.
  - Resource shortages.
- Examine how feedback changes subsequent activities.
- Investigate whether organisers can acknowledge mistakes.
- Distinguish adaptation from abandoning the movement's objectives.

### Possible feedback failure

- A creative symbol becomes popular.
- Participation expands.
- The symbol receives increasing attention.
- People directly affected raise concerns.
- Organisers interpret criticism as a threat to participation.
- The movement becomes defensive.
- Attention shifts towards protecting the symbol.
- The original institutional problem receives less attention.

- Treat this as a possible mechanism to investigate, not a universal description of creative protest.

### Possible constructive feedback

- A creative activity attracts participants.
- Participants encounter existing community organisations.
- Organisers receive feedback.
- Activities are adapted where appropriate.
- New participants contribute skills and resources.
- Relationships continue beyond the initial event.
- Existing organisations gain additional capacity.
- Participants learn more about the underlying institutional problem.

- Examine evidence for each stage.
- Central question:
  - Does the movement respond to information from affected people, or does it become organised around preserving its own public image?

## ✨ Stardust

- Creative protest is not necessarily superficial.
- Enjoyment and political seriousness are not opposites.
- Public visibility is not the same as institutional accountability.
- Newly participating people can bring valuable resources and energy.
- Longstanding organisers possess experience that should not be erased by sudden public attention.
- The people most directly affected may experience the same political symbols differently.
- Listening requires opportunities for feedback and a willingness to consider changes.
- Solidarity does not require identical identities or experiences.
- Practical participation can be quiet, repetitive and administrative.
- Movements need institutional memory as well as public enthusiasm.
- The question is not whether knitting a hat counts as political action.
- The question is what relationships, knowledge and possibilities develop around the people making it.
