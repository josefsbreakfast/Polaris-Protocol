# 🛟 But Immigration
**First created:** 2026-10-09 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-15  
*Borders, asylum, administrative capacity and the question that is too often used to end the conversation rather than begin one.*

---

<a id="top"></a>

## 🛰️ Orientation

“But immigration.” Two words that arrive like a procedural objection. Somebody describes a child in detention, a workplace raid, a family separated, or a person denied a meaningful opportunity to explain why returning home could endanger them. Then somebody else announces: *But immigration.*

Yes. Immigration. That is the subject. It is not an answer to the question.

A state can regulate admission, assess claims, maintain records, enforce lawful decisions and plan public services. None of those propositions establishes that every method used in its name is necessary, lawful, effective or humane. Conversely, identifying abuses in enforcement does not resolve the practical questions of how admissions, asylum, employment, housing, courts and public services should be organised. Both things can be true, and the administrative work does not disappear because a slogan has been deployed.

This node is the practical hinge in the [🧊🔥 Abolish ICE](./README.md) cluster. It asks what the objection *“but immigration”* is trying to protect, which questions are legitimate, which are being collapsed together, and what a functioning system would actually have to do. It does not assume that criticism of ICE entails abolishing immigration law, nor that support for immigration controls entails approval of every enforcement practice.

> **Working principle:** A border is a jurisdictional arrangement. It is not a suspension of evidence, accountability or the humanity of people crossing it.

## 🧭 1. What Is the Question, Exactly?

Public arguments about immigration routinely combine several separate policy problems. They need separating before they can be answered.

| Question | What it actually concerns | What it does not automatically establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who may enter? | Admission rules, visas, border processing | That detention is the default solution |
| Who may remain? | Residence status, family ties, protection and removal law | That all people without status present the same circumstances |
| Who needs protection? | Refugee law, asylum, non-refoulement and individual evidence | That a claim is fraudulent because it has not yet been decided |
| Who can work? | Labour-market rules, permits, employers and enforcement | That punishing workers alone prevents exploitation |
| What can services support? | Housing, schools, healthcare, local budgets and planning | That every service shortfall is caused by migration |
| How are decisions enforced? | Courts, appeal rights, supervision, return arrangements | That coercion is always the most effective tool |
| What makes communities safe? | Actual crime, exploitation, policing and safeguarding outcomes | That nationality or immigration status is a proxy for danger |

A serious discussion can hold these questions simultaneously. A slogan tends to flatten them into a single supposed emergency. The first useful intervention is often embarrassingly simple: *Which problem are we solving, and how would we know if we had solved it?*

The United States has multiple pathways into and through its immigration system: temporary visas, lawful permanent residence, family sponsorship, humanitarian protection, and procedures for people encountered without the documents required for admission. They operate under different statutes and agencies. “Immigrant” is not a single legal category; neither is “illegal”.

## ⚖️ 2. The Law Is Real; So Are Its Limits

The principal statutory framework is the Immigration and Nationality Act, amended repeatedly by Congress. Federal authority over admission and removal is extensive, but it is not unlimited. Constitutional rules, statutes, treaties implemented in domestic law, judicial decisions and procedural requirements shape what officials may do. Those constraints vary with the person's circumstances and location; it would be inaccurate to claim that everyone has an identical procedural entitlement at a port of entry and after years of residence.

An asylum claim is not the same as an approved asylum grant. Under US law, asylum generally concerns persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution connected to race, religion, nationality, political opinion or membership of a particular social group, with exclusions and procedural requirements. Poverty alone does not establish asylum eligibility; neither does a government's disagreement with an applicant make their fear fictional. Withholding of removal and protection under the Convention Against Torture are separate legal routes with different standards and consequences.

Nor is every immigration violation a crime. Many removability questions are civil or administrative. Certain conduct, including improper entry under specified circumstances and unlawful re-entry after removal, may carry criminal penalties. The distinction matters when detention, prosecution and public rhetoric are discussed as though they were interchangeable.

The Congressional Research Service's explanation of credible-fear and defensive-asylum processes is useful precisely because it sets out the legal pathways without pretending that the pathways are simple. The challenge is to make determinations accurately and promptly while preserving safeguards against returning people to persecution or torture. Faster is not automatically fairer; slower is not automatically safer.

## 🧳 3. Why People Move

Migration has multiple causes and multiple time horizons. People move for employment, family, education, safety, environmental pressures, discrimination and combinations of these. Some movements are planned over years. Others follow an immediate threat. Some people travel with visas and later fall out of status; others arrive at a border to request protection. Treating all of these as one population obscures the policy tools available.

For the United States, regional conditions in Latin America, Caribbean states and beyond matter, as do American economic demand, family networks and the design of lawful routes. A person can be responding to violence *and* hoping to earn a living. Mixed motives do not erase the obligation to assess a legally cognisable protection claim individually.

Historical context matters too. US foreign policy, trade, military intervention and regional economic relations can influence conditions in sending countries, although a credible account must establish the relevant causal chain in each case rather than assigning every migration movement to one government. A border policy operates downstream of conditions it cannot control alone.

## 🏘️ 4. Housing, Schools, Hospitals: Capacity Is a Real Question

Public-service capacity is not imaginary. Schools require teachers, clinics require staff, homes require land, investment and construction, and councils or municipalities need funding that reflects actual demand. Sudden population changes can strain systems already operating close to capacity. Denying that possibility is not a persuasive answer to residents experiencing it.

But it is equally misleading to treat migration as a complete explanation for shortages. Housing supply, zoning, infrastructure investment, wage levels, service funding, regional inequality and demographic change all matter. Migrants also rent and buy homes, work in public services, pay taxes, establish businesses and become part of the labour force. Effects differ by place, skill, time period and legal status; a national average does not describe every neighbourhood.

The appropriate test is distributional. Who receives the costs, who receives the benefits, and how quickly can institutions respond? If a municipality bears immediate schooling or accommodation costs while other levels of government control admissions and funding, that is a fiscal-coordination problem. It cannot be solved merely by calling local concerns prejudice; nor by blaming migrants for budget decisions made elsewhere.

A useful administrative response would include transparent forecasts, flexible funding, housing delivery, accessible language support, labour standards and publication of local outcomes. People are entitled to ask how this will work. They are also entitled to an answer containing something more concrete than “send everyone away”.

## 🛠️ 5. Work, Wages and the Conveniently Invisible Employer

Employers may benefit from migrant labour, including in agriculture, care, hospitality, logistics and construction. Labour migration can fill vacancies and contribute to production; it can also expose workers to exploitation where status depends on a sponsor or where reporting abuses risks losing employment or residence. Both outcomes can exist in the same labour market.

The useful question is not simply whether migrants compete with existing workers. It is which employers can set pay and conditions, how bargaining power is distributed, whether labour protections are enforced, and what happens when a worker reports wage theft, unsafe conditions or trafficking. A system that punishes workers while leaving employers' incentives intact can displace rather than correct the underlying harm.

There is a particularly awkward political arithmetic here: demanding cheap food, low-cost care and rapid construction while opposing the labour conditions or workforce arrangements that make those prices possible. The bill still exists. It is merely being sent to somebody whose name does not appear in the speech.

Distinguish undocumented employment, visa overstays, employer sanctions, labour trafficking and criminal exploitation. They overlap in particular cases; they are not synonyms. Enforcement data should count investigated employers, substantiated violations, remedies and worker safety, not merely raids.

## 🧑‍⚖️ 6. Backlogs Are Not a Moral Theory

An immigration-court backlog is an operational condition: more pending cases than the system can resolve at its current throughput. It can result from high filings, changing enforcement priorities, insufficient judges and support staff, procedural complexity, adjournments, representation gaps and policy changes. It is not evidence that all pending applicants are dishonest or that everyone awaiting a hearing has a valid claim.

The Council on Foreign Relations reported that the asylum-case backlog exceeded 2.4 million at the end of fiscal year 2025. TRAC's court data and September 2026 analysis document the scale of pending cases and declining access to representation. These sources do not use identical populations or reporting periods; their figures must not be merged into a synthetic total.

Representation matters because immigration law is complicated, and the stakes can include removal to a country where someone fears serious harm. Representation is associated with case outcomes, although observational figures do not by themselves establish that lawyers cause every difference: case selection, location, nationality and judicial assignment also matter. A fair system requires enough adjudicators, interpreters, reliable notices, accessible evidence and meaningful review.

“Clear the backlog” is a goal. How it is cleared determines whether the goal is achieved lawfully. Denying hearings, misdirecting notices or making legal help inaccessible may change a dashboard before they improve a system.

## 🚧 7. Border Security Is Not the Same as Interior Raids

Border inspection, investigation of trafficking networks, visa adjudication, workplace enforcement, immigration-court proceedings and interior arrest are different operations with different legal and practical justifications. The existence of a legitimate border-management function does not validate every operation conducted by an immigration-enforcement agency.

This distinction is essential to the question “abolish ICE”. ICE was created in 2003 within the Department of Homeland Security. Its Enforcement and Removal Operations and Homeland Security Investigations components have different functions. Abolishing or restructuring an agency is not the same proposition as repealing immigration statutes, ending customs inspections, or eliminating investigations of exploitation. Conversely, merely renaming an agency does not remove harmful incentives, legal powers or operational practices.

A serious reform proposal therefore has to specify which powers would be repealed, which functions would move, what independent oversight would exist, how staff and contracts would be handled, and how safeguards would survive the transition. The administrative details are not an annoying appendix to politics. They are where people either acquire enforceable rights or lose them.

## 🧸 8. “Think of the Children” Requires Looking at the Children

Child protection is one of the strongest justifications advanced for immigration controls. It is also one of the easiest claims to test badly. The relevant outcomes are not merely the number of adults questioned or detained. They include whether children are safe, whether trafficking is identified, whether family links are preserved, whether medical care is available and whether a child can report abuse without fear.

The companion [🍎 Kids in Cages](./🍎_kids_in_cages.md) traces the Flores litigation, family detention, the 2018 separation policy, sponsor-screening failures and subsequent controversies about information-sharing between child-welfare and enforcement institutions. It distinguishes children who missed hearings, children whose addresses were incomplete, children who could not be located and children confirmed to have been trafficked. Those categories must not be collapsed.

The related idea of **protective displacement** describes a possible failure mode: genuine fear for vulnerable people is redirected towards a substituted threat, and coercion against that target is counted as protection even when the original harm has not diminished. The mechanism is a hypothesis to examine in specific policies, not a verdict on every trafficking investigation. Ask which children became safer, by what mechanism, and according to which independently verifiable measure.

## 🧯 9. Crime, Risk and the Temptation of Collective Suspicion

Governments have legitimate responsibilities to investigate offences and protect residents. An individual who commits a crime is accountable for that conduct under applicable law. But immigration status, nationality and ethnicity are not interchangeable with criminality. Claims about migrant crime must identify the population, location, offence, time period and comparison group; arrest rates and conviction rates measure different things.

There are real policy questions about serious offences, detention pending proceedings and removal following conviction. They should be addressed through individual evidence and legal standards, not collective suspicion. Otherwise a security system can begin using its own enforcement selections as proof of the threat it was built to find: more stops produce more recorded contacts, which are then used to justify more stops.

The same discipline applies in reverse. Acknowledging that many migrants do not commit crimes does not mean particular offences should be minimised. Victims deserve investigation and remedy regardless of the accused person's status. Public safety improves when institutions can distinguish actual threats from convenient categories.

## 🧱 10. Detention: What Problem Does It Solve?

Immigration detention is often defended as a way to verify identity, arrange removal or ensure appearance at proceedings. Each claimed purpose requires separate evidence. Some cases may present genuine flight, identity or safety concerns. That does not establish that large-scale detention is the only available response, or that longer detention reliably produces better compliance.

Detention carries costs: facilities, staffing, transport, healthcare, litigation, contractors and the consequences for people held. It can disrupt families, employment and access to counsel. It can also create a stock of infrastructure and contracts whose continued use becomes administratively convenient. The companion [🇺🇸 Carceral Profit Complex](./🇺🇸_carceral_profit_complex.md) examines those incentives without assuming that every expenditure is illicit or that revenue equals profit.

Alternatives differ. A case-management programme that helps someone attend hearings is not equivalent to an electronic ankle monitor. Community support, check-ins, legal orientation and surveillance should be evaluated separately for appearance rates, cost, privacy, family stability and fairness. Historical cost comparisons must be dated; figures from 2013 are not 2026 prices.

## ♻️ 11. The Cybernetic Problem: Measuring the Wrong Success

A system can become exceptionally good at doing what it counts while remaining poor at doing what it claims to value.

**Enforcement-centred loop:** public concern → pressure for visible action → arrests and detention → impressive operational totals → renewed funding and authority. This loop can continue even when the underlying problems—unsafe employment, unresolved asylum claims, family instability, trafficking or local service shortages—remain.

**Outcome-centred loop:** identified problem → proportionate intervention → independently observed results → correction when harm persists → transparent public account. Here the feedback concerns whether hearings occur, protection claims are resolved accurately, trafficking victims are safeguarded, employers comply with labour law, and communities have the services they need.

Neither loop requires assuming universal bad faith. Officials may sincerely want to do useful work while responding to incentives that reward throughput rather than quality. The distinction between *activity* and *outcome* is central. A removal total cannot tell us whether a legal process was fair. A detention total cannot tell us whether a child is safe. An arrest total cannot tell us whether exploitation has stopped.

CRAKEN's useful question is not “Did the machine move?” but “What did the machine change, for whom, and who was allowed to check?”

## 🧮 12. What Would a Credible Immigration Policy Have to Publish?

If immigration policy is to be debated as administration rather than theatre, its public reporting needs consistent definitions and disaggregated measures. At minimum, it should explain:

- **Admissions and pathways:** visa categories, asylum applications, lawful residence decisions and processing times, with definitions that prevent double counting.
- **Decisions and review:** grants, denials, withdrawals, appeal outcomes, reversals, notice failures and access to representation.
- **Enforcement:** arrests, detentions, releases, removals, length of custody, legal basis and independently investigated complaints.
- **Safeguarding:** verified trafficking cases, referrals, confirmed harms, family reunifications, child-placement outcomes and remedial action.
- **Labour:** employer inspections, substantiated exploitation, wage recovery, worker protection and prosecution outcomes.
- **Capacity:** local housing, schools, health provision, fiscal transfers and infrastructure delivery.
- **Cost:** public expenditure, contract terms, comparative programme outcomes and oversight findings.

Publication alone does not guarantee accountability. Definitions can change, data can omit important experiences, and privacy protections are necessary, especially for children and asylum applicants. Independent auditing and the ability to challenge decisions are as important as dashboards.

## 🛟 13. Reform Without Pretending the Trade-offs Vanish

There is no frictionless policy that maximises every objective. Faster decisions can put pressure on review; additional checks can lengthen waiting times; strict employer enforcement can protect workers but also drive abuse underground if reporting routes are unsafe; local accommodation needs can rise before funding catches up. A credible proposal admits these tensions and explains how they will be managed.

Possible areas for serious design include expanded adjudication capacity, legal orientation, accessible lawful pathways, employer accountability, timely sponsor vetting, independent detention monitoring, transparent intergovernmental funding and non-custodial case management. None should be treated as proven merely because it sounds benevolent. Each needs a legal basis, a budget, a delivery mechanism and outcome evaluation.

The argument for retaining strong immigration enforcement generally emphasises sovereignty, predictable rules, public confidence and compliance. The argument for reducing detention and coercive enforcement emphasises proportionality, due process, humanitarian protection, civil liberties and the documented costs of confinement. Those priorities are not resolved by claiming that the other side does not understand borders. They require evidence about what each instrument actually accomplishes.

## 🪞 14. When “But Immigration” Stops Being an Answer

Return to the opening interruption. Somebody describes an avoidable injury caused by an enforcement system. Somebody replies: *But immigration.*

The useful response is not to deny that governments must make immigration decisions. It is to ask what the particular decision was, who authorised it, which law applied, what alternatives existed, what harm occurred, and whether the intervention achieved its stated purpose. If the subject is a child, ask what happened to the child. If it is a worker, ask who controlled the workplace. If it is an asylum seeker, ask whether the claim received a meaningful assessment. If it is a town struggling to house people, ask where the resources and housing plan are.

Immigration is a difficult administrative and political subject. That is an argument for greater precision, not less. A policy cannot borrow legitimacy from the existence of a problem it has not shown itself capable of solving.

**Yes, immigration. Now tell us what the policy actually does.**

**Research boundaries:** This node does not supply a synthetic 2026 total for “illegal immigration”, a single causal estimate of migration's effect on wages or housing, or a universal crime-rate comparison. Such figures require a specified population, geography, period and method. Its discussion of institutional incentives is a framework for investigation, not proof of an individual official's motives.
