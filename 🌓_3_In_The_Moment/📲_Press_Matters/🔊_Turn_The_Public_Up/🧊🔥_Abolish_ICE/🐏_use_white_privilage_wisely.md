# 🐏 Use White Privilage Wisely

## 🛰️ Orientation

- Examine how racial privilege affects people's encounters with immigration enforcement, policing and public institutions.
- Investigate how people who experience relative protection can use their position to support others.
- Distinguish:
  - Racial privilege.
  - Citizenship privilege.
  - Class privilege.
  - Professional authority.
  - Institutional access.
  - Language fluency.
  - Legal knowledge.
  - Financial security.
  - Social visibility.
- Recognise that these advantages overlap but are not interchangeable.
- Examine the difference between solidarity, allyship, intervention and self-appointed leadership.
- Investigate the practical limits of individual action.
- Avoid treating whiteness as a guarantee of protection or non-whiteness as a uniform experience.
- Central question: How can people use the advantages available to them to reduce harm, strengthen accountability and support those facing greater institutional risk?

## 🪞 1. What Privilege Actually Means

- Define privilege as a relative advantage within a particular social or institutional context.
- Distinguish structural advantage from individual wealth, happiness, security or moral worth.
- Examine how racial privilege can influence:
  - Police suspicion.
  - Institutional credibility.
  - Access to legal assistance.
  - Employment.
  - Housing.
  - Public perceptions of belonging.
  - Treatment by administrative authorities.
- Investigate empirical evidence concerning racial disparities.
- Recognise that individuals can experience privilege in one context and discrimination in another.
- Examine how disability, poverty, gender and other circumstances complicate access to institutional protection.
- Distinguish privilege from immunity.
- Central question: Which advantages are actually available to a person in a given situation?

## 🛂 2. Why Immigration Enforcement Makes These Differences Visible

- Examine the relationship between immigration status and racialisation.
- Investigate how appearance, accent, language and perceived nationality affect suspicion.
- Distinguish:
  - Citizenship.
  - Lawful immigration status.
  - Undocumented status.
  - Racial or ethnic identity.
- Examine the consequences of treating these categories as interchangeable.
- Investigate documented cases of citizens or lawful residents being questioned or detained.
- Examine the role of identification documents.
- Investigate how institutional assumptions affect encounters.
- Recognise that immigration enforcement can affect families and communities with different legal statuses.
- Central question: How can perceived identity influence encounters with immigration authorities independently of someone's actual legal status?

## 🐏 3. The Difference Between Being Brave and Being Useful

- Examine the motivations behind public intervention.
- Distinguish:
  - Helping someone obtain assistance.
  - Documenting an incident.
  - Supporting legal representation.
  - Providing practical resources.
  - Public protest.
  - Direct confrontation.
- Investigate the risks associated with each action.
- Examine whether intervention is wanted by the affected person.
- Consider whether an action could escalate an encounter.
- Recognise that a bystander may not possess enough information to assess immediate risks.
- Examine the consequences of treating another person's emergency as an opportunity for personal political expression.
- Central question: Does the proposed action improve the affected person's position, or primarily express the bystander's own convictions?

## 📱 4. Witnessing and Documentation

- Examine the role of public observation in institutional accountability.
- Investigate applicable laws governing recording in public places.
- Distinguish recording from physically interfering with enforcement.
- Examine how documentation can support:
  - Legal challenges.
  - Independent investigations.
  - Journalism.
  - Complaints.
  - Public accountability.
- Investigate the importance of preserving original recordings.
- Examine timestamps, location information and contemporaneous notes.
- Avoid editing source material in ways that compromise its evidential value.
- Distinguish verified observations from interpretations.
- Consider the privacy and safety of people appearing in recordings.
- Do not automatically publish identifying information.
- Examine how footage might expose affected people or witnesses to additional risks.
- Central question: How can documentation preserve evidence without creating new vulnerabilities?

## ⚖️ 5. Know the Legal Boundaries

- Examine constitutional protections concerning speech, assembly and observation.
- Investigate the legal limits of interfering with federal officers.
- Distinguish lawful observation from obstruction.
- Examine the consequences of trespass, threats or physical interference.
- Investigate the difference between judicial and administrative immigration warrants.
- Recognise that legal rules vary according to location and circumstances.
- Avoid assuming that perceived racial privilege prevents arrest or prosecution.
- Examine how legal observers are trained.
- Investigate the value of obtaining qualified legal guidance before organised action.
- Central question: How can people exercise their rights effectively without increasing legal risks for themselves or others?

## 🛟 6. Practical Support Without Spectacle

- Examine practical needs arising from immigration detention.
- Investigate:
  - Access to qualified immigration lawyers.
  - Family communication.
  - Childcare.
  - Transportation.
  - Translation.
  - Food and essential supplies.
  - Employment disruption.
  - Medical needs.
  - Document retrieval.
- Examine existing community support networks.
- Investigate whether affected families have identified particular needs.
- Avoid assuming that unsolicited assistance is welcome.
- Examine confidentiality requirements.
- Recognise the importance of sustained support after public attention declines.
- Central question: What assistance is useful when there are no cameras present?

## 💰 7. Money, Time and Material Resources

- Examine how financial resources affect access to legal remedies.
- Investigate the costs associated with:
  - Immigration representation.
  - Detention-related travel.
  - Lost income.
  - Family support.
  - Document preparation.
- Examine established legal-aid and mutual-aid organisations.
- Investigate financial transparency and governance.
- Distinguish direct assistance from fundraising undertaken without consent.
- Examine the value of recurring support.
- Recognise that not everyone can safely donate money or volunteer.
- Investigate non-financial contributions.
- Central question: How can available resources be directed towards needs identified by affected people?

## 🏛️ 8. Institutional Access Is a Resource

- Examine how professional and social position affects access to decision-makers.
- Investigate the roles of:
  - Lawyers.
  - Doctors.
  - Teachers.
  - Journalists.
  - Academics.
  - Trade union representatives.
  - Local councillors.
  - Elected legislators.
  - Civil servants.
- Examine professional responsibilities and confidentiality.
- Investigate how institutional knowledge can support accountability.
- Examine lawful reporting and complaint mechanisms.
- Investigate public-records requests.
- Examine legislative correspondence.
- Distinguish legitimate advocacy from attempts to improperly influence proceedings.
- Central question: How can people use institutional access to make existing accountability mechanisms work?

## 🕎 9. Solidarity Across Identity Differences

- Examine the relationship between privilege and coalition-building.
- Investigate how people can cooperate without claiming identical experiences.
- Recognise differences in:
  - Historical memory.
  - Legal status.
  - Racialisation.
  - Religion.
  - Language.
  - Political beliefs.
  - Personal risk.
- Examine how Jewish, Palestinian, migrant and other communities may experience different forms of political targeting.
- Avoid treating communities as homogeneous political groups.
- Distinguish solidarity from speaking on behalf of others.
- Investigate the importance of listening to affected people without making them responsible for educating every potential supporter.
- Examine how disagreement can coexist with shared commitments to rights and due process.
- Central question: How can people support one another without erasing differences in experience or political position?

## 🪢 10. Don't Become the Centre of the Coalition

- Examine the relationship between visibility and leadership.
- Investigate whether privileged participants receive disproportionate media attention.
- Examine how organisational resources influence decision-making.
- Investigate whether affected people retain meaningful authority over campaigns concerning them.
- Recognise that public visibility may be safer for some participants than others.
- Examine whether people with greater protection can take on administrative work rather than public leadership.
- Investigate:
  - Research.
  - Transport.
  - Fundraising administration.
  - Legal coordination.
  - Accessibility support.
  - Recordkeeping.
  - Communications infrastructure.
- Avoid assuming that every affected person wants public representation.
- Central question: How can relative privilege increase a coalition's capacity without displacing the people whose rights are at stake?

## 🕸️ 11. The Cybernetics of Useful Solidarity

- Examine solidarity as a system of information, resources and feedback.
- Identify:
  - Who needs assistance.
  - Who can provide it.
  - What risks exist.
  - What information is reliable.
  - What actions are authorised.
  - How outcomes are assessed.
- Investigate how organisations identify changing needs.
- Examine whether participants can report problems safely.
- Investigate how mistakes are corrected.
- Examine whether resources reach their intended recipients.
- Distinguish visible activity from useful outcomes.
- Examine the dangers of centralising sensitive information.
- Investigate data minimisation and secure communication.
- Central question: How can solidarity networks remain responsive, accountable and safe for the people they serve?

## 🧊 12. The Risks of Unhelpful Intervention

- Examine how well-intentioned actions can increase harm.
- Investigate:
  - Escalating confrontations.
  - Publishing identifying information.
  - Spreading unverified claims.
  - Misrepresenting legal rights.
  - Interfering with professional legal work.
  - Speaking without authorisation.
  - Making promises that cannot be fulfilled.
  - Exposing confidential information.
- Examine the importance of recognising personal limits.
- Investigate whether people have the training required for particular tasks.
- Recognise that courage does not substitute for competence.
- Examine the importance of stepping aside when more qualified assistance becomes available.
- Central question: How can people recognise when their involvement is becoming counterproductive?

  - Religious sanctuary networks.

- Avoid romanticising historical solidarity.
- Investigate whether actions produced measurable institutional changes.
- Central question: What distinguishes effective historical solidarity from symbolic identification with a cause?

## 🗳️ 14. Political Participation and Accountability

- Examine lawful forms of democratic participation.
- Investigate:
  - Voting.
  - Public consultation.
  - Contacting elected representatives.
  - Attending public meetings.
  - Supporting independent journalism.
  - Participating in peaceful demonstrations.
  - Supporting litigation.
  - Monitoring public expenditure.
- Examine the relationship between political privilege and access to decision-makers.
- Investigate whether people with citizenship rights can advocate for those without equivalent political representation.

- Examine the value of sustained participation between elections.
- Central question: How can democratic rights be used to strengthen accountability for people who cannot exercise the same rights?

## 🪿 15. A Practical Decision Framework

### Before acting

- What is happening?
- What do I actually know?
- What am I assuming?
- Who is directly affected?
- What have they requested?
- What legal risks exist?
- What relevant skills or resources do I possess?
- Is someone better qualified already helping?

### During an intervention

- Am I making the situation safer?
- Am I remaining within lawful boundaries?
- Am I preserving useful information?
- Am I respecting confidentiality?
- Am I following the affected person's wishes where possible?
- Could my behaviour increase the risks faced by others?
- Do I need to withdraw or seek professional assistance?

### Afterwards

- Was the assistance useful?
- Did the affected person receive the support they needed?
- Was any information exposed unnecessarily?
- Does evidence need to be preserved?
- Are there outstanding practical needs?
- Is there an appropriate accountability mechanism?
- What should be done differently next time?
