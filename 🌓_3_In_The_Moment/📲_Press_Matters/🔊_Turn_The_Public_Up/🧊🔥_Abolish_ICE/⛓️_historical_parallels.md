# ⛓️ Historical Parallels
**First created:** 2026-10-09 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-15  
*How empires, slave economies, welfare institutions and security states organise confinement — and what that history asks of immigration enforcement now.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Before we start pointing at Nazi Germany, Stalin's Soviet Union or Franco's Spain, we need to talk about America. Not because Americans are uniquely wicked. Because the United States did not acquire its institutions in a historical vacuum, and its systems of imprisonment and immigration enforcement did not arrive neatly packaged from some foreign dictatorship.

The United States developed through European settler colonialism, the dispossession of Indigenous peoples, and a transatlantic economy in which millions of Africans were forcibly transported, enslaved and exploited. Those were not merely prejudices held by unfortunate people in old-fashioned hats. They were systems of law, property, finance, labour, surveillance, punishment and political membership. They helped decide who counted as a person with enforceable rights, who could move, who could own, who could vote, and whose body could be made available to somebody else's economic project.

European powers were deeply implicated: Portugal, Spain, Britain, France and the Netherlands among the principal imperial participants, with other European states, merchants and financial networks involved in different ways and to different extents. It is not historically sound to assign every European country an identical role. Nor does European responsibility absolve the later United States of its own decisions. Both things can be true. We inherited and built things together; then we made our own choices about what to keep.

This node also asks directly when colonial practices and specific atrocities warrant examination as genocide, while distinguishing historical scholarship from legal findings and criminal jurisdiction.

This node is not an argument that every prison is a slave plantation, that every immigration officer is a Nazi, or that every restrictive institution exists for the same reason. Those claims would obscure precisely the mechanisms we need to understand. It is an investigation into **historical inheritance, institutional adaptation and comparable techniques of power**. We will distinguish documented descent from resemblance, and both from deliberate borrowing.

Most importantly, this is future-facing work. A society can have changed enormously and still have more changing to do. That is not a contradiction. It is the job.

## 1. 🌊 Atlantic Slavery Was an Industrial and Political System

Enslavement has existed across many places and periods, with radically different legal, religious and economic arrangements. The racialised plantation slavery of the Atlantic world was not simply an interchangeable example of a timeless practice. It developed through expanding maritime commerce, colonial conquest, insurance, credit, commodity production, hereditary legal status and the systematic extraction of labour from enslaved Africans and their descendants.

Race was not an incidental description of the workforce. Colonial laws increasingly made ancestry and inherited status fundamental to the distribution of rights. Enslaved people could be treated as transferable property while simultaneously being punished as persons when the system required obedience. This was an architecture of contradiction sustained by force.

The trade's commercial organisation was transnational. European states and commercial interests were central, as were colonial elites and, in distinct and historically variable roles, African political and trading actors. Acknowledging the latter does not dissolve responsibility for the Atlantic plantation system or its racial legal orders. Nor should the language of industrialisation suggest that all plantations were mechanised factories: the point is the scale, integration, commodification and disciplined organisation of production and human trafficking.

This matters for American carcerality because the institutions that controlled enslaved people's movement and labour were not confined to what we would now recognise as prisons. Passes, patrols, private violence, courts, jails, property rules and commercial incentives operated together. The boundary between economic management and coercion was deliberately porous.

The central question was not only *what crime has this person committed?* It was also *what status has the law assigned this person, and what may someone else therefore do to them?*

## 2. 🪶 Indigenous Dispossession Was Another Foundational System of Confinement

American history cannot be reduced to slavery alone. Settler colonialism involved territorial seizure, warfare, treaty violations, forced removal, reservation policies, assimilation programmes and restrictions on Indigenous autonomy. These practices were not identical to chattel slavery, but they also organised space, movement, belonging and political authority through coercion.

The forced removal of Indigenous peoples, including the nineteenth-century removals associated with the Indian Removal Act of 1830, demonstrates the use of state power to reorganise where populations were permitted to live. Later reservation systems and federal administration imposed further constraints that varied across time and communities. Indigenous people were never simply passive subjects of these policies: resistance, legal action, cultural survival and sovereign political organisation are indispensable parts of this history.

Federal and federally supported Indian boarding schools belong here too. Children were separated from families and communities under assimilationist policies; institutional records reveal systems of discipline, labour and cultural suppression. The US Department of the Interior's Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative reports are an important documentary starting point, not a substitute for Indigenous testimony or community-led research.

When we examine contemporary immigration detention, the relevant parallel is not that every facility has the same purpose as a reservation or boarding school. It is that **administrative categories and geographic controls can become powerful instruments for governing populations with unequal practical access to challenge**.

## 3. ⛓️ Emancipation, the Punishment Exception and Convict Leasing

The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in 1865, abolished slavery and involuntary servitude in the United States **except as punishment for crime following due conviction**. That exception is in the constitutional text. It does not, by itself, explain every later prison practice, but it matters enormously to the history of penal labour.

After the Civil War, Black Codes and discriminatory enforcement practices subjected many freed Black Americans to arrest and punishment for conduct including alleged vagrancy and breaches of labour regulations. Southern states and private businesses used convict leasing and other penal labour arrangements to extract work from incarcerated people, disproportionately Black men and women. The Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture documents this relationship, including the history of Angola prison and plantation land.

There is no need to pretend emancipation accomplished nothing. It transformed the constitutional order. Nor is there any need to pretend the transformation was complete. A person could cease to be legally enslaved and still encounter laws, violence and economic arrangements designed to limit the practical meaning of freedom.

Here is the distinction we must preserve: **the American prison system has multiple historical lineages**. Early penitentiaries, religious reform movements, urban policing, industrial labour, racialised criminal law and twentieth-century penal expansion all contributed. But racial slavery, the post-emancipation criminalisation of Black life, and convict labour are demonstrable and consequential strands of that history. They cannot be edited out to make the present more comfortable.

## 4. 🗳️ Citizenship Is a Legal Status — and a Lived Relationship

The Fourteenth Amendment established birthright citizenship for people born or naturalised in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction, alongside equal protection and due process guarantees. The Fifteenth Amendment prohibited denying voting rights on account of race, colour or previous condition of servitude. These were monumental legal changes, achieved amid sustained struggle.

Yet Reconstruction was followed by disenfranchisement, racial terror, segregation and discriminatory administration. The distance between constitutional promises and lived experience became a central terrain of Black American political organising. Black scholars, journalists, litigants, civil-rights activists and ordinary families have documented this for generations. It does not require an outsider to discover it on their behalf.

W. E. B. Du Bois's account of the *public and psychological wage* of whiteness helps explain one mechanism: racial status could provide social and political advantages to white workers who remained materially exploited. That did not make their poverty imaginary, nor did it make their experiences equivalent to those of enslaved or segregated Black Americans. It helped maintain a hierarchy in which someone else's exclusion could be mistaken for one's own security.

A democracy is not only a set of declarations about the *demos*. It is also a collection of practices determining who can actually participate, obtain protection, contest decisions and move through society without disproportionate coercion. Formal equality and experienced equality are related, but not interchangeable.

## 5. 🏥 The Carceral System Does Not End at the Prison Gate

We need a wider vocabulary than *prison* without losing legal precision. Incarceration ordinarily means confinement in custody; **carcerality** is a broader analytical concept encompassing institutions and practices that restrict movement, autonomy or exit, sometimes under the names of care, protection, rehabilitation or administration.

Consider the historical poorhouse and workhouse; involuntary psychiatric confinement; large segregated disability institutions; eugenics-era sterilisation policies; Indigenous boarding schools; immigration detention; and residential settings in which people had little meaningful capacity to refuse or leave. These did not share a single purpose, and contemporary services must not be condemned by analogy alone. Some restrictions can be lawful and necessary to protect people. Others have been abusive, discriminatory or prolonged far beyond defensible purposes.

The point is to investigate *how the power operates*. Who decides that someone may not leave? Is the restriction individually justified? What independent review exists? Are there accessible appeals? Are people separated from family? Can they obtain legal advice? Does a nominally protective institution become a mechanism for silencing the person it claims to protect?

Eugenics provides a particularly uncomfortable historical example. Ideas about heredity, disability, reproduction, dependency and social worth influenced laws and institutions across several countries, including the United States and Britain. We should not assume that every modern rule was designed by a eugenicist. We should ask which structures survived, what purposes they now serve, and whether the people subject to them have real autonomy.

A building does not become ethical simply because its sign changes.

## 6. 💰 Follow the Money, Then Follow the Prosecutorial Decisions

The separate node `🇺🇸_carceral_profit_complex.md` will take the detailed economic work. Here, we need the connection clearly signposted. Penal labour, convict leasing, prison construction, detention contracting and the sale of goods and services to captive populations create different kinds of economic incentives. **Private prisons are only one part of a much wider carceral economy**; most people imprisoned in the United States are not held in privately operated prisons.

The second question is equally important: which offences receive investigative attention, prosecution, custodial sentences and public condemnation? Drug offences, public-order offences, immigration violations, financial crimes, corporate wrongdoing and sexual offences operate through different laws, agencies, evidence requirements and resource constraints. We cannot infer selective prosecution from a headline alone. But neither should we treat the resulting distribution of punishment as a natural phenomenon beyond scrutiny.

Contemporary controversy surrounding Jeffrey Epstein and the Department of Justice illustrates why these distinctions matter. Epstein's abuse of girls and the institutional decisions surrounding his prosecution are legitimate subjects of investigation. Documents naming other people are not, by themselves, proof of those people's crimes. Questions about disclosure, victim privacy, investigative integrity and prosecutorial discretion should be answered with records and legal analysis, not the automatic conversion of association into guilt.

We should ask whether people with wealth, influence or institutional connections encounter different routes through accountability — and test that question against evidence. The answer must not be decided in advance. This is exactly why a functioning justice system needs transparent procedures rather than a hierarchy of whose alleged wrongdoing is worth noticing.

## 7. 🌍 Europe Cannot Pretend It Was Merely Watching

The United States developed from European colonial societies, then became a distinct political entity. Both halves of that sentence matter. Britain, France, Spain, Portugal, the Netherlands and other European powers built imperial systems involving forced labour, racial hierarchies, land seizure, restrictive movement controls and detention. Their histories differ, and so do their contemporary constitutional arrangements.

British imperial governance provides several useful comparisons: pass and movement controls in colonial territories; emergency detention and counterinsurgency; the criminalisation of populations classified as troublesome; and the later treatment of people entitled to live in Britain, including the Windrush scandal. The British **Criminal Tribes Act of 1871**, enacted in colonial India, is a striking example of collective classification and surveillance through an inherited status label. Its precise legal history and subsequent repeal must be examined on their own terms, rather than treated as an exact ancestor of American immigration law.

South African apartheid similarly organised rights, residence and movement through racial classification and pass laws. The point of comparison is the operation of documentation and restricted mobility, not a claim that contemporary US immigration law and apartheid were designed for identical ends.

We might ask whether European empires would have continued longer without two world wars. That is a counterfactual, not a historical fact we can prove. Anti-colonial resistance, imperial economics, international law and political change also mattered. The more useful question is which practices states deliberately abandoned, which they were forced to abandon, and which were quietly adapted.

## 8. ⚖️ Genocide, Colonial Histories and the Problem of Naming What Happened

There is a word that cannot be left outside this discussion simply because it makes Western states uncomfortable: **genocide**. We have become accustomed to identifying genocidal persecution in other countries' histories. We must be equally willing to investigate what that word can mean when the historical subjects are the United States, Britain, or other European empires. The point is not to attach the strongest possible label to everything. It is to refuse to make national familiarity a defence against scrutiny.

The term was coined by Raphael Lemkin in 1944, before the United Nations adopted the **Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide** in 1948. Article II requires specified acts committed **with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such**. The acts include killing, serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately imposing conditions of life calculated to bring about physical destruction, measures intended to prevent births, and forcibly transferring children. The distinctive requirement of intent matters. So does the fact that the definition is **not confined to killing**.

A historian may use *genocide* as an analytical description of an earlier event; a legal scholar may test the available evidence against the later Convention definition; a court adjudicating criminal responsibility faces additional questions of jurisdiction, proof and the law applicable at the time. These are related but **not interchangeable** exercises. The Convention's adoption in 1948 does not mean that comparable acts were harmless or unnamed before then, nor does it automatically create retroactive criminal jurisdiction over every historical perpetrator.

### 🪶 Indigenous nations and settler colonialism

The histories of Indigenous peoples in North America include massacres, territorial dispossession, forced removals, the destruction of food sources, compulsory assimilation and the separation of children from their families and communities. Scholarship by Indigenous historians and other researchers examines whether **particular campaigns**, **government policies**, or the **wider settler-colonial process** should be understood as genocidal. Benjamin Madley's study of nineteenth-century California and Jeffrey Ostler's work on US expansion are especially useful here. Patrick Wolfe's influential argument about the *logic of elimination* helps analyse settler colonialism as a structure, but it is not itself a legal finding that every instance of colonial settlement satisfies Article II.

Forced Indigenous child transfer warrants special attention because the Genocide Convention expressly identifies forcible transfer of children as a potentially genocidal act **where the required intent is present**. The history of boarding schools therefore belongs in this inquiry. It does not follow that every school, official or policy can be assigned the same intention without examination of the record. We should attend to Indigenous testimony and historical scholarship, not just state archives written by the authorities who ran the institutions.

### ⛓️ Atlantic slavery and the question of genocidal acts

The transatlantic slave trade and plantation slavery involved mass kidnapping, forced migration, hereditary captivity, sexual violence, family destruction, coerced reproduction, physical brutality and enormous loss of life. Those are not in doubt as historical features of the system. The question of whether **the whole Atlantic system**, or **particular practices and episodes within it**, constituted genocide under the later Convention's terms is a distinct scholarly and legal argument.

One central issue is specific intent: an exploitative system organised to extract labour and reproduce an enslaved workforce could inflict catastrophic group harm without every participant sharing a demonstrable purpose to destroy a protected group *as such*. That does **not** preclude findings of genocidal acts in particular settings. Nor does it mean that atrocities falling outside the Convention definition cease to be atrocities. Enslavement and certain forms of trafficking, persecution and systematic violence can fall within other international criminal-law categories, including crimes against humanity, subject to their own elements and jurisdictional limits.

**We do not need to stretch one legal word until it covers every wrong. We do need to be willing to use it where the evidence supports it.**

### 🌍 The British and European empires are not exempt

The United States is not an isolated case. British, Portuguese, Spanish, French, Dutch and German colonial histories contain episodes that demand close examination for exterminatory violence, forced displacement, coerced labour, starvation policies, population destruction and other acts potentially relevant to genocide analysis. Their histories are not identical, and **an empire is not one single criminal incident**. Specific peoples, periods, campaigns, official intentions and consequences must be identified.

The German colonial war against the **Herero and Nama** in what is now Namibia (1904–1908) is a well-established example widely described in scholarship as genocide. Other imperial episodes have substantial literatures debating the application of that term, including the relation between structural colonial violence, mass mortality and evidence of intent. We should neither assume every imperial atrocity meets the Convention definition nor quietly exempt Britain or its neighbours from asking the question.

This is also why the Atlantic story cannot be reduced to a uniquely American moral failure. European imperial powers built and profited from many of the economic and legal arrangements from which American slavery and racial hierarchy developed. Later US institutions also bear responsibility for their own decisions. Shared origins are not an alibi for anyone.

### 🏛️ The ICC is not a time machine

The **International Criminal Court** operates under the Rome Statute, which entered into force on **1 July 2002**. Its jurisdiction is limited by rules governing time, territory, nationality and other conditions. The United States is **not a state party**; the United Kingdom and several other former European imperial powers **are**. Those facts do not mean that the ICC can try seventeenth-, eighteenth- or nineteenth-century colonial crimes. Generally, it cannot: the Court's temporal jurisdiction does not reach before the Statute entered into force, and other limits apply.

We must also separate **historical description**, **state responsibility**, and **individual criminal liability**. Recognising a historical episode as genocidal does not, by itself, prove the personal criminal responsibility of someone alive today. Nor should an argument about historical responsibility be mistaken for an allegation that present-day citizens inherit personal guilt.

It is tempting to say that US or British political discomfort with genocide allegations or international courts is *caused* by their colonial histories. That would be a claim about motive requiring evidence from particular actors. The stronger, testable question is whether governments apply principles of accountability consistently when those principles might illuminate conduct by their own states or allies.

### 🧭 What acknowledgement is for

We are not asking contemporary people to remain trapped in ancestral guilt. We are asking institutions to recognise what they inherited: land distributions, wealth, legal categories, borders, administrative habits, archives, and sometimes the very structures used to restrict freedom. Historical acknowledgement can inform restitution, education, institutional redesign, Indigenous sovereignty, reparative measures and prevention. It does not decide every remedy in advance.

**If the aim is a society less capable of repeating these harms, refusing to name the past is a peculiar place to begin.**

## 9. ⚫ Nazi Germany: Racialised Citizenship and Persecution

Nazi persecution of Jews was central to a racial and eliminationist worldview. Jews were portrayed as the source of mutually contradictory dangers, from capitalism to communism, and increasingly stripped of rights through propaganda, law, economic exclusion, registration, dispossession, deportation and ultimately genocide. The 1935 Nuremberg Laws are a crucial example of ancestry being made an administrative determinant of civic status.

Nazi persecution also targeted Roma and Sinti people, disabled people, political opponents, gay men and others, through different policies and rationales. We must not flatten those histories or treat all Nazi victims as having experienced identical forms of persecution.

The comparative question for this node is narrower than *is America Nazi Germany?* It is: how do governments transform social categories into legal vulnerabilities, and what happens when an identity or ancestry begins to substitute for individual conduct? The United States' own racial laws are relevant here; James Q. Whitman's research documents how Nazi jurists studied aspects of American race law. This is evidence of intellectual and legal comparison, not proof that all Nazi policy was copied from America.

The detailed chronology belongs in `🕯️_what_in_the_1933.md`. **1933 was not 1945**. Understanding escalation requires attention to what was done at each stage rather than projecting the endpoint backwards.

## 10. 🔴 Stalin's Soviet Union: Enemies, Denunciations and Administrative Terror

Stalinist repression developed through different ideological and institutional arrangements. Categories such as kulaks, wreckers, saboteurs, counter-revolutionaries and alleged foreign agents were used in campaigns of confiscation, deportation, imprisonment and execution. The Great Terror of 1937–38 involved mass operations, denunciations, coerced confessions and fabricated cases. The Soviet state also carried out collective deportations of nationalities and late-Stalin antisemitic campaigns, including the Doctors' Plot.

Here, the comparison is not that class categories and Nazi racial ancestry rules were the same. They were not. It is that **administrative identification of an enemy can become a self-reinforcing exercise**, especially where officials face incentives or pressure to produce suspects, admissions and convictions.

The Rosenberg prosecution and American anticommunism belong to a different legal context. Soviet espionage was real; so was Stalinist repression. Neither fact establishes that every American accused of ideological disloyalty was guilty or that every method of investigation was justified. That distinction is explored in `🧊_cold_war_history.md`.

## 11. 🇪🇸 Franco's Spain and the Uses of Political Captivity

Francoist Spain adds a European dictatorship whose political imprisonment, repression of opponents, military courts, forced labour arrangements and long aftermath cannot be explained through Nazi racial ideology or Soviet class politics alone. Political prisoners, defeated Republicans and people associated with the opposition experienced repression that changed over the regime's long duration.

This comparison lets us ask how a state converts victory, emergency or supposed national restoration into durable institutions of exclusion. It also helps prevent the false impression that the only useful European parallels are Germany and Russia. Histories of imprisonment, political purification and forced labour require attention to local law, religion, civil war, class and national identity.

Again, the analytical value lies in comparing mechanisms while retaining the differences in purpose, scale and outcome.

## 12. 🛰️ After 9/11: Security Suspicion and Immigration Administration

The attacks of 11 September 2001 produced major changes in American national-security institutions, surveillance, border administration and immigration enforcement. The Department of Homeland Security was established under legislation enacted in 2002, and Immigration and Customs Enforcement began operating in 2003. ICE did not invent immigration detention or deportation; it inherited and reorganised existing functions in a new security architecture.

Muslims and people perceived as Muslim faced intensified suspicion, hate crimes and discriminatory scrutiny. Sikh, Arab, South Asian and other communities were also affected, sometimes because outsiders conflated religion, ethnicity, nationality, appearance and political allegiance. A security category need not accurately describe a person to have consequences for them.

Programmes such as the National Security Entry-Exit Registration System (NSEERS), launched in 2002, illustrate how nationality-linked registration and reporting requirements could place disproportionate burdens on particular noncitizens. The legal and administrative histories of registration, watchlists, visa screening, detention and removal are not interchangeable. Their shared question is how exceptional security rationales alter routine encounters with the state.

It would be too simple to claim that post-9/11 policy *caused* every later development in transnational far-right politics. The alt-right and related movements drew on older white supremacist networks, internet subcultures, economic and political grievances, conspiracy communities and many other influences. But the post-9/11 period is important to understanding the normalisation of certain forms of suspicion and the institutional resources available to act upon it.

## 13. 🎭 Scapegoats, Moral Panics and the Spectacle of Punishment

During periods of hardship, fear or perceived disorder, political movements may seek a visible group or individual to blame. That is a recurrent possibility, not a universal law of history. People also respond to crisis with mutual aid, reform and solidarity. The question is which response institutions reward.

The Salem witch trials, McCarthy-era investigations, the Satanic Panic and QAnon are different episodes, but they illuminate how an alleged concealed threat can encourage increasingly expansive interpretations of ordinary behaviour. Some cases involved unsupported supernatural or conspiratorial claims; others arose amid real espionage, crime or abuse. **A real underlying offence does not validate every accusation made in its name.** Equally, the existence of false allegations does not erase real victims.

Mike Warnke's influential and later discredited stories of Satanic involvement are useful for tracing how dramatic testimony can acquire authority through religious and media circulation. Arthur Miller's *The Crucible* offers a cultural bridge between Salem and McCarthyism. These references are explored in the Cold War node; here they serve the broader investigation of suspicion becoming social permission to punish.

A *show trial* is not simply a trial that attracts cameras or public anger. It generally describes proceedings manipulated to produce a politically predetermined outcome. A publicised prosecution, execution announcement or punitive spectacle may be troubling for other reasons without meeting that definition. For any contemporary allegation of a proposed televised execution or unusual punishment, verify the individual, jurisdiction, legal status and exact official proposal before presenting it as fact.

Public anger deserves better than being pointed at whichever person has been selected to carry the weight of an entire system's failures.

## 14. 🧊 Why Immigration Enforcement Is the Point of Return

States have legitimate administrative reasons to know who enters, resides in and leaves their territory: planning services, adjudicating lawful status, providing asylum procedures, investigating offences and managing borders. Those purposes do not, by themselves, justify every detention, surveillance practice, raid or removal.

Immigration enforcement is particularly revealing because the state can exercise enormous power over people whose ability to challenge it may be limited by language, money, legal status, detention, fear or distance from family. Immigration detention is often civil rather than criminal custody. That distinction is legally important, but the person deprived of liberty still experiences confinement.

The comparisons in this node are not invitations to pronounce historical equivalence by vibe. They are a set of practical questions: Who is classified? On what evidence? Under what authority? For how long? Can they contest it? Are decisions individualised? What role do race, nationality, poverty and disability play? What happens when the state is wrong? Who is paid? Who is accountable?

And what happens when political messaging treats the number of arrests or detentions as proof of the scale of a threat, without asking whether enforcement priorities themselves produced that number?

That is the risk of **paranoia laundering**: an allegation justifies expanded scrutiny; expanded scrutiny generates more official records; the records are presented as independent confirmation of the allegation. The cycle can occur without a single mastermind. Ordinary institutional incentives may be enough. Whether it is occurring in any specific case must be established through evidence, not assumed.

## 15. 🕸️ The Mechanisms — Not a Competition in Historical Horror

A useful comparison keeps three categories separate:

**Documented inheritance:** An institution, statute, funding arrangement, property relationship or administrative practice can be traced historically from one system into another. Convict leasing's relationship to post-emancipation racial control is an example with substantial documentary support.

**Documented borrowing:** Officials consciously study or adapt practices from another jurisdiction. Research on Nazi legal interest in American race law belongs here, with care about exactly what was studied and adopted.

**Structural resemblance:** Two systems use similar mechanisms — registration, collective suspicion, restricted movement, coerced labour, denunciation — without evidence that one copied the other. This is analytically useful but is not proof of shared origins or equivalent outcomes.

We should also compare **differences**: criminal conviction versus civil detention; judicial sentence versus administrative decision; temporary restriction versus indefinite confinement; individual wrongdoing versus collective identity; democratic contestation versus dictatorship; legal rights on paper versus access to remedies in practice.

Without those distinctions, history becomes a collection of frightening costumes. With them, it becomes a tool for investigating institutions.

## 16. 🧭 What Progress Would Actually Look Like

This work is not a declaration that nothing has improved. Slavery was abolished. Constitutional rights expanded. Civil-rights movements changed law and public life. Institutions were dismantled, reformed and challenged. Those achievements were real, fought for and incomplete.

A future-facing approach asks whether institutions distribute liberty more fairly *in practice*, not only whether their founding documents sound enlightened. It means examining conditions of confinement; the necessity and duration of detention; independent oversight; accessible legal representation; disability accommodations; family separation; racial disparities; contracting incentives; and whether alternatives to detention achieve legitimate objectives with less harm.

It also means listening to the people who have been saying this for generations. Black American scholars and organisers, Indigenous communities, migrants, disabled people, survivors of institutional abuse and formerly incarcerated people are not illustrative footnotes to someone else's theory. Their evidence, analysis and demands are part of the intellectual foundation.

Reform and abolitionist approaches disagree about the ultimate role of prisons, detention and policing. That disagreement need not prevent rigorous assessment of specific practices or the reduction of preventable harm. We can investigate whether a restriction is necessary, proportionate, reviewable and effective without requiring everyone to settle the entire philosophy of punishment first.

The work will not be finished in a parliamentary term, a presidential administration or probably our lifetimes. That is not an excuse to postpone it. It is a reason to build methods, archives, safeguards and habits of correction that can outlast us.

## 17. 🪿 What This Node Is — and Is Not — Saying

We are not telling Americans that they are uniquely evil. Much of the machinery under examination came from European empires, and European states have their own unfinished work. Nor are we denying that the United States is now a distinct country with its own responsibilities, reforms, resistance movements and choices.

We are not saying poor white people in slaveholding societies suffered nothing. We are saying that hardship and racial privilege could coexist, and that an exploited person was not therefore subjected to the same regime as someone legally enslaved. The comparison matters precisely because the experiences were different.

We are not saying that everyone who supports immigration administration wants persecution. We are saying that powers to classify, confine and remove human beings demand evidence, limits and meaningful accountability — especially when political rhetoric encourages collective suspicion.

And we are certainly not saying that civilisation peaked two or three thousand years ago. The Greeks and Romans had their own hierarchies of citizenship, status and enslavement. The rather obvious ambition is to do better than that. We have had some time to think about it.

**The question is not whether we have changed. It is what we have changed, what we have kept, and what we are prepared to change next.**

### Genocide, colonial violence and international criminal law

- [United Nations: “Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide” (1948)](https://www.un.org/en/genocide-prevention/1948-convention) — treaty definition and the acts listed in Article II; essential for distinguishing atrocity from the specific legal elements of genocide.
- [International Criminal Court: “Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court” (1998)](https://www.icc-cpi.int/sites/default/files/2024-05/Rome-Statute-eng.pdf) — primary treaty text; see Articles 5–12 and 24 for subject-matter, temporal and other jurisdictional limits. **Check the Court's current official version if citing amendments.**

## ✨ Stardust

A constitution can change overnight. A law can be repealed in a sitting. An institution can change its name and print new stationery before lunch.

The distribution of freedom takes longer.

That is not an argument against progress. It is the reason to keep doing the work: to ask who can leave, who can challenge, who is believed, who is protected, and who is still expected to be grateful for rights that ought to belong to them already.

We do not need to pretend the past never happened to build something better than it.
