# 🧊 Cold War History

## 🛰️ Historical orientation

- The Cold War did not invent American immigration restriction.
- It inherited systems already shaped by racial exclusion, labour regulation, territorial expansion and political deportation.
- Cold War security concerns introduced or strengthened additional mechanisms of ideological screening.
- Investigate the relationship between immigration law and the American national-security state.
- Distinguish:
  - Genuine espionage and security threats.
  - Political affiliation.
  - Ideological disagreement.
  - Refugee status.
  - Immigration status.
  - Demonstrated criminal conduct.
- These categories could overlap but were not equivalent.
- Central question: How did immigration control become incorporated into Cold War national-security policy?

- Examine the emergence of the United States and Soviet Union as rival superpowers.
- 1947: Truman Doctrine.

- Investigate the creation of the CIA and the reorganisation of American intelligence institutions.
- 1948–1949: Berlin Blockade.
- 1949: Establishment of NATO.
- 1949: Soviet Union tested its first atomic bomb.
- 1949: Communist victory in the Chinese Civil War.
- 1950: Korean War began.
- Examine how these developments influenced domestic perceptions of communist infiltration.
- Investigate the relationship between external geopolitical rivalry and internal political suspicion.

## ⚖️ The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952

- Also known as the McCarran–Walter Act.
- Consolidated and revised existing immigration and nationality legislation.
- Enacted during the early Cold War.
- Retained the national-origins quota system established in the 1920s.
- Removed racial restrictions on eligibility for naturalisation.
- Did not establish equal immigration opportunities across national origins.
- Included provisions concerning political and security-related exclusion and deportation.
- Examine Senator Pat McCarran's anticommunist politics.
- Examine Representative Francis Walter's role.
- Investigate congressional debates and competing legislative objectives.
- 25 June 1952: President Harry Truman vetoed the legislation.
- Truman objected particularly to the discriminatory national-origins quota system.
- 27 June 1952: Congress overrode the veto.
- Distinguish legislative consolidation from the political objectives embedded within the legislation.
- Central question: Why did Congress retain discriminatory immigration-selection mechanisms while presenting the legislation partly as a national-security measure?

## 🧳 Refugees and Cold War foreign policy

- Examine American refugee policy following the Second World War.
- 1948: Displaced Persons Act.
- 1953: Refugee Relief Act.
- Examine refugee admissions from communist-controlled states.
- 1956: Hungarian uprising and subsequent refugee movements.
- 1959 onwards: Cuban Revolution and Cuban migration.
- Investigate how geopolitical relationships affected refugee classifications and admission.
- Examine the relationship between humanitarian protection and anticommunist foreign policy.
- Compare treatment of people fleeing communist governments with people fleeing US-aligned governments.
- Avoid assuming all differences resulted from ideology; examine the specific legal and political contexts.
- Central question: How did foreign-policy priorities influence which displaced populations received protection?

## 🧬 The 1965 immigration reforms

- 1965: Immigration and Nationality Act amendments.
- Abolished the national-origins quota system.
- Introduced a new immigration-preference framework.
- Examine family reunification and employment-based preferences.
- Investigate the civil-rights context of immigration reform.
- Examine how the reforms changed migration patterns.
- Identify restrictions and enforcement powers that remained in place.
- Distinguish changes to admission criteria from changes to deportation and detention powers.
- Central question: Which parts of the earlier immigration system were dismantled, and which survived?

## 🧊 After the Soviet Union

- 1989: Fall of the Berlin Wall.
- 1991: Dissolution of the Soviet Union.
- Examine which Cold War immigration-security provisions remained legally operative.
- Identify provisions subsequently repealed, amended or restricted by courts.
- Avoid assuming institutional continuity without tracing the relevant statutory authority.
- Investigate changes in the political justification for immigration enforcement.
- Examine the growing emphasis on crime, border security and unauthorised migration.
- 1990: Immigration Act.
- 1996: Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act.
- 1996: Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act.
- Investigate expansions of detention, removal and enforcement powers.
- Distinguish the legal changes of the 1990s from the institutional reorganisation following 9/11.
- Central question: Why did immigration enforcement expand during a period when the Cold War's principal geopolitical threat had disappeared?

## 🕸️ Institutional persistence

- Investigate how security institutions retain powers after their original justification changes.
- Distinguish:
  - Legislative authority.
  - Administrative organisation.
  - Operational practice.
  - Political justification.
  - Measurable security outcomes.
- Examine whether powers were reviewed when threat assessments changed.
- Examine whether institutions acquired new responsibilities without relinquishing older ones.
- Investigate how enforcement success was measured.
- Distinguish arrests, detention and removals from demonstrated reductions in security threats.
- Examine the relationship between intelligence quality and enforcement volume.
- Consider whether broad enforcement activity can divert resources from targeted investigations.
- Treat this as a research hypothesis requiring evidence, not an established finding.
