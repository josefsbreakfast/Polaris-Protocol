# ✏️ La presse géniale : vous n’avez jamais été Charlie
**First created:** 2026-10-21 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-21  
*An open challenge to journalists, editors and media owners: defend the freedom to draw, report, criticise and investigate — without turning students into convenient ideological targets.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

This is an address to the press, not a neutral dispatch from a committee. It asks journalists to remember why their independence matters: not merely to withstand threats against their own profession, but to investigate power rather than accept its preferred targets. The 2015 murders at *Charlie Hebdo*, the historical destruction of independent journalism under fascism, and contemporary disputes over press access make that obligation concrete.

The press has power. It can use that power to investigate institutions, defend colleagues and make itself impossible to ignore. Or it can spend another afternoon looking for the most inflammatory placard in a crowd of children. This piece has a preference.

---

## ✏️ La presse géniale : vous n’avez jamais été Charlie

La presse géniale: vous n’avez jamais été Charlie.

You were not “edgy”, you are not “defending la laïcité” by witchunting for marks of Islam in a crowd of students, and you are not “defending to the death” anyone’s right to say anything.

An ambush of town criers, paid to keep the richest, happy enough, so that they might decide not to kill us all.

...and your complaints are with the children of the revolution?

You could be investigating just how much the LLMs are dependant on unpaid human labour, with the absence of students, a move that would protect your own industry...

Instead the bosses have you out here, machine-gunning other workers.

If you were Charlie in 2015, please remember how easy Hebdo has been on Emmanuel.

## 🕯️ 1. What *Je suis Charlie* was supposed to mean

The January 2015 murders at *Charlie Hebdo* were an attack on people exercising the freedom to draw, publish and satirise. The attack was committed by jihadist gunmen claiming affiliation with al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula. The principle at stake was not that every cartoon was good, kind or above criticism. It was that a cartoonist, journalist or editor should not face murder for publishing a drawing.

That principle also protects the right to **criticise** cartoonists, newspapers, governments, religious institutions, political parties and the people who pay for media. Freedom of expression is not a special licence reserved for the editorial desk. It extends to students, protesters, Muslims, secularists, workers, cartoonists and those who disagree with all of them.

*Vous n’avez jamais été Charlie* is a question of consistency. Freedom of expression cannot be an occupational benefit that expires when the speaker is somebody else.

## 📰 2. Please develop a spine; it is occupational equipment

The request is not for journalists to endorse a demonstration. It is for them to do journalism.

When young people protest, ask what they are demanding, which institutions they are confronting, what their conditions are, and what happened on the ground. Where somebody alleges that a protest is an “Islamist intifada”, a “globalist” conspiracy or a threat to secularism, ask **who is making the allegation, what precisely it describes, and what evidence would distinguish a fringe example from the crowd as a whole**.

A single sign, slogan or clip may be genuine and still be unrepresentative. A headline can be factually composed of true fragments and misleading in its selection. A newspaper can publish an official account accurately and still fail to interrogate it.

Report antisemitism, Islamism, intimidation and violence where the evidence leads. Just do not use those words as a substitute for doing the reporting.

## 🧭 3. Do not let either the far right or jihadist violence set the terms

There are two traps here, and they are not identical.

First, some far-right narratives frame Muslim presence, international youth solidarity or ordinary cross-border political communication as inherently suspicious. The same footage can be made to carry a civilisational argument it does not substantiate. That claim should be examined on its merits, not adopted because it supplies an exciting headline.

Second, the legacy of the 2015 attack must not be converted into an argument for journalists to avoid contentious drawings, abandon scrutiny or police other people's speech out of fear. Terrorist violence must not acquire a retrospective veto over legitimate expression.

The press can criticise religion, investigate extremism and reject collective suspicion at the same time. Distinctions are the job.

**If defending cartoonists means anything, it cannot mean granting extremists — of any persuasion — control of the editorial frame.**

## 🔎 4. The suspiciously convenient clip

The British framing questions raised elsewhere in *Jeunesse En Feu* belong here as an editorial audit:

- Who first recorded and uploaded the footage? Can the original be recovered?
- Who selected it for circulation, and from how much material?
- What happened immediately before and after the excerpt?
- What did other demonstrators say or do, including people who disagreed?
- Is the claim about a specific individual, an organised group, or the entire movement?
- Did several outlets independently verify the story, or receive the same upstream selection?
- Did a subsequent correction reach an audience comparable to the original framing?

A coordinated influence operation is one possible explanation for patterned selection. So are editorial routines, commercial incentives, ideological priors, platform recommendations and a shared source. **Similarity is not proof of coordination; a manipulated information environment does not automatically imply malware or a foreign state.**

## 🧑‍🎓 5. The students are not your enemy's stock footage

Young people in different countries can recognise shared problems without receiving orders from a hidden international directorate. They can compare housing costs, school provision, insecure work, public services, policing and the prospects offered to them. Sometimes this becomes transnational solidarity. Sometimes it remains a conversation.

Calling that “globalist” may describe nothing more sinister than the internet functioning as a communications network. In some political contexts the word also carries antisemitic conspiratorial baggage. Neither conclusion should be assumed from the word alone; the speaker's meaning, evidence and framing matter.

**Sometimes it really is a group chat with receipts.**

## 💼 6. Your own industry has a labour problem, too

The challenge about large language models deserves an investigation. AI systems may benefit from journalism, public writing, community moderation, volunteer expertise and other human-generated material. The relevant legal and economic questions vary: copyright, licensing, consent, compensation, collective bargaining, substitution of paid work, and the distribution of value.

It would be a mistake to claim that the physical absence of students from social media directly interrupts RLHF. RLHF uses selected feedback and optimisation procedures; pretraining corpora, model evaluations, platform signals and journalism are related but distinct information channels.

The stronger question is: **whose unpaid or underpaid work supplies the informational commons, who captures the returns, and what happens when people redirect their attention into offline organising?**

Journalists and students may have overlapping interests in resisting the devaluation of human labour and maintaining independent sources of public knowledge. That does not erase differences between them. It gives editors a rather more consequential story to investigate than another hunt for a conveniently provocative placard.

## ⚖️ 7. Laïcité is not a visual-identification game

French *laïcité* concerns the relationship between public authority, freedom of conscience and religion; its legal application depends on context. It is not established by scanning protest footage for visible or presumed Muslim identity.

Report actual conduct and specific claims. Distinguish a student's faith, a political organisation's programme, a religious symbol, unlawful intimidation and advocacy of violence. These are not interchangeable categories.

The presence of Muslims at a protest does not make the protest Islamist. Equally, an expressly Islamist organisation's documented activity should not be concealed. **The point is to do the classification properly.**

## 🕰️ 7A. How the press helped make authoritarianism possible — and how authoritarianism then destroyed the press

**The useful historical question is how press freedom was eroded: which habits, incentives and accommodations made the next restriction easier?**

The early twentieth-century German press was not a single obedient institution. Weimar Germany had a varied and substantial newspaper culture, including fierce opposition to the Nazis. The distinction matters: **some publishers actively promoted the movement, some normalised its claims, some accommodated it for commercial or professional survival, and others resisted and were crushed**. None of those positions should be flattened into “the press supported Hitler”.

The Nazi Party also built its **own** media infrastructure. The *Völkischer Beobachter* promoted party ideology, while Julius Streicher's *Der Stürmer* disseminated vicious antisemitic propaganda. The latter was not merely provocative cartooning: it presented Jews as a conspiratorial threat and helped habituate readers to their exclusion and persecution. The Nazis exploited existing fears, resentment and political polarisation; their opponents were not imaginary, but the supposed unified conspiracy and emergency threat were manufactured or grossly distorted. [H01][H02][H03]

### 📅 A short timeline: from political press to controlled information environment

| Date | Event | Why the newsroom should care |
|---|---|---|
| **1920s–early 1930s** | Nazi newspapers, rallies and propaganda cultivated antisemitic and anti-democratic narratives amid real economic and political crises. | Genuine public distress can be used to sell a false explanation of who caused it. |
| **30 January 1933** | Hitler was appointed chancellor; Germany still had a diverse press and constitutional institutions. | Dictatorship was not yet inevitable or complete. Institutions still had choices. |
| **4 February 1933** | The government restricted press activity and authorised prohibitions on political meetings. | Attacks on reporting and assembly can precede the headline emergency. |
| **27–28 February 1933** | After the Reichstag fire, the Nazi-led government alleged an imminent Communist uprising and secured the Reichstag Fire Decree, suspending press freedom and other rights. | **Security panic → collective accusation → exceptional powers** can become an institutional ratchet. The fire was real; the alleged Communist insurrection was not. |
| **March 1933** | The regime established Goebbels's propaganda ministry; the Enabling Act of **23 March** enabled government by legislation without parliamentary approval. | Control of information and concentration of executive power reinforced one another. |
| **Spring–summer 1933** | Opposition papers and printing infrastructure were suppressed or seized; Jewish-owned and other independent publishers came under pressure. In July, Germany became a one-party state. | A hostile information environment can be produced through law, violence, ownership and economics—not merely false stories. |
| **4 October 1933** | The Editors Law imposed political and racial exclusions and required professional conformity. | Journalists' capacity to work became conditional on ideological compliance. |
| **1930s–1940s** | Daily instructions shaped newspaper content; some publishers self-censored or cooperated. By the war years, Nazi ownership and directives dominated the press. | Editorial independence can be lost incrementally, including where nominally private newspapers remain open. |
| **1945 and aftermath** | Allied authorities treated reconstruction of an independent press as part of rebuilding democratic life. | Free journalism is infrastructure, not a decorative constitutional slogan. |

This is a deliberately compressed timeline. It is **not** a claim that one newspaper caused the Third Reich, or that today's democratic states are on the same historical timetable. For the historical sequence, see the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum's research. [H01][H02][H04][H05]

### 🧩 The enabling mechanisms — not just the villains' speeches

1. **Scapegoat substitution:** a real problem (unemployment, institutional failure, social conflict) is redescribed as the fault of a stigmatised population.
2. **Manufactured representativeness:** selected crimes, images, quotations or incidents are presented as typical of an entire group. Authentic examples can still support a dishonest generalisation.
3. **Emergency laundering:** an alarming incident is presented as evidence of an organised existential threat, then used to justify exceptional powers before the underlying claim has been tested.
4. **Professional accommodation:** editors accept access, ownership pressure, censorship or career incentives as the price of remaining in the room.
5. **Suppression of corrective information:** independent newspapers, witnesses and political opponents lose the ability to contest the official narrative.
6. **Economic capture:** control of advertising, distribution, publishers, printing and ownership makes the editorial position easier to dictate without a public instruction.

**The causal claim must stay proportionate:** these are documented mechanisms in the Nazi case, and useful questions to test elsewhere. Similar rhetoric alone does not establish identical intentions, organisations, outcomes or culpability.

### ✏️ The cartoonist is not an optional extra

There is a historical distinction between **satire that scrutinises power** and propaganda that depicts a vulnerable population as subhuman or collectively dangerous. A democratic press must be free to publish uncomfortable criticism of religion, government, institutions and ideology. It must also be capable of criticising a cartoon's content without accepting violence against its maker.

The 2015 attack on *Charlie Hebdo* was a murderous attack on people working in a newsroom. Its perpetrators do not acquire editorial authority because their grievance was religiously expressed. Nor does an authoritarian political movement acquire that authority by claiming that protecting the public requires journalists to demonise an entire minority. **Neither a jihadist threat nor a far-right political campaign gets to appoint itself editor-in-chief.**

The histories and methods differ. The professional principle travels: **nobody gets to dictate the editorial line through intimidation.**

### 🛡️ An inoculation protocol for editors, reporters and publishers

Before running the apparently irresistible story, ask:

- **Who benefits from this frame?** Not just who benefits politically: who gains attention, access, revenue, protection or bargaining power?
- **What is the denominator?** If three clips are circulating, how many hours of footage, locations and demonstrators were available? How were these three selected?
- **What is the original event?** Identify uploader, timestamp, location, full sequence, witnesses and the strongest contrary evidence. An authentic clip can still be a misleading sample.
- **Are we converting identity into culpability?** Religious clothing, slogans, nationality, student status or association are not evidence of participation in an organised violent plot.
- **Are we repeating an emergency claim before testing it?** Separate specific threats, attributed official assertions, political rhetoric and verified findings.
- **Who has been excluded from the account?** Interview participants, affected minorities, independent specialists and people who can contradict the favoured frame.
- **Could the newsroom withstand this pressure if directed at itself?** Consider restrictions on assembly, sources, publication, professional licensing, data access and the right to criticise government.
- **Will we correct the distribution, not just the article?** A buried correction does not repair a viral headline or decontextualised clip.

**Report violence, antisemitism, Islamism, far-right organising and threats to public safety rigorously.** And stop treating a crowd of students as evidence for a conclusion selected before the investigation began.

And for the proprietors: the journalists who normalise attacks on other people's rights may discover that **press freedom was one of the rights they needed for themselves**. Historical Germany offers a particularly brutal demonstration of that occupational hazard.

## 📰 7B. You have more power than you think. Please use it brilliantly.

Press freedom is not merely an individual journalist's permission to enter a room. It is a **collective professional good**. When access can be restricted for one outlet because officials dislike its editorial decisions, every newsroom has an interest in challenging the precedent—even those that disagree with the excluded outlet's work.

The 2025 dispute over Associated Press access to White House events provides a concrete case study. Following AP's decision to retain the name *Gulf of Mexico* in its style guidance while acknowledging the US administration's preferred terminology, the White House restricted some AP access. Other news organisations and press-freedom groups objected to the implications for viewpoint-based access. The separate dispute over Pentagon access conditions raises the same practical question: can journalists collectively resist terms that compromise independent reporting? Solidarity is something to organise and demonstrate, not merely praise.

The principle is wider than either American dispute. Defending another outlet's access does not mean endorsing its editorial line. It means recognising that an authority allowed to punish unfavourable reporting can eventually punish yours. Journalists need not write identical stories, adopt a party programme or become a professional protest bloc to defend that boundary.

And solidarity is only half the job. **The other half is being too brilliant to ignore.**

Investigate the story an institution would rather not explain. Follow the money, the ownership structures, the procurement trail and the technical details. Find the original recording, not merely the most combustible repost. Understand enough about labour markets, AI training, policing and public services to spot a misleading answer. Publish the evidence that survives scrutiny. Correct what does not. Defend a colleague's right to ask questions even when you dislike their conclusions.

When one reporter is excluded, other reporters can keep asking the unanswered question. When one publication is intimidated, others can investigate the mechanism. None of this requires personal nastiness or performative confrontation. It requires competence, independence, professional generosity and the refusal to let access become payment for obedience.

**You do not have to be a dickhead to be dangerous to authoritarianism.**

You can be precise. You can be fair. You can be extraordinarily difficult to bullshit.

**And you can be too brilliant to ignore.**

## 🗞️ 8. A newsroom checklist that might actually help

Before publishing a sweeping account of a protest, an editor could ask:

1. Have we interviewed participants about their material demands, rather than merely interpreted their imagery?
2. Can we quantify how representative the selected footage is?
3. Have we separated eyewitness material, official statements, allegations and editorial analysis?
4. Have we investigated injuries to protesters, bystanders, journalists, medics and officers with comparable care?
5. Have we considered whether our preferred explanation flatters our existing assumptions?
6. Do we know whether the clips came from independent sources or a common intermediary?
7. Are commercial, political or organisational conflicts relevant to our reporting, and have we disclosed what is evidenced?
8. Can readers inspect the underlying material and correction history?
9. Are we protecting free expression consistently, including speech critical of our own institution?
10. Have we asked what would **disprove** our headline?

## 🔁 9. The cybernetic consequence of getting it wrong

Student grievance → assembly → selective recording → amplification → media interpretation → political response → altered policing and public trust → further grievance.

That loop is neither automatic nor always malign. But journalism is part of it. A newsroom cannot plausibly describe itself as a neutral spectator if its reporting changes the incentives and responses of institutions and audiences.

The corrective is not silence. It is **better sensors, transparent provenance, editorial courage, and an ability to update when the evidence changes**.

## 🧾 10. Research desk — claims worth following

The historical sources below support the Third Reich chronology. For the contemporary reporting, the useful next step is to compare dated British headlines and their original footage, trace who supplied the clips, and examine the wider sample from the demonstrations. The *Charlie Hebdo*–Macron question calls for a review of the cartoons themselves. The White House and Pentagon access disputes deserve a sourced chronology of what journalists did together and what changed.

For the AI and labour argument, investigate licensing, compensation, newsroom employment, training-data provenance and the distinction between RLHF and broader data collection. Keep the documents and corrections visible so readers can follow the work rather than simply take the paper's word for it.
