# 🥖 La France fait grève. Et alors?
**First created:** 2026-10-08 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-08  
*The French Republic, collective action, the long history of making the state listen, and the considerably longer process of making it do something.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

France is on strike again.

In Britain, this is sometimes delivered as news with the same astonishment normally reserved for discovering that the North East has experienced weather. France has gone on strike. Again. Somebody has blocked a road. Somebody else has made a placard. The trains are doing whatever French trains do when the French decide the trains should stop doing it.

There is, however, a fairly substantial political history underneath the joke.

A *grève* is a strike: the organised withdrawal of labour. A *manifestation* is a demonstration. A *blocage* is a blockade, which might interrupt access to a lycée without involving employees withdrawing their labour. An *occupation* involves taking and holding space. A *revendication* is a demand or claim. A *rapport de force* describes the balance of power — and the leverage through which people try to alter it.

These things are not synonyms. Nor is every protest lawful, representative, effective, or even particularly well organised. What they have in common is a question: **what can people do when the institutions empowered to make decisions do not respond adequately to the people living with their consequences?**

In autumn 2026, that question can begin with something less cinematic than a barricade: pupils reporting that their school lacks teachers, welfare staff and disability support. The Republic can issue a consultation. It cannot issue a school nurse by press release.

This node follows the history of French collective action before returning to those pupils. It is a historical and institutional explanation, not a claim that every French protest belongs to one uninterrupted revolution.

---

## 1. 🥖 The French Are At It Again

The familiar British joke depends on treating protest as a national temperament. The French, apparently, are born knowing how to strike; the British are born knowing how to complain about the French striking. Neither is a serious account of political institutions.

Collective action is a **method**. Workers can withhold labour. Students can organise assemblies, petitions, walkouts or blockades. Residents can demonstrate, refuse a charge, occupy a square or pressure elected representatives. These tactics have different legal positions, risks and consequences. They also reach different decision-makers.

The first question should therefore be not *Why are the French like this?* but **who is acting, what do they want, who has the power to provide it, and what happens when they refuse?**

| French term | Contextual English | Why the distinction matters |
|---|---|---|
| *Grève* | Strike | Normally involves workers withdrawing labour; it has a specific legal history. |
| *Manifestation* | Demonstration | A collective public expression, not necessarily a strike. |
| *Blocage* | Blockade | Interrupts access or activity; its legality depends on circumstances. |
| *Occupation* | Occupation | Participants hold a workplace, campus or public space. |
| *Revendications* | Demands | The claims by which participants want a response measured. |
| *Rapport de force* | Balance of power / leverage | The capacity to make inaction politically or materially costly. |
| *Concertation* | Structured consultation | Not automatically bargaining, agreement or implementation. |

It is worth keeping that glossary nearby. Otherwise a pupil outside a school gate, a train driver refusing a shift and an angry crowd in a square all become the same cartoon Frenchman with a baguette. This is a considerable loss of information. It is also unfair to the baguette.

## 2. ⚖️ Rights, Rules and the Republic

French political life has long combined an expansive language of citizenship with a state that regulates the forms through which citizens exercise it.

Article 11 of the **1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen** protects the free communication of ideas and opinions, subject to legal limits on abuses. The **1946 Constitution's Preamble**, incorporated into France's constitutional framework, recognises the right to strike *within the framework of laws governing it*. These protections do not mean every blockade or occupation is legally authorised. They mean that the state cannot treat organised dissent as illegitimate simply because it is inconvenient.

Demonstrations on public roads are governed by notification and public-order rules; restrictions and policing decisions remain subject to legal scrutiny. The position of a pupil at school differs from that of an employee in a workplace. The existence of a political grievance does not erase duties concerning safety, access or other people's rights. Equally, the existence of public-order powers does not make any particular use of force proportionate.

The useful distinction is between **a right to participate in political life** and **a right to compel a particular political outcome**. A democratic government can reject a demand. It must still govern through lawful institutions, and it can be challenged for the choices it makes.

For detailed incident analysis and the standards governing police conduct, see `🚨_la_police_face_a_la_jeunesse.md`. This node has enough French history to get through without pretending to be a police disciplinary tribunal as well.

## 3. 🏛️ The People Have Entered the Chat

The French Revolution did not invent political grievance. It radically altered the language in which authority could be justified.

In 1789, fiscal crisis, unequal political representation, food prices and the legitimacy of monarchy converged. The Estates-General became the site of a dispute over representation; the National Assembly claimed political authority; the fall of the Bastille became a revolutionary symbol. None of this happened in a neat sequence of enlightened gentlemen agreeing upon universal liberty.

In October, **women marched from Paris to Versailles**, driven in part by bread shortages and prices, and compelled the royal family to return to Paris. Their intervention was political, material and organised. Women were central to the revolutionary process even while the emerging order denied them equal political rights. If the history is told as men inventing democracy while women worried about shopping, the history has gone badly wrong.

The enduring tension is this: representative institutions claim authority in the name of the nation, but people do not cease to exist politically once representatives have been elected. They can continue to challenge the institutions that claim to speak for them.

That does **not** make whichever crowd occupies the largest square the sole legitimate embodiment of the people. It means representative government and collective mobilisation remain in tension. France's history makes that tension especially visible.

## 4. 🔥 1871: When the Argument Is About Who Governs

The **Paris Commune** emerged in the aftermath of the Franco-Prussian War and the siege of Paris. From March to May 1871, Parisian insurgents established an alternative municipal government in conflict with the national government based at Versailles. The Commune adopted social and administrative measures, and its supporters included organised working-class women. **Élisabeth Dmitrieff** and **Nathalie Lemel**, associated with the Union des femmes, belong in this history as political organisers, not decorative supporting characters.

The Commune was crushed during the *Semaine sanglante* — Bloody Week — with mass killing and repression. Historical estimates of deaths vary and should not be collapsed into a falsely exact figure.

This is not an example of an ordinary industrial strike obtaining better conditions. It is a struggle over **where governmental authority resides**. Its place in French political memory helps explain why collective action can be understood in terms larger than a dispute about wages or timetables.

But the comparison has limits. A pupil asking for a qualified teacher in 2026 is not thereby founding a rival government. One can understand a historical tradition without dressing every subsequent disagreement in its most dramatic costume.

## 5. 🛠️ Organising the Withdrawal of Labour

The revolutionary period also produced restrictions on workers' collective organisation. The **Le Chapelier Law of 1791** prohibited professional corporations and collective combinations. In other words, the Revolution's account of individual liberty was not automatically friendly to workers organising together.

Legal change followed unevenly. The **Ollivier Law of 1864** removed the general criminal prohibition on concerted work stoppages, while leaving significant constraints. The **Waldeck-Rousseau Law of 1884** recognised trade unions. Twentieth-century constitutional protection then gave the right to strike a different legal footing.

Why does any of this matter? Because a grievance is not the same thing as **leverage**. One person complaining about working conditions can be ignored. Workers who can collectively stop production or essential services can impose a cost on refusal. A union can formulate demands, coordinate members and bargain with an employer or government.

None of this guarantees a good outcome. Organisations have leaders, disagreements, membership boundaries and competing interests. And a student assembly is not automatically a union with a bargaining mandate. Still, organised action changes the informational and political environment: the complaint is no longer a private inconvenience. It has acquired a collective form.

## 6. 🌹 1936: From Occupied Workplaces to Paid Holidays

The **Popular Front** victory in 1936 coincided with a huge wave of strikes and workplace occupations. The **Matignon Agreements** between employers and unions addressed wages and collective bargaining; legislation enacted under Léon Blum's government established, among other reforms, **paid annual leave** and the **forty-hour working week**.

The sequence matters:

**Election → mobilisation → negotiation → agreement → legislation → implementation.**

These are not interchangeable events. An agreement negotiated with employers is not identical to a statute passed by parliament. A statute is not identical to the conditions experienced by every worker. And the reforms had gendered effects within a labour market where women's paid employment and unpaid domestic labour were treated differently.

The lesson is not that striking magically produces paid holidays. It is that organised collective pressure can alter the negotiating environment in which governments and employers make decisions. Then somebody has to write the law. Then somebody has to make it work.

France has spent quite a lot of its history working through that sequence. The forms remain an important part of the ceremony.

## 7. 🎓 May 1968: The Students and the Workers Are Not the Same Organisation

May 1968 began with student unrest and confrontations over university life, authority and policing. It expanded into mass strikes involving millions of workers, workplace occupations and a profound political crisis. Negotiations at **Grenelle** produced proposals on wages and labour conditions, although their reception by workers and the movement's longer-term outcomes were complicated.

The temptation is to tell this as one unified revolution with one collective demand. It was not. Students, workers, trade unions and political groups had overlapping but distinct priorities. A movement can be historically connected without possessing a single command structure or programme.

The mythology also has a gender problem. Women participated as students, workers and organisers, but later images of May 1968 often privilege male speakers and spectacular confrontation. Political labour includes the work of maintaining organisations and sustaining daily life, not merely being photographed standing on something.

For 2026, this distinction is essential. A pupil blockade, a teacher's industrial complaint, a university union's declaration and a firefighter's strike notice do not become one organisation because they happen during the same month.

**Solidarity is not identical to joint leadership.**

## 8. 🚆 1995: The Cost of Saying No

The French public-sector and transport strikes of **1995** challenged elements of Prime Minister Alain Juppé's reform programme, especially measures affecting public-sector pensions and social security. Transport disruption was extensive. The government withdrew its plans for public-sector pension changes, while other parts of its reform agenda continued.

This is a useful corrective to the fairy tale in which every strike either wins everything or achieves nothing.

Disruption can increase the political cost of pursuing a policy. It also creates costs for passengers, workers and people who cannot rearrange their lives around a shutdown. Public support cannot be inferred simply from the number of people inconvenienced; nor can the inconvenience itself settle whether the demands are justified.

The historical question is more exacting: **which measure was withdrawn, which remained, and what happened afterwards?**

## 9. 🏙️ 2005: When There Is No Negotiating Table

The unrest that spread through French urban areas in **2005** followed the deaths of **Zyed Benna and Bouna Traoré** in Clichy-sous-Bois. Their deaths and the subsequent response unfolded against longer histories of inequality, racialised exclusion, relations with police and the geography of the banlieues.

The events cannot be reduced either to an industrial dispute or to an account in which young people were simply behaving badly. Nor should property damage and harm be romanticised away. The analytical difference is that diffuse unrest does not necessarily have a recognised negotiating committee, a single list of demands or an authorised spokesperson.

That affects the state's ability — and willingness — to translate events into policy. It also affects how journalists narrate the unrest: the spectacle of disorder can become more visible than the conditions that preceded it.

The dedicated nodes `⚖️_histoire_de_la_police_francaise.md` and `🎬_la_haine_na_jamais_disparu.md` carry the deeper historical and cultural analysis. A film is not evidence of a contemporary incident, even when it remains painfully useful for thinking about the society in which incidents occur.

## 10. 🦺 The Yellow Vests and the Spokesperson Problem

The **Gilets jaunes** movement began in 2018 around fuel taxes and purchasing power, then expanded into broader demands concerning economic inequality, political representation and democratic participation. Roundabouts, roads and decentralised online networks became organising spaces.

The movement's diffuse structure was both a strength and a difficulty. It enabled people who did not belong to established parties or unions to mobilise. It also made it harder to answer a question governments often find convenient: **Who speaks for all of you?**

The government suspended and then abandoned the planned fuel-tax increase and introduced further measures, alongside the *Grand débat national*. But a consultation is not a receipt proving every demand was addressed. Nor can the outcomes of a heterogeneous movement be reduced to one tidy success or failure.

This is an enduring political problem. Institutions prefer an interlocutor. People do not always organise themselves into the kind of interlocutor institutions would prefer to receive.

## 11. 🧓 2023: A Huge Movement Does Not Possess a Parliamentary Veto

In **2023**, French unions organised repeated mass mobilisations against pension reforms raising the statutory retirement age from 62 to 64. The government used constitutional procedures including **Article 49.3** to secure passage of the legislation without a final vote on the bill in the National Assembly. The Constitutional Council upheld central parts of the reform, which proceeded to implementation.

This is another reminder that **size, popularity, legality and success are different variables**. A movement may mobilise millions and still fail to stop the policy it opposes. That does not make the mobilisation imaginary or politically irrelevant. It does mean that an account of its outcome must include the law that actually took effect.

When somebody says a protest 'worked', ask what they mean. Was a proposal withdrawn? Was a minister forced to negotiate? Did an institution change its language? Did a public service improve? Those are different achievements.

## 12. ♀️ The People Are Not All Men on Barricades

Across this history, women are often easiest to find when they appear in an iconic painting or photograph. The more ordinary work of political organisation is less obliging to the archive.

Women marched on Versailles. Women organised in the Commune. Women worked and mobilised in the Popular Front era and in 1968. Women are present in public-service unions, community campaigns and student organising. Their participation does not become less political because they are not the person a television crew selects to explain the situation.

Nor does everyone possess the same practical capacity to protest. Disability, caring responsibilities, wages, transport, age, immigration status and exposure to policing shape who can turn up and what risks they take. A pupil who cannot afford to miss another lesson may have an exceptionally strong grievance against their school. Their absence from a blockade does not refute it.

If the evidence disproportionately records the loudest participants, we should treat that as a problem of **visibility**, not a census of whose anger counts.

## 13. 🤝 How a Grievance Becomes Collective Power

A useful model of organised mobilisation is:

> **Experienced problem → shared account → collective organisation → chosen tactic → recognised interlocutor → institutional response → decision → implementation → experienced change.**

Each arrow can fail. People may disagree about the problem. An organisation may misrepresent participants. A government may agree to discuss an issue while refusing the substantive demand. Money may be allocated without a post being filled. A post may be filled without the promised service reaching a particular pupil.

| Episode | Form of mobilisation | Who could respond? | What the outcome illustrates |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1936 | Strikes and workplace occupations | Employers and government | Negotiation, legislation and implementation are separate steps. |
| 1968 | Student mobilisation and mass strikes | Universities, employers and state | Allied groups need not share one programme. |
| 1995 | Public-sector and transport strikes | National government | Some measures can be withdrawn while others remain. |
| 2005 | Diffuse urban unrest | Police, government, local institutions | A grievance may lack a single recognised negotiating delegation. |
| 2018–19 | Decentralised Yellow Vest mobilisation | National government | Consultation and concessions do not resolve representation. |
| 2023 | Mass inter-union pension mobilisation | Government and parliament | Mobilisation does not confer a legislative veto. |
| 2026 | Reported lycée blockades and wider student action | School authorities, rectorats, ministries | Promised dialogue must be tested against actual provision. |

This is the point where history meets cybernetics: collective action changes which signals a governing system can ignore, and at what cost. It does not guarantee that the system will interpret those signals accurately.

## 14. 🏫 September 2026: Start with the School

The research assembled for this constellation identifies **Lycée Saint-Exupéry in Créteil** as an important early case. Contemporary accounts report staff mobilisation around **17 September**, pupil blockade activity from **18 September**, and a local statement dated **20 September** listing shortages involving teaching, school leadership, nursing, social work, vocational support and *AESH* — assistants who support pupils with disabilities.

The dates and precise staffing counts in our Stage One register are **working reported claims**; the relevant school and rectorat records have not been independently reconciled here. That qualification matters because a missing authorised post, an absent employee, a delayed appointment and a pupil receiving no support are related but not identical facts.

What is not complicated is why pupils might care. A timetable without a teacher is not an abstract governance problem. A school without sufficient disability support changes whether a pupil can access education. Missing health and welfare provision affects the people who need it, not merely the institution's staffing spreadsheet.

Our research register also records reporting of a **separate serious incident on 23 September**, involving fireworks and injuries. The reported incident belongs in the chronology, but it must not be indiscriminately attributed to everyone involved in the original blockade. Distinguishing an event from an allegation of responsibility is basic historical hygiene.

By early October, contemporary reporting described lessons resuming and some recruitment action, while identifying continuing concerns about disability support and school maintenance. **The available register does not establish that every original deficiency had been resolved.**

Here, then, is the important change of scale. After two centuries of revolutionary symbolism, we are back to whether someone can attend French on Tuesday morning.

## 15. 🎓 Ten Demands, Five Workstreams

The Stage Two corpus records a **ten-point programme attributed to L'Union Étudiante**, covering educational facilities, staffing and welfare support, timetables, access to higher education, pupil democracy, financial assistance, affordable meals, student fees, housing and policing. It is an organised student-union programme, not evidence that every pupil in every blockade personally endorsed every point.

The Stage One audit also records a government letter dated **4 October 2026**, setting out five areas for consultation: teacher replacement, school buildings, timetables, transparency around Parcoursup and pupil democracy. These are **reported source summaries** carried forward from the research files; original-document comparison remains a publication verification task.

| Student-side issue in the collected programme | Reported government workstream | What still needs to be tested |
|---|---|---|
| Staff shortages and pupil support | Teacher replacement | Replacement arrangements versus sustained recruitment and support services. |
| Buildings and conditions | School buildings | Timetabled repairs, financing and completed work. |
| Timetables and school day | Timetables | Whether pupils' requested changes survive the consultation. |
| Selection and higher-education access | Parcoursup transparency | Transparency is not the same policy as ending selection. |
| Representation and participation | Pupil democracy | Which pupils participate, and whether they influence decisions. |
| Grants, meals, housing, fees | No direct equivalent in the five headings | Whether other ministries or processes address these demands. |
| Policing and protest rights | No direct equivalent in the five headings | Whether the issue is acknowledged elsewhere and by whom. |

**Five headings do not equal five accepted demands.** An institution can receive a demand, translate it into a more administratively convenient question, and then announce that the question has been discussed. The translation is itself a political decision.

The companion node `🎓_ce_que_reclame_la_jeunesse.md` should hold the line-by-line documentary comparison, with exact French wording, source dates and any subsequent official decisions.

## 16. 🗳️ You Have the Floor. Who Has the Decision?

French offers a useful sequence of verbs:

*Écouter* — listen.  
*Entendre* — hear, or take account of.  
*Consulter* — consult.  
*Concerter* — confer or conduct structured dialogue.  
*Négocier* — negotiate.  
*Décider* — decide.  
*Mettre en œuvre* — implement.

These are not the same operation.

The Stage One research notes record a national lycée consultation beginning around **5 October**, with a proposed closing date of **23 October**, and parliamentary scrutiny on **7 October**. As of this node's date, future consultation deadlines and proposed later decisions are **commitments**, not completed outcomes.

The government can legitimately seek more information. The pupils can legitimately ask why information already provided through school complaints, staff reports and demonstrations was insufficient. Both questions deserve an answer. What would be misleading is to treat the opening of a consultation as proof that the underlying problem has been fixed.

A further difficulty is representation. Which pupils are invited? Which can participate? How are their responses categorised? Who decides that a complaint about missing support staff is actually a complaint about timetabling? Can pupils inspect the official summary and contest it?

In an information system, the person receiving the message has considerable power over what the message is said to mean.

## 17. 💰 Follow the Money. Then Follow the People

The Stage One audit records competing descriptions of the **proposed 2027 education budget**: a headline increase of approximately **€1.2 billion** on one reported perimeter; net staffing growth that includes trainee teachers; and a proposed reduction in established teaching posts. It also records disagreement between reports over a headline staffing total and budget presentation. **The official programme tables were not fully reconciled in the underlying research.** These figures must therefore not be presented as a settled, like-for-like accounting table.

The central mechanism is sound without pretending to settle that accounting dispute.

A national budget can rise while particular schools remain short of staff. An authorised position is not a recruited employee. A trainee teacher is not automatically interchangeable with an experienced permanent teacher. A nurse on a ministerial staffing chart is not necessarily a nurse available at Saint-Exupéry. An announced building allocation is not a repaired ceiling.

To evaluate the government response, we need to follow the chain:

**Proposal → enacted appropriation → allocated posts and funds → recruitment or procurement → service delivered → pupil experience.**

The companion node `💰_des_livres_pas_des_bombes.md` handles the detailed fiscal comparison, including the dangers of mixing nominal and inflation-adjusted figures, different accounting perimeters and defence-versus-education spending without equivalent measures.

A country cannot solve a school staffing problem by moving a number around a press release until it looks like a teacher.

## 18. 🚨 Public Order Is Not a Moral Shortcut

Demonstrations can cause real disruption. Blockades can interfere with access to education. Some incidents can involve violence or serious risk. These facts deserve attention, including where minors or bystanders are affected.

But the state also has duties. Police action requires a lawful basis, and its necessity and proportionality can be challenged. A reported use of tear gas does not, by itself, prove unlawful force or intentional targeting. Equally, describing an operation as *maintaining order* does not independently establish that every action within it was justified.

For any disputed incident, preserve the sequence:

**What was reported → by whom → what independent material exists → what authorities said → whether an investigation occurred → what was found.**

That standard protects the people alleging harm from casual dismissal, and it protects the historical record from converting suspicion into a finding. The dedicated policing and mayoral-incident nodes have the space to do this properly.

The question for this historical node is narrower: when a state responds to political action as a public-order problem, what happens to the grievance that produced the action in the first place?

## 19. ♻️ Protest as Feedback, and Feedback as a Political Choice

Institutions operate through partial models of the world. Budgets, staffing returns, inspections, school councils, complaints, parliamentary questions and ministerial briefings all produce information. None gives a complete account of what it feels like to be a pupil waiting for a class that cannot take place.

Collective action can make a neglected condition harder to ignore. It can increase the volume of a signal, connect isolated experiences and change the cost of administrative inaction. But louder signals are not necessarily more representative, and the state's ability to hear them is not the same thing as its willingness or capacity to act.

Consider the stages of a response:

| Stage | Observable evidence | What it does **not** prove |
|---|---|---|
| Acknowledgement | Minister recognises a complaint | The complaint is accepted as accurate in full. |
| Consultation | Pupils are invited to contribute | Their priorities control the agenda. |
| Negotiation | Parties discuss possible changes | Agreement has been reached. |
| Commitment | Government announces a plan | Funding or legal authority is secured. |
| Authorisation | Budget or staffing decision is approved | The person or service is in place. |
| Implementation | Staff hired; repairs completed | Every affected pupil can use the provision. |
| Experienced outcome | Pupils and staff can verify improvement | All other grievances have disappeared. |

This is the connection to **Embodied Information Ecology**. A vacant post is an administrative description. The absence of support is an experience occurring in a body, a classroom and a life. An institution may mark the vacancy *actioned* while the pupil is still waiting.

The feedback loop is not closed because a form has been completed. It is closed — or at least improved — when the affected people can test whether the condition has changed.

There is also a second loop: the public should be able to inspect the account of what government heard, compare it with the original demands and ask where the meaning changed. Without that, consultation risks becoming a machine for transforming political pressure into a beautifully formatted document about political pressure.

## 20. 🥖 Et Alors?

So. France is on strike again.

Sometimes collective action has helped secure major reforms. Sometimes it has forced negotiation or partial retreat. Sometimes enormous movements have failed to prevent a law taking effect. Sometimes the institutions receiving the grievance have preferred to discuss the form of the protest rather than the conditions that produced it.

None of those outcomes can be explained by declaring that French people simply enjoy disruption. Nor does the history justify every tactic carried out in its name.

The useful questions are more practical:

**What was demanded? Who had the power to act? What did they say? What did they decide? What was implemented? And what changed for the people who raised the problem?**

Those questions take us from Versailles to the Commune, from factories to universities, from transport networks to lycée gates. They also prevent the story from ending at the most photogenic moment. The protest photograph is not the outcome measure. The ministerial announcement is not the outcome measure. The pupil's access to education might be.

The Republic has received the paperwork.

**The pupils would still quite like the school nurse.**
