# 🏫 L’École et les Inégalités
**First created:** 2026-10-09 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-09  
*A school can be open, a position can be funded, and a child can still be missing an education.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The gates are open. The timetable exists. The ministry has a budget. Somewhere, presumably, there is an organisational chart in which every person has been allocated a reassuring little rectangle.

But is there a teacher in the classroom?

That is not a trick question. It is the question around which this node turns. France’s lycée mobilisations in September and October 2026 exposed a basic difference between **the existence of an education system** and **the delivery of education to the people who depend on it**. A school can remain administratively operational while lessons go untaught, disability support goes undelivered, a nurse is unavailable, or buildings become difficult to use. These are different failures, and they do not distribute their consequences equally.

Our starting point is **Lycée Saint-Exupéry in Créteil**, where pupils and staff publicly raised concerns about provision. It is a case study, not a statistical portrait of every French school. We will follow the complaints, the reported mobilisation, the administrative response and the unresolved questions; then travel outwards to the larger structures that determine whose education is easiest to interrupt and hardest to restore.

**Evidence boundary:** This node is drafted on 9 October from the Polaris Stage One evidence audit and the accompanying plan. Those records preserve original publication links and explicitly identify unresolved checks. This drafting pass does **not** constitute a new independent inspection of the school, a complete audit of rectorat records, or a national statistical analysis. Statements below distinguish reported testimony, organisational claims, proposed government measures and analytical inference. The last substantive contemporary reporting in the working evidence register is dated **5 October 2026**. We do not silently upgrade it into a verified account of conditions on the ninth.

---

## 1. 🏫 The School That Was Open, Except for the Bits That Weren’t

At Saint-Exupéry, a staff mobilisation was reported on **17 September**. Pupils began a blockade on **18 September**, according to a participant’s account later carried in *Le Monde*. On **20 September**, a local political-support statement published a list of alleged staffing deficiencies. During the following weeks, reports described disruption, a resumption of lessons, and complaints that some services remained inadequate.

Notice how quickly the story becomes susceptible to a misleadingly tidy ending. The school was blocked; authorities acted; the school reopened. Excellent. File closed. Except reopening is an event, not a measure of educational restoration.

A pupil can pass through an open gate into a classroom without a subject teacher. A disabled pupil can appear on the register without receiving the support required to participate. A parent can be told that provision has been arranged without knowing whether a qualified adult will be present when needed. These are not rhetorical flourishes about a school in Créteil; they are **distinct mechanisms by which formal access and usable access can diverge**. The question is which occurred, for whom, and for how long.

The distinction matters because pupils experience provision as time. The missing Tuesday lesson is not automatically returned by the appointment announced on Friday. A school cannot retroactively teach last week’s French simply by updating this week’s staffing return.

## 2. 🧮 Counting the Gaps Without Inventing a Vacancy Total

A statement dated **20 September** by a local political group supporting the mobilisation reported the following deficiencies. It is an **attributed advocacy statement**, not a school staffing database.

| Reported category | Initial complaint | What remains to establish |
|---|---|---|
| Deputy head | One missing | Whether the post was vacant, temporarily uncovered or subsequently filled |
| Teaching staff | Three missing teachers | Subjects, classes, duration and replacement arrangements |
| School nurse | One missing | Official allocation, cover, hours and service availability |
| Social worker | One full-time position missing | Allocated hours and actual access to social work |
| Vocational provision | Two support positions missing | Exact titles, duties and later staffing |
| Disability assistance | One AESH allocation reportedly withdrawn | Decision, affected support hours and later provision |

Source: [Créteil Citoyenne: “Mobilisation au lycée Saint-Exupéry de Créteil” (20 September 2026)](https://www.insoumisdecreteil.fr/2026/09/20/mobilisation-lycee-saint-exupery-creteil/).

These six rows are **not six equivalent vacancies**. Some describe a person, some a function, and one an allocation. Adding them together to produce a dramatic staffing total would create false precision. The original statement identifies what its authors said was missing; it does not by itself prove the formal establishment of each post or the exact number of hours lost.

There is a chain that public accounts routinely compress:

**Authorised post → money allocated → person recruited → person assigned → person present → service delivered.**

Every arrow can fail. A post can exist on paper without an appointment. An appointment can be made without the person starting. A person can be present without covering the needed subject or hours. And a delivered hour is not necessarily the same as adequate continuity over a term.

A staffing announcement may be true. A pupil’s account of missed education may also be true. The task is to locate the gap between the two.

## 3. 🧑‍🏫 When the Teacher Is Missing, What Exactly Has Been Lost?

“Missing teacher” can mean an unfilled established position, sickness, an absence awaiting replacement, a replacement who cannot cover every scheduled lesson, or a timetable assembled around an unresolved shortage. Each produces a different administrative record and a different educational experience.

The distinction is particularly consequential for examinations. Missed instruction can require pupils to catch up independently; inconsistent cover can interrupt the sequence in which a subject is taught; uncertainty can make preparation harder. Those are plausible educational mechanisms, **not evidence that every Saint-Exupéry pupil experienced them**. Establishing their scale would require subject-level timetables, cancelled-hours data, duration of gaps and testimony from the relevant classes.

The project’s Stage One audit notes that *Le Monde*’s **5 October** follow-up reported a teacher’s concern that some pupils had received their first French lesson only the preceding week. The exact French wording, year group and scope of that observation still require checking against the article before anyone turns it into a general statement about the school.

A useful audit would ask: how many French lessons were scheduled; how many were delivered; by whom; over what period; and what arrangements existed to make up the missed teaching? A statement that “recruitment has taken place” answers precisely none of those questions by itself.

And no, supervised study is not automatically a French lesson merely because a child has remained indoors for the correct number of minutes.

## 4. ♿ Disability Support Is Not an Optional Attachment

**AESH** are *accompagnants d’élèves en situation de handicap*: staff who support pupils with disabilities in accessing school life and learning. Their work may involve participation in lessons, communication, movement, practical tasks and other assistance depending on the pupil’s assessed needs and the support arrangements in place.

The initial local statement described **one withdrawn AESH allocation**. The 5 October follow-up, as recorded in our evidence audit, described **several disability-support assistants still missing**. Those statements are not necessarily contradictory. One could concern a particular allocation; the other could concern staffing across multiple pupils or classes. We cannot equate them without the underlying decisions and service records.

For a pupil who requires assistance, “the school is open” does not settle whether they can access the lesson. Nor is an authorised support entitlement the same as support actually provided. The relevant questions are practical: what assistance was recognised as necessary, what was allocated, what was scheduled, what was delivered, and whether the arrangement was continuous enough to be usable.

These are sensitive records involving children. A credible audit should use anonymised or aggregated evidence wherever possible, without publishing diagnoses or identifying combinations of circumstances. The right to ask whether provision works does not create a right to expose the pupil who needs it.

Disability inclusion is sometimes treated as a specialist footnote to the main educational story. It is the main story for any pupil whose access to the classroom depends upon it. Equality that excludes the person needing support is a fairly adventurous definition of equality.

## 5. 🩺 The Nurse Is Not a Luxury Item

The local complaint also identified missing nursing and social-work provision. These services are not interchangeable. A nurse provides health-related support; a social worker addresses different welfare and social needs; psychologists, counsellors and educational advisers have their own roles and qualifications. Collapsing them into “support staff” may make a budget table shorter while making the lived problem harder to understand.

The project audit records a **family-reported concern about insulin support** in the 5 October follow-up. That is an important reported service concern. It is **not**, on its own, proof of the precise vacancy status of the nurse’s post, nor grounds for identifying a child or making assumptions about their individual medical arrangements.

What should be checked? Nursing hours assigned to the school; cover arrangements; whether a qualified professional was available when needed; how parents and staff were informed; and whether gaps affected access to education. The same applies, in different ways, to social work: what is the actual route by which a pupil can obtain help, and does somebody have the capacity to respond?

A school’s capacity to keep pupils learning depends partly on its capacity to keep them safe, supported and able to attend. That work is easy to overlook when the public discussion has narrowed to how many teachers appear in a national announcement.

## 6. 🪟 Buildings: Apparently the Walls Are Also Part of Education

Parents interviewed in the **5 October** reporting were recorded in our evidence audit as raising concerns about **water leaks and a broken window**. These details need precise rechecking against the published account before being presented as a complete facilities inspection. They are, however, the sort of concerns that demonstrate why buildings belong in an educational inequality node.

A building is not simply the box in which education happens. Temperature, ventilation, accessibility, sanitation, safety and usable rooms affect what teaching can take place and who can participate. A defect that one pupil can navigate may be a serious access barrier for another. A building can remain formally open while parts of it are practically unusable.

The organisational demands in our companion [🎓 programme node](./🎓_ce_que_reclame_la_jeunesse.md) include renovation of schools and universities. The government’s reported response also identified buildings as a workstream. But “we will examine buildings” and “the window has been repaired” are not the same kind of statement.

Responsibility is divided. In the French system, **regions generally have major responsibilities for lycée buildings**, while national education authorities carry different responsibilities for teaching and staffing. The precise statutory boundaries, exceptions and funding routes must be checked before assigning blame for any particular repair. A national minister cannot necessarily authorise every maintenance job, and a regional authority cannot simply conjure a missing teacher into existence.

This division is an administrative fact worth understanding, not a magical absolution for everybody involved. The child still has to use the room.

## 7. 🗺️ One School Is Not a Map of France

Saint-Exupéry tells us about a documented local dispute. It does not establish the prevalence of staffing gaps across France, the relative condition of all suburban schools, or a simple division between privileged centres and neglected outskirts.

To examine national inequality properly, we need comparable measures. France publishes education statistics, including measures of pupils’ socioeconomic circumstances such as the **indice de position sociale (IPS)**. But a school-level social-composition indicator is not a direct measure of how many lessons were delivered, and an average can conceal substantial differences between pupils at the same institution.

A defensible national comparison would separate at least the following dimensions:

| Dimension | Useful evidence | Main comparability problem |
|---|---|---|
| Social composition | School IPS and pupil background indicators | Averages conceal within-school differences |
| Teaching provision | Scheduled versus delivered lessons, replacement duration | Definitions of absence and replacement vary |
| Disability inclusion | Recognised, allocated and delivered assistance | Entitlement and staffing are different measures |
| Buildings | Condition, accessibility and repair data | Ownership and recording differ by territory |
| Economic barriers | Transport, meals, equipment, internet and tutoring | Costs vary by household and location |
| Territory | Urban, rural, overseas and education-stage comparisons | Different school types cannot simply be pooled |

This node does not yet possess a harmonised national dataset for these comparisons. We should not manufacture one by stringing together vivid local examples. The next evidence pass should retrieve original DEPP statistical publications, school IPS data, relevant disability-access findings and regional building information, always preserving year, geography, denominator and school type.

There is plenty of room for a structural argument here. It simply has to be built on a structure.

## 8. 👩‍🎓 The Cost of a Gap Is Not Equally Shared

Suppose two pupils miss the same lesson. One has a quiet home, reliable internet, a parent able to help and money for private tutoring. The other has none of those things. The timetable records the same missed hour. The consequences need not be the same.

That is a **hypothetical mechanism**, not a claim about two identified pupils at Saint-Exupéry. But it identifies the kind of unequal burden that national research should test: the capacity to replace lost teaching privately, navigate institutional complaints, travel to alternatives or absorb disruptions to care and work.

Disability support makes the issue especially stark. If one pupil can participate independently and another needs assistance that has not arrived, their nominal attendance conceals a difference in actual access. The register says both are there. The experience says something else.

There is also inequality in the capacity to protest. Some pupils can risk missing a lesson, travelling to a demonstration or challenging a school authority more easily than others. Work, caring responsibilities, accessibility, fear of sanctions and the cost of transport may shape who becomes publicly visible. **Absence from a demonstration is not evidence of satisfaction with the school.**

The Stage Two corpus preserves individual pupils’ interviews from different places. They should be used as situated testimony, not converted into a representative survey by rhetorical enthusiasm.

## 9. 🚧 How a Local Complaint Became Public Information

The provisional Saint-Exupéry sequence shows how an internal provision problem can become an event that outsiders are obliged to notice:

| Date | What the working record supports | Important limit |
|---|---|---|
| **17 Sep** | Staff mobilisation reported | Precise sequence needs original account |
| **18 Sep** | Pupil blockade reported | Participant account via journalism |
| **20 Sep** | Local group publishes staffing complaints | Advocacy statement, not official return |
| **21 Sep** | Further action announced | Announcement does not prove attendance |
| **23 Sep** | Serious fireworks incident and injuries reported | Separate incident; no blanket attribution |
| **Week of 28 Sep** | Lessons reportedly resume | Reopening does not establish complete restoration |
| **3 Oct** | TF1 reports resumption and additional resources | Headline is not a post-by-post audit |
| **5 Oct** | Follow-up reports continuing deficiencies | Latest dated local follow-up in this evidence set |

The **23 September** incident requires particular care. Reports of fireworks and injuries must be recorded accurately, without implying that every pupil who organised a blockade was responsible. The wider questions of policing, disorder and accountability belong in the dedicated specialist nodes; they should not displace the original question of educational provision here.

The chronology suggests a pathway: staff complaints became pupil action; pupil action made the school publicly visible; authorities reportedly made recruitment decisions; lessons resumed; some concerns persisted. It does **not** prove that every subsequent appointment was caused exclusively by the blockade. Timing is relevant evidence, not a complete causal explanation.

## 10. 🛠️ What Was Actually Fixed?

The easiest way to overstate the outcome is to treat “additional resources” as a synonym for “every original complaint resolved”. The available evidence does not support that conclusion.

| Service | Initial reported problem | Follow-up in working audit | Responsible conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deputy leadership | One missing | Appointment mentioned in earlier research, not independently substantiated | **UNKNOWN** |
| Teaching | Three missing | Lessons resumed; positions not individually reconciled | **PARTIAL OUTCOME REPORTED** |
| Nursing | Nurse reportedly missing | Family-reported insulin-support concern | **SERVICE CONCERN REPORTED** |
| Social work | Full-time position reportedly missing | No post-specific update | **UNKNOWN** |
| Vocational support | Two positions reportedly missing | Earlier appointment claim unconfirmed | **UNKNOWN** |
| AESH | Allocation reportedly withdrawn | Several assistants reportedly still missing | **SHORTAGE REPORTED** |
| Building maintenance | Parents raised defects | Unresolved concerns reported 5 October | **ISSUES REPORTED** |

This table is deliberately unsatisfying. That is a virtue. It distinguishes what is known from what the evidence has not yet established.

A proper follow-up would seek dated staffing returns from the rectorat or school, appointment start dates, subject coverage, anonymised assistance-hours data, nursing arrangements and repair records. It would also ask pupils and families whether the service actually changed. An appointment letter is evidence of an appointment; it is not automatically evidence of a restored lesson.

The school’s reopening is meaningful. So are the deficiencies reportedly left behind. Both belong in the account.

## 11. 🏛️ Whose Department Is the Missing Teacher?

The phrase “the government should fix it” is politically understandable and administratively incomplete. French education involves different authorities whose responsibilities intersect without being identical.

| Issue | Likely authority to investigate | What must be checked |
|---|---|---|
| Teacher recruitment and deployment | State education system and rectorat | Staffing establishment, recruitment, replacement and deployment powers |
| Lycée buildings | Region and relevant property services | Ownership, maintenance duty, repair budget and procurement |
| School timetable and local operations | School leadership within national rules | Actual discretion, constraints and escalation route |
| Disability assistance | Education authorities and relevant support-allocation processes | Entitlements, allocation, employment and delivered hours |
| Health and social-work provision | Relevant education and health/welfare authorities | Assigned staffing, cover and access arrangements |

This is a **working responsibility map**, not a definitive legal opinion about a particular incident. The precise current law and institutional arrangements require checking against official French legal and administrative texts before publication as a fault-finding account.

Split responsibility can create a familiar administrative conversation. The teacher belongs to one budget, the window to another, the assistance hours to a third process. Everybody may be able to explain their own spreadsheet perfectly. Meanwhile the pupil has to attend one actual school, not three neatly separated accounting jurisdictions.

The purpose of mapping authority is not to make blame evaporate. It is to find the person or body capable of changing the outcome and the evidence by which their response can be judged.

## 12. 💰 A Budget Is a Proposal About Resources, Not a Delivered Lesson

The project’s Stage One audit records contemporary reporting on the proposed **2027** French education budget: **1,300 additional AESH positions**, **3,041 trainee-teacher positions**, and a reported reduction of **1,588 established teaching posts**. It also records unreconciled differences in total job figures and budget accounting. These are **reported proposals**, not enacted or delivered staffing outcomes, and the official detailed budget tables still require reconciliation.

The figures should not be casually combined. A trainee post is not an established teaching post. An AESH position is not a French teacher. A net increase in one staffing category can coexist with a reduction in another. And a national increase does not tell us which school receives an additional adult, on what date or for how many hours.

Most obviously, a draft budget for **2027** cannot retroactively supply a French lesson missed in **September 2026**. It may indicate policy priorities, but it is not proof that a local service gap has been closed.

For the detailed fiscal comparison, including the unresolved difference between reported totals, see [💰 Des Livres, Pas des Bombes](./💰_des_livres_pas_des_bombes.md) when that node is completed. This node’s narrower test is the path from **money promised** to **provision experienced**.

A larger budget can coexist with a local shortage. A local shortage does not, by itself, prove a national budget cut. Both propositions are possible because they describe different levels of the system.

## 13. 📐 What Would Equality Look Like If We Measured the Right Thing?

If the question is whether pupils can actually learn, the indicators need to get closer to their experience. Useful measures might include:

1. **Scheduled lessons delivered:** by subject, year group and period, with replacement teaching identified separately.
2. **Continuity:** whether pupils receive sustained qualified teaching rather than a rotating patchwork of cover.
3. **Disability assistance delivered:** anonymised comparison between recognised support needs, allocated hours and actual support.
4. **Health and welfare access:** availability of relevant staff and continuity of arrangements, without exposing children’s medical details.
5. **Building usability:** accessibility, urgent defects, repair completion and disruption to teaching spaces.
6. **Pupil and family experience:** documented barriers, complaint outcomes and whether reported improvements were felt in practice.

Not all these indicators are necessarily available nationally in comparable form. Nor should they be collapsed into a single moral score in which a repaired window compensates mathematically for an inaccessible lesson. Different services meet different needs.

For Saint-Exupéry, a modest, dated **service-restoration scorecard** would be more informative than a triumphal headline. Each row could say: reported gap; responsible body; intervention announced; intervention verified; service delivered; evidence date; unresolved questions.

The most important column may be the least glamorous: **unknown**. It tells the next investigator exactly where to look.

## 14. ♻️ Information Is Experienced

This is where the case meets Polaris’s **Embodied Information Ecology**. Institutions produce representations: budgets, posts, timetables, appointments, registers, consultation results. People encounter the system through their bodies and days: a classroom, a staircase, a nurse’s availability, a lesson that happened or did not.

Those two accounts can diverge without anyone necessarily fabricating a number. An authorised position may be recorded correctly. A replacement may genuinely have been recruited. A budget increase may be real within its defined accounting perimeter. None of these facts alone establishes that a particular pupil received the required service at the required time.

That is a **measurement mismatch**. It can be intensified by administrative delays, divided responsibilities and feedback channels that register complaints without measuring whether the complaint was resolved.

The pupils’ mobilisation changes the information environment. Problems that might otherwise remain inside timetables, staff rooms and family correspondence become public. Journalists ask questions. Officials respond. Politicians quote figures. The process can produce useful information and even practical changes. But it can also stop at the point where the state has acknowledged a problem and mistaken acknowledgement for resolution.

The difference between *we heard you* and *we fixed it* is not a matter of tone. It is an empirical question.

Return, then, to the school gates. A pupil arrives. The building is open. The timetable has been published. Somewhere in the Republic, a spreadsheet may show that the position exists.

Very good.

**Is the teacher in the room?**
