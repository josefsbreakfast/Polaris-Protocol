# 🤝 Syndicats et Solidarités
**First created:** 2026-10-09 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-09  
*Who stood beside the French pupils in autumn 2026, who could speak for whom, and what happens when solidarity becomes an administrative category.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

France is a country in which a demonstration can contain a school pupil, a teacher, a university student, three different union banners and somebody who has come because they are furious about the price of lunch. This is not necessarily evidence of a single organisation. It may, however, be evidence of a political problem that has become difficult to keep in its designated administrative compartment.

That distinction is the purpose of this node. The autumn 2026 mobilisation over education brought questions of missing teachers, school support, university access, student finances and democratic participation into public view. Organisations could recognise common interests without possessing identical programmes. Some participants had the authority to negotiate for members; others had no interest in surrendering the independence of their school-level organising. A photograph of people walking together establishes that they were in the same place. It does not establish who authorised the route, wrote the demands, paid for the banners or agreed to the terms of any meeting with a minister.

**The governing proposition is simple: standing alongside somebody is not the same as speaking for them.** Solidarity can amplify an original demand, but it can also translate that demand into something more convenient for a larger organisation—or for the government receiving it. We therefore need to track the relationships, the words, the mandates and the outcomes separately.

This is a source-conscious synthesis of the *Jeunesse en Feu* research assembled by 9 October 2026. The contemporary claims identified below are accompanied by their available source leads and explicit evidence limits. It is not a declaration that every reported meeting, membership list or local coordination claim has been independently audited against the underlying documents.

## 1. 🪧 A Crowd Is Not a Committee

Picture the familiar photograph: young people at the front of a demonstration, teachers nearby, a union banner above the crowd. A headline says that the unions have joined the lycée pupils. The image may be perfectly accurate. The grammar is where the trouble starts.

*Joined* could mean a union issued a statement of support. It could mean members attended as individuals. It could mean a local section helped organise transport. It could mean several organisations jointly called the demonstration. It could even mean that representatives negotiated a shared platform and were mandated to speak on behalf of a coalition. These are different propositions. They should not all be smuggled through the door under the same verb.

Contemporary reporting collected for this constellation includes a *Public Sénat* account of teachers and unions alongside protesting lycée pupils. That is a useful starting point for reconstructing visible support, not proof of a national command structure. The report needs to be paired with the actual event calls and dated organisational statements before we claim who organised what.

Four questions should accompany every photograph: **Who called the action? Who attended? Who spoke? Who decided?** A fifth is especially useful after the ministers appear: **Who was accountable to the people being represented?**

France has produced a remarkable number of committees. It has not, to our knowledge, abolished the need to read their minutes.

## 2. 🧑‍🎓 The Lycée Pupils Are Political Actors

A *lycéen* is a secondary-school pupil. A pupil may be a minor, may participate in an elected school body, may belong to a lycée organisation, or may simply have helped arrange a blockade through friends and messaging groups. None of these statuses is interchangeable.

The distinction matters because adults can be remarkably quick to discover a representative for young people, particularly when that representative is already familiar with a microphone. A visible spokesperson might have been chosen by a local assembly, elected within a national organisation, nominated by friends, or approached by a journalist because they were willing to talk. Each may be speaking truthfully about their own experience. Only some have a demonstrable mandate to speak for others.

The earlier language corpus identifies the **Union Syndicale Lycéenne** and student organisers in several localities. It also contains a reported Strasbourg interview in which a pupil organiser expressed concern about larger organisations eclipsing the pupils' own voices. That concern should be read as a specific attributed position, not silently upgraded into the opinion of every young person at the demonstration. The interview's full original wording and context remain a source-check requirement.

An unaffiliated pupil does not become politically absent merely because they lack a membership card. Nor does an elected representative become illegitimate because other pupils disagree. Democracy includes both representation and the right to challenge it.

## 3. 🎓 University Students Are Not Lycée Pupils With Better Stationery

The education system joins these constituencies together, but it does not give them the same immediate problems.

A pupil missing a teacher is losing instruction now. A pupil whose disability support is unavailable may be excluded from participating in the school day. A university student struggling with rent or meals confronts a different material constraint. An applicant challenging selective admissions is asking about access to a future place. All are education questions. They do not become the same demand because they can fit on a shared placard.

Our Stage Two source collection identifies a ten-measure programme published by **L'Union Étudiante**. The programme includes buildings, staffing, student support, admissions, democratic participation, meals and housing. It is valuable evidence of that organisation's published platform. It is **not automatically the collectively adopted programme of every lycée blockade**. Before describing it as such, we would need dated evidence of adoption or endorsement by the relevant lycée organisations.

This distinction protects the political content of the demands. Making Parcoursup more transparent, for example, does not answer an organisation demanding an end to selection through Parcoursup. Likewise, a government consultation on the school day is not a substitute for a specific staffing commitment simply because both appear under *education reform*.

Solidarity can produce a wider language of educational access. It must not erase the fact that a teenager waiting for an AESH and a university student facing unaffordable accommodation have different routes to an effective remedy.

## 4. 🧑‍🏫 Teachers, AESH, Nurses and the Other People Who Make a School Work

Schools do not operate on teachers alone. They require disability-support workers, nurses, social workers, administrators, maintenance staff, leadership and the people who make ordinary routines possible. When one of those functions disappears, somebody else is often expected to absorb the gap. Sometimes that somebody is a colleague. Sometimes it is a parent. Sometimes it is the pupil who loses access.

That creates genuine intersections between labour conditions and educational rights. A teacher describing an unfilled post and a pupil describing missed lessons may be reporting different sides of the same shortage. The teacher's concern about workload is not a lesser version of the pupil's concern about instruction; nor should it be allowed to replace the pupil's account. Both are needed to understand the system.

The Saint-Exupéry case in Créteil is especially useful. The constellation's earlier evidence audit records staff concerns preceding pupil mobilisation in September and reports that lessons subsequently resumed while some support deficiencies remained. **This does not, on its own, establish a formally co-organised staff-and-pupil campaign.** To make that stronger claim, we need joint calls, school-level statements or testimony describing actual coordination.

Parents' associations deserve their own line in the register. A parent speaking about their child's access to school is not a teacher-union delegate. A teacher supporting a pupil's blockade is not thereby authorised to negotiate on that pupil's behalf. The interests may align; the mandates still need checking.

## 5. 🤝 Five Levels of Solidarity

The word *solidarity* is doing rather a lot of work. We can help it by asking for receipts.

| Level | Relationship | Evidence that would support it | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Sympathy | Attributed expression of concern | Endorsement or membership |
| **2** | Endorsement | Dated organisational statement supporting an action or demand | Shared logistics or mandate |
| **3** | Coordination | Evidence of exchanged plans, shared resources or practical arrangements | Identical programmes |
| **4** | Joint action | Jointly signed call or documented co-organisation | Permanent united leadership |
| **5** | Shared decision-making | Delegated representatives, agreed rules, minutes or formal common decisions | Agreement by every participant |

These levels are not a ladder on which all respectable movements must eventually climb. A local collective might want support without handing over decision-making. Two unions might cooperate on one demand and publicly disagree about another. A national organisation might offer legal or logistical assistance while explicitly leaving pupils to determine their own actions.

Relationships also change. An endorsement on Monday might become coordination on Thursday. A shared demonstration might be followed by a disagreement about negotiations on Friday. We should date the relationship rather than assign an eternal label to the organisations involved.

Where the evidence cannot distinguish levels, the answer is **unresolved**. This is a perfectly acceptable answer. It is considerably more useful than a fictitious coalition chart.

## 6. 📜 What an *Intersyndicale* Is—and Isn't

An *intersyndicale* is a form of coordination among trade unions, often around a particular dispute or set of demands. It is not necessarily a permanent institution and it does not transform every participating union into a branch of one organisation.

French union terminology matters. A *confédération* can bring together unions across sectors; a *fédération* may organise a particular sector; a local *section syndicale* has a more bounded workplace or territorial role. The same acronym in a national declaration and a local leaflet may not establish the same authority. The correct unit of attribution is the **actual signatory**.

A dated statement attributed in the earlier corpus to an inter-union grouping in higher education is a useful case. The source lead is a **7 October 2026** declaration hosted by **CGT FERC Sup**, concerning the proposed higher-education budget and participation in a ministerial meeting. The earlier research identifies organisations from the FSU, CGT, SUD, FO, UNSA and CFDT families. Their precise signatory names, the wording of the declaration and the sequence of the meeting should be read from the original before publication as verified detail.

Even if that declaration is confirmed in full, it establishes the position of its named signatories in **higher education and research**. It does not establish that every lycée pupil, schoolteacher, parent or firefighter agreed. One ministerial room does not contain the whole Republic, despite the Republic's evident enthusiasm for rooms.

## 7. 📣 How a Missing Lesson Travels

Follow a complaint as it moves through institutions.

A pupil says that a lesson did not happen. Their classmates identify a pattern. A local group demands a replacement teacher. A staff organisation describes an unfilled position and unsustainable workload. A national organisation adds the problem to a programme of recruitment and investment. A ministry responds with a workstream on improving teacher replacement.

These statements may all refer to the same underlying deficiency. They are not identical.

| Stage | Speaker or institution | Question being asked | Risk of translation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lived experience | Pupil | Did I receive my lesson? | Individual experience dismissed as anecdotal |
| Local organising | Pupils or school collective | Will the school restore provision? | Local specificity lost |
| Worker organisation | Staff or union | Will posts and conditions support delivery? | Pupil access recast solely as workforce policy |
| National programme | Student or labour organisation | What structural changes are required? | Immediate needs folded into a wider platform |
| Government response | Ministry or rectorat | What administrative action can we announce? | Announcement mistaken for implementation |

This is not an argument against translation. Institutions need categories to allocate money and staff. It is an argument for **checking whether the translated answer still solves the original problem**.

A replacement-teacher system can improve on paper while a particular class continues to miss French. A staffing authorisation can be real while recruitment fails. A meeting can happen while a school nurse remains absent. The original complaint is the test, not the final press release.

## 8. 🏛️ Who Was Actually in the Ministerial Room?

The research assembled for this constellation identifies at least three different channels that should never be merged casually: a government consultation addressed to lycée pupils; contacts with university student organisations; and meetings involving higher-education worker unions.

The earlier research attributes a 7 October withdrawal from a ministerial meeting to higher-education inter-union representatives. **That is not evidence that lycée pupils walked out of the same meeting.** Nor does the existence of a lycée consultation establish that participating pupils had authority to negotiate the government's budget.

A meeting register should record the invitation, the people who attended, the agenda, what each organisation requested, what the minister said, whether decisions were made and what followed. In particular:

- **Invitation is not attendance.**
- **Attendance is not agreement.**
- **Consultation is not negotiation.**
- **Negotiation is not implementation.**
- **Walking out is an action by the people who walked out, not by everybody who supports their demands.**

The difference between being asked *What do you think?* and being asked *Will you approve this decision?* is a difference in power, not merely conversational style. The difference between either question and *Here is the funded appointment* is a difference in material outcome.

A minister may sincerely listen. The pupil still needs the missing lesson.

## 9. ⚖️ Strike, Blockade, Demonstration, Petition

The French language of collective action includes *grève*, *blocus*, *manifestation*, *occupation*, *pétition* and *assemblée générale*. These are not synonyms.

A **strike** ordinarily concerns workers withholding labour in the context of employment. A **school blockade** may involve pupils obstructing access to an educational institution. A **demonstration** gathers people publicly to express a political position. A **petition** communicates demands through signatures. Each raises different questions about legal rights, school duties, public safety and organisational responsibility.

The fact that pupils are sometimes described as *en grève* in political language does not mean their absence from class has the same legal status as an employee's protected industrial action. Nor does the presence of union banners automatically turn a blockade into a trade-union strike.

The distinction is particularly important where minors are involved. Their participation is politically meaningful; their safety, access to education and treatment by authorities also require careful attention. Those questions are developed in [🚨 La police face à la jeunesse](./🚨_la_police_face_a_la_jeunesse.md).

A useful node on solidarity should be able to admire the shared impulse without mislabelling the legal mechanism. The paperwork is not the revolution. Unfortunately, the paperwork does occasionally determine who can be disciplined.

## 10. 🗺️ Many Towns, Not Necessarily One Headquarters

The research itinerary spans Créteil, Agen, Toulouse, Montpellier, Strasbourg, Sevran, Chelles, Cahors and Paris. The presence of actions or interviews in different places is evidence of geographic breadth **only to the extent that each local event is separately documented**. It does not establish that all were directed by the same national committee.

Similar slogans can spread through social media, friendship networks, travelling organisers, union materials and news coverage. They can also be independently adapted from an older repertoire. The earlier slogan audit found indications that some wording predated the October protests. We should not award authorship to the first placard that happens to appear in our archive.

Distributed organising is not necessarily disorganised. Pupils can learn from one another, share a tactic and produce recognisable national patterns without central command. Conversely, a national organisation can provide a common call while local groups retain substantial autonomy. Both arrangements are possible; the evidence must distinguish them.

A city-by-city register should therefore include date, school, named organisers, original call, participating organisations and evidence of actual coordination. The map becomes more interesting when we resist drawing connecting lines merely because the dots are attractive.

## 11. 🚒 And What About the Firefighters?

The companion node, [🚒 Les pompiers en grève](./🚒_les_pompiers_en_greve.md), records a reported **CFDT Interco strike notice covering 8 September to 20 October 2026**. The notice is relevant to a wider discussion of public-service staffing, resources and working conditions.

But a strike notice is not a national walkout, and a parallel dispute is not proof of a joint movement. To place firefighters inside the student coalition we would need evidence of explicit endorsement, shared demonstrations, joint calls or coordination with education organisations. Without that, the proper description is **contemporaneous industrial dispute with potentially comparable structural grievances**.

The comparison remains worth making. Schools and emergency services are experienced by the public through actual provision, not merely staffing plans. Workers can identify stresses that the public only notices when services fail. Public-sector organisations may find common language in the distance between authorised resources and workable delivery.

Two departments can be short-staffed at once without having exchanged a single memo. Indeed, the Republic has a long and distinguished administrative history of precisely this possibility.

## 12. ♀️ The Work That Makes Solidarity Possible

Political coverage tends to notice the microphone, the banner and the dramatic confrontation. Organising also requires somebody to prepare leaflets, answer messages, arrange accessibility, check meeting times, contact families, coordinate travel, translate statements and explain to journalists what the demand actually says.

Who does that work? Who gets credited? Who becomes the public face of the action? Are those the same people?

These are questions for evidence, not assumptions about the identities of people in photographs. Women and girls may be prominent, marginalised or differently represented in particular organising spaces; the answer must come from testimony, records and a defined sample of reporting. Disability access, class, racism and the costs of participation likewise need actual sources rather than generic observations pasted onto French pupils.

The companion node [♀️ Les filles aussi sont en colère](./♀️_les_filles_aussi_sont_en_colere.md) can examine the wider gendered experience. Here the narrower question is **who maintains the networks that allow people to act together**.

An organisation can become famous through its spokesperson while depending on people whose names never enter the article. That is not a uniquely French problem. It is simply one that French administrative ingenuity has not yet solved with an additional subcommittee.

## 13. 🧿 Disagreement Is Not a Data-Cleaning Error

There is a temptation to describe solidarity as the moment when everyone finally agrees. This is a poor description of most political organising and an especially poor method for studying it.

Participants may disagree about blocking a school, joining a union march, negotiating with a ministry, prioritising university admissions, or accepting a partial staffing concession. An organisation may support pupils' right to protest without endorsing every tactic. Pupils may welcome adults' resources while refusing to let those adults choose their demands.

The Strasbourg interview noted in the source collection offers a possible illustration of anxiety about larger organisations overshadowing pupils. It needs its original context, including the speaker's role and exact French, before being used as a quotation. Its analytical value is not that it proves a universal split. It shows why **representation is itself a political question**.

When a statement announces that *the movement has agreed*, ask who attended the decision, what authority they held, how dissent was recorded and whether the agreement applied to every local group. This is not nit-picking. It is the difference between a coalition and somebody else's name on your complaint.

Solidarity that permits disagreement may be stronger than unity imposed for the benefit of a headline.

## 14. 📺 Who Gets to Explain the Movement?

Journalism has to select speakers. The selection can change what audiences understand the movement to be.

An article quoting a union official may explain labour demands with considerable precision while leaving pupils' accounts in the background. Another may feature a pupil's dramatic description of a blockade without explaining the staffing dispute. A third may interview the ministry and describe its consultation as the central event. None of these articles needs to be deliberately misleading for the cumulative picture to become distorted.

The remedy is a modest, replicable media sample: record the outlet, date, headline, location, named organisations, attributed quotations and the role of each speaker. Then compare what the headline implies about organisation with what the report actually demonstrates.

Useful categories include *lycéens* (secondary-school pupils), *étudiants* (higher-education students), *enseignants* (teachers), *syndicats* (unions) and *manifestants* (demonstrators). They overlap in a crowd. They should not be collapsed in an evidence register.

The full media-framing investigation belongs in [📺 Qui raconte la révolte](./📺_qui_raconte_la_revolte.md). Here the question is who receives the authority to describe the movement's decisions—and whether that authority was actually granted.

## 15. 🧮 What Did Solidarity Change?

Crowd size can tell us that an event attracted participation. It does not tell us whether a pupil received a lesson, whether an AESH was deployed or whether a university fee changed.

An outcome register should follow the entire chain:

**Grievance → organising → support → coordination → institutional contact → decision → implementation → experienced change.**

| Claimed result | What would demonstrate it | What would not be enough |
|---|---|---|
| Greater public visibility | Dated coverage and documented audience reach | One striking photograph |
| Joint organisation | Shared call, logistics or formal agreement | Banners in the same street |
| Access to decision-makers | Confirmed meeting and participants | Invitation alone |
| Staffing improvement | Appointment **and** evidence of service delivered | Ministerial announcement |
| Budget improvement | Approved and allocated resources | Proposed headline figure |
| Resolution of dispute | Documented agreement and observed change | Resumption of ordinary activity alone |

Saint-Exupéry offers a particularly useful case. Earlier reporting indicates lessons resumed after interventions, but some shortages remained. The correct question is not simply whether mobilisation ended. It is which demands were met, which were deferred and which pupils still experienced missing provision. The precise post-by-post audit remains incomplete.

Solidarity may improve bargaining power. It may also impose costs: diluted demands, diminished local control, pressure to adopt someone else's tactics or greater exposure to confrontation. These are hypotheses to investigate, not outcomes to declare in advance.

## 16. ♻️ Coalition as Information Routing

The Polaris question is not merely *Who was there?* It is *How did information travel, and what happened to its meaning on the way?*

**Sensing:** pupils, teachers, support workers, parents and students encounter different failures in educational provision. Their knowledge is situated. A pupil knows the lesson did not happen. A teacher may know why the timetable cannot be staffed. A parent may know what happens when a child cannot access support.

**Encoding:** the experience becomes testimony, a placard, a union resolution, a demand list or a strike notice. Each form emphasises some features and omits others.

**Routing:** local networks, organisations, journalists and meetings carry the message to new audiences. This can increase reach and negotiating capacity. It can also introduce gatekeepers.

**Translation:** a missing lesson becomes a staffing category; a demand for accessible education becomes a budget line; a protest becomes a public-order problem. Administrative translation may be necessary. It is not neutral.

**Feedback:** the original participants compare the institution's response with what they needed. Was the teacher appointed? Did the nurse arrive? Did the pupil receive support? Did the organisation that negotiated the outcome return to its members and explain what was agreed?

**Failure modes:** false consensus, representative capture, diluted demands, selective quotation, fragmented responsibility and the substitution of consultation for implementation.

Coalition-building is neither inherently liberating nor inherently corrupting. It is an arrangement of information, resources and authority. Its quality depends on whether the people whose problems generated the signal can still recognise—and contest—the response.

A pupil can be grateful that a national organisation has brought a minister to the table and still say: **That is not what I asked for.**

## 17. 🗂️ The Evidence Ledger We Still Need

The node's argument can be stated now; its strongest empirical claims require a maintained register rather than a decorative bibliography.

| Register | Essential fields | Current limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Actors | Exact French name, constituency, local/national level, mandate | Membership and mandates not comprehensively checked |
| Relationships | Two actors, date, solidarity level 1–5, supporting record | Joint action cannot be inferred from shared attendance |
| Meetings | Date, ministry, invitation, attendees, demands, decisions | Full original meeting records outstanding |
| Demands | Original French, author, addressee, later adoption, government reply | University and lycée programmes must remain distinct |
| Local actions | Town, school, organisers, call, evidence of coordination | No exhaustive national event register yet |
| Outcomes | Promise, authority, funding, deployment, observed service | Saint-Exupéry post-by-post outcome unresolved |
| Media | Headline, attributed speakers, organisational claims, framing | A systematic article sample has not yet been coded |

Use the evidence labels **PRIMARY_ORGANISATION**, **PRIMARY_INSTITUTION**, **FIRST_PERSON_INTERVIEW**, **CONTEMPORARY_REPORT**, **INDEPENDENT_CORROBORATION**, **ANALYTICAL_INFERENCE** and **UNRESOLVED**. These identify kinds of evidence, not a magic hierarchy in which an institution is always correct and a pupil always mistaken.

In particular, before publication as a fully verified account, check the original 7 October higher-education declaration and signatories; the full Strasbourg pupil interview; each ministerial meeting's participants; the authorship and intended constituency of the ten-measure programme; and the original calls behind any claim of joint organisation.

This is also a reminder not to manufacture a tidy history. The archive may be incomplete. An absent record does not prove an organisation opposed a protest, supported it or was never asked.

## 18. 🥖 Back to the School Gate

Return to the school gate. The pupil has a demand. The teacher has a concern. The union has a programme. The ministry has a consultation. The journalist has a headline. They may all be discussing the same school and still be answering different questions.

The achievement of solidarity is not that everybody learns to say exactly the same thing. It is that people can bring different knowledge and resources together without making the original grievance disappear. Good representation makes the people affected harder to ignore. Good coordination makes action possible. Good accountability lets those people challenge what has been done in their name.

And good government, when it eventually arrives, does something with the information.

The Republic is welcome to convene an *intersyndicale*, invite a delegation, establish a working group and commission a summary of the consultation. None of those activities is inherently pointless. Some may be necessary. But somewhere in the system is a child who was supposed to have a lesson on Tuesday.

**The test is whether Tuesday gets better.**
