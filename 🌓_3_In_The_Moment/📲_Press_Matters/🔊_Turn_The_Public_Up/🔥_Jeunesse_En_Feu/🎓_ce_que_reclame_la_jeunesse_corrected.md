# 🎓 Ce Que Réclame La Jeunesse
**First created:** 2026-10-08 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-09  
*The students have made demands. The Republic has produced a consultation. These are related activities, but they are not synonyms.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

A school is an unusually convenient place to teach children about democracy. There are rules, elections, representatives, assemblies, adults explaining the importance of civic participation, and quite a lot of posters about listening respectfully to one another.

The experiment becomes more interesting when the children begin to ask where the teachers have gone.

Across the French lycée mobilisations of September and October 2026, pupils described missing lessons, inadequate support, uncertainty about their futures and the conditions in which they were expected to study. Their protests overlapped with broader demands made by student organisations about university admissions, living costs, housing and the right to organise. In response, the government presented a national consultation and a programme of work on aspects of lycée life.

All three things matter: **what pupils experienced; what particular organisations demanded; and what the state offered to do.** They are not interchangeable. The aim here is to keep them in the same frame without permitting one to impersonate the others.

The ten-part programme discussed below belongs to **L’Union Étudiante**, a particular organisation. It is not a referendum on the opinions of every French teenager. Local lycée demands, named pupils' testimony and education-worker union statements are separate sources of information. Where this node uses a working English rendering of a French demand, it is a translation, not an original English quotation.

**Evidence note, 8 October 2026:** This draft is grounded in the project's Stage One and Two research collections and the detailed plan for this node. The underlying materials describe source checks, but this drafting pass has **not independently reopened every original publication**. The ten policy categories and the five reported government workstreams are therefore presented as a documented *working comparison*, with unresolved exact wording, numerical targets and implementation outcomes explicitly marked. In particular, future commitments are not treated as completed reforms. Links to the underlying publications appear in the source shelf.

## 1. 🎤 “We want to be heard” — heard saying what?

A pupil interviewed in Toulouse, Léo, was reported by *Le Monde* on 6 October as saying: **« On veut être entendus ! »** — *We want to be heard!* The sentence is short enough to fit into almost any ministerial speech. That is part of its danger. It can be repeated by somebody who has not yet asked what the speaker wanted heard.

Being heard is not, on its own, a policy. It is a demand for a relationship: somebody with relevant authority must receive the information, understand its content and make a decision that can be examined. Sometimes that decision will be no. Sometimes it will be partly. Sometimes the requested change belongs to another ministry, another public body or Parliament. But each of those is an answer that can be tested. A microphone is not.

Another reported placard puts the matter rather efficiently: **« On manque nos leçons pour vous en donner une »** — *We're missing lessons to teach you one.* The line has been documented in reporting from Cahors and Quimper; an earlier attribution to Montpellier should not be recycled without evidence. It turns the school's ordinary hierarchy upside down. Pupils are expected to learn. Here they have decided that the adults require instruction in what the school is failing to provide.

Missing a lesson to protest can carry a real educational cost. Missing a lesson because there was no teacher also carries a cost. The fact that the first absence is politically conspicuous does not make the second less consequential.

## 2. 🧑‍🎓 There is no single person called “the youth”

A pupil blockading a particular lycée may want a mathematics teacher, a nurse, working toilets or support for a disabled classmate. Another pupil may participate out of solidarity with schools in worse circumstances. A lycée union may propose changes to representation and disciplinary rules. A university student organisation may want to alter the entire structure of higher-education admissions and living support. A staff union may oppose budget proposals for reasons that overlap with, but do not duplicate, student concerns.

Those positions can reinforce one another without collapsing into one voice.

This matters especially when a ten-point document travels through the press. It is tempting to call it *what French pupils demand*. That sounds wonderfully decisive. It also quietly transfers the authority of a named organisation to millions of people who did not necessarily author, endorse or even read its programme.

For this node, the categories are kept separate:

- **Individual pupils:** testimony about their own conditions, experience and political choices.
- **Local school groups:** demands tied to an identifiable institution and its deficiencies.
- **Lycée organisations and elected pupil representatives:** positions whose representational mandates must be identified.
- **L’Union Étudiante:** the organisation responsible for the ten-measure programme used here.
- **Higher-education staff unions:** employees and their representatives, not substitutes for pupil voices.
- **Government and public bodies:** decision-makers, consultation organisers and service providers, not neutral narrators of what everybody else meant.

The distinction is not administrative fussiness. It is the basic question of who has said what, and who gets to decide what that statement means.

## 3. 📜 Ten measures, several different systems

The working primary document is [L’Union Étudiante's ten-measure programme](https://union-etudiante.fr/10mesures/). Its categories range from buildings and staff to admissions, grants, housing and policing. Some concern secondary education, others higher education, and several reach beyond the remit of a single education minister.

The ten areas reconstructed in the project research are:

| No. | Working demand area | What must be distinguished |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Renovate schools and universities | Announced investment versus buildings made usable |
| 2 | Recruit teachers and support professionals | Replacement cover versus sustained capacity and specialist provision |
| 3 | Reform the school day | Consultation on timetables versus an adopted schedule |
| 4 | End selection through Parcoursup and Mon Master | Admissions procedures versus the principle of selection |
| 5 | Strengthen lycée democracy | Being asked for views versus possessing decision-making power |
| 6 | Improve grants and financial autonomy | A promised entitlement versus money actually received |
| 7 | Expand access to €1 meals | A headline price versus accessible, adequate food provision |
| 8 | End differentiated international fees and the CVEC | Changes to separate charges and their legal rules |
| 9 | Protect and expand student housing | Planned units versus affordable homes occupied |
| 10 | Oppose repression and police violence | Protest rights, discipline, operational conduct and accountability |

**Source precision:** These are explanatory English category labels, **not a purported verbatim transcription of the programme's ten French headings**. The precise headings, page history and numerical targets require checking against a preserved version of the original document before the labels are treated as quotations. Nor does inclusion here prove that every participating lycée collective adopted all ten measures.

That said, the breadth of the programme is itself politically informative. A school is not simply a place in which information is transmitted. Pupils and students must reach it, eat, live somewhere, receive support, navigate admissions and exercise rights while doing so. Those conditions are not decorative extras to education. They determine who can actually use it.

## 4. 🏫 Buildings: a promise cannot keep the rain out

The first demand concerns the physical conditions in which education happens. It is easy to describe a building as *in need of renovation*. The phrase sounds calm and reasonably manageable. It can cover anything from peeling paint to inaccessible rooms, unreliable heating or maintenance problems that affect whether lessons can be held.

The working research around Lycée Saint-Exupéry in Créteil records concerns about maintenance alongside staffing. Those reports describe one school at particular dates, not a statistically representative sample of France. Their importance lies in the difference between the scale at which a problem is announced and the scale at which somebody experiences it. A national commitment to improve buildings is an announcement. A repaired, accessible classroom is a condition a pupil can verify.

There is also the question of **who is actually responsible**. French education involves several levels of public authority. Secondary-school buildings, national staffing decisions, universities and their estates do not all belong to the same administrative budget. A serious government answer should name the responsible institution rather than sending every complaint on a tour of the Republic until it dies of old age.

The test is not whether renovation appears in a letter. It is whether a specific defect is assessed, financed, repaired and independently confirmed as usable.

## 5. 🧑‍🏫 Teachers, nurses, psychologists and AESH: a headcount is not a lesson

The second demand is broader than recruitment of classroom teachers. The programme, as recorded in our working source collection, includes nurses, psychologists, guidance professionals and **AESH** staff who support disabled pupils. These are different roles with different consequences when they are absent.

A school may have a nominally funded position that is vacant. It may have appointed somebody who has not started. It may have a temporary replacement who covers only part of the timetable. It may count a staff member in an overall total while particular pupils still do not receive the support they need.

The chain is worth spelling out:

**Budgeted post → vacancy advertised → person recruited → person present → service available → pupil receives the service.**

Every arrow is a potential point of failure. Each can be reported separately. A ministry can truthfully announce recruitment without demonstrating that a named pupil has recovered the teaching hours already lost.

At Saint-Exupéry, a local statement dated 20 September identified multiple alleged shortages, while subsequent reporting described lessons resuming and some difficulties continuing into early October. The detailed post-by-post reconciliation remains incomplete. The record supports neither *nothing improved* nor *everything was fixed*.

This matters when we examine the government's reported workstream on **teacher replacement**. Improving cover for an absent teacher may be worthwhile. It is not automatically the same thing as adding permanent capacity, recruiting a nurse or guaranteeing specialist support. A response can be relevant and still be narrower than the demand.

A working project summary also records a proposed ratio of welfare staff to pupils and an additional AESH target. Those figures are **not repeated here as established numerical commitments** because the exact wording, denominator and programme version have not yet been reconciled against the original page.

## 6. 🕰️ Time belongs to the pupils too

The third demand concerns the organisation of the school day. A timetable looks like a neutral grid until somebody has to inhabit it. A long day, inadequate time to eat, inaccessible transport or an impossible sequence of classes may impose very different burdens on different pupils.

Our planning material records a proposed **90-minute lunch interval**. This is a useful research lead, not yet a verified universal demand in that precise form. The underlying text must establish whether it concerns all pupils, particular school types or a specific timetable reform.

A government workstream on *temps scolaires* overlaps with the topic. It does not, by its existence, prove agreement on any particular arrangement. A discussion about the school day can end with the same school day. To measure change, we would need an adopted timetable, the population covered, implementation dates and evidence that the reform operates in practice.

The children are, after all, already spending quite a lot of their time in school. It seems reasonable to ask whether the people drawing the boxes have considered what living inside them entails.

## 7. 🎓 Parcoursup: transparency is not the opposite of selection

One of the most important distinctions in the entire comparison is contained in the programme's working French formulation:

> « Mettre fin à la sélection via Parcoursup et Mon Master »

*End selection through Parcoursup and Mon Master.*

The line concerns **selection**, not necessarily the physical existence of centralised application websites. An online platform could, in principle, remain in place under a different admissions regime. Conversely, a platform can become more transparent while still operating selection criteria that an organisation opposes.

These are different political proposals:

- **Transparency:** explain criteria, decisions and procedures more clearly.
- **Capacity:** provide more available places.
- **Rules:** alter who is eligible and how applicants are ranked.
- **Selection:** change or remove the contested principle itself.

Our working Stage One material reports that the government included **Parcoursup transparency** among five lycée workstreams. That is a response in the same policy territory, but it does not establish acceptance of the organisation's demand to end selection. Classifying it as **reframed** is more accurate than marking the demand fulfilled.

A working figure of **250,000 additional university places** also appears in the earlier research plan. Its precise source, period and scope need to be confirmed before publication as an exact programme target. There is no intellectual prize for printing an impressive number that we cannot securely attribute.

## 8. 🗳️ Democracy: the difference between a suggestion box and power

The fifth demand concerns democracy within lycée life. Schools already contain representative mechanisms, including elected pupil structures. But *having a mechanism* is not identical to *having influence over the decisions that matter*.

Consider four increasingly consequential arrangements: pupils may be invited to express concerns; they may elect representatives who convey concerns; representatives may be consulted before decisions; or pupils may acquire defined powers to shape, challenge or review those decisions. These are not equivalent arrangements, even if each can be described as participation.

The government reportedly identified *démocratie lycéenne* as one of its workstreams. The key questions are therefore practical. Who can put an issue on the agenda? What happens to proposals that officials dislike? Are reasons given for rejection? Can pupils examine the published summary of what they said? Is there a route to challenge misrepresentation?

A consultation is a means of gathering information. Democratic authority concerns what can be done with that information and who is entitled to decide. A ministry may sincerely want more pupil feedback while remaining unwilling to share any particular decision-making power. We need the actual proposals to determine which arrangement is on offer.

## 9. 💶 Grants and financial autonomy: education does not pay the rent

The sixth category turns from the classroom to the student's ability to remain in education at all. The cost of food, transport, accommodation and materials can determine whether an apparently available opportunity is realistically accessible.

The earlier research register includes **a €200 grant increase** and **€1,337 in proposed autonomy income**. The precise French terminology, frequency, eligibility and relationship between those measures have not yet been verified from the original programme. Accordingly, they remain **numerical leads**, not firm quotations or costed policy descriptions in this node.

That qualification does not diminish the underlying question. A government may expand places, advertise courses and improve admissions guidance, yet still leave a student unable to afford the conditions necessary to attend. Access is not only an admissions decision. It is the ability to continue once admitted.

The five reported lycée workstreams do not, on their face, provide a complete answer to student grants or income support. That does **not** prove that no separate government policy exists. It tells us where to look next: higher-education and finance decisions, eligibility regulations, budget measures and actual payment arrangements.

## 10. 🍽️ The €1 meal: the price and the queue

The seventh category concerns access to **€1 student meals**. The detail matters. A reduced price for a limited group is not the same policy as a universal entitlement. A price that appears on a poster is not the same thing as an accessible meal when a student has a short break, no nearby facility or a queue longer than the time available.

The original programme needs to be checked for the exact eligibility demand. Implementation would then require more than a ministerial declaration: we would need to know which providers are covered, how many meals can be served, whether the entitlement is available in practice and whether students can use it without disproportionate administrative obstacles.

This is an especially neat example of the difference between an official representation and a material outcome. The government may record that meals are available at €1. The student may still be hungry. Both statements can be true, and that is precisely why the second one must not disappear into the first.

## 11. 🌍 International fees and the CVEC: which barriers count?

The eighth category concerns differentiated tuition fees for some international students and the **CVEC**, the *Contribution de vie étudiante et de campus*. These are distinct charges with distinct rules, purposes and exemptions. Abolishing one would not automatically abolish the other.

This is also where the limits of a lycée-only government response become particularly visible. International university fees and student-life contributions do not fit neatly into a consultation about secondary-school timetables. That is an issue of institutional jurisdiction, not a reason to delete them from the original organisation's programme.

A useful response would specify whether the government accepts the proposed change, what legal instrument would be required, who has authority to enact it and when a revised fee schedule would take effect. Silence in a letter about lycée reform is **not** itself a formal rejection of a higher-education demand.

The politics of who gets to study in France should not be reduced to whether the correct departmental inbox received the message.

## 12. 🏠 Housing: an announcement is not a room

The ninth category addresses student accommodation, including rent protection and additional housing associated with the **CROUS** network. The working research records a target of **15,000 additional units**; its exact timeframe and whether it refers to construction, acquisition or another measure must be verified before presenting it as a settled programme figure.

Housing policy is full of quantities that sound similar and mean different things: places announced, projects approved, funding allocated, units built, units ready for occupancy, units available at an affordable rent, and students actually housed. An honest progress report should distinguish them.

There is also a geographical problem. A national increase in accommodation does not necessarily help the student whose course is in a city where affordable rooms remain unavailable. As with teachers, a total can rise while the experienced service remains absent at the point where somebody needs it.

The student does not sleep in the funding announcement. The student needs the room.

## 13. 🚨 Policing and the right to organise

The tenth category concerns repression, police violence and the treatment of student mobilisation. It requires especially careful language because several different processes can become entangled in a single account of a protest: a lawful demonstration, a school blockade, an allegation of excessive force, a disciplinary decision, a police operation, an injury, and a later investigation.

These are not interchangeable. Nor is an allegation proof of a particular officer's intent. The specialist policing nodes must establish individual events with dates, locations, evidence and the relevant legal authority.

Nevertheless, the presence of policing in a student programme is itself politically significant. It indicates that the ability to express a demand, not only the content of that demand, is contested. A pupil who believes participation risks an unjustified sanction or harmful intervention is dealing with a different democratic environment from one in which dissent can be expressed safely.

The reported five-workstream lycée response does not include a specific policing strand. That is a **mapping observation**, not proof that no relevant state response exists elsewhere. Police and prefectural responsibilities cannot be silently reassigned to the education ministry simply because it is the institution receiving a letter.

A placard documented in Montpellier captured the contrast in wonderfully direct terms: **« Envoyez-nous des profs, pas la police »** — *Send us teachers, not police.* A slogan is not a staffing audit or a police incident report. It is a political comparison of which public-service presence the speaker thinks has been prioritised.

## 14. 🏛️ The Republic replies: five workstreams

The Stage One audit records a letter attributed to Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu and dated **4 October 2026**, followed by a national lycée consultation opening on **5 October**. The working source is the Education Ministry's publication, **« Lycéennes, lycéens : vous avez la parole »** — *School students: you have the floor.* The audit identifies five areas: teacher replacement, buildings, timetables, Parcoursup transparency and lycée democracy.

Those areas deserve examination on their own terms. Improving teacher cover might prevent missed lessons. Renovating buildings might remove serious barriers. A clearer admissions process could be valuable to applicants even if it does not satisfy the demand to end selection. Not every narrower measure is worthless because it is narrower.

But neither should a narrower measure be relabelled as the thing originally requested.

| Reported government workstream | Closest demand area | Comparison | What the available material does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Teacher replacement | Staffing and welfare provision | **Partial** | Permanent posts or specialist support delivered |
| Buildings | Safe, usable learning spaces | **Overlap** | Particular repairs completed |
| School timetables | Organisation of the school day | **Overlap** | Requested timetable changes adopted |
| Parcoursup transparency | Admissions and selection | **Reframed** | End of selection through the platform |
| Lycée democracy | Pupil representation and authority | **Overlap** | New binding powers for pupils |

**Not identified in this five-part summary:** most of the programme's grants, meals, university fees, housing and policing proposals. This is a statement about the **scope of the reviewed response**, not a claim that the government has rejected every other proposal or taken no other action.

The exact letter, deadlines and any commitments to implementation require checking against the original publication. A dated intention to act is still an intention, however reassuringly it is typeset.

## 16. 🤝 Who was in the meeting?

The project audit records a **7 October** meeting involving Higher Education Minister Philippe Baptiste and an inter-union group representing higher-education **staff** organisations. A statement published through CGT FERC Sup reportedly explained the group's opposition to the proposed budget and its decision not to continue the meeting.

That event matters, but it must not be described as though lycée pupils collectively walked out of a meeting with the minister. Staff representatives have their own mandates, grievances and strategies. Their demands can overlap with those of pupils and students without authorising them to speak on behalf of everybody in the movement.

Likewise, a national lycée consultation does not automatically mean every pupil organisation has negotiated with the Prime Minister. A questionnaire, a meeting, a delegation, an elected council and a public demonstration are different channels for conveying information. Each should have its own date, participants, original statement and documented outcome.

Where no verified minutes have been recovered, we cannot write a plausible-sounding conversation and pass it off as history. France has supplied enough real bureaucracy without us having to invent additional minutes.

## 17. 🗣️ “You have the floor”: who controls the questions?

A government consultation can be useful. It may collect information from pupils who would not attend a demonstration or join a union. It can identify problems that national organisations missed. It can create a public record against which subsequent decisions are assessed.

But the design matters. Who decides the questions? Can pupils introduce an issue outside the preselected categories? Are responses published in full, summarised or sorted into administrative themes? Are pupils told which proposals were rejected and why? Can they challenge the official interpretation of what they said?

The Stage One audit attributes to Education Minister Édouard Geffray a statement in the **7 October National Assembly plenary** that the consultation had received **35,000 responses in 24 hours**. That is a ministerial reported figure. It does not, without the underlying methodology, establish 35,000 unique respondents, representativeness, support for a particular policy or the number of problems subsequently solved. The committee hearing held on the same date is a **separate parliamentary event** and should not be confused with the plenary exchange.

There is an important cybernetic distinction here. A system may become very efficient at **receiving signals** while remaining poor at **changing the conditions that generate them**. Counting responses measures one stage of the process. It is not a measure of educational restoration.

The useful sequence is:

**Complaint received → meaning preserved → authority identified → reasoned decision → resources allocated → change implemented → outcome experienced → correction possible.**

If a grievance falls out between the second and third steps, a government can announce a very successful listening exercise while the person who raised it continues to experience exactly the same problem.

## 18. ⚖️ Parliament, budgets and the limits of a letter

Different demands require different kinds of authority. A ministry may be able to issue guidance. A rectorat may organise recruitment or deployment. A region may have a role in a lycée's physical estate. A university or CROUS body may administer a service. Parliament may need to change a law or approve funding. Some demands cross several of these boundaries at once.

That complexity is real. It should be explained accurately. It should not be allowed to become an all-purpose device for making the original complaint disappear.

The Stage One research identifies a **7 October parliamentary exchange** between Lisa Belluco and Édouard Geffray concerning the government's response and education spending. It also identifies disputes over the interpretation of the proposed **2027 education budget**, including nominal increases and real-terms arguments. Those figures belong in the separate funding node, where the accounting basis and official tables can be compared properly.

For this node, the governing question is simpler: **what resources and powers would be required to deliver each demand?** A proposed budget is not enacted expenditure. An authorised post is not a teacher in a classroom. An invitation to participate is not a transfer of authority. An announced housing target is not a student holding a key.

The distinctions are not hostile to government. They are the minimum conditions for finding out whether government has achieved what it says it is trying to do.

## 19. 🏫 Saint-Exupéry: a practical examination for the state

Lycée Saint-Exupéry in Créteil provides a concrete test of the difference between an announcement and a changed condition. The project chronology records staff mobilisation in mid-September, pupil action beginning around **18 September**, and a **20 September** local account of missing posts. Subsequent reporting described classes resuming, administrative recruitment decisions and continuing problems in early October, including concerns about disability support and maintenance.

The school should not be turned into a symbolic miniature of every lycée in France. Nor should one report of resumption be used to conclude that every deficiency was resolved. The proper questions are specific: which positions were vacant on which date; which appointments followed; who actually started work; what services were restored; and which pupils still lacked provision?

That is what it would mean to evaluate a response rather than merely report one. The same method applies to the national programme. A demand should remain recognisable all the way through the administrative process, and the claimed result should be visible where the problem was first experienced.

For pupils waiting on a nurse, the outcome is not that a workstream now contains the word *health*. It is whether there is a nurse.

## 20. ♻️ The translation machine

The most revealing comparisons are not always between support and opposition. They are between **different descriptions of the problem**.

**“We need teachers”** can become **“we will improve teacher replacement.”** The second may be useful, but it can address temporary cover without resolving a structural shortage or specialist vacancy.

**“End selection”** can become **“make Parcoursup more transparent.”** The second can improve procedural clarity while preserving the system whose legitimacy is being challenged.

**“Give us a say”** can become **“tell us what you think.”** The second may create information without creating any right to influence the decision.

This is institutional translation. Translation is unavoidable: an organisation's programme cannot be implemented without legal categories, budgets, staff responsibilities and administrative decisions. The problem begins when translation is treated as permission to alter the demand without saying that it has been altered.

A faithful response can disagree. It can explain a constraint. It can offer a narrower alternative and name the difference. It can say which authority has to act next. What it cannot reasonably do is present the narrower alternative as though it were identical to the original request.

That is why the ledger matters. It preserves the original signal alongside the official interpretation. Readers can see whether a proposal was accepted, modified, rejected, referred elsewhere or simply not addressed in the documents reviewed. The original speaker does not vanish inside the government's preferred vocabulary.

## 21. 🧿 What would an answer actually look like?

A meaningful answer to a student demand needs several parts. First, it should state the demand accurately, with the original speaker or organisation identified. Second, it should identify who has the power to act. Third, it should say what has been decided, including where the answer is no. Fourth, it should describe the mechanism: funding, recruitment, rule change, procurement, timetable or enforceable protection. Fifth, it should provide a date and a means of checking the result. Finally, it should leave room for the people affected to report that the change did not work.

A government is not obliged to agree with every demand. Democracy does not mean that every placard becomes legislation by teatime. But the people making the demand should be able to find out whether their proposal was understood, what happened to it and why.

That is particularly important when the complainants are young. Institutions routinely teach pupils that their voices matter. The proposition deserves to be tested against the adult institution's behaviour when those voices become inconvenient.

The students have already supplied the words. They have supplied examples, placards, interviews and programmes. The Republic has responded with a consultation and a set of administrative categories. Now the question is whether those categories preserve what was said, whether decisions follow, and whether somebody can point to a changed condition at the other end.

The children have handed in their work. It is the government's turn to show its workings.
