# 🎬 La Haine n’a jamais disparu
**First created:** 2026-10-09 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-09  
*The film is thirty-one years old. Its questions about power, visibility and the right to be heard are considerably less settled.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

France has a film for this. Of course France has a film for this. France has an entire cultural vocabulary for the moment when young people, police officers, journalists and public officials all arrive in the same place with radically different ideas about what is happening.

Mathieu Kassovitz’s *La Haine* (1995) remains an extraordinarily potent way to think about the relationship between young people, exclusion and police power. But a recognisable film is not a substitute for evidence. Nor does the reappearance of a familiar political conflict mean that every intervening institution, community and circumstance has remained frozen since 1995.

This node keeps three things separate: **what the film depicts**, **what historical and social research establishes**, and **what the young people mobilising around education in September–October 2026 have actually said and experienced**. The last group are not extras in a sequel. They have demands of their own, and the courtesy of reading them is a relatively modest request to make of the Republic.

**Scope and evidence:** Film analysis below is interpretive, based on the film’s widely documented premise and narrative; this draft has **not** been checked against a fresh scene-by-scene viewing or timecoded transcript. Historical background is distinguished from the still-incomplete incident and statistical verification required for the 2026 constellation. Specific contemporary allegations should be checked against original reports before publication.

## 1. 🎞️ A film that refuses to become merely historical

*La Haine*, directed by Mathieu Kassovitz and released in 1995, follows three young men — Vinz, Saïd and Hubert — through the aftermath of unrest in a Parisian suburban housing estate. Shot in black and white, it concentrates social and political pressures into an unusually intense stretch of time. Police violence and its consequences are central to the story, but so are friendship, humour, masculinity, boredom, fear, reputation and the question of what power a young person can exercise when powerful institutions seem interested chiefly in containing him.

It is a fictional film. That is not a diminishment. Fiction can arrange experience so that viewers notice relationships they might otherwise miss: between space and movement, between speech and authority, between an official account of public order and the people required to live inside it.

The problem begins when recognition is mistaken for verification. Somebody sees an image of a French protest, remembers *La Haine*, and concludes that the film has explained the event. Perhaps it has suggested a useful question. It has not established who did what, to whom, under which powers, with which consequences, on a particular day in October 2026.

We need both capacities: the imagination to recognise patterns and the discipline to investigate them.

## 2. 🧱 The banlieue is a place, not a personality disorder

The French word *banlieue* means suburb. It does not inherently mean a deprived neighbourhood, a social-housing estate, an immigrant community or a site of unrest. There are affluent suburbs and disadvantaged suburbs; different communes contain very different histories, services and opportunities. French policy uses more specific categories, including *quartiers prioritaires de la politique de la ville* (QPV), when identifying neighbourhoods for targeted intervention.

That distinction matters. *La Haine* represents a particular social world. It cannot stand in for every French suburb or every young person who lives beyond central Paris. Nor should the social and ethnic composition of a neighbourhood be inferred from the existence of a police operation there.

To investigate exclusion materially, the questions are prosaic: Where are the jobs? How reliable is transport? Which schools have stable staffing? What housing is available? How are healthcare, disability support and public services distributed? What are the relevant figures, over which years, and compared with which populations?

Cinema can show a character waiting, travelling, being stopped or having nowhere particularly safe to go. Statistics and administrative records are needed to establish the distribution of those experiences. Both can matter. They are not the same evidence.

## 3. 🚨 Who does the state turn up for?

One of the film’s enduring provocations concerns the visibility of the state. Institutions can be experienced as distant or ineffective when people need support, yet suddenly become immediate and physically consequential when those same people are treated as a public-order problem.

That is an interpretive reading of the film, not a claim that policing is the only state presence in disadvantaged communities. Schools, hospitals, social workers, local government and other services also exist, and their quality varies. The question is which parts of the state become most visible, to whom, and in what circumstances.

Now consider the 2026 education mobilisation. Its documented demands, as assembled in the companion research, concern teaching staff, disability assistance, buildings, admissions, student welfare and representation. Some protests have also involved police deployment and allegations about police conduct. Those are important events to investigate. But an account that begins and ends with the police can make the original reason for the mobilisation vanish.

A pupil asks where their teacher is. The state’s public-order machinery may have an answer about access to the school gates. It does not necessarily have an answer about the teacher.

The distinction is not that one question is illegitimate. Schools must remain safe, and different protest tactics raise different legal issues. The distinction is that **restoring access, restoring public order and restoring educational provision are three separate outcomes**. A government can achieve one while leaving the others unresolved.

## 4. 🗣️ A camera is also an argument about whose account matters

*La Haine* places Vinz, Saïd and Hubert at the centre of the viewer’s attention. They are allowed to be contradictory. They can be funny and frightening, thoughtful and performative, close to one another and at odds with one another. That is a significant part of the film’s political intelligence: the protagonists are not reduced to a single public-order category.

Yet the camera’s generosity is selective, as every camera’s must be. A film makes choices about whose interior life becomes available to the audience. Journalism makes similar choices about whom to interview and which quotation to place in a headline. Institutions make choices about whose account enters an official record, and under what heading.

The students of 2026 have supplied their own language. The companion node `📣_les_mots_de_la_colere.md` preserves reported placards, interviews and collective statements in French, with translations and provenance notes. That language ranges from practical complaints to jokes, declarations of rights and demands for political participation.

A placard about missing teachers is not a line of film dialogue. It belongs to a person or group participating in a real dispute. It must be attributed and contextualised accordingly. The joke can be funny without its speaker becoming a fictional character for the convenience of a cultural essay.

## 5. ⏳ Three dates, three different historical questions

### 1995: the film and its moment

The film belongs to a period of public debate about police violence, social exclusion, immigration, race and the relationship between the French Republic and its suburban communities. Its release and reception form part of that history. A serious historical account should check contemporary reviews, interviews with Kassovitz, the production record and scholarship on the period rather than retroactively assigning the film a single cause or political programme.

### 2005: unrest beyond the cinema

The deaths of teenagers Zyed Benna and Bouna Traoré in Clichy-sous-Bois preceded widespread unrest in France in 2005. The events, subsequent legal proceedings and official responses are historical subjects in their own right. The film may provide an evocative comparison, but the 2005 participants were not replaying a screenplay. Their circumstances and actions require their own sources.

### 2026: pupils with a programme

The September–October 2026 education mobilisation, as represented in this project’s working evidence registers, involves school conditions, student organisations, education staff, government consultation and budget proposals. It includes reported police encounters, but it is not identical to either the film’s fictional conflict or the 2005 unrest.

| Question | 1995 | 2005 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| What are we examining? | A fictional narrative and its contemporary reception | Historical deaths, unrest and state responses | Contemporary education mobilisation and institutional responses |
| Whose voice matters? | Characters, filmmakers, critics and contemporary communities | Participants, families, witnesses, investigators and public authorities | Pupils, unions, staff, families, ministers and witnesses |
| What establishes facts? | Film text and production/reception records | Contemporary reporting, archives and proceedings | Dated statements, reporting, footage, budget documents and school records |
| What must not be assumed? | Fiction is a literal incident record | Every locality had identical motives | Every pupil shares one experience or every police encounter is unlawful |

A thirty-one-year span is enough time for institutions, law, population and social conditions to change. Whether those changes have solved the problems that matter is an empirical question. The film’s continuing emotional force cannot answer it on its own.

## 6. ⚖️ The police are not one character either

French policing is institutionally differentiated. The *Police nationale*, *Gendarmerie nationale* and *police municipale* do not have identical powers, territorial roles or chains of command. Specialist public-order units, prefectural authority and judicial supervision add further distinctions. A police officer conducting an identity check and a unit managing a large demonstration are not performing interchangeable functions.

These distinctions matter for accountability. Who ordered a deployment? Which force attended? What legal basis was invoked? Who could authorise particular measures? What review mechanisms applied? A political argument about policing becomes stronger, not weaker, when it identifies the correct institution.

Our companion `⚖️_histoire_de_la_police_francaise.md` covers the longer genealogy, including the monarchy, republican transformations, Vichy, colonial policing, 1968 and modern oversight. The present node has a narrower job: to prevent cinematic shorthand from obscuring the actual structure of authority.

The Republic does not have a single police character whose motivations can be revealed in the final act. It has laws, offices, operational chains and records. Unromantic, admittedly. Also rather useful when somebody needs to establish responsibility.

## 7. ♀️ The boys get the camera. Who else was there?

*La Haine* is centred on three young men. Its account of masculinity is not incidental: friendship, bravado, humiliation, the performance of fearlessness and the pressures of status shape how the characters understand their options.

That focus gives the film much of its power. It also sets limits on what the film can represent. The lives and political actions of girls and women are not made visible merely because the male protagonists belong to a marginalised community. An analysis of the film’s gendered perspective need not demand that it become a different film; it should notice what the chosen perspective excludes.

The same discipline applies to 2026 coverage. If a newspaper photographs boys at a blockade, that does not establish that girls were absent from organising. If a union spokesperson is a man, it does not establish that the movement’s agenda is male. Equally, a single interview with a girl does not prove equal influence within a collective.

For `♀️_les_filles_aussi_sont_en_colere.md`, the useful evidence will be attributed testimony, organisational roles, speeches, leaflets, photographs with reliable context and any documented differences in participation or treatment. We should ask **who organises, who is quoted, who is believed and who bears the consequences**, rather than simply count faces in a few images.

## 8. 🌍 Race, citizenship and the universal Republic

The film’s characters, and the way viewers discuss them, raise questions about racism, migration, belonging and the universal language of French citizenship. Those questions cannot be resolved by using *banlieue*, *immigrant*, *Muslim*, *Black*, *Arab*, *Jewish* and *young* as if they were synonyms. They are not.

French republican universalism is both a political tradition and a subject of dispute: a commitment to equal citizenship can coexist with disagreement about whether institutions recognise and remedy unequal treatment in practice. Investigating that tension requires historical context, carefully defined categories and the testimony of people who actually experience the institutions concerned.

Our working student-language corpus includes a reported interview in which a pupil connects his participation to being born in France and having rights. The quotation should only be used in the finished published node with its original context and attribution checked against the source. Its significance is not that it proves the circumstances of every pupil; it illustrates how a protest about education can also become a claim about who is entitled to be heard as a citizen.

The film helps make that question legible. It does not supply the answer on behalf of a student speaking three decades later.

## 9. 📺 A recognisable plot is very convenient for journalism

Journalists work under pressures of time, space and audience attention. A familiar cultural reference can communicate a great deal quickly. *La Haine* offers an instantly recognisable frame for stories about policing, youth and the French suburbs.

That can be useful. It can also produce a narrative shortcut: young people are angry, police arrive, familiar images circulate, and the story becomes another instalment of an already known national drama. The original dispute over teaching hours, disability support or school maintenance may receive less attention than the confrontation at the gates.

**This is a hypothesis to test, not a finding about all October 2026 coverage.** To establish it, we need a dated sample of reporting: headlines, images, who was quoted, whether educational demands were explained, whether official statements were challenged, and whether *La Haine* was actually invoked. No specific 2026 article should be accused of using the film as a frame until we have read it.

The companion `📺_qui_raconte_la_revolte.md` will undertake that media comparison. Here the question is conceptual: when does cultural memory help a reader understand an event, and when does it give the reader permission to stop investigating?

## 10. 🧿 Recognition is not proof

There is a useful research sequence hidden inside the impulse to say, “This looks like *La Haine*.”

1. **Recognition:** Something about the event resembles a pattern represented in the film.
2. **Hypothesis:** Perhaps a similar relationship between institutions and young people is operating.
3. **Evidence request:** What would show whether that relationship exists here?
4. **Investigation:** Collect original statements, dates, footage, administrative records, legal provisions and independent reporting.
5. **Finding:** State what the evidence supports, what contradicts the hypothesis and what remains unknown.

That sequence allows art to contribute to political understanding without being promoted into an eyewitness.

| Film motif | Contemporary question | Evidence needed | What the film cannot establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Young people treated as a security problem | Were educational grievances displaced by public-order framing? | Protest reporting, original demands, official communications | Why an individual deployment was ordered |
| Unequal access to institutional attention | Were complaints about provision addressed before or after protest? | Dated school complaints, staffing records, response chronology | The staffing level of a particular lycée |
| Distrust between communities and police | What do participants report, and what do oversight records show? | Attributed testimony, legal and oversight material | Whether a particular use of force was unlawful |
| Male-centred public narrative | Whose organising and testimony reached the public? | Interviews, organisational records, media sample | The gender composition of the movement |

A resemblance can justify asking a question. It cannot justify skipping the answer.

## 11. ♻️ Embodied Information Ecology: the fight over the frame

The film offers a way of feeling the organisation of space, attention and threat from the protagonists’ point of view. That is one of cinema’s distinctive capacities. A viewer is not merely told that a situation is tense; the film’s pacing, composition and character relationships organise the viewer’s experience of it.

Institutions have their own framing devices. A school records attendance and staffing. A ministry records consultations, appropriations and posts. Police record incidents, orders and offences. Journalists record quotations, photographs and headlines. None of these descriptions is necessarily false. Each can be incomplete in ways that matter.

Consider the chain:

**Missing provision → pupils’ experience → collective complaint → demonstration → administrative or police classification → media account → political response → actual service outcome.**

Information is selected at every transition. The pupil’s experience of an untaught lesson may become a staffing vacancy, a budget line, a disruption statistic or a quotation about unrest. Some translations make action possible. Others make the original problem harder to see.

This is where *La Haine* remains useful to Polaris: it reminds us that the viewpoint through which an event is processed changes what becomes salient. But the project must then do the unglamorous work of reconnecting those viewpoints to verifiable conditions.

**Did the lesson happen? Was the support worker present? Did the repair occur?** The answers are not found in a cinematic analogy or a ministerial adjective. They are found in the school.

## 12. 🎥 The danger of a beautiful ending

**Film-ending discussion; broad thematic spoilers follow.**

*La Haine* builds towards a confrontation that refuses a comforting sense of resolution. Its narrative force depends partly on the sense that what matters is not only one dramatic moment, but the conditions that have brought people to it. The film’s well-known falling motif makes the distinction between an apparently tolerable trajectory and its eventual consequences central to its political meaning.

A finished film can leave the viewer with an unforgettable image. Public institutions do not have the luxury of ending their obligations at the credits. If the 2026 education mobilisation produces a ministerial meeting, a consultation or a staffing announcement, that is not automatically the end of the story. There are classrooms to open, support hours to deliver, buildings to maintain and students to teach.

There is a temptation to end this essay with a grand claim that France has learned nothing since 1995. It would be rhetorically tidy and evidentially lazy. Some things have changed. Others may not have changed enough. Different populations experience different combinations of progress and failure. Those are questions for records, not a dramatic flourish.

The more uncomfortable ending is less cinematic: **the people who raised the problem remain entitled to find out what happened next**.
