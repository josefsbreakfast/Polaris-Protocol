# 🦺 Jaunes et Jeunes : Nos Luttes Se Rencontrent

**First created:** 2026-10-17 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-17  
*The yellow vests, the young, and the political engineering of finding one another without becoming the same thing.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

France has an administrative gift for declaring an argument finished while the people having it are still standing outside. Sometimes they are standing outside a school. Sometimes a hospital. Sometimes a petrol station. Sometimes, with magnificent geographical specificity, they are standing on a roundabout.

On 17 October 2026, organisers using the name **« Jaunes et jeunes : nos luttes se rencontrent »** — *Yellow and young: our struggles meet* — called for a convergence of Yellow Vest networks, pupils, students, workers, firefighters, farmers, public-service staff, climate campaigners, people from working-class neighbourhoods, pensioners and others. The [17 October mobilisation website](https://17octobregiletjaune.net/) publishes an appeal, a calendar, a mobilisation map, a petition, and downloadable publicity materials. Most strikingly for this constellation, it reproduces **Union Étudiante's ten demands** in a series of campaign graphics.

This is evidence of an attempt to construct political solidarity. It is **not**, on its own, evidence that every advertised movement formally joined, that every advertised action occurred, or that the announced convergence succeeded. The distinction is essential. A political invitation is an event in the information environment; a coalition is something that must be built and sustained.

The title is careful about this. *Se rencontrent*: meet. Not merge, not surrender their separate histories, not acquire a new central committee and a complimentary laminated badge. **Meet.**

This node reads the organisers' published materials as primary sources: what they say, what they distribute, whom they address, and which political relationships they attempt to make possible. Statistics and allegations appearing in the appeal are attributed to its authors unless independently checked.

---

## 1. 🦺 A Movement That Refuses the Past Tense

The original Gilets jaunes mobilisation erupted in 2018 around fuel taxation and the cost of living, before developing a much wider set of demands about purchasing power, taxation, political representation and democratic accountability. The yellow high-visibility vest became an extraordinary political object: ordinary, cheap, compulsory equipment for motorists, suddenly repurposed as an emblem of collective visibility.

Its genius was partly logistical. You did not need to order a banner, belong to a party or obtain permission from a trade-union branch. The equipment was already in the car. The roundabout, meanwhile, was not simply a picturesque French eccentricity. It was a piece of infrastructure through which people, deliveries, commuting and local economic life moved. A place where those normally treated as passing traffic could stop and become visible to one another.

Eight years later, the 17 October appeal explicitly invokes that history. Its organisers write of putting the vest on again and of new Yellow Vests joining. That is **their claim of continuity**, rather than proof that the 2018 and 2026 mobilisations have identical participants, leadership, social composition or politics.

The distinction matters because nostalgia can be an organisational shortcut. Calling something a return may make it legible to the public while concealing what is new. Here the new element is not incidental. It is announced in the title: **the young are not merely an audience for somebody else's remembered revolt.**

The appeal describes pupils who were children during the original protests and now possess their own reasons to mobilise. It presents the generation shaped by school blockades and digital communication alongside the generation remembered for roundabouts. That is a deliberate generational bridge, not a demonstration that the generations already agree on everything.

## 2. 📅 The Calendar Is Doing Political Work

The site's calendar brings otherwise distinct campaigns into one view. It lists climate and social-justice marches on 26 September; firefighters' mobilisation on 27–29 September; a public-sector strike on 29 September; anti-pesticide actions on 3 and 10–11 October; a campaign against sexual violence on 5 October; national student demonstrations on 6 October; a decolonial march on 12 October; a convergence at Matignon on 13 October; and several events on 17 October itself.

Those 17 October entries are especially revealing. The calendar separately names the **World Day for the Eradication of Poverty**, commemorations of **17 October 1961**, and **« Jaunes et jeunes : nos luttes se rencontrent »**. Later entries advertise meetings and actions extending into November 2026 and spring 2027. The organisers are presenting a political season, not merely a single Saturday.

But a calendar is not a membership register. It can record an event the editors support, an event they wish their readers to attend, or an event they consider part of a related political landscape. It does not, without corroboration, establish reciprocal endorsement by the organisers of every listed action.

That qualification does not make the calendar insignificant. Quite the reverse. It tells us what relationships the editors wish visitors to **see**. A calendar is a classification system with dates attached. Its politics lie partly in what appears alongside what.

The firefighters are next to the public-sector workers. The students appear alongside climate activists. The memory of colonial police violence shares a date with an appeal against contemporary police violence. The organising work begins before anybody reaches the street: somebody has arranged for these subjects to become neighbours on a page.

## 3. 📣 The Students' Homework Has Been Distributed

The strongest evidence of active cross-movement communication is not the calendar. It is the site's **Visuels à partager** — *visuals to share* — section.

The website offers a graphic listing Union Étudiante's ten emergency measures, followed by individual graphics for each demand. Each is offered in **square, portrait and story** formats. This is a small but consequential design choice. A policy programme has been translated into objects that can travel through different social-media interfaces and audiences.

The demands reproduced there include:

1. An emergency programme to renovate secondary schools and universities.
2. Large-scale recruitment of education and support staff.
3. Changes to school and university timetables.
4. Ending selection through Parcoursup and Mon Master.
5. Greater pupil participation in decisions.
6. Higher grants and housing support.
7. A one-euro meal for all students.
8. Repeal of the *Bienvenue en France* fee policy and abolition of the CVEC charge.
9. Frozen Crous rents and more student housing.
10. An end to repression and police violence against student mobilisation.

These are not ten variations on a demand for more pocket money. They concern buildings, staffing, time, access, democratic participation, housing, food, migration status and policing. The programme describes an educational system as a lived environment, not merely an examination-production facility.

The source of the programme is explicitly identified on the graphics as **Union Étudiante**. The 17 October website reproduces it and directs readers towards the union's programme. That establishes **redistribution**. It does not automatically establish that Union Étudiante signed the 17 October appeal, helped edit the site, or accepted the wider movement's entire political platform. Those questions require statements from the union itself.

Still: a political network has decided that these ten demands belong in the material it asks supporters to circulate. That is not nothing. The political homework has escaped the classroom. The children may wish to discuss marking criteria.

## 4. 🏫 The Republic Has a Chart; the Toilets Do Not Work

The first student graphic calls for **« Un plan d'urgence pour rénover les lycées et les facs »** — *an emergency plan to renovate secondary schools and universities*. Its supporting text names cooling during heatwaves, insulation and heating in cold weather, and clean, functioning toilets.

This is an almost aggressively material demand. Not an abstract argument about national greatness. Not a philosophical symposium about the future of education. **Could the room be a tolerable temperature, and could the toilets work?**

Those questions reveal why school infrastructure belongs in a wider argument about living costs and public provision. A classroom is simultaneously a learning environment, a workplace, a public asset and a physical environment experienced by children and adults. Poor insulation means public expenditure, thermal discomfort, and potentially lost concentration. Inadequate sanitation means more than an embarrassing facilities report: it affects health, dignity and whether a person can comfortably remain in the institution throughout the day.

Different observers may see the same failing building as different problems. The pupil experiences it through their body. The teacher experiences it through working conditions. The parent experiences it through worry and household routines. The local authority experiences a maintenance backlog. The treasury sees a spending line. All are observing the same infrastructure, but not receiving the same information from it.

This is precisely where **Embodied Information Ecology** becomes useful. Information is not simply the number in the facilities spreadsheet. It is also what happens when a child cannot concentrate in a room that is too hot, or plans their day around avoiding a toilet they cannot use. Those experiences are signals about institutional functioning. Whether the institution recognises them as signals is another matter.

The Republic has an impressive organisational chart. The children would still quite like the toilets to work.

## 5. ⛽ From the Price on the Pump to the Refinery Margin

Fuel prices form the clearest historical bridge between 2018 and the organisers' 2026 appeal. The website displays a comparison between May 2017 and 2026, including a rise in the stated average diesel price from **€1.210 to €2.257 per litre**, against a stated rise in the monthly gross minimum wage from **€1,480.27 to €1,867.02**. Its headline contrasts approximately **86.6%** diesel-price growth with **26.1%** growth in the SMIC.

These are the organisers' displayed figures and selected comparison dates. A percentage change in the pump price is not identical to a change in total household fuel expenditure; neither does a nominal wage comparison measure purchasing power on its own. Vehicle use, income, taxation, geography, inflation and alternative transport all affect the burden. The figures matter politically, but the interpretation requires more than dividing one number by another and declaring the Republic mathematically solved.

A separate explanation on the site turns from retail prices to refining. It displays a gross refining-margin indicator of **$4.53 per barrel in May 2017**, **$8.72 as the 2025 average**, and **$38.05 in August 2026**. The displayed August figure is approximately **4.36 times** the 2025 average. The page itself notes that the indicator is calculated from a Rotterdam basket of refined products and **is not the realised profit margin of each individual refinery**. That qualification must travel with the number.

The organisers distinguish the upstream refining indicator from the retail distribution margin and explain why they are publicising actions near petrol stations, refineries and depots. Their political argument is that the visible pump price connects to less visible economic relationships further along the supply chain.

This does not establish that any one company's profits caused a particular pump-price change. It establishes the **economic story the mobilisation wants to make visible**: the consumer encounters the price at the pump, while the organisers want the argument to travel upstream.

The roundabout, once again, has acquired an interest in supply chains.

## 6. 💶 The Petition's Catalogue of Grievances

The site's petition section reproduces a text attributed to **Peuple RÉVOLTÉ**, with a stated capture date of **16 September 2026**. The screenshots supplied for this node show **21,942 signatures** displayed at the moment they were taken. That is a snapshot of the site's reported petition count, not a count of demonstrators or a forecast of attendance.

The petition invokes the beginning of the Yellow Vest movement in November 2018 and argues that living conditions have deteriorated. It names petrol and diesel, energy bills, food, rents, poverty, pensions, unemployment insurance, climate inaction and what it characterises as repeated violations of democratic principles.

It supplies a sequence of quantitative claims: petrol up 47%, diesel up 55%, food up 30%, electricity up 40%, gas up 92%, rents up approximately 11.6%, and general inflation up approximately 22%, each attached to different end dates. It also describes changes to poverty, pension eligibility and unemployment-insurance rules.

**These figures should be treated as claims made by the petition, not as an already audited statistical table.** The periods, price series, population definitions and policy assumptions differ. The site's separate 2017–2026 fuel table uses different baselines and therefore gives different percentage increases; those are not necessarily contradictions, but they cannot be silently blended into one historical comparison.

This is itself informative. A petition is not a neutral statistical yearbook. It is a political document assembling reasons to act. The analytical task is to preserve its language and identify its evidentiary status without pretending that the urgency of the argument makes every denominator interchangeable.

What binds the grievances is the claim that economic insecurity and democratic powerlessness are connected. The petition is not simply asking whether people can afford their weekly shop. It is asking who decides the conditions under which they must live, and what recourse they have when those decisions hurt.

## 7. 🧩 Three Different Meanings of Convergence

The organisers' central verb is **converger**: to converge. That word can describe at least three different political processes.

**Issue convergence** occurs when people recognise that apparently separate problems may share causes, institutions or policy choices. School maintenance, hospital staffing and income support are not the same problem, but each may involve public spending, labour conditions and access to basic services.

**Mobilisation convergence** occurs when people coordinate communication, dates, routes or events. The website clearly attempts this: it publishes a calendar, a map, publicity graphics and an appeal for common action. The organisers propose both declared joint marches and other forms of mobilisation at different locations.

**Organisational convergence** goes further. It would require evidence of agreed representation, decision-making, resources, negotiating positions or sustained joint structures. A list of individual signatories and participating organisations provides evidence of support for the appeal; it does not, by itself, establish that every group named in the prose has formally joined a governing coalition.

The site does publish **named first signatories** and an **organisations signataires** section. At the time of review, the latter names Coordination régionale Bloquons tout Île-de-France, Indignons-nous/Bloquons tout, Maintenant!, and Peuple RÉVOLTÉ. It also identifies these groups in its site-editor information. This is more specific evidence than a calendar entry, and should be recorded as such.

The distinction between *named as a constituency*, *listed on a calendar*, *having one's demands reproduced*, and *signing the appeal* is not bureaucratic nit-picking. It is how we avoid accidentally assigning political commitments to people who have not made them.

A coalition should be able to recognise different levels of participation. Otherwise, the moment somebody says 'we', somebody else must ask who authorised the pronoun.

## 8. ♻️ How a Political Message Travels

The site is an unusually legible example of political communication infrastructure. It aggregates dates; presents a shared story; provides a map of proposed actions; routes visitors towards a petition; distributes images in platform-friendly formats; and invites people into a Telegram channel.

Each component performs a different function. The calendar says **these events belong in the same field of attention**. The petition says **these grievances belong in the same argument**. The graphics say **here is language you can carry elsewhere**. The map says **here is where an intention might become a physical encounter**. The channel offers a route for subsequent communication.

None of those mechanisms guarantees turnout. They reduce some of the costs of discovering, recognising and sharing a mobilisation. Whether they generate trust, participation or durable organisation is an empirical question.

The Union Étudiante graphics are especially important because they preserve the provenance of the student programme while placing it within a new distribution network. The message may remain textually stable while its meaning changes for its audience. A student reading about school toilets may recognise daily humiliation. A parent may recognise a public-service failure. A Yellow Vest organiser may recognise a broader pattern of austerity or political neglect. A sceptical observer may see an opportunistic attempt to expand the coalition.

The information has not magically become four different facts. It has entered four different interpretive systems.

This is the same mechanism explored in the Polaris Austen cybernetics work: **the information produced by an observation depends partly on the observer's model, position, interests and prior knowledge**. Lady Catherine attempts to control what an exchange means and accidentally carries information that another recipient reads differently. Here, the process is not accidental. The organisers are deliberately giving student demands new audiences.

The question is whether that creates solidarity, misunderstanding, or some of each.

## 9. 🕰️ The Young Are Not a Historical Re-enactment Society

There is a temptation to tell the 2026 story as a sequel: the original Yellow Vests return, now accompanied by younger supporting characters. The organisers' title suggests something less hierarchical.

Young people in 2026 have their own political experiences. Their demands concern contemporary admissions systems, student housing, grants, school conditions, climate exposure and policing. The experiences of pupils mobilising around their schools are not simply the inherited memories of adults who stood on roundabouts eight years earlier.

Equally, the Yellow Vest tradition is not reducible to fuel. Its participants raised questions about social status, political representation, geographical inequality and the ability of ordinary people to influence decisions between elections. The 17 October appeal explicitly revives that democratic vocabulary.

The potential bridge is therefore not that everybody has suffered identically. It is that different groups may recognise something about **how their problems are governed**.

The appeal invokes a generation who watched the roundabouts as children and now organise through social media. This is the organisers' framing, not a measured account of all young participants' political development. But it captures a real question for coalition-building: can an older movement offer memory and infrastructure without demanding that a younger movement surrender its own agenda?

The word *rencontrent* leaves that possibility open. Meeting somebody is not the same as inheriting their wardrobe.

## 10. 🕯️ The Seventeenth of October Is Not an Empty Date

The calendar's placement of several events on 17 October deserves more than a passing mention.

The **World Day for the Eradication of Poverty** foregrounds material deprivation and the claim that poverty is a matter of rights, not personal failure. The commemorations of **17 October 1961** invoke a much darker history: the violent repression of Algerian demonstrators in Paris under the authority of the French police, during the Algerian War. These histories cannot be collapsed into a single slogan.

The organisers explicitly refer to 17 October 1961 in their appeal and connect historical racism and state violence to their contemporary political analysis. That is their chosen historical analogy. It deserves careful examination, including attention to the specificity of colonial rule, racism, the Algerian struggle and the people killed. It should not be treated as a decorative precedent for every contemporary dispute with police.

The juxtaposition is nevertheless revealing. The calendar makes visible a political landscape in which poverty, colonial memory, policing and contemporary social protest can be considered together. The existence of that juxtaposition does **not** establish that the organisers of the commemorations endorse the 17 October Yellow Vest appeal.

Historical memory is not a free-floating resource available for any coalition to borrow without consequences. It is carried by people, families, communities and institutions. Respectful solidarity requires retaining those particular histories even when identifying structural connections.

## 11. ♀️ Who Gets to Say 'We'?

The appeal's list of constituencies is extensive: students, farmers, firefighters, workers, teachers, healthcare staff, environmental campaigners, working-class neighbourhoods, pensioners, disabled people and people facing unemployment or precarious work. Its calendar also includes campaigns against sexual violence and violence towards children.

That breadth is politically ambitious. It also poses a question that broad coalitions cannot evade: **who gets to define the common struggle?**

A student may want more educational staff without endorsing every economic claim on the site. A climate campaigner may share anger at fossil-fuel profits while disagreeing with other participants about fuel-price interventions. A survivor campaigning against sexual violence may be wary of being treated as one more constituency in a general anti-government photograph. A disabled person may need to know whether the advertised gathering is physically accessible before a declaration of solidarity has practical meaning.

These are not allegations of conflict within this mobilisation. They are tests that any coalition must pass if its inclusive language is to become inclusive practice.

The practical details matter: accessible transport, safe routes, clear information, the ability to participate without joining a particular platform, representation in decision-making, and mechanisms for disagreement. A website can say *everyone*. The actual organising must explain what that means for people whose bodies, resources and risks are not interchangeable.

There is also a gendered danger in the phrase 'the young'. Young women and girls are often asked to stand for the innocence of a movement while being denied authority over its strategy. The student programme itself includes demands for democratic participation. We should ask whether that principle survives when the programme enters a larger political network.

## 12. 📱 A Petition Signature Is Not a Body on a Roundabout

The website is explicit about its digital mechanisms. It offers a petition link, publicity downloads, a mobilisation map, a Telegram channel and a list of signatories. Its privacy notice states that the site retains information associated with signatures to the appeal and distinguishes its own operation from the external Telegram service.

Those features are worth documenting without inflating their meaning. A petition signature indicates an action taken in a particular interface, under that interface's rules. A page view is not an endorsement. A download is not proof that a graphic was shared. A map entry is not proof that an action occurred. A declared demonstration is not a turnout figure.

These are not reasons to dismiss digital mobilisation. They are reasons to understand it properly. Political coordination increasingly depends on the movement of messages between interfaces, but each interface sees a different part of the process. The website can tell us what it published. The petition can report signatures. Independent reporting may establish what happened at a demonstration. Participants may describe how an event felt and what followed. No single sensor supplies the whole account.

For this node, the supplied screenshots preserve the petition display at **21,942 signatures**, as well as the campaign calendar and a student-demand graphic. The live site independently confirms the presence of the appeal, calendar, student graphics and signatory lists. These sources overlap, but they are not identical kinds of evidence.

The distinction is especially important on the date of mobilisation itself. An invitation to gather at a refinery is not an observation of a refinery blockade. We should not let the future tense quietly change into the past tense because a headline looks better that way.

## 13. 🚧 Where Convergence Could Become Difficult

The organisers are attempting to bridge constituencies whose interests may align in some respects and diverge in others. That is not a defect. It is the ordinary condition of democratic politics.

The most obvious tension concerns **the end of the month and the end of the world**: immediate household energy costs and the longer-term environmental consequences of fossil-fuel dependence. The appeal treats both as connected to corporate power. That framing may create a common target, but it does not automatically settle difficult questions about energy transitions, taxation, transport dependence and who bears the costs of change.

Another tension concerns institutional relationships. Students, informal Yellow Vest networks, unions, public-sector workers and campaigning organisations have different decision-making cultures. Some rely on elected representatives or formal bargaining; others value decentralised action. Some can issue legally meaningful strike notices; others cannot. A common graphic does not dissolve those differences.

A third concerns political attribution. When one organisation's demands are reproduced by another network, who speaks for the original authors? Does the wider network preserve their priorities, or use them as supporting evidence for a different campaign? The website's explicit Union Étudiante attribution is a good start. Reciprocal statements, joint decisions and subsequent actions would tell us much more.

Finally, there is the familiar danger of **symbolic convergence without operational convergence**. It is relatively easy to publish a page on which everybody appears. It is harder to coordinate routes, keep people safe, accommodate disagreement, share resources fairly and decide what happens after the day itself.

None of these difficulties disproves the project. They identify the work the project would have to do.

## 14. 🔎 What Would Count as Success?

We should resist the temptation to grade the movement solely by the size of a single demonstration.

**Communication:** Did the student demands reach audiences beyond the original student networks? The website's reproduction of all ten measures provides direct evidence of an attempt to do so. Further evidence would include independent reposting and identifiable discussion in other communities.

**Participation:** Did people from the named constituencies attend common events? This requires contemporaneous reporting, photographs with verified context, participant accounts or other reliable documentation — not merely an advance calendar.

**Organisation:** Did groups establish ongoing channels, assemblies, agreed demands or decision-making processes? The website advertises future meetings, but subsequent records would be needed to establish their occurrence and outcomes.

**Policy:** Did officials respond, negotiate or change decisions? A government statement acknowledging a mobilisation is not the same as granting its demands; conversely, policy effects can be gradual and difficult to attribute.

**Culture:** Did the public begin to interpret school conditions, energy costs, public services and democratic participation as connected political questions? This is harder to measure, but it may be one of the most durable effects of cross-movement communication.

The node therefore needs a later **17 October outcome update**. As first drafted on the day itself, it records the call and the organising architecture, not a completed verdict on events still unfolding.

## 15. 🦺 They Meet. That Is the Point.

The most important word in the title is not *jaunes*. Nor is it *jeunes*.

It is **rencontrent**.

The students have not necessarily become Yellow Vests. The Yellow Vests have not necessarily become student organisers. A firefighter's working conditions are not identical to a pupil's classroom. A pensioner's income is not the same issue as a university applicant's access to a degree. The histories of racism, colonial violence, sexual violence and environmental destruction cannot be made interchangeable simply because they fit on the same calendar.

And yet people can recognise connections without denying differences. They can share information, routes, attention, practical help and political pressure. They can lend one another visibility without demanding ownership of one another's experiences.

That is what makes the organisers' publication of the Union Étudiante graphics worth preserving. It is a concrete act of political transmission. A demand written for one institutional audience is being offered to another. Whether the recipients recognise it, act on it, misunderstand it, or reshape it remains open.

There is no need to announce the birth of a perfectly unified popular front on the strength of a website. There is also no need to pretend that nothing has happened until the entire Republic signs a memorandum of understanding.

Somebody has opened a channel between movements. Somebody has put the pupils' demands into circulation. Somebody has arranged for the roundabout and the lycée to appear on the same map of political possibility.

The students have not necessarily put on yellow vests. The Yellow Vests have not necessarily gone back to school.

**But somebody has started distributing the homework.**

And the homework concerns the Republic.

### Claims requiring independent verification before reuse as facts

- Reported protest attendance and arrests in the organisers' appeal, including allegations of serious injuries to minors.
- Whether every calendar-listed demonstration or strike actually occurred, and with what participation.
- All cost-of-living and poverty figures in the petition, including their statistical baselines and definitions.
- Refining-margin series and how the benchmark relates to company-specific accounts.
- Whether Union Étudiante formally endorsed the 17 October appeal or participated in joint organisational decisions.
- Actual outcomes of 17 October actions and any subsequent negotiations or organisational meetings.

### Evidentiary rule

An **appeal** is evidence that somebody appealed. A **calendar** is evidence that somebody advertised an event. A **graphic** is evidence that somebody circulated a message. A **signature list** is evidence of publicly stated support, subject to its provenance. An **observed demonstration** requires evidence that it happened. A **political outcome** requires evidence of what changed.

Those distinctions protect the very thing the node is interested in: the possibility of solidarity that does not erase the people doing the solidarising.
