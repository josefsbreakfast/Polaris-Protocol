# 💰 Des Livres, Pas Des Bombes
**First created:** 2026-10-09 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-21  
*Jeunesse en Feu — the price of an education, the price of a state, and the rather important difference between authorising a teacher and having one turn up.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

A government can spend more money on education and still leave pupils with fewer lessons. It can announce more jobs while reducing a particular kind of job. It can promise a renovation without having identified who will pay the contractor. And it can invite thousands of young people to describe their problems without yet having changed a single one of the conditions they described.

None of these statements requires us to assume that the government is lying. They require us to understand what a budget measures, what it leaves out, and how many decisions stand between a line in a finance bill and somebody actually teaching French on a Tuesday.

The title — **Des livres, pas des bombes**: *Books, not bombs* — asks a political question about priorities. Education and defence are both exercises of state power, and both consume public resources. But a political comparison is only useful if we resist the temptation to compare an annual school budget with a decade-long weapons commitment and declare that we have discovered a missing teacher in the difference.

This node follows the money, the posts, the students' demands, and the institutional hand-offs. It distinguishes **reported proposals**, **parliamentary claims**, **delivery evidence**, and **questions still awaiting documentary answers**. Figures below are labelled as reported proposals, direct calculations from specified inputs, or unresolved comparisons. A source URL is not a substitute for checking the original annex and its accounting perimeter.

## 1. 💰 The Minister Has an Increase. The MP Has a Decrease.

On **7 October 2026**, during a National Assembly plenary exchange, MP **Lisa Belluco** challenged the government's education budget in inflation-adjusted terms. The parliamentary proceedings are cited for her claim of a **€650 million reduction in constant euros**. Education Minister **Édouard Geffray**, defending the proposed 2027 budget, cited a **€1.2 billion increase**. These are competing *attributed statements*, not two figures independently certified by this node. The plenary is distinct from the committee hearing held on the same date.

The apparent contradiction is precisely why the units matter. An allocation can rise in **nominal euros** — the number printed on the page — while falling in **real purchasing power** if the prices of the things it buys rise faster. Equally, two speakers may be counting different programmes, including different pension obligations, or comparing different baselines. We cannot decide which explanation applies here without the actual budget tables and the assumptions behind Belluco's calculation.

Consider a hypothetical, not a statement about French inflation: a budget rises from €100 to €102 while the cost of its inputs rises by 3%. It now buys approximately €99.03 worth of the previous year's goods and services. The nominal increase is 2%; the real change is approximately −0.97%. Both numbers describe the same budget, but they answer different questions.

The minister has an increase. The MP has a decrease. **The pupil has a timetable.** The first two claims deserve a fair accounting test; the third deserves to be remembered while we perform it.

## 2. 🧾 What a French Budget Actually Is

A **projet de loi de finances** is a proposed finance bill. It is not the same as an enacted budget, and an enacted budget is not the same as spending actually executed. In French public-finance documents, an **autorisation d'engagement** permits commitments within defined rules; a **crédit de paiement** concerns payments. They are especially important to distinguish for construction and defence programmes that extend across years.

A **mission** groups public-policy programmes; a **programme** identifies a more specific spending responsibility. If one commentator discusses school education and another adds higher education, they are not necessarily discussing the same perimeter. The same problem arises when one total includes pensions and another excludes them. Rounding can make smaller differences; scope can make much larger ones.

A workforce figure has its own complications. **Headcount** is not necessarily **full-time equivalent**. A post authorised in a budget is not a vacancy filled. A teacher trainee is not automatically equivalent to an established teacher available for a full teaching timetable. A nurse employed somewhere in an academy is not necessarily the nurse at the school where pupils have been waiting.

Finally, not everything in a lycée building belongs to the same decision-maker. National education policy, staffing, regional responsibilities for lycée premises, and the delivery of particular works interact. If a minister promises better buildings, the immediate question is not whether the minister sounds enthusiastic. It is **who can authorise, finance, procure and sign off the work**.

The Republic has several spreadsheets. Unfortunately, they do not all measure the same Republic.

## 4A. 🧮 CAS Pensions: The Republic's Other Column

A useful budget comparison begins by asking whether the displayed total includes contributions to the **Compte d’affectation spéciale « Pensions »**. These are real costs associated with public employment and retirement, not an accounting fiction; equally, they are not money available to purchase additional classroom hours in the same way as a recruitment allocation. In 2026, Senate material described school education at approximately **€64.49bn excluding** and **€89.64bn including** pension contributions. That is a different perimeter, not evidence of a secret second school system.

For proposed 2027 spending, the reported school-education figures of **€65.53bn excluding pensions** and **€90.86bn including pensions** must be kept in different columns. The latter figure comes from a secondary mission comparison and remains subject to original-annex verification. Defence must receive exactly the same treatment: **€63.4bn excluding pensions** is not directly comparable with a **€73.15bn including-pensions** defence figure. The same warning applies to any higher-education total.

**The Republic has several spreadsheets. It is entitled to several spreadsheets. It is not entitled to remove the column headings before inviting everyone to applaud.**

## 4B. 📉 A Real-Terms Test, With the Working Shown

Take the reported pension-exclusive school mission totals of **€64.43bn in 2026** and **€65.53bn proposed for 2027**. Their nominal difference is **€1.10bn**, or about **1.71%**. If — and only if — we apply a **1.8% general inflation assumption** to the full comparable spending perimeter, the 2027 figure expressed in 2026 euros is:

`€65.53bn / 1.018 = approximately €64.37bn`.

Compared with €64.43bn, that is a difference of approximately **−€58m**, or **−0.09%**. This is an **illustrative derived calculation**, not an official real-terms estimate: a general consumer-price assumption need not match teachers' pay, energy, procurement, or other education input costs. Nor does it settle whether the two published totals use identical programme coverage.

It **does not reproduce Lisa Belluco's €650m claim**. Her precise baseline, deflator and included programmes are still required. It demonstrates the narrower point: nominal growth and a small real-terms decline can coexist without mathematical contradiction. The minister's reported **€1.2bn** and the **€1.10bn** subtraction likewise remain separately attributed pending perimeter reconciliation.

The minister has an increase. The MP has a decrease. **The pupil has a timetable.** The timetable is not impressed by a badly labelled denominator.

## 5. 🧑‍🏫 A Trainee Is Not an Entire Timetable

The reported **+3,041 trainee-teacher positions** are politically and administratively significant. Teacher training matters; a profession without new entrants eventually becomes a profession without teachers. But training posts cannot be treated as interchangeable with established posts without examining the reform's actual terms: teaching obligations, supervision, qualification stage, subject, academy and hours available to pupils.

Similarly, a reported **−1,588 established teaching posts** does not tell us which school, if any, will lose a particular subject teacher. Staffing allocations depend on pupil numbers, subjects, location, replacement needs and the decisions taken within the education administration. A national total is a useful starting point, not a map of who has French on Tuesday.

The relevant chain is longer than the press release:

**Funded → authorised → advertised → recruited → assigned → present → lessons delivered.**

At each stage, something can fail. An advertised vacancy may not attract a qualified applicant. An appointment may begin after the term starts. A teacher may be present but covering a different subject or year group. A replacement system can record a response to absence without restoring every lost lesson.

The questions we should ask are measurable: How many **subject-specific teaching hours** are available? How many are lost? How many are replaced? How does the answer differ by academy and school? And how much of any announced improvement is already in the classroom rather than due in a future financial year?

## 6. ♿ Support Is Part of Education, Not a Consolation Prize

The reported **+1,300 AESH posts** must not disappear into an argument about teachers. *Accompagnants des élèves en situation de handicap* support disabled pupils' access to education. That work has its own staffing pressures, working conditions and consequences when provision fails.

A national authorisation tells us little about an individual pupil's support without further measures: the hours assessed as necessary, hours allocated, hours staffed, actual attendance, continuity and whether the support is suitable. Counting people rather than delivered hours can hide part-time arrangements and gaps across the school week.

The same principle applies to **CPEs**, school psychologists, nurses and social workers. A pupil who needs a nurse does not become less in need of one because the ministry can announce a trainee-teacher recruitment programme. These are different functions. They should be assessed as different functions.

Our existing **Saint-Exupéry** research records complaints about missing support and reporting of continuing AESH shortages in early October. It does **not** supply a verified, post-by-post staffing return for 9 October. We can use the school as a test of what ought to be measured, but we cannot convert an aggregate budget proposal into proof that its pupils have received their support.

A properly accountable staffing announcement should make it possible to follow authorised support **all the way to delivered hours**, while protecting individual pupils' private information.

## 7. 🏫 Buildings Do Not Repair Themselves in a Press Conference

Buildings are the other place where budget language can become particularly slippery. A lycée can need urgent maintenance, accessibility works, ordinary repairs, energy upgrades or a major renovation. Those are different projects with different lead times and funding arrangements.

France's decentralised responsibilities mean that **regional authorities play a central role in lycée premises**, alongside national education responsibilities and other public bodies. It would be careless to treat every building problem as a single ministry's direct maintenance invoice. It would be equally careless to let divided responsibility become an excuse for nobody to answer the pupils.

Contemporary accounts describe a **school-buildings workstream** in the Prime Minister's reported October response; the original letter still needs to be checked before treating its exact terms as settled. What it does not yet establish is a complete, publicly traceable schedule of funded projects, responsible authorities, procurement decisions and completion dates for the schools whose pupils mobilised.

A useful building ledger is simple:

**Problem reported → responsible authority identified → funding agreed → works ordered → works completed → classroom usable.**

At Saint-Exupéry, the 20 September complaints and 5 October reporting are useful evidence of concerns and their persistence. They are not a building survey. We should neither dismiss those concerns nor pretend we possess a signed-off defects schedule. The question for the relevant authorities is whether the reported problems have been inspected, costed and fixed.

## 8. 🎓 Universities Are Not an Appendix to the School Budget

The student programme in our research is broader than lycée staffing. It addresses **Parcoursup and Mon Master**, university places, financial assistance, meals, housing, international fees and the **CVEC**. Those demands do not all belong to the same budget programme, and some involve regulatory choices rather than simply spending another euro.

A proposal to make **Parcoursup more transparent** is not the same as a demand to **end selection through Parcoursup**. The former concerns the visibility of a process; the latter challenges how places are allocated. The two can be discussed together, but we cannot score one as delivery of the other.

Likewise, an announcement about schoolteacher recruitment does not tell us whether a university can admit more students, whether a grant has increased, or whether a student can afford accommodation. Higher education and research have their own appropriations and administrative structures. Contemporary reporting gives a higher-education headline of approximately **€31.8 billion**, with an increase of around **€500 million**; the perimeter and precise year-on-year comparator still need to be established against the original mission annexes. This is a reported proposal, not an audited figure.

Nor should every participating organisation be collapsed into a single student voice. A lycée pupil, a university union and an education-worker inter-union can support overlapping goals while holding different positions and different negotiating authority. Our companion node on demands keeps those distinctions visible.

## 9. 🍽️ The Price of Being Allowed to Study

Formal access to education is not identical to practical access. A student can receive an offer of a place and still struggle to pay for food, transport, rent or the materials needed to complete the course. That is why the programme's demands around **grants, €1 meals, housing and fees** belong in an education-funding discussion rather than being treated as unrelated social policy.

Contemporary reporting identifies proposed allocations of approximately **€93m for €1 student meals**, **€33m for a restaurant-access card**, and **€15m for Santé Psy Étudiant**. These are **reported budget lines**, not evidence that the corresponding service was universally accessible. Their precise programme attribution and treatment in the 2027 finance bill still require original-table confirmation.

Each measure requires its own delivery test. For a subsidised meal: who qualifies, at which sites, at what price, with what public subsidy and how reliably? For grants: which eligibility rules, payment levels and application procedures change? For CROUS housing: how many places are **announced**, how many are **built**, how many are **available**, and at what rent? For fees: which students pay which charge, and has the rule actually changed?

The original ten-point programme contains numerical targets, but this node does not promote an unverified figure into a settled entitlement. Readers can consult the linked organisational programme; exact amounts should be checked against its dated version and the applicable government rules before being used as a definitive before-and-after comparison.

This is not an argument that every student has the same financial circumstances. It is an argument that **costs shape whose theoretical opportunities can become real opportunities**.

## 10. 🪖 Books and Bombs: A Serious Comparison, Please

The title is intentionally political. It asks why some forms of collective security receive resources while other forms of collective security — education, disability support, safe buildings, the capacity to live and learn — are presented as constrained. That question does not disappear because the state has defence obligations. Nor does defence expenditure become a fictional pool of instantly transferable cash because a slogan is satisfying.

To compare the two responsibly, we need **the same year**, **the same public-spending perimeter**, **the same pension convention**, and **the same treatment of inflation**. We must also distinguish annual **payment credits** from multi-year **commitment authorisations**. A ten-year military programme cannot be placed beside a one-year school budget as though both sums are spent on the same Tuesday.

We should distinguish, too, between **state procurement**, **military aid**, **arms exports** and **private defence-industry turnover**. An export sale is not automatically a cost to the French education budget. A government procurement commitment is not automatically a payment already made. Different numbers tell different stories.

A Ministry of the Armed Forces budget presentation has been identified for the proposed 2027 defence appropriation, but a fully matched, annex-level comparison is not yet established. The figures below are therefore **provisional reported comparisons**, not audited equivalents.

| Category | Period | Comparable amount | Evidence status |
|---|---|---:|---|
| School education, excluding pensions | Proposed 2027 | **€65.53bn** | *Public Sénat* reported mission figure; annex confirmation pending |
| Defence, excluding pensions | Proposed 2027 | **€63.4bn** | Armed Forces ministry presentation; annex confirmation pending |
| Higher education and research | Proposed 2027 | **~€31.8bn** | Contemporary reported headline; scope confirmation pending |

The two pension-exclusive school and defence figures differ by **€2.13bn**, but their classification and pension conventions must be confirmed against the same 2027 bill before treating that difference as fully like-for-like. The caution is what prevents the comparison becoming theatre dressed as arithmetic.

The stronger political question is one of **opportunity cost**: what else could a marginal, genuinely available euro fund, and what public purposes does the government say justify its allocation? Defence has real security functions, contractual commitments and strategic arguments in its favour. Those arguments can be assessed and challenged. We do not improve the challenge by mislabelling the figures.

Books and bombs may both appear in the Republic's accounts. **The accounts still need to balance before the argument can begin.**

A **secondary compilation**, *Dépenses Publiques*, reports the following pension-inclusive mission-level payment-credit totals. These are a **provisional comparison to be checked against the original 2027 finance-bill annexes**; the table should not be cited as an audited government reconciliation.

| Mission — pension-inclusive, reported | 2026 | Proposed 2027 | Nominal change | Approximate growth |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Enseignement scolaire | €89.62bn | €90.86bn | +€1.24bn | +1.38% |
| Défense | €66.48bn | €73.15bn | +€6.67bn | +10.03% |
| Recherche et enseignement supérieur | €31.63bn | €32.05bn | +€0.42bn | +1.33% |

On **those reported figures**, the defence mission's nominal growth rate is approximately **7.3 times** the school-education mission's rate. Its absolute nominal increase is approximately **5.4 times** the school-education increase. These ratios are *derived from the reported inputs*; they are not independent measurements of effectiveness, urgency, or what a teacher costs. Defence and education have different baselines, contracts, time horizons and institutional purposes. Neither percentage proves that a euro allocated to defence was taken from a particular school.

But a budget is a choice about **relative priority**. The government can make the strategic case for defence expansion. Students can make the democratic case that replacing a missing teacher, repairing a school, or funding disability support is also urgent. A fair hearing requires the state to answer the second case with something more substantial than the first case's accounting conventions.

## 10B. 🛡️ What Counts as National Security?

Military readiness matters. A state also depends on people able to read, reason, build, maintain, organise, care and participate. Schools and universities reproduce the technical and social capacities on which defence, emergency response, public administration and democratic oversight themselves depend. Disability access is part of that capacity, not a discretionary garnish. So are safe buildings and the material possibility of remaining in education.

This does **not** make a school budget interchangeable with a defence budget. It challenges a narrower habit: speaking as though the state's capacity to respond to external threats is the only serious meaning of security, while the reproduction of society's own capacities is merely a cost pressure. A defence ministry cannot procure its way out of a generation's lost education. An education ministry cannot deliver air defence. The Republic requires both; that is precisely why relative growth, delivery and opportunity cost are legitimate questions.

**A state can successfully fund its institutions while failing to maintain the society those institutions exist to protect.**

The Republic has demonstrated a willingness to argue for rapid expansion when it regards a threat as urgent. The children would like to discuss its definition of urgency.

## 11. 🏛️ Parliament Is Supposed to Ask for the Working

The **7 October plenary exchange** between Belluco and Geffray gives us a useful example of parliamentary scrutiny in action. The question is not merely whether a politician can produce a dramatic number. It is whether the executive can show what its number includes, what it excludes, and how it corresponds to services.

For the reported **€650 million real-terms reduction**, we need Belluco's precise baseline, spending perimeter and inflation measure. For Geffray's **€1.2 billion increase**, we need the same. Only then can we test whether the two are reconcilable, or whether one relies on an incompatible comparison.

A separate **7 October committee hearing** has been mentioned in working accounts, but no original transcript is established here. Do not transfer statements from the authenticated plenary into a different parliamentary forum.

The scrutiny questions practically suggest themselves: How much is planned per pupil? What happens to established teaching hours? How are vacancies distributed? Which lines support AESH, nurses and school psychologists? Which building works are funded, and by whom? What is scheduled for delivery this term rather than next year?

Parliamentary answers are evidence that a minister made a statement. **Implementation records** are evidence of what subsequently happened. Both are necessary; neither can substitute for the other.

## 12. 📉 More Per Pupil? Show the Calculation.

A national budget total is not a per-pupil allocation. If pupil numbers change, the same total can mean a different amount per person. If prices change, the same nominal amount can buy different levels of provision. If the distribution changes, the national average can conceal deterioration in particular academies or schools.

Here is the calculation framework — **not a set of calculated results for 2027**:

- **Nominal percentage change:** `(budget_2027 / budget_2026 − 1) × 100`.
- **Real percentage change:** `(budget_2027 / (budget_2026 × (1 + inflation_rate)) − 1) × 100`, using comparable budgets and an appropriate price index.
- **Per-pupil spending:** `comparable expenditure / comparable pupil population`.
- **Teaching intensity:** `comparable established-teacher FTE / pupil population`, ideally broken down by subject, age group and location.
- **Service delivery:** teaching hours, replacement hours, AESH support hours and completed works, rather than posts announced alone.

None of these calculations should be published as a French 2027 result until the inputs are sourced. The minister's reported expectation of demographic change is relevant, but fewer pupils nationally would not prove that every school has fewer needs. A small rural school, a specialist subject and a pupil requiring disability support cannot always be reduced to a smooth national ratio.

The denominator is not an inconvenience. It is part of the question.

## 13. 🧭 Follow the Money All the Way Down

The chain from political announcement to lived educational provision has multiple gates:

**Political pledge → draft budget → parliamentary authorisation → allocation → recruitment or procurement → local deployment → service delivered → pupil experience.**

Each gate has an actor and a record. The finance ministry and parliament shape appropriations. Education authorities allocate and recruit. Regional or other competent authorities may manage works. Schools schedule staff and lessons. Pupils experience the outcome — and are often the first to know when an apparently completed administrative process has not delivered the promised service.

Take **a teacher**. A funded position must become a recruited, qualified person assigned to the relevant school and subject, present for the necessary lessons. Take **AESH support**. An authorised post must translate into the hours and continuity that pupils need. Take **a building repair**. A budget line must become an approved job, completed work and a usable classroom.

In each case, an administrative record is necessary but insufficient. 'We funded it' does not answer 'Has it happened?' 'We appointed somebody' does not necessarily answer 'Were the lessons covered?' And 'We opened a consultation' certainly does not answer 'Is the roof still leaking?'

These questions can be asked publicly without publishing pupils' sensitive personal data. Aggregated staffing, delivered hours, project milestones and completion records can reveal whether the system is working while preserving individual privacy.

## 15. ♻️ The Cybernetics of an Appropriation

A budget is a model of what a state intends to do. A timetable is one record of what a school intends to do. A pupil sitting in a classroom without a teacher is information about what the system has actually delivered. All three are real; they are not equivalent.

This is the connection to **Embodied Information Ecology**. Information about institutions is produced at different scales, by people with different access and different reasons for measuring. A national budget may be perfectly recorded while a local service fails. A pupil's experience can identify a gap that an aggregate metric obscures. A protest can carry that gap out of the classroom and into a system capable of allocating resources — if that system chooses to process the signal.

Several recurrent substitutions are worth watching:

**Category substitution:** counting trainees when pupils are asking about established subject teachers. **Time substitution:** citing posts proposed for next year as though they repair this term's absence. **Jurisdiction substitution:** announcing a building commitment without identifying the authority that can deliver it. **Consultation substitution:** counting responses instead of changes. **Aggregate substitution:** treating a national net increase as evidence that no particular school remains short-staffed. **Security substitution:** treating the protection of state institutions as equivalent to maintaining the people and capacities those institutions exist to protect. The last is an analytical extension, not a budget category.

These are possible failure modes, not proof of intentional deception. Sometimes the problem is bureaucratic fragmentation, recruitment difficulty, slow procurement or a badly chosen metric. Sometimes political incentives reward announcements more quickly than delivery. The remedy begins by asking for the evidence that distinguishes those possibilities.

A functioning feedback loop would make **commitments, responsible actors, deadlines and observed outcomes** visible. It would also give pupils and staff a way to report that the official account and their experience do not match — and to have that discrepancy investigated rather than filed as an attitude problem.

## 17. 📚 What Arrives on Tuesday?

There are at least three kinds of government success. **Announcing** a policy establishes an intention. **Authorising** resources makes some actions possible. **Delivering** the service changes the conditions people live in. They are related achievements, but they are not synonyms.

The students' demands make this distinction difficult to avoid. A school can be open without its classes being fully staffed. A university can offer places without making study affordable. A budget can rise without providing more of the service that was missing. A minister can listen without yet changing the rule the students asked to change.

None of that settles how France should allocate every euro between education and defence. It does, however, establish what a defensible argument about priorities must measure. Not just how much money was announced, but what it was for, who controlled it, and whether it reached the people whose needs justified the announcement.

France has a defence budget because it believes there are things worth protecting. It has an education budget because there are people who must be able to inherit, understand and sustain those things. These are not identical functions, nor are they interchangeable expenditures. They are not competing teams in a spreadsheet football match.

But the relative rate at which a government proposes to expand one mission and another tells us something about its priorities. The pupils' question is not answered by an aggregate increase, an unreconciled pension column or an announcement about jobs that do not yet exist in their classrooms. **What did you fund? Who can deliver it? When will it arrive?**

The Republic may explain its appropriations at whatever length it likes. **The pupil is still entitled to ask whether the teacher has arrived.**
