# ♻️ The Cybernetics of RLHF
**First created:** 2026-10-21 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-21  
*When younger generations leave the feed, enter the street, and discover that the labour market has also been taking notes — badly.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

What happens to an artificial intelligence system trained to approximate human preferences when the humans expressing those preferences stop posting and start protesting?

This node connects **embodied political agency**, **commercial information environments**, **AI training and evaluation**, and **the political economy of youth employment**. The internet is not humanity; the digitally observable population is not necessarily representative of the people an institution claims to serve. And when the people being measured change their behaviour, the measuring system can mistake a change in observability for a change in human preferences.

**Technical boundary:** RLHF is a specific post-training technique, not a synonym for all model training or all online feedback. The proposed effects of offline participation fall most directly on online data availability, sampling, platform metrics and downstream representations. Any claim of a direct effect on an RLHF reward model requires evidence about that model's actual feedback pipeline.

---

## 🧠 1. RLHF is not the whole learning system

In simplified form, reinforcement learning from human feedback uses **human judgements → preference data → reward modelling or equivalent preference optimisation → model behaviour**. Contemporary systems may also use supervised fine-tuning, AI feedback, curated evaluations, synthetic examples, product research and other post-training methods. Pretraining corpora and recommendation systems are distinct again.

Who annotates? Who writes the rubric? Which answers are rewarded? Which communities are represented in the evaluations? A model can become good at producing answers preferred by its evaluators without those evaluators constituting a representative electorate. **Optimisation against observed preferences is not democratic representation.**

---

## 📱 2. The internet is not a representative sample of humanity

Population → access → participation → posting → amplification → collection → curation → training or evaluation. Every arrow is a possible selection effect. The person reading is not necessarily the person posting; the person posting is not necessarily the person promoted; the person promoted is not necessarily the person most affected.

Age, disability, geography, income, language, platform rules, moderation, safety concerns and time all shape visibility. **Absence from a dataset is not absence from society.**

---

## 🔥 3. What happens when the young go outside?

Imagine students spending more time at assemblies, demonstrations, mutual-aid tables and union meetings, and less time writing long public posts. This is a **research hypothesis**, not a measured generational migration. Protest movements also organise online, and physical mobilisation can *increase* digital output.

But the *composition* of the output may change. The participants are busy organising while commentators, institutions, journalists and commercial accounts describe what they are doing. The experience and its digital description begin to diverge.

**The underlying human state changes. The observation channel may not preserve that change accurately.**

---

## 🪞 4. The representation inversion

A movement can become more visible online even while its participants become less directly audible there. Reports, reactions, clipped videos, political labels and institutional statements may outnumber first-person accounts. That is the proposed **representation inversion**: people experiencing an event become less influential over its digital description than people interpreting it.

This can happen without a mastermind. Recommendation incentives, deadlines, language barriers, selective sourcing and the simple fact that somebody is busy being arrested or administering first aid can all matter.

**The Republic is being protested against. The internet would like to offer its interpretation of the Republic being protested against.**

---

## 🔁 5. The feedback loop

**Material conditions → human response → online/offline participation choices → selective digital observation → media and platform representation → AI training/evaluation/deployment → AI-mediated descriptions → public and institutional response → material conditions.**

This is a *possible* reinforcing loop, not a claim that any named model has absorbed a distorted account of the October 2026 French demonstrations. Test the transitions before attributing effects.

---

## ⚠️ 6. Whose preferences count?

Human-feedback systems are shaped by recruitment, instructions, annotation conditions, language, safety rules and institutional priorities. A preference label can indicate which of two answers an evaluator favours *under a given task*, not what an entire generation wants from work, politics or life.

A useful audit asks whether an answer is being rewarded for truthfulness, conformity, brevity, agreeableness, defensibility, or an unstable mixture. It also asks whether the system can preserve uncertainty and disagree with the user when the evidence demands it.

---

## 🏪 7. Commercial incentives: the feedback may not mean what you think

Clicks, watch time, return visits and outrage are **behavioural signals**, not straightforward expressions of endorsement. Somebody investigating a disturbing claim may generate the same engagement metric as somebody celebrating it. A platform optimising engagement can amplify the most provocative interpretation, not the most representative one.

Commercial actors, political organisations, states and non-state actors can all attempt to shape the information environment. Mechanism comes before attribution: authentic but selectively retrieved material can still produce a misleading account. Editorial self-selection, recommendation effects, coordinated amplification and technical compromise are different hypotheses with different evidentiary requirements. **No particular actor is implicated merely because an information pattern suits their interests.**

---

## 💼 8. The labour market has received feedback. Unfortunately, it thinks the feedback is broken.

The UK government says it wants fewer young people classified as **NEET** — *not in education, employment or training*. But this is an administrative category, not a diagnosis of motivation, talent or social worth. It can include people managing illness, providing unpaid care, searching for suitable jobs or learning outside formal programmes. The definition and statistical breakdowns matter.

There is a cybernetic question here: **what if a young person's non-participation is partly a signal about the offer being made, rather than a defect in the person declining it?**

The state may optimise for fewer NEETs, employers for labour availability and costs, investors for returns, unions for bargaining power, and young people for pay, security, dignity, health and an actual future. Those objectives overlap, but they do not magically coincide. Some businesses need secure, trained workers; others benefit from precariousness or low bargaining power. **Global capitalism does not have a single will**, but its prevailing incentive structures can make the government's stated ambition harder to achieve.

The difference between the observed metric and the underlying condition is decisive:

> **Sensor reading:** Young people are not entering education, employment or training.  
> **Possible underlying messages:** There are too few viable jobs; training is inaccessible; care and illness are unaccommodated; wages do not support independence; or the available pathways are not trusted.

These are alternative hypotheses, not interchangeable explanations. Investigate the mix before deciding the solution is another campaign to fix the young people.

### 🏛️ Labour is not identical to organised labour

The **Labour Party**, **the trade-union movement**, **workers as a population**, and **policy organisations associated with prominent Labour figures** are distinct institutions and constituencies. Labour's historical roots do not prove that every current policy represents organised labour's interests.

Recent remarks attributed to the **Tony Blair Institute** about workers' protections merit a **specific source-and-wording check** before the node claims what TBI proposed. An institute's position is not automatically government policy, nor does disagreement alone prove covert influence. The research task is to compare the actual proposal with union responses, government commitments, legislative text, and effects on bargaining power, dismissal protection, flexibility and enforcement.

The sharper question is not whether a party can use the word *Labour*. It is **whose labour-market preferences survive translation into policy**.

### 🔄 From structural signal to individual deficit

Young people experience an economic arrangement → some do not enter its offered pathways → the state registers a NEET rate → policy interprets non-entry as a skills or motivation deficit → interventions target the individual → the underlying terms of participation remain unchanged → the metric returns.

That is one *possible* maladaptive policy loop. It must be tested against actual barriers and programme outcomes. But if the labour market keeps returning an error message, perhaps somebody should inspect the labour market before rebooting the children.

## 🌍 8A. “Globalist”, or have the children compared notes?

A young person in France describes unaffordable housing, insecure work, inaccessible services and an institution that appears to treat their grievances as a behavioural defect. A young person in Britain, or elsewhere, recognises the pattern. They compare wages, rents, training routes, labour protections, policing, university conditions and political responses across borders. They exchange footage, documents, tactics and solidarity. Sometimes they organise together; sometimes they independently arrive at similar conclusions.

**Cross-border recognition is not, by itself, evidence of cross-border command.** People can identify common structural pressures without a central organiser. They can learn from each other because they have internet access and comparable experiences. The international circulation of ideas is an ordinary feature of political life, not a sufficient indicator of clandestine orchestration.

In some far-right rhetoric, *“globalist”* functions as an elastic accusation that can turn transnational contact or shared political vocabulary into an insinuation of hidden coordination. It can also carry antisemitic conspiracy associations, depending on speaker and context. That does **not** mean every use of the term has an identical meaning; investigate the actual statement, its target and the evidence offered. Nor should the accusation be accepted as an analytical category merely because it has been repeated.

### 🔄 Convergent conditions, networked learning, coordinated action: three different things

1. **Convergence:** Similar labour-market and institutional conditions produce similar grievances in different countries, even without contact.
2. **Diffusion and mutual learning:** People communicate across borders, recognise common patterns, share arguments and adapt each other's methods. This is observable political agency, not inherently covert.
3. **Organised coordination:** Specific actors jointly plan or resource particular actions. Coordination can be lawful and public; a claim of *covert* direction requires additional evidence of direction, concealment, financing or operational control.

The distinction matters in both directions. Genuine coordination should not be erased simply to defend a movement; equally, a familiar slogan, similar poster or simultaneous grievance does not establish a hidden controller. **Common cause is not automatically common command.**

### ♻️ The policy-learning opportunity

Governments can also compare notes. If housing costs, insecure employment, poor training access or mistrust of institutions recur across countries, policymakers need not invent a new national diagnosis every time. Compare the institutions, interventions and outcomes that differ; ask participants what changed; examine whether bargaining power, public services or employer incentives explain the results. Cross-national learning is not proof that every country is identical, and importing a policy without its institutional context can fail.

The cybernetic irony is that young people may be running a better transnational feedback process than the institutions tasked with listening to them. Their observations circulate horizontally, while official systems may keep treating each manifestation as an isolated domestic anomaly.

**The children have discovered that the same shit is happening elsewhere.**

This is not necessarily a conspiracy. Sometimes it is a group chat with receipts.

## 🧪 9. How to test the claims

| Research question | Evidence to seek | Do not infer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Are younger people changing online participation? | Longitudinal time-use and platform surveys | That all youth have gone offline |
| Does protest participation displace posting? | Consented interviews, diaries and longitudinal observation | Causation from coincident trends |
| Who produces online protest narratives? | Provenance and source-composition audits | Coordination from similar headlines |
| Are first-person accounts missing in AI answers? | Controlled model evaluations with and without primary accounts | That RLHF alone caused the omission |
| Are feedback evaluators representative? | Published recruitment and annotation documentation | That all models use the same process |
| Why are young people NEET? | Disaggregated official data, longitudinal interviews, disability/care measures, job quality | That NEET means unwilling to work |
| Do employer incentives conflict with youth outcomes? | Pay, vacancy quality, training investment, retention and productivity evidence | A unified capitalist conspiracy |
| Are similar youth grievances convergent or coordinated? | Cross-country comparisons, primary demands, communication timelines, public organising records | Covert orchestration from shared slogans or mutual learning |
| How is “globalist” used in coverage of youth mobilisation? | Exact quotations, speaker, target, context, corroborating evidence | A single fixed meaning or a hidden network from rhetoric alone |
| What did TBI actually say about worker protections? | Original dated statement, proposals, union responses and policy text | That TBI speaks for the government |

**Corrections rule:** Record source, date, precise claim, confidence level and any later change. Do not upgrade an inference to a finding because it is rhetorically satisfying.

## 🧭 10. Human agency: you are allowed to steer

The internet is not a conveyor belt. You can choose what to search for, seek primary documents, look for the strongest contrary account, ask whose footage was selected, and notice when a story feels *particularly* persuasive because it flatters your prior beliefs. We all have cognitive biases; the point is to build habits that catch them, not pretend to be immune.

Yes, proper research occasionally means looking up some **fucking weird shit**. Context is not endorsement. Neither a search history nor a single click is a reliable description of someone's politics.

Interrogate the robit too. Ask an LLM for sources, uncertainty, competing explanations and what would falsify its answer. Check citations yourself. Models can be overly agreeable, overconfident, outdated or biased by their training and evaluation environments. Challenging them is useful; merely demanding they confirm your preferred conclusion is not an audit.

**Not everything a human does needs to become machine-readable feedback.** Political agency exists whether a platform records it or not.

---

## 🧑‍🔬 11. The AI researcher has a sampling problem

When a population becomes less visible in one channel, it may have moved platform, moved into private communications, changed format, gone offline, been filtered out or been misclassified. More data from the same channel does not necessarily solve the problem. The issue may be **observability**, not compute.

If an AI system describes people back to themselves using mostly third-party descriptions, how would its developers detect the gap? Build evaluations with participant testimony, provenance diversity and explicit uncertainty rather than assuming online frequency measures social importance.

---

## 🦚 12. The humans have escaped the laboratory

An AI can classify a million posts about a protest and still misunderstand the people attending it. A government can measure a NEET rate and still misunderstand the terms on which young people are being asked to participate. A party can carry the name *Labour* and still need to demonstrate what it has done for workers.

**The internet is not humanity. Human feedback is not human consent. A training dataset is not a democratic mandate. And a labour-market participation target is not proof that the labour market is offering a decent bargain.**

The children have gone outside. Some are protesting, some organising, some caring for other people, and some looking at the jobs on offer and asking whether this is supposed to be a joke.

Somewhere, a machine is trying to predict what they want.

**Unfortunately for the machine, the humans have developed legs.**
