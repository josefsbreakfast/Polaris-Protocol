# 📣 La Calle No Se Calla
**First created:** 2026-10-08 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-09  
*The street does not fall silent: one threatened home, a neighbourhood at the door, and the complicated business of making a country listen.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

A court order can identify the people entitled to enter a property. It cannot, by itself, determine what everyone outside the property is entitled to think about it. That is where the street comes in.

In September 2026, supporters assembled around the Madrid home of **Maricarmen Abascal**, an 87-year-old woman facing the enforcement of an eviction order after decades living in the same flat. The reported removal on **23 September** became the centre of a much wider housing mobilisation. Accounts described demonstrations in multiple Spanish cities, protest activity at **Puerta del Sol**, negotiations over her possible return, and national political responses. Maricarmen died on **7 October**, before the reported return could take place. [1–5]

This is the **mobilisation node** of the [🌹 Por Todas Las Maricarmen](./🌹_por_todas_las_maricarmen.md) cluster. The [🕯️ biographical node](./🕯️_la_historia_de_maricarmen.md) carries the personal and legal chronology. Here, the questions are different: **who organised, what they asked for, how information travelled, how the campaign widened, and what institutions demonstrably did in response**.

A caution about provenance: this draft uses reporting and source leads retained in the two existing cluster nodes, together with established historical context. The URLs listed below are **source leads, not a claim that their full contents were freshly checked while drafting this file**. Precise crowd totals, a definitive twelve-city list, the mechanics of the encampment and the attribution of legislative causation remain open verification tasks. The distinction matters. A movement deserves a history, not a mythology assembled from convenient blanks.

## 🏠 1. Before the Camera Arrived

The television cameras did not invent the housing dispute. Nor did a national audience invent the people who had been trying to help Maricarmen stay in her home.

Reporting retained in the biographical chronology describes earlier scheduled evictions in **October 2025** and **June 2026**, with legal interventions and public support before the final September enforcement. [3, 6] That sequence is important. An eviction date is both a procedural event and, for people trying to prevent removal, a deadline. Lawyers need documents. Supporters need reliable information. Neighbours need to know whether their presence is wanted. Someone must distinguish a court postponement from a rumour that the eviction has been cancelled.

The difference between an individual dispute and an organised campaign is not simply the number of people who turn up. It is whether there are durable relationships capable of carrying information and practical assistance across time. A neighbour may know that somebody needs help reaching a meeting. A lawyer may know the significance of an enforcement notice. A tenants' organiser may know how to communicate a call to action. These are different forms of knowledge, and none is automatically interchangeable with the others.

### What the early history must establish

- The earliest **dated** public call concerning Maricarmen; the original issuing account or organisation; and the precise demand.
- Which earlier enforcement dates actually brought supporters to the building, rather than being postponed in advance.
- Which neighbours participated as individuals and which, if any, represented formal neighbourhood associations.
- What Maricarmen herself said about support and collective action.
- Which legal developments changed the planned gatherings, and **who** made those legal decisions.

An enforcement delay may coincide with protest without having been caused by it. If the reason was a court ruling about notice, that is the mechanism to report. Protesters may still have provided material support or political visibility; those are separate claims requiring their own evidence.

## 🚪 2. The Day at the Door — 23 September 2026

The apartment was in Madrid's Retiro district, on **Calle Alcalde Sáinz de Baranda**. Contemporary reporting described supporters gathering before the scheduled eviction and remaining at or near the building as the judicial enforcement proceeded. The reports describe a police presence, a judicial commission and Maricarmen's removal by ambulance to Hospital Gregorio Marañón. [1–3]

It is tempting to compress all of that into a single dramatic photograph: a door, a crowd, a stretcher. Resist the temptation. The sequence contains different actors exercising different powers, and a photograph does not explain which decision each actor made.

### The sequence to preserve

**Before arrival:** When did the union or supporters publish the call? Who stayed overnight? Was any legal application still pending? What did Maricarmen ask supporters to do?

**At the building:** Which officials attended? What restrictions were placed on access? How did demonstrators behave? Which actions were independently witnessed and which were later described by campaigners or authorities?

**Enforcement:** At what time was possession executed? Which details are supported by contemporary observation rather than retrospective reconstruction?

**After removal:** Who confirmed where Maricarmen had been taken? Who issued the first public statements? When were further demonstrations called?

This is not an instruction to turn the event into a police operational log. It is an instruction to keep the human story attached to a verifiable sequence. The distinction between *scheduled*, *attempted*, *postponed* and *executed* matters enormously when reconstructing an eviction.

Maricarmen's agency matters just as much. Supporters can describe what they believe a case represents. That does not make them authorised narrators of every thought or wish of the person at its centre. Where her own words are available, they take precedence over a convenient campaign paraphrase. [7]

## 🗺️ 3. From One Address to Several Cities

The earlier research describes **same-day demonstrations across twelve cities** following the eviction. That claim is sufficiently significant to investigate, but the present source register does not securely establish a complete city-by-city list, individual organisers, meeting places and comparable attendance figures. We should not quietly populate a map with guesses.

A national mobilisation can mean several different things. A confederation might issue a common call; local unions might respond with independently arranged gatherings; allied organisations might circulate the announcement without participating in formal planning; individuals might turn up because they have seen the story in the news. Those arrangements can produce similar photographs but tell us very different things about how a movement works.

### City-by-city evidence register

| Madrid demonstration location and time | Original call + contemporary coverage | Reported; precise event details to reconcile |
| Claimed twelve-city total | Confederation announcement or contemporaneous reporting | **Reported; complete list outstanding** |
| Individual city locations | Local original calls or local reporting | Outstanding |
| Local organisers | Signed calls, posters or organisational accounts | Outstanding |

A useful map will eventually show **confirmed events**, not an impressionistic Spanish archipelago of righteous dots. A marker should carry a date, place, organiser where known, and source. Crowd-size circles should not imply scientific precision where the only surviving evidence is that there were “hundreds”.

### What geographical expansion does tell us

The wider reporting suggests that Maricarmen's circumstances were legible to people far beyond her building. That is a meaningful observation. But geographic reach is not the same as shared membership, and shared anger is not the same as an agreed programme. To understand the campaign, we need both its common demands and its local variations.

## 🕸️ 4. Who Was Organising Whom?

Spanish housing activism has a long history, but history is not a membership list. The **Sindicato de Inquilinas e Inquilinos de Madrid** and wider tenants' organising are central leads in contemporary accounts of the case. The reported involvement of a **Confederación de Sindicatos de Inquilinas** in coordinated calls also needs to be traced to its actual statements and constituent organisations. [1, 4]

The broader history includes the **Plataforma de Afectados por la Hipoteca (PAH)**, **Stop Desahucios**, neighbourhood associations, and autonomous social centres. Those traditions help explain the available repertoire of collective action. They do **not** prove that each group formally organised Maricarmen's demonstrations.

A practical organisational register should distinguish four roles:

| Role | What would establish it? | What it does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct organiser** | Original event call, coordination statement, named responsibility | Control over every attendee |
| **Participating organisation** | Its own announcement or documented delegation | Authorship of the whole campaign |
| **Supporting organisation** | Endorsement, material assistance, public statement | Formal coalition membership |
| **Commentator or political representative** | Speech, interview, parliamentary statement | Involvement in planning or logistics |

This prevents a familiar historical accident: everyone who has ever tweeted support becoming a founding member of the organising committee. There may have been a committee. We should find out before appointing one retrospectively.

### The older organisational traditions

Spain's neighbourhood associations grew in importance during late Francoism and the democratic transition, organising around services, housing, planning and local democratic participation. The *okupa* movement developed its own traditions of occupation and self-managed social space, especially from the 1980s. PAH, founded in Barcelona in **2009**, became prominent through collective mortgage-debt advice, anti-eviction interventions and legislative campaigning. [8–11]

These histories are related, but not identical. Housing need, autonomous space, mortgage debt and tenant representation do not produce one uniform political organisation. The distinctions belong in [✊ Stop Desahucios](./✊_stop_desahucios.md), [🗝️ El Movimiento Okupa](./🗝️_el_movimiento_okupa.md) and [🕸️ Cómo Se Organiza Un Barrio](./🕸️_como_se_organiza_un_barrio.md). Here they explain why an anti-eviction campaign in 2026 could draw on established political languages and practices without being reducible to any one of them.

## 📲 5. How a Call Becomes a Gathering

For someone to attend a protest, information has to become actionable. They need to know **what happened, where to go, when to arrive, what is being asked of them and whether the plan has changed**. That sounds obvious until the legal timetable moves and a message from yesterday is still being circulated as though it were current.

This is where the information ecology becomes visible. A lawyer's account of the court timetable, a neighbour's description of local conditions, a union's call to assemble and a broadcaster's report may concern the same event. They are not the same information product. They have different audiences, different standards of verification and different consequences if wrong.

### Channels to verify rather than assume

- **Original union announcements:** date, platform, authorship, precise wording, subsequent edits.
- **Neighbourhood communications:** publicly documented posters, meetings, leaflets and calls.
- **Press coverage:** which outlet first reported which development, and which accounts were independently confirmed.
- **Public social-media posts:** URLs, timestamps, screenshots and whether posts remained accessible.
- **Messaging applications:** only where their actual use is publicly documented or participants have consented to its description.
- **Institutional communications:** court announcements, municipal statements, parliamentary notices.

It would be easy to write “the WhatsApp groups sprang into action”. It would also be fiction unless we have evidence of those groups. Distributed organisation does not require us to invent a platform architecture. It requires us to identify what information travelled and which relationships made it useful.

### The correction problem

During a fast-moving campaign, reports can be accurate when issued and obsolete an hour later. A postponed eviction, an announced negotiation, a draft agreement and a signed contract are not equivalent updates. The communications record should preserve **time of claim**, **source**, **later correction** and **current evidential status**. If we cannot recover those steps, say so.

The lesson is not that the public cannot be trusted with complicated information. It is that complicated information needs provenance. Otherwise a campaign ends up doing its opponent's work for it by circulating errors that are easy to disprove.

## ⛺ 6. Puerta del Sol — Staying Visible

Reports and earlier research place continuing protest activity or an encampment at **Puerta del Sol** after the eviction. [4] The square has a substantial political history, including its association with Spain's **15-M** mobilisations in 2011. That historical resonance is worth explaining, but it is not proof that the 2026 organisers consciously designed their protest as a 15-M revival.

A one-off demonstration and a continuing presence have different practical requirements. An assembly can begin with a time and a place. Remaining there introduces questions of supplies, accessibility, rest, communications, collective decisions, safety and relations with authorities. Those questions are useful for analysis even where the specific answers are not yet available.

### Questions for the encampment record

1. Who issued the initial call, and on what date?
2. When did people first stay overnight, if they did?
3. What did participants call the action: *acampada*, vigil, assembly or something else?
4. Were there public meetings, published decisions or common demands?
5. What was the documented duration?
6. Were there municipal restrictions or police interventions?
7. Which practical support arrangements were visible and documented?
8. How did the action change after the announced negotiations and after Maricarmen's death?

The square may function as an information environment: a place where people hear news, meet others, compare accounts and decide what to do next. But that is a **mechanism to investigate**, not a licence to invent the internal organisation of a camp whose records we have not examined.

## 📣 7. The Demands — Not Just “Housing Justice”

A campaign's demands should be quoted or paraphrased from **dated, attributable statements**. “Housing justice” may be a fair umbrella description, but it is too vague to tell us what organisers asked any particular institution to do.

The demands may be grouped analytically, while keeping their original wording intact:

**For Maricarmen:** suspension of removal, permission to remain, negotiation, return to the property, or provision of acceptable accommodation — **only where the specific demand is documented**.

**For other tenants:** protections against eviction, rent regulation, social housing provision, or changes to the enforcement framework — again, with the actual issuing organisation identified.

**For institutions:** specific action by the owner, courts, Madrid City Council, the Community of Madrid, national government or Congress. Those bodies possess different powers. A slogan aimed at “the government” cannot be silently translated into a demand that a municipal office change national tenancy law.

| Date | Exact or carefully attributed demand | Issuer | Intended decision-maker | Outcome / status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Before 23 September | Recover original statement | Local organisers | To establish | Open |
| 23 September | Recover national calls | Relevant unions / confederation | To establish | Open |
| During Sol mobilisation | Recover assembly or campaign demands | To establish | To establish | Open |
| After 28 September | Identify whether demands changed after reported agreement | To establish | To establish | Open |
| After 7 October | Identify commemorative and continuing political demands | To establish | To establish | Open |

There is an important political difference between asking for **this person to be allowed home** and asking for **a change in the law for everyone**. The two demands can coexist. Their relationship needs to be shown, not assumed.

## 🏛️ 8. Which Institution Could Actually Do What?

One of the more astonishing achievements of public administration is that a single human problem can be divided among institutions until each is able to explain, with some accuracy, why it is not solely theirs. The explanations may even be legally correct. Maricarmen still needed somewhere she could live.

The reported chronology includes municipal discussions about accommodation and mediation, an agreement announced on **28 September** for a possible return, and national political activity concerning housing measures. [4, 5, 12] These developments should be reconstructed separately.

| Institution or actor | Relevant kind of decision | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| **Property owner** | Offers, settlement terms, contractual agreement | Dated proposals, signed documents, attributed statements |
| **Courts / enforcement authorities** | Possession and procedural enforcement | Orders, notices, judicial decisions |
| **Madrid City Council** | Municipal services, accommodation offers, mediation | Official statements, meeting records |
| **Community of Madrid** | Regional housing and social-care responsibilities | Specific recorded decisions |
| **National government** | Policy and decree-law proposals | BOE text, government records |
| **Congress** | Parliamentary consideration and validation | Votes, debate transcripts, official record |
| **UN human-rights mechanisms** | Communications and requested interim measures | Original communication and response |

### The agreement is not the return

Contemporary accounts cited in the existing cluster report an arrangement announced around **28 September** involving a possible **eight-year tenancy** and rent linked to income, subject to Maricarmen's acceptance or completion of further steps. [5] An announcement, an agreement in principle, a signed tenancy, receipt of keys and a physical return are **different events**. The biography node preserves the individual chronology; this node asks which public or collective pressures can actually be documented as influencing the negotiations.

### The causation test

A decision made after a demonstration may have been influenced by the demonstration. It may also have been under consideration before it. To say the campaign *caused* a decision, look for contemporaneous acknowledgement, documentary links, changes in institutional position and plausible alternatives. **After** is a date. **Because of** is an argument requiring evidence.

The same discipline applies to reported September–October decree-laws. Their exact texts, legal status, parliamentary votes and explanatory statements must be established from the **BOE** and **Congreso**, not from a compressed campaign timeline. The legal analysis belongs in [⚖️ El Derecho a la Vivienda](./⚖️_el_derecho_a_la_vivienda.md).

## 🕯️ 9. After Maricarmen — Mourning Is Not a Marketing Department

Maricarmen's reported death on **7 October 2026** changed the context of public action. [4] A campaign focused on preventing removal or securing a return could no longer pursue those goals for her in the same way. The public response therefore needs its own chronology: commemorations, statements, demonstrations, continued demands and legislative references, each dated and attributed.

Public mourning can express grief, solidarity, anger, political commitment or several things at once. It does not follow that every participant came for the same reason. Nor does the later use of a person's name mean that the person agreed with every policy subsequently advanced under it.

*Por Todas Las Maricarmen* is a powerful plural. It allows a particular story to make other experiences recognisable. But the plural must not erase the singular. Maricarmen was not born to serve as a symbol of a housing crisis. She had spent decades being herself before most of the country learned her name.

Her own publicly documented statements should remain distinct from what organisers said **about** her, and from what institutions said **after** her death. The campaign's history is worth recording. Her life is not campaign property.

## 🪿 10. The Longer History Beneath the Street

To understand why collective action around an eviction could become nationally intelligible, the node needs historical depth without pretending that every movement is a branch of one tree.

**Neighbourhood associations** organised around housing, infrastructure and local democracy during late Francoism and the transition. Their history demonstrates that collective urban knowledge long predates contemporary digital platforms. [8]

**The *okupa* movement** developed traditions of occupied and self-managed social space. Its history concerns housing, but also the social use of buildings and autonomous political infrastructure. [9]

**PAH**, established in Barcelona in 2009, brought mortgage-debt problems into collective assemblies, practical legal advice and anti-eviction campaigning. Its 2013 popular legislative initiative illustrates the difference between mobilising support, reaching Parliament and securing the legislation originally demanded. [10–11]

**Tenants' unions** address the specific conditions of rental housing and tenant representation. Their contemporary organising may intersect with older traditions without becoming identical to them.

These histories offer a repertoire: assemblies, shared advice, public calls, legal coordination, solidarity at a threatened home, and pressure for legislative change. They do not establish direct organisational descent in Maricarmen's case. That requires actual membership, statements, cooperation or other documented links.

## 🕸️ 11. The Street as an Information Environment

The same event can generate different information for different observers. A supporter sees an opportunity to stand with someone. A lawyer sees a procedural timetable and possible remedies. A journalist sees claims that must be checked. Police see a gathering requiring operational decisions. A government department sees demands that may or may not fall within its remit. Maricarmen sees a struggle concerning **her own home**.

These are **analytical positions**, not assertions about the private thoughts of any named participant. Their usefulness is that they explain why making something visible is not the same as making everyone understand it in the same way.

### Three loops to examine

**Local loop:** threatened eviction → legal and neighbourhood information → gathering → revised plans following court or enforcement developments.

**Public loop:** local action → reporting and public statements → wider awareness → additional mobilisation or commentary.

**Institutional loop:** demands → official response → campaign interpretation → further demands, negotiations or action.

These loops may operate simultaneously. They can reinforce one another, stall, or carry contradictory information. Their presence in the model does not prove every arrow occurred in this case. The drafting task is to attach **dated evidence** to each arrow we wish to claim.

### Distributed capacity is not magic

A neighbour's knowledge of a person's needs is not a substitute for a court judgment. A lawyer's understanding of procedure is not a substitute for the person's preferences. A union's ability to assemble supporters is not the same as a council's power to allocate accommodation. A broadcaster can extend visibility without acquiring the authority to settle the dispute.

What collective organisation can do is **connect** these forms of knowledge and action. Sometimes the connections create capacities that none of the participants has alone. Sometimes they expose the fact that no participant can resolve the entire problem. Both are significant findings.

That is the difference between treating the street as background noise and treating it as a political information environment.

## 🌹 12. What the Street Can and Cannot Decide

The street does not overturn a court judgment by applause. It does not sign a tenancy for an owner, legislate for Parliament or supply a clinical assessment for a doctor. Those limits are real.

But neither is the street merely an audience waiting for institutions to perform. Public assembly can connect people confronting similar circumstances, distribute practical knowledge, give neglected problems visibility and require decision-makers to explain what they are doing. It can also reveal disagreement: about tactics, legal remedies, political priorities and what counts as an acceptable outcome.

The question is not whether everyone in a square had the same theory of housing. They almost certainly did not. The question is what they were able to **do together**, what institutions actually **heard**, what changed, and what remained unresolved.

In Maricarmen's case, that final distinction cannot be wished away. There was public action. There were reported negotiations. There was an announced possibility of return. And Maricarmen did not return home before she died. [4–5] We do not improve that history by converting it into a tidy victory or an equally tidy explanation of every cause.

*La calle no se calla.* The street does not fall silent. The next question is whether anyone with the power to act has understood what it was saying.
