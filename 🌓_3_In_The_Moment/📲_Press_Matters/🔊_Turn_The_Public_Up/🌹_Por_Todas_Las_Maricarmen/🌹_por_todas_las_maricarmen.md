# 🌹 Por Todas Las Maricarmen
**First created:** 2026-10-08 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-09  
*One woman's home, a neighbourhood's refusal to look away, and the uncomfortable distance between housing as property and housing as a life.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

A house is a physical structure. A property is an object of law and finance. A home is where somebody has worked out how to live.

Those three things may occupy the same address, but they do not necessarily appear in the same administrative file. One department sees a tenancy. Another sees a person requiring accommodation. A court sees a claim to possession. An investor sees an asset. The person who has lived there for seventy-one years sees the rooms where her family lived, the route to the front door, the neighbourhood she knows, and the place she would rather not be forced to leave.

In September 2026, that person was **Maricarmen Abascal**, aged 87, a tenant in Madrid's Retiro district. After years contesting the termination of a longstanding tenancy, she was removed from her home on 23 September. She left on a stretcher, was taken to hospital, and died on 7 October. Between those dates, a deal was announced that would have allowed her to return. She did not make it home. [1][2][3][4]

Her eviction prompted demonstrations and encampments across Spain and became entangled with a national dispute over housing legislation. But *Por Todas Las Maricarmen* must not make the mistake of turning Maricarmen herself into a convenient illustration of somebody else's political argument. She had her own wishes. She spoke. She resisted. And, from hospital, she encouraged other people to defend their homes. [5]

This node follows the story from **home → tenancy dispute → attempted evictions → neighbourhood mobilisation → national institutions → the proposed return → what remains**. Its linked nodes carry the deeper histories of housing law, Spanish neighbourhood movements, financialisation and collective organising.

---

## 🕯️ 1. Seventy-One Years Is Not a Short-Term Let

Maricarmen had lived in her Madrid apartment since childhood, following her family's arrival under a tenancy dating to 1956. Contemporary reporting describes the flat on Calle Alcalde Sáinz de Baranda as the home in which she had spent roughly seventy-one years. The tenancy passed through her family, and the later legal dispute centred on whether her right to remain under the historical arrangement had endured. [1][6]

Seventy-one years is not merely an unusually long duration on a spreadsheet. It means a whole life has accumulated around a particular place. The geography becomes practical knowledge: which room is easiest to use, where the familiar services are, who lives nearby, how to move through the neighbourhood, which ordinary arrangements make independent life possible.

That does **not** mean everyone wants to remain in one dwelling forever. Nor does it mean every old tenancy can continue regardless of law. It means that a decision about possession may carry consequences extending well beyond the property transaction being decided.

Maricarmen was also a disabled older woman with mobility difficulties. Her circumstances made the question of suitable alternative accommodation especially consequential. The details of disability recognition and the historical tenancy rules require careful legal treatment; neither can be collapsed into the other. [1][6]

There is another danger in the way these stories are told. An elderly woman may become publicly visible primarily because she is perceived as vulnerable. Yet Maricarmen's own words and decisions are central to this history. She was not an inanimate object around which younger activists organised. The question was where **she** wanted to live.

For the full personal and legal chronology, see [🕯️ La Historia de Maricarmen](./🕯️_la_historia_de_maricarmen.md).

## 🗝️ 2. Three Descriptions of One Flat

In the reported dispute, Urbagestión owned the property and sought possession. Maricarmen occupied it under a historical tenancy whose continuation had been challenged. The courts had to resolve questions of tenancy succession and enforcement. Those are legal questions, not matters to be settled by the volume of a crowd outside the building. [2][6]

But the legal categories do not make the lived consequences disappear.

- **Ownership** identifies a property right and its holder.
- **Tenancy** identifies a contractual and statutory basis for occupation.
- **Home** describes the dwelling as part of someone's actual life.

The distinction is not an argument that one category magically cancels the others. It is an argument against pretending that answering a question in one category answers every question in the remaining two.

Reporting identified a sharp difference between Maricarmen's longstanding rent and rents sought under a new arrangement. That difference makes the economic stakes intelligible. A flat let for hundreds of euros a month produces a different gross rental income from one let for several thousand. Yet **an identifiable financial incentive is not proof of every motive attributed to an owner**. Corporate structure, acquisition terms and individual negotiations need evidence of their own. [2][6]

The law also matters in its actual detail. Historical *renta antigua* arrangements, succession rules and disability-related exceptions cannot be described accurately as though they were ordinary new tenancies. The reported litigation included an initial decision favourable to Maricarmen followed by a reversal on appeal. The original judgments are still needed for a complete account of the reasoning. [6]

So, no, the answer is not simply that property rights do not exist. Nor is it that a possession order exhausts the question of what public authorities owe a person whose home is being taken away. Spain's constitutional housing commitments, tenancy law, civil procedure, disability protections and international obligations are different legal mechanisms with different jobs.

A wonderfully efficient way to make a person vanish is to distribute her circumstances across enough files that nobody's file contains the whole woman.

For the technical treatment, see [⚖️ El Derecho a la Vivienda](./⚖️_el_derecho_a_la_vivienda.md) and [🏦 Casas Para Vivir, No Para Especular](./🏦_casas_para_vivir_no_para_especular.md).

## 🚪 3. An Eviction Is Not Just the Day of the Eviction

By September 2026, Maricarmen had already faced three earlier attempted removals: one in October 2025 and two in June 2026, according to RTVE's retrospective chronology. A contemporary June report described a postponement obtained through a procedural challenge concerning notice before enforcement. The earlier attempts matter because the final removal was not an isolated surprise. [7][8]

Months and years of litigation are part of the experience. So are the arrangements people must make when they do not know whether they will be able to remain at home: contacting lawyers, responding to documents, organising help, preparing for another date, trying to maintain ordinary life while the place in which ordinary life occurs remains uncertain.

On **22 September 2026**, the Madrid tenants' union and neighbourhood groups called for supporters to gather at the building. People stayed overnight. On **23 September**, the judicial commission executed the eviction amid a police presence and public protest. Around 13:00, Maricarmen was carried to an ambulance and taken to Hospital Gregorio Marañón. [1][2][7]

Those details are not metaphors. They are the documented sequence.

What the documentation does **not** establish on its own is a medical conclusion that the eviction caused her death. The chronology is grave enough without borrowing a death certificate we have not seen. She was removed, hospitalised and remained in hospital until she died fourteen days later. Medical causation is a separate evidential question. [4]

An eviction is often reported as an event: *possession recovered*. For the occupant, the uncertainty preceding it and the disruption following it may be equally important. An enforcement date is a point in a much longer human sequence.

## 📣 4. The Neighbourhood Was Already There

The people outside Maricarmen's building did not materialise from nowhere on 23 September. Contemporary reporting identifies the **Sindicato de Inquilinas e Inquilinos de Madrid**, neighbourhood supporters and her lawyer, **Beatriz Duro**, as participants in the struggle over her tenancy. The union and local groups were already organising around the scheduled removal before the final eviction. [2][7]

That matters because there are at least two kinds of knowledge in play.

Maricarmen knew her own circumstances, her home and her wishes. A lawyer could work with the legal procedure. Experienced housing organisers could bring knowledge of previous disputes, public communication, collective support and institutional routes. Neighbours could bring familiarity, presence and relationships. These are not interchangeable contributions. Together, they can create capacities that no single participant possesses alone.

There is a longer Spanish history here. Neighbourhood associations organised around housing, services and urban conditions under late Francoism and during the democratic transition. Later, the *okupa* movement developed practices of occupation and autonomous social space. The Plataforma de Afectados por la Hipoteca (PAH), founded in 2009, organised people confronting mortgage debt and eviction after the financial crisis. Contemporary tenants' unions address another overlapping but distinct terrain: rental housing and collective tenant representation.

It would be inaccurate to say that all these organisations are the same movement with one command structure. Their histories, membership and methods differ. But Spain did not enter September 2026 with no collective memory of resisting displacement.

For those histories, see [✊ Stop Desahucios](./✊_stop_desahucios.md), [🗝️ El Movimiento Okupa](./🗝️_el_movimiento_okupa.md) and [🕸️ Cómo Se Organiza Un Barrio](./🕸️_como_se_organiza_un_barrio.md).

## 🌹 5. Por Todas Las Maricarmen

After the eviction, protests spread beyond the immediate neighbourhood. RTVE's chronology records demonstrations in several Spanish cities and the development of an encampment at **Puerta del Sol** on 26–27 September, with further encampments elsewhere. The Sindicato de Inquilinas and other housing organisations helped make the case a national point of mobilisation. [7][9]

Here is the change in scale: one person's contested tenancy becomes a public question about what should happen to people facing eviction without suitable alternative accommodation.

The plural in *Por Todas Las Maricarmen* is doing political work. It says: this is not only about one named woman. There are others whose circumstances differ, but whose security of housing also depends on legal protections, money, public services and the capacity to resist displacement.

There is a risk in that plural, too. Once a person's name becomes a slogan, everyone else can begin speaking over her. The better safeguard is to keep returning to her documented words. From hospital, Maricarmen said she had fought so that others might learn to fight for their own homes, and urged them to continue. [5]

She did not need to be transformed into a symbol against her will in order for her experience to matter to other people. She was already describing a collective purpose.

For the detailed protest sequence, see [📣 La Calle No Se Calla](./📣_la_calle_no_se_calla.md).

## 🏛️ 6. Which Institution Was Supposed to Do What?

The story became national, but the responsibilities were not held by a single institution.

| Institution | Distinct responsibility or role |
| --- | --- |
| Courts | Decide legal disputes and supervise enforcement under applicable law. |
| Madrid municipality | Assess and offer certain emergency accommodation and municipal mediation options. |
| Community of Madrid | Exercise relevant regional housing and social-service responsibilities. |
| National government | Propose and adopt measures within its constitutional powers, including decree-laws. |
| Congress | Decide whether decree-laws receive parliamentary validation. |
| UN human-rights mechanisms | Examine relevant treaty obligations and, where appropriate, request interim measures. |

Contemporary reporting says Madrid City Council offered Maricarmen an emergency place in residential care, which she declined. It also reports attempts to find a negotiated solution involving municipal housing services. The crucial question is not whether **any** offer was made, but whether what was offered was suitable for her needs and compatible with her wishes. A care-home place and an independent home are not necessarily equivalent. Equally, declining an offer does not, by itself, prove that the offer was unsuitable. [10][11]

This is what institutional fragmentation looks like when the subject is a human being rather than a workflow. The court may be addressing lawful possession. The council may be addressing an immediate accommodation need. A social-care assessment may address mobility and support. The person concerned is living all of those things at once.

The case also reached national housing politics. Reporting documents government proposals for emergency protections, parliamentary rejection of initial measures and replacement decree-laws. The precise provisions, commencement dates and parliamentary status must be read from the **Boletín Oficial del Estado** and Congress records, not inferred from a headline or a campaign slogan. [7][12]

That distinction also prevents a common analytical shortcut: a legislative response following a highly visible case is evidence of political attention; it does not prove that every clause was drafted because of that case or that every subsequent decision had the same cause.

## 🏘️ 7. The Crisis Did Not Begin at Her Front Door

Spain's housing difficulties did not start with Maricarmen, and they are not reducible to her tenancy. Housing costs, constrained access to suitable homes, differences between regions, the legacy of mortgage distress, rental insecurity and the treatment of homes as investment assets all form part of the larger setting.

Those problems should not be compressed into one grand statistic. An eviction count measures judicial removals under particular definitions. A rent-to-income ratio measures affordability for a defined group. A housing-supply estimate measures another part of the problem. None is a substitute for the others.

Nor are older people a single housing population. An older owner without mortgage debt, an older market renter and a person living under a historical tenancy may face very different risks. Maricarmen's particular combination of age, disability, lengthy residence and disputed *renta antigua* succession was distinctive. The wider mechanisms of housing insecurity were not.

The history matters, too. PAH's organising after 2008, tenants' union campaigns and the much older neighbourhood-association tradition show that housing has been a site of collective politics across several economic and institutional periods. The people gathering in 2026 inherited a landscape of laws, buildings, organisations and practical knowledge that existed before the cameras arrived.

See [🏘️ Ni Casas Sin Gente](./🏘️_ni_casas_sin_gente.md) for the statistics and [🏦 Casas Para Vivir, No Para Especular](./🏦_casas_para_vivir_no_para_especular.md) for the economic structures.

## 🕯️ 8. A Safe, Dependable Home Is Part of How Someone Lives

The distinction between a house and a home becomes particularly important when a person is older, disabled or reliant on established support.

A familiar home can make daily life manageable. The location of furniture, accessible routes, proximity to services, established neighbours, informal help and personal routines may all contribute to independence. Moving may sometimes improve those conditions. It may also disrupt them. The effect depends on the person, the destination and the support available.

This is why **ageing in place** is not simply an emotional preference for an old address. It concerns the conditions that allow somebody to remain safely and meaningfully connected to their chosen environment. The concept should not be used to insist that everyone ought to stay in the same home, either. Choice is the point. An unwanted move into residential care is not made equivalent to independent living merely because both provide a roof.

Housing security is therefore more than the absence of literal homelessness. It can support autonomy, continuity, privacy and the ability to make decisions about care and later life. The more a person's routines and support depend on a known environment, the more important it becomes to assess what is lost or changed by relocation.

The same principle reaches the end of life. There is no universal obligation to want to die at home. But where someone does want to remain at home, that preference cannot be meaningful if the conditions necessary to stay there have already disappeared.

**Dignity in death is connected to the conditions of dignity in life.** Not because every death following an eviction can be medically attributed to that eviction, but because a person's choices about where and how they live remain significant until the end.

This is one of the central Embodied Information Ecology questions in the cluster: what happens when an institution treats an environment as replaceable, while the person living in it has built years of embodied knowledge and social connection around its particular features?

## 🗝️ 9. The Door Was Going to Open Again

On **28 September**, reporting described an agreement between Maricarmen's representatives and Urbagestión that would allow her to return as a tenant, with rent not exceeding **30% of her net income**. Municipal mediation was part of the negotiations. The agreement still required completion and her ratification. [3][7]

This is the point at which the story becomes almost unbearably bureaucratic.

A mechanism for return was reportedly agreed **after** she had been removed. She remained in hospital. She died on 7 October, before she could return home. [3][4]

It is reasonable to ask: **if an arrangement allowing her to stay in or return to the flat could eventually be negotiated, why was an equivalent resolution not reached before the eviction?**

That is a research question, not a completed finding about any participant's intentions. The earlier proposals, administrative constraints, contractual conditions and sequence of decisions need to be established before an answer can be assigned. An agreement achieved after an event is not proof that exactly the same agreement was available at every earlier moment.

But the timing is not incidental. It is central to understanding the gap between an eventual institutional solution and the life of the person waiting for it.

## 🌱 10. What Survives the Case?

There are several different outcomes here, and they should not be confused.

- **The personal outcome:** Maricarmen died before returning to the home she had fought to keep.
- **The legal outcome:** The possession dispute and enforcement proceeded through the courts; the original judgments require separate examination.
- **The negotiated outcome:** A proposed route back to the property was announced after the eviction.
- **The collective outcome:** Neighbours, tenants' organisations and supporters mobilised in Madrid and elsewhere.
- **The institutional outcome:** The case entered a wider national argument about housing policy and parliamentary action.

None of these outcomes automatically cancels the others. A movement can win attention and still fail to secure the result sought by the person at its centre. A legal process can be completed while a social question remains unresolved. A new policy can be introduced after a crisis without repairing what happened to the individual involved.

What organisations may carry forward is practical knowledge: which legal deadlines mattered, what forms of support worked, which institutions could act, where coordination failed and what should be attempted earlier next time. Whether those lessons are actually retained must be documented, not merely hoped for.

For the distinction between immediate support and long-term collective capacity, see [🌱 Cuando El Barrio Responde](./🌱_cuando_el_barrio_responde.md) and [🕸️ Cómo Se Organiza Un Barrio](./🕸️_como_se_organiza_un_barrio.md).

## 🌹 11. For All the Maricarmen

There is no need to pretend that housing is not property, or that contracts and courts can simply be wished away. The more difficult question is whether a society's housing arrangements can recognise the person who lives inside the legal object.

A home can be transferred on paper. Its market value can change. A tenancy can be contested. A court can decide possession. An authority can offer accommodation. Each step may be intelligible within its own rules.

And yet none of those steps, considered alone, describes seventy-one years of somebody's life.

Maricarmen's case became a national argument because people recognised a gap between the narrow question of who was entitled to a flat and the much larger question of what it meant to remove the woman who had made her life there. Her supporters used the plural to insist that this gap was not hers alone.

The plural should never swallow the singular. **Maricarmen was Maricarmen first.**

The question she leaves is not whether every person can always remain in the same dwelling, regardless of circumstances. It is whether housing law, public services and collective institutions can act early enough, and with enough attention to the person concerned, to make safe and dependable housing something more than a promise that arrives after the door has closed.
