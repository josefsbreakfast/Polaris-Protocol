# 🕯️ La Historia de Maricarmen
**First created:** 2026-10-08 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-09  
*Seventy-one years in one Madrid home; the legal proceedings that threatened it; the woman who insisted on remaining; and the agreement that came too late for her to return.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Before Maricarmen Abascal became the name on banners, the woman on television, or a reference in Spanish housing legislation, she had a life. She lived in a flat on Calle Alcalde Sáinz de Baranda, in Madrid's Retiro district, for roughly seventy-one years. Her family had rented it since 1956. She grew older there; the city grew and changed around her. By September 2026, she was 87, had mobility difficulties, and was still insisting that she wanted to live in the place she knew. [1][2][3]

This is the story of **Maricarmen herself**, rather than a substitute for the history of Spain's housing movements. Her case became national news because it brought together a historical tenancy, disputed succession rights, a new property owner, repeated eviction dates, lawyers, neighbours, public institutions and thousands of demonstrators. The argument was larger than one flat. The woman living inside it was not. [1][4]

The distinction matters. A legal file can determine whether a tenancy continues. A housing authority can offer somewhere else to stay. A company can calculate a rental return. None of those records, on its own, can tell us what seventy-one years in one home meant to Maricarmen. Where she spoke publicly, her words have priority over our interpretation. Where the record is silent, it stays silent.

This node follows **the home → the tenancy → the courts → the attempted removals → 23 September → Maricarmen's words from hospital → the negotiations → her death → what remains**. A chronology and source register are included below. The underlying judgments, complete medical records and signed settlement have not been obtained; journalism must not be mistaken for those primary documents.

---

## 🏠 1. Before Anybody Called It a Case

Maricarmen's family tenancy dated to 1956. Accounts published in September and October 2026 describe her as having spent most or all of her life in the same flat. Some reports round the period to seventy years; others say seventy-one. The consistent point is a residence spanning approximately seven decades, not an exact number of days that we can independently calculate. [1][3][5]

The original agreement was reportedly signed by her father. The tenancy subsequently passed to her mother and then to Maricarmen. The family succession history became legally decisive years later, but it was a family history before it was an argument over the transitional provisions of rental law. [5]

A longstanding home contains more than possessions. It can hold familiar routes through rooms, relationships with neighbours, access to services, knowledge of the surrounding streets and arrangements that make ordinary life manageable. These are possible dimensions of long residence, **not invented details of Maricarmen's daily routine**. We do not know which particular rituals, neighbours or household objects mattered most to her unless she or a reliable witness described them.

She did publicly express her attachment to the home and her opposition to being removed. RTVE interviewed her before the September eviction. Her statements were not the voice of a woman who had surrendered the right to decide what she wanted simply because she was elderly or disabled. [3]

That is our starting point: not a helpless emblem, but a woman stating her own position in a dispute about the place she lived.

## 🗝️ 2. An Old Tenancy Meets a New Owner

The family's rental arrangement belonged to a different legal era. Spanish tenancy legislation changed substantially with the **1994 Urban Leases Act** (*Ley de Arrendamientos Urbanos*, LAU), but older contracts continued to be governed in part by transitional provisions. Those provisions made succession—who could step into a tenant's legal position after their death—central to some longstanding tenancies. [5][6]

The apartment was acquired in 2018 by the property company **Urbagestión**. Reporting describes a dispute about whether Maricarmen could continue to occupy it under the historical tenancy after successive family transfers. The company's acquisition did not, by itself, establish the answer to that legal question; the ensuing litigation did. [2][5]

Accounts of the case report a disagreement about a second succession and the application of a disability-related exception. The reported disability recognition was **50%**, against a **65%** threshold relevant to the exception discussed in reporting. These figures should not be read as a general measure of how much assistance Maricarmen needed, nor as a complete description of disability law. They belong to a particular tenancy-succession question. The precise statutory reasoning requires the judgments. [5]

The rental sums made the dispute economically consequential. Contemporary reporting describes a longstanding rent around **€500 per month** and proposals for substantially higher payments after the tenancy's termination was asserted. These are **reported negotiating and rental figures**, not audited company accounts or proof of profit. A larger possible rent explains why the legal status of an older tenancy can matter financially; it does not, alone, establish every motive behind the owner's decisions. [1][5]

One flat, then, appeared in several registers at once: an inherited home, an old contract, a contested legal entitlement and an asset with possible future rental income. The paperwork could classify each. Maricarmen had to live with all of them together.

## ⚖️ 3. The Courts: What We Know, and What We Still Need

The reported legal chronology begins with a challenge to the tenancy after the 2018 acquisition. Retrospective reporting describes proceedings beginning around 2020, an initial outcome favourable to Maricarmen, a subsequent appellate reversal, and unsuccessful attempts to prevent enforcement. It identifies a **June 2022** appellate decision as a significant turning point. [5]

That is enough to outline the dispute, **not** enough to reproduce judicial reasoning as though we had the rulings on our desk. The original first-instance judgment, *Audiencia Provincial* decision and any Supreme Court procedural order should be added to the source register if obtained. In particular, a refusal to admit an appeal is not the same thing as a new merits judgment affirming every aspect of a lower court's reasoning.

The legal issues should be kept separate:

- **Tenancy succession:** whether Maricarmen retained a contractual right to occupy the flat under the relevant transitional law.
- **Enforcement:** when and how an order requiring her to leave could be executed.
- **Vulnerability and disability:** what procedural, housing and social-service safeguards applied to her circumstances.
- **Adequate alternative accommodation:** whether any offered arrangement met her needs and respected her wishes.

A judgment about the first question does not automatically settle the other three. Equally, recognising the importance of her circumstances does not, by itself, establish that the tenancy legally continued. For the detailed legal framework, see [⚖️ El Derecho a la Vivienda](./⚖️_el_derecho_a_la_vivienda.md).

A concrete procedural intervention **did** change the enforcement timetable. Telemadrid reported on **19 June 2026** that the eviction was postponed until September after Maricarmen's defence challenged the notice period under **Article 704 of the Civil Procedure Act**. That provision concerns enforcement affecting a habitual residence. The reported postponement was a procedural result, not a reversal of the underlying tenancy decision. [7]

There is an institutional absurdity worth noticing without mistaking it for a legal argument: a system can spend years deciding who is entitled to remain in a home and still need a separate challenge to ensure the occupant receives the notice the system itself requires. Procedure is not decorative. It is one of the ways people get time to respond.

## 🚪 4. Four Scheduled Evictions, Three That Did Not Happen

RTVE's chronology of **8 October 2026** identifies the final September removal as the **fourth attempted eviction**, after three earlier dates: one in **October 2025** and two in **June 2026**. [4]

The first reported attempt took place on **29 October 2025**. That date is corroborated by RTVE's subsequent fact-check of a video later misrepresented online as showing Maricarmen after her September 2026 eviction. The footage actually came from October 2025. This matters not only for the chronology but for how easily old images can be redeployed to cast doubt on a person's current condition. [8]

The June dates need to be reconstructed carefully from the original notices and orders. A scheduled enforcement that is postponed by a court is not necessarily the same event as officers arriving and beginning to remove someone. The distinction is important for counting attempts and understanding what legal representation or public mobilisation actually achieved.

By **22 September 2026**, the tenants' union and neighbourhood groups were calling supporters to gather outside Maricarmen's building ahead of the next day's scheduled enforcement. Some people stayed overnight. The case was already public, and the possibility of removal was no longer confined to the people named in the court file. [4][9]

It is tempting to describe the first three dates as victories and the fourth as defeat. That would make a neat story. It would also flatten months of uncertainty into a scoreboard. Each postponed date left Maricarmen facing the prospect of another one. **Housing insecurity was not suspended merely because enforcement was delayed.**

## 🚑 5. Wednesday, 23 September 2026

On the morning of **23 September**, a judicial commission arrived at the building on Calle Alcalde Sáinz de Baranda. Contemporary reporting describes a substantial police presence and supporters gathered nearby, some separated from the entrance by a cordon. [1][2][9]

At approximately **13:00**, Maricarmen was removed from the flat **on a stretcher** and transferred to an ambulance attended by emergency medical personnel. She was taken to **Hospital Gregorio Marañón**, close to her home. EFE and RTVE independently reported the removal and hospital transfer that day. [1][2][4]

The images circulated widely. But the spectacle of the stretcher should not become the whole account. She had already endured years of proceedings and repeated scheduled removals. She had a position on her own tenancy and had spoken about it publicly. The photograph is evidence of a moment, not a substitute for her life before it.

Her lawyer, **Beatriz Duro**, told reporters that the owner had not accepted the proposals put forward to avoid or delay the eviction. This is the lawyer's contemporaneous account of the negotiations; the full written exchanges have not been examined. [2]

Reporting also described an emergency residential-care placement offered by Madrid municipal authorities, which Maricarmen declined. The relevant question is not whether she was offered *something*—reports indicate that she was—but what that arrangement involved, whether it was suitable for her, and how it related to her expressed wish to remain independent and return home. An emergency bed and an established home are not interchangeable concepts, even though both may be described in official records as accommodation. [10]

## 🩺 6. Disability, Hospitalisation and What We Cannot Infer

Maricarmen was 87 and had reported mobility and disability-related difficulties. She left her home on a stretcher and was admitted to hospital after the eviction. On **24 September**, RTVE reported that she remained in hospital in a delicate condition and had not been able to retrieve her belongings. [1][11]

These facts are serious enough without adding an invented clinical narrative. The public reporting does not give us her complete diagnoses, pre-eviction medical assessments, hospital notes, or a clinical finding that the eviction caused her death. Statements about anxiety, exhaustion or deteriorating health should be attributed to the people or reports making them, not converted into a medical certificate by repetition.

One useful warning about the information environment came on **2 October**. RTVE debunked social-media claims using footage of Maricarmen standing without a wheelchair. The video had been recorded **eleven months earlier**, on 29 October 2025. Her mobility in one earlier recording could not establish her condition in late September 2026. [8]

This is a small but revealing example of a much larger failure of contextual information: an image is not a timeless clinical assessment. The date matters. The circumstances matter. The person matters.

## 📣 7. Maricarmen Speaks from Hospital

On **25 September**, RTVE published Maricarmen's recorded message from hospital. She thanked those supporting her and encouraged other people to defend their own homes. Her message included the appeal: **“No ha podido ser, pero por favor luchad”**—it had not been possible, but please keep fighting. [12]

That statement belongs to her. It is not necessary to retrofit a political identity onto her or imagine what she privately believed about every policy being debated in her name. She publicly linked her own struggle to others facing housing insecurity. We can report that accurately and let it carry its own weight.

The response to the eviction had already spread beyond the street outside her flat. Demonstrations took place in Madrid and other Spanish cities, followed by an encampment at **Puerta del Sol**. The Sindicato de Inquilinas and other housing organisations were prominent in that mobilisation. The full history, demands and organisational relationships belong in [📣 La Calle No Se Calla](./📣_la_calle_no_se_calla.md) and [🕸️ Cómo Se Organiza Un Barrio](./🕸️_como_se_organiza_un_barrio.md). [4]

The biographical point is narrower and more important: Maricarmen was not simply being spoken *about*. Even from hospital, she spoke *to* other people.

## 🏛️ 8. The Institutions Enter the Room

After the eviction, attention turned to whether Maricarmen could return to the same flat. That required negotiation among people and institutions with different powers. The owner could offer a tenancy; Maricarmen would need to accept its terms; municipal officials could mediate or examine available housing schemes; her lawyer could represent her interests. None of those actors could simply substitute their preference for hers. [13][14]

On **25 September**, RTVE and Europa Press reported that Urbagestión had proposed an arrangement involving Madrid's **ReViva** programme. The city council considered that proposal unworkable within the programme's requirements, while indicating a willingness to mediate. This is an important correction to any narrative in which the municipality either did absolutely nothing or could unilaterally compel the owner to restore the previous tenancy. The precise programme rules and proposals would need separate documentary examination. [13][14]

The wider institutional dispute—courts, city council, regional government, national government, Parliament and reported UN intervention—should not be compressed into one imaginary actor called *Spain*. Each had different responsibilities, procedures and limitations. The question is how those interventions interacted around a single woman's needs.

## 🗝️ 9. Monday, 28 September: A Route Home

On the evening of **28 September**, RTVE reported that negotiations involving **Madrid City Council**, **Beatriz Duro** and **Urbagestión** had produced an agreement intended to allow Maricarmen to return to her flat. The proposed tenancy would last **eight years**, with rent set at **30% of her income**. [15]

There was a vital qualification in the same report: the arrangement still required **Maricarmen's own approval** before becoming definitive. An announced agreement, an accepted offer, a signed contract, receipt of keys and physical return are separate events. The available reporting does not permit us to pretend that they all happened together. [15]

Her lawyer welcomed the prospect of return while saying that the resolution had come late. That comment is attributable to Duro, not an independently established account of what would have happened under any hypothetical earlier settlement. [15]

Still, the chronology raises a legitimate question: **why did a possible route back into the home emerge only after she had been removed from it?** To answer that properly would require the earlier offers, the company's replies, the municipal correspondence and the final contractual documents. We can ask the question without inventing the answer.

There is something bleakly bureaucratic about arriving at a plan to return a woman to her home after completing the elaborate administrative exercise of taking her out of it. The irony belongs to the sequence of decisions. The suffering does not belong to the joke.

## 🕯️ 10. Wednesday, 7 October: She Did Not Return

Maricarmen Abascal died on **7 October 2026**, aged 87. Her death was reported by major Spanish outlets and international news agencies. She had not returned to live in the flat despite the agreement announced nine days earlier. [4][5][16]

Reporting describes the toll of the prolonged dispute and her hospitalisation. Some participants and commentators drew a causal connection between the eviction and her deterioration. **The publicly examined sources do not establish a clinical cause-of-death finding**, and this node does not present one. A temporal sequence, however distressing, is not the same thing as medical proof.

Nor is medical causation the only question that matters. It is possible to examine the legal process, the suitability of alternatives, the timing of negotiations, her expressed wishes and the consequences of housing insecurity without claiming a diagnosis that has not been documented.

She wanted to return. A route home had been announced. She did not get to live there again. The distinction between those three statements is the ending of the personal chronology.

## 🌹 11. What Happened to Her Name

After Maricarmen's death, demonstrations and commemorations continued. Her name had become part of a national housing debate, and the official preamble to **Royal Decree-Law 29/2026**, adopted on 6 October and published on 7 October, explicitly referred to **María del Carmen Abascal** and the collective attention awakened by her eviction. That is a documented governmental reference, not proof that every provision of the decree originated in her case. [4][17]

Her name was now being used in a plural political claim: *por todas las Maricarmen*. That plural has a purpose. It connects one person's experience with other people's insecurity. But the plural has a risk as well. A person can become an icon so efficiently that the icon starts replacing the person.

The wider campaign, parliamentary events and longer history of Spanish housing movements are developed in [🌹 Por Todas Las Maricarmen](./🌹_por_todas_las_maricarmen.md). This node keeps the focus on the woman whose name those arguments carried.

She had a family tenancy. She fought over its continuation. She spoke publicly about wanting to remain. She was removed on 23 September. She encouraged others from hospital. An agreement was announced for her return. She died on 7 October without having gone home.

No one needs to add an invented thought, an invented medical conclusion or an invented last scene to make that history matter.

## 🔬 13. What the Record Cannot Tell Us

Several missing documents matter materially:

1. **The original tenancy and succession documents.** Without them, the contractual history is reconstructed from reporting.
2. **The first-instance and appellate judgments, and any Supreme Court order.** Without them, precise judicial reasoning remains attributed rather than independently examined.
3. **The enforcement notices and postponement orders.** These would distinguish each scheduled attempt from actual enforcement and establish procedural grounds.
4. **The reported individual UN communication and Spain's response.** Broader UN case law is not a substitute for the original communication in this case.
5. **The complete pre- and post-eviction settlement proposals.** These would help establish why negotiations failed or changed.
6. **The final agreement and any acceptance or signature.** An announced agreement must not be described as an executed tenancy without evidence.
7. **Clinical documentation.** The reporting establishes hospitalisation and death, not a verified medical chain of causation.
8. **Corporate and land-registry records.** These would confirm the exact ownership vehicle and acquisition history.

This is not an invitation to suspend every conclusion. We can establish the eviction, her public statements, the reported agreement and her death. We cannot claim to know everything she experienced or everything every institution privately considered.

The distinction is part of respecting her, not an obstacle to telling her story.
