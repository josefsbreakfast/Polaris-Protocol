# 🏦 Casas Para Vivir, No Para Especular
**First created:** 2026-10-08 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-09  
*Housing as shelter, collateral and investment — and the administrative achievement of making somewhere to sleep compete with somewhere to park capital.*

---

## 🧭 Orientation

A home is a place to live, an object of property law, a source of rental income, collateral for borrowing and sometimes a component of an international investment portfolio. These descriptions can all be true at once. They do not, however, tell us the same thing. A lender asks what a building secures; a fund asks what it earns; a tenant asks whether they can stay. Spain's housing history makes the friction between these models especially visible.

This node follows the **financial machinery**: homeownership policy, mortgage credit, the property boom and crash, distressed assets, SAREB, SOCIMIs, public housing sales, ownership structures, pricing feedback and housing rights. It distinguishes documented historical facts from general economic mechanisms and case-specific allegations. Its purpose is neither to condemn every owner nor to treat a financially viable property market as evidence that everybody is adequately housed.

**Scope boundary:** The Maricarmen material is a research interface, not proof that a particular landlord pursued a speculative strategy. Her legal position and any relevant ownership transactions must be established from independent case records. The housing stock and affordability analysis belongs primarily in `🏘️_ni_casas_sin_gente.md`; the rights analysis in `⚖️_el_derecho_a_la_vivienda.md`.

## 🏗️ 1. Before the asset class: Spain’s property settlement

Spain’s housing economy cannot be explained by beginning in 2008. The long history of state-supported ownership, urban migration, land development and comparatively restricted permanent social renting shaped the market into which modern finance entered. Franco-era governments promoted homeownership, but that policy history should not be confused with an uninterrupted, uniform national experience. There were differences between industrialising cities, rural areas and rapidly developing coastal regions. A protected dwelling sold to a household, moreover, is not equivalent to a dwelling retained in a public rental portfolio. Both can involve state assistance, but they create different long-term institutional capacities.

Housing became central to household wealth because a purchased dwelling combines a place to live with a potentially appreciating asset. For many ordinary owners, that is a practical response to a system in which private ownership offers security and public rental alternatives are scarce. The household that buys one flat to live in should not be assigned the same motives, powers or risk profile as an investor acquiring a portfolio. The point is not to declare one category virtuous and the other wicked. It is to examine the incentives and institutions operating at different scales.

The development chain connects land, planning permissions, developers, builders, lenders, buyers and local government. Public infrastructure and zoning decisions can change the value of land; mortgage credit can change how much buyers can bid; developers make decisions based on anticipated sales and financing costs. None of those actors controls the entire chain. Nor does a finished dwelling necessarily appear where the people who need it can afford to live. That is our first structural warning: housing construction is not the same measurement as housing access.

## 💶 2. Financialisation: define the machinery

Financialisation describes the growing importance of financial markets, institutions, instruments and logics in organising economic activity. In housing, the concept directs attention to how homes are financed, bundled, valued, traded and managed in anticipation of financial returns. Housing was bought, sold and rented long before the contemporary term existed. Commodification, landlordism, speculation, concentration and financialisation overlap, but none is an adequate synonym for all the others.

A dwelling can generate a stream of rental income; a lender can treat it as collateral; a developer can assess expected resale prices; a fund can evaluate a portfolio using projected net operating income, financing costs and exit value. These calculations matter because they influence decisions about acquisition, maintenance, rent, refurbishment and disposal. A rent increase may be explained by costs, market conditions, contract rules, investment targets or some combination. The label *financialised* is not evidence of a particular motive. We need transactions, ownership records, business models and legal circumstances.

Speculation, more narrowly, involves taking positions influenced by expectations of future gain, often including appreciation. Investment in new construction may add dwellings; purchase of existing units changes ownership without itself adding supply. Acquisition of a deteriorating building may finance rehabilitation, or it may pursue a different strategy. The same capital inflow can have different consequences depending on local vacancy, planning rules, tenancy protections, credit conditions and the type of asset. This is why a national slogan needs local evidence.

## 🏦 3. Mortgages, credit and collateral

A mortgage links household accommodation to a credit contract. Principal is borrowed, interest compensates the lender, and the property generally provides security. The loan-to-value ratio compares the debt with the assessed property value; debt-service burdens compare required payments with income. Those are not the same measures. A household can have substantial equity and still face a cash-flow problem, or have manageable payments while holding little equity.

Credit availability changes effective demand. When lenders offer more credit on easier terms, some buyers can bid more. If the supply of suitable homes responds slowly, part of the additional purchasing power may be capitalised into prices. Rising prices can in turn increase collateral valuations, reinforcing lending capacity. This is a possible feedback loop, not a claim that credit explains every price change. Household formation, migration, wages, interest rates, regulation and construction capacity also matter.

Spain’s pre-2008 expansion involved lending to households and property developers, including important exposure among savings banks (*cajas de ahorros*). The crisis exposed the risks of highly leveraged construction and falling asset values. Some borrowers faced foreclosure and, under relevant legal circumstances, continuing personal liability after the sale of collateral. The latter issue became central to PAH’s campaigns for *dación en pago*. A mortgage dispute, however, is not the same legal problem as a tenancy dispute; the distinction must survive the political narrative.

## 📉 4. Boom, crash, restructuring

During the property boom, construction, employment, bank credit, land values and household borrowing interacted. When expected prices and credit conditions changed, developers could find themselves holding unfinished projects, unsold dwellings and debts that could not be repaid from anticipated sales. Banks then faced deteriorating loans and collateral whose market value was uncertain. Financial stress moved through institutions as well as households.

A useful historical reconstruction follows the asset rather than relying on a single dramatic label: who financed the development; who held the loan; whether it became non-performing; whether collateral was enforced; whether the loan or property was transferred; who ultimately managed or sold it. Those stages can involve different legal entities. They also have different implications for public money and household rights.

Public intervention to stabilise a financial system can create difficult distributional questions. Who bore losses: original borrowers, bank shareholders, subordinated creditors, public authorities or later purchasers? The answer varies with the transaction. It is not sufficient to say that ‘the taxpayer bought all the houses’, nor that public support had no housing consequences. The proper unit of analysis is the particular rescue, transfer, guarantee or accounting treatment.

## 🏚️ 5. SAREB: when distressed property becomes a public question

SAREB — *Sociedad de Gestión de Activos Procedentes de la Reestructuración Bancaria* — was incorporated on 28 November 2012 to manage assets transferred in the restructuring of Spain’s banking sector. It was not simply a warehouse containing ready-to-let flats. Its portfolio has included loans, land, unfinished developments and completed properties, each with different legal, financial and practical characteristics. A loan secured on a building is not ownership of an available apartment.

The FROB initially subscribed 45% of SAREB’s shares. Following Royal Decree-Law 1/2022, the FROB acquired a majority stake in April 2022, reaching 50.14%. These are documented institutional changes, not evidence that every asset suddenly became social housing. Public control created questions about governance, accounting, disposal and social utility, but those objectives can conflict. A distressed asset may be in the wrong location, require expensive works or remain subject to legal constraints.

A rigorous SAREB case study needs a stock-and-flow account: number and type of assets transferred; subsequent loan resolutions; property sales; housing assigned or transferred for social use; financial performance; and the date and definition of each reported measure. It must distinguish commitments, agreements and completed occupancy. The public can reasonably ask what social value emerges from public control of housing-related assets, but a count of assets cannot answer that question without knowing what they actually are.

**Verified institutional anchors:** FROB’s SAREB overview and its 5 April 2022 acquisition announcement are listed in the sources below.

## 🏢 6. Who owns the rental stock?

Ownership data are deceptively difficult. A count of landlords is not a count of dwellings. A count of corporate entities is not a count of ultimate beneficial owners. A property manager collecting rent may have no equity interest in the building. A pension fund may invest indirectly through shares in a property company rather than hold title to any specific flat. Researchers therefore need to specify the unit: person, company, building, dwelling, rental unit, portfolio or floor area.

Individual owners, families with additional properties, developers, banks, specialist property companies, public bodies and institutional investors may all participate. Their relative shares vary across locations and market segments. It is entirely possible for individual landlords to predominate nationally while one company holds a consequential proportion of units in a particular development or neighbourhood. Local concentration and national market share answer different questions.

The distribution of ownership matters because different actors possess different capacities. A household landlord may depend heavily on one tenant’s rent and face concentrated maintenance risk. A large investor may diversify across thousands of units, obtain specialist legal and accounting advice, and make portfolio-level decisions. Neither size alone nor legal form proves misconduct. But size and structure affect bargaining power, information access and the consequences of changing management strategy.

## 🧾 7. SOCIMIs: the legal wrapper

Spain’s SOCIMI regime was established by Law 11/2009, with significant later amendments including Law 16/2012. A *Sociedad Anónima Cotizada de Inversión en el Mercado Inmobiliario* is a regulated company form for investment in income-producing real estate. The legislation sets conditions concerning assets, listing, income and profit distribution, together with a special tax regime. A SOCIMI can hold commercial as well as residential property; counting all SOCIMI assets as Spanish rental homes would be wrong.

The important analytical move is to separate the legal vehicle from its actual portfolio and conduct. Questions include what assets it owns, whether it develops or acquires existing stock, how it finances purchases, what dividend obligations apply and how changes in rental income affect returns. A company’s market value reflects investor expectations as well as current property income. Those expectations may influence management choices, but a particular tenancy decision still requires case evidence.

**October 2026 statutory caution:** The BOE’s consolidated Law 11/2009 records changes published on 30 September and 7 October 2026, including a September measure subsequently disapplied and a new measure taking effect in October. Consequently, a description of the SOCIMI tax regime based solely on the pre-2026 text is not current. The precise tax effects and commencement rules must be read from the relevant dated BOE texts, rather than guessed from the headline rate. This node deliberately does not assert an unverified current effective rate.

## 🦈 8. Distressed assets and fondos buitre

The phrase *fondos buitre* — ‘vulture funds’ — is a political description commonly applied to investors purchasing distressed assets. It is not itself a precise Spanish legal category. A distressed investor may buy loans at a discount, acquire buildings, restructure debt, seek rental income or sell after market recovery. Those strategies have different consequences for borrowers and tenants. The rhetorical label can communicate criticism; it cannot replace the transaction history.

After the financial crisis, distressed loans and real estate moved through banks, restructuring vehicles, funds and property managers. The tenant at the end of that chain may not know the ultimate economic owner or the business plan governing their building. This is an information asymmetry: one side can see the portfolio and financing, while the other may only receive a new rent demand or a letter from a new manager.

Research should identify a named transaction, its seller and buyer, date, price where disclosed, assets involved, subsequent management and independently documented tenant outcomes. A sale price below an earlier valuation does not automatically establish wrongdoing; valuations are time-specific and depend on asset condition and risk. Nor does the lawful acquisition of an asset establish that all subsequent treatment of residents was fair.

## 🏘️ 9. When the building changes hands and the people stay

A property can be sold without becoming empty. The financial owner changes, but residents may retain contractual and statutory rights. Spain’s tenancy rules have changed across decades, making the start date, applicable legal regime, succession rights and precise contract terms important. A long-established tenant is not interchangeable with a new tenant under a current standard lease. Nor is a disputed lease termination identical to an unauthorised occupation.

Investors may calculate the difference between the income produced by existing tenancies and the income expected under another lawful use or arrangement. That creates a potential tension between investment plans and residential continuity. Whether a particular landlord acted on such an incentive must be demonstrated through documents, conduct or credible reporting. The existence of a court case proves neither speculative intent nor the absence of hardship.

For the Maricarmen cluster, the correct procedure is to reconstruct ownership and tenancy chronologically: registered owner at each relevant date; contract history; relevant legislation; parties’ legal positions; judicial decisions; enforcement; negotiations; and the resident’s stated wishes. The general financialisation framework is context, not a licence to invent an owner’s motives. A home can become a line in an acquisition spreadsheet without ceasing to be the centre of somebody’s daily life.

## 📈 10. Price formation and speculative feedback

Housing prices are influenced by households’ incomes, available credit, population movement, construction, planning, existing stock, investor demand and expectations. Investor purchases of existing dwellings can increase competition for those assets without adding units. Investment in construction or rehabilitation can increase usable supply. The effect of ‘investment’ therefore depends on what is being financed.

One reinforcing mechanism is plausible: anticipated appreciation attracts buyers; additional demand supports higher prices; observed price rises strengthen expectations. But a price increase is not proof that this loop is operating. In a growing city with limited construction, household formation alone may put pressure on rents. Conversely, an investor boom may coincide with new construction. Empirical analysis needs local comparisons, timing, controls and explicit uncertainty.

The Banco de España’s 2024 annual report estimated that the cumulative difference between net household creation and new housing completions during 2022–2024 was approximately **400,000–450,000 dwellings**. This is a comparison of two flows, **not** a claim that precisely that many people are homeless or that Spain has no empty homes. The report identifies Madrid, Barcelona, València, Alicante and Málaga as provinces accounting for just over half the estimated difference. The geographical concentration matters: one cannot solve a shortage in Madrid by pointing to a vacant dwelling somewhere with different jobs, services and household demand.

## 🧳 11. Tourist accommodation and competing uses

A dwelling may generate different revenue as a long-term rental, seasonal accommodation or short-term tourist let. Where the expected tourist income exceeds residential rent after costs and regulatory risk, some owners may seek conversion. If the conversion removes an otherwise available long-term rental, it can affect local supply. But the size of the effect depends on whether the property was previously a principal residence, second home, occasional home-share or commercial tourist unit.

Spain is not one tourist housing market. Barcelona, Madrid, coastal municipalities and island communities differ in demand, licensing, enforcement and housing pressures. National counts can hide intense neighbourhood concentration. Evidence should distinguish registered tourist dwellings from platform listings and active commercial operations; duplicate listings and unlicensed properties complicate measurement.

Regulation can affect housing access, visitor accommodation, household income and municipal enforcement costs. It should be evaluated through observed changes in available rentals and local prices, not through a universal claim that every holiday let displaced a resident. The housing question remains material: which units could realistically serve as suitable long-term homes, and under what conditions?

## 🏛️ 12. Public housing sales and public capacity

Public housing is not merely a portfolio of buildings with a government logo. It is a continuing capacity to allocate homes under social objectives. Selling a unit may produce immediate revenue while reducing future public control over tenure and rents. Whether that trade-off is defensible depends on valuation, fiscal circumstances, contractual safeguards, replacement provision and the rights of existing tenants.

Madrid provides a documented case. A 2018 municipal statement concerned the **2013 sale of 1,860 EMVS social homes across 18 developments** to investment funds. The statement also recorded that an advisory body had opposed the route of administrative review sought to annul the transaction, while the city intended to pursue other proceedings. That municipal statement establishes what the council reported at the time; it does not by itself settle every subsequent judicial or accounting question. The complete case requires sale contracts, audit findings, court outcomes and later ownership or repurchase records.

The key accounting question is not only ‘How much did the council receive?’ It is also ‘What continuing housing service did the public lose, what obligations survived the sale, and what replaced that capacity?’ A transaction can balance one year’s books while altering decades of housing options. The spreadsheet may be accurate and still answer the wrong public-policy question.

## ⚖️ 13. Property rights and housing rights

Spain’s Constitution contains both property protections and a housing provision. Article 33 recognises private property and inheritance while addressing their social function; Article 47 sets out the principle concerning decent and adequate housing and public action to prevent speculation in land. Their legal status and enforcement mechanisms are not identical. A constitutional statement of principle is not necessarily an immediately enforceable individual entitlement to a particular flat.

Civil law and tenancy legislation determine specific rights of ownership, possession, contract continuation and recovery of property. Public housing policy, land regulation and taxation operate through additional statutes and institutions. Spain’s Law 12/2023 provides a national housing framework, but implementation and legal disputes require attention to competences across national, autonomous-community and municipal government.

A serious analysis can recognise the legitimate interests of owners and the vulnerability of residents without pretending those interests are automatically equal in consequence. Legal rights, political priorities and ethical judgments are separate analytical layers. If a court establishes a right to possession, that is a legal conclusion. It does not establish that displacement carries no social cost. Conversely, the social importance of a home does not automatically nullify another party’s rights.

## 📊 14. Measuring financialisation without inventing a statistic

The easiest way to produce a misleading housing analysis is to gather impressive-looking numbers that measure different things and treat them as one phenomenon. House-price growth, rental burdens, investor share, vacant stock and eviction counts can all matter. None individually constitutes a financialisation index.

A sound statistical register must record **value, unit, geography, date, population or stock covered, definition, source, limitations and revision status**. Asking rents are not existing contractual rents. A dwelling count is not a household count. A judicial *lanzamiento* is not necessarily a unique person or household. The number of investment firms is not their share of dwellings. Publicly supported construction is not necessarily permanent public renting.

Use Banco de España for lending and financial stability, INE for housing and household measures, CGPJ for judicial statistics, the housing ministry for policy and stock, corporate reports for portfolios, and cadastral or property records where legally accessible for ownership. Independent scholarship can help identify causal mechanisms. Where a key indicator is unavailable, mark it unavailable rather than teaching future readers a plausible-looking lie.

## 🕸️ 15. One building, six institutional models

Consider a single block of flats. A lender models its collateral value and credit exposure. An investor models yield, financing and exit price. A landlord models costs, repairs and net income. A tenant models affordability, access, relationships and the probability of remaining. A municipality models population, services and housing need. A court models rights, evidence, procedures and remedies. All may be looking at the same building while processing different information.

This is not merely a metaphor. Each system makes some variables visible and others peripheral. A financial valuation may include expected vacancies and renovation costs while excluding the cost to a resident of losing nearby care, transport or established support. A social-service assessment may recognise vulnerability without controlling a landlord’s contractual rights. A judicial decision may resolve possession without adding a single accessible dwelling to the local market.

Feedback loops connect these models. Credit conditions influence bids; prices influence valuations; valuations influence lending; ownership decisions influence rents; rents influence household formation and mobility; those changes alter demand. Delays are crucial. Financial assets can trade quickly while new housing takes years to plan and construct. Administrative responses may be slower than a household’s next payment deadline.

The cybernetic question is not whether finance is an evil computer. It is which feedback signals institutions privilege, how errors are corrected and who bears the costs when the system optimises one metric while neglecting another. **A balance sheet can describe the building accurately and still fail to describe the home.**

## 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 16. Who bears which risks?

Risk is distributed unevenly. A mortgage borrower bears repayment and interest-rate exposure; a tenant bears insecurity and relocation costs; a small landlord may face concentrated vacancy and maintenance costs; a fund can sometimes diversify across assets; banks face credit risk; public authorities may bear fiscal and social-service costs. Those risks are not identical or directly comparable.

A household’s home concentrates more than money. It can anchor schooling, employment, care arrangements, accessibility adaptations, social relationships and memories. For an older resident or disabled person, a move may entail practical losses not captured by the difference between two market rents. That does not mean financial contracts are irrelevant. It means the social consequences of a decision require their own accounting.

Property also structures intergenerational advantage. Families able to purchase earlier may accumulate equity and help younger relatives; families excluded from ownership may remain exposed to rents and have fewer assets to transfer. But tenure does not map neatly onto wealth or security in every household. Income, debt, location, household composition and health alter the picture. The proper question is who has room to absorb shocks and who does not.

## 🌍 17. A local street in an international market

Residential property is physically immobile, but its financing can cross borders. An investor based abroad may own shares in a company that owns a Spanish property subsidiary; a bank may obtain funding from international markets; interest-rate policy can alter borrowing costs across the euro area. A tenant’s street remains local while the ownership and financing chain may extend far beyond the municipality.

That is why the distinction between foreign buyers, non-resident buyers, foreign-owned companies and international institutional funds matters. Those categories measure different phenomena. It is also why a claim that ‘foreign capital caused the crisis’ needs more than a nationality label. We must identify the capital channel, transaction, local market and plausible counterfactual.

International comparison is useful but treacherous. Ireland, Portugal, Germany, Britain and the Netherlands differ in social renting, mortgage law, planning, tax and tenancy protections. A policy cannot be imported as though it were a plug-in for a universal housing operating system. The institutional environment changes its effects.

## 🌱 18. Policies: what problem does each instrument solve?

There is no single switch marked STOP SPECULATION. Social-rental construction can expand durable affordable supply but takes time, land and funding. Public acquisition can change the tenure of existing stock more quickly, but acquisition costs and refurbishment matter. Rent stabilisation can improve predictability for covered tenants while its effects on investment and allocation depend on design. Stronger tenancy rights can reduce insecurity but require clear rules and enforcement.

Vacancy measures may encourage owners to put suitable dwellings into use, but only if vacancy is accurately identified and local demand exists. Tourist-accommodation rules may return some units to residential use in particular areas; they cannot build accessible homes elsewhere. Mortgage regulation can reduce systemic risk while also affecting access to credit. Ownership transparency can improve accountability but does not itself produce a single new dwelling.

Policy evaluation should separate **affordability, adequacy, security, accessibility, supply, distribution, fiscal cost and implementation time**. A scheme can succeed on one and fail on another. Publicly funded construction that later exits affordability restrictions may meet a short-term target while eroding long-term social capacity. Equally, restricting investment without solving construction bottlenecks may leave shortages intact. The point is not to find a morally perfect instrument. It is to build a system whose incentives produce habitable, secure homes.

## 🕯️ 19. Maricarmen: the balance sheet meets the front door

This node belongs in the Maricarmen cluster because it explains the economic environment in which ownership, tenancy and housing security can collide. It must not turn her into an illustration of an unverified financial transaction. The case-specific questions are concrete: who owned the property at each relevant date; who managed it; what tenancy rights were claimed; what did each party submit; what did the court decide; and what documented negotiations followed?

Only after establishing those facts can we ask whether a portfolio sale, rent strategy, redevelopment plan or other financial incentive played a role. If no reliable evidence supports such a connection, the answer is **not established**. That is not a failure of the structural analysis. It is respect for the distinction between a general mechanism and an individual life.

The lived timescale is still analytically important. A property may be valued using a short investment horizon while a resident understands it through decades of continuity. The law determines rights according to its own rules; financial markets determine prices through theirs. Neither process automatically captures the entire human meaning of the place. The research must hold all three without silently substituting one for another.

## 🏦 20. Conclusion: houses for living

*Casas para vivir, no para especular* is a demand, not a fully specified macroeconomic programme. A housing system needs finance, construction, maintenance, legal certainty and investment. It also needs people to be able to access suitable homes and remain in them under predictable conditions. The problem is not that buildings have economic value. The problem is what happens when financial incentives become exceptionally influential while the social function of housing receives weaker protection or less effective institutional attention.

Private ownership does not equal harmful speculation. Nor does a functioning property market prove that housing needs are being met. Both statements can be true. Financialisation matters when the methods for raising capital, transferring risk, managing portfolios and projecting returns shape who can live where, at what cost and with what security.

We should therefore follow the transaction, trace the institution, identify the legal rights, measure the actual housing outcome and preserve the voice of the person living inside. The spreadsheet may know exactly what the building is worth. The person at the front door would quite like to know whether they can stay.

**The building may be an asset. The home is where somebody has to live.**

### Measurement rules

- Do not confuse **stock** (homes existing at a date) with **flow** (new homes or households during a period).
- Do not confuse **advertised rents** with **rents actually paid** under existing contracts.
- Do not confuse **company ownership** with **ultimate beneficial ownership** or with a property-management contract.
- Do not confuse **registered tourist accommodation** with **all platform listings** or **all vacant homes**.
- Do not confuse **foreclosure proceedings**, **possession orders**, **scheduled enforcement** and **completed evictions**.
- Do not treat **correlation** between investor presence and rent growth as sufficient evidence of causation.
- For legal changes, record **the statutory version and effective date**, especially for the October 2026 SOCIMI amendments.

## 🗺️ 23. Proposed diagrams — Mechanisms, not decorative arrows

### 🏦 A. Housing finance and prices

```text
Mortgage credit ──► Household purchasing power ──► Bids for housing
      ▲                                               │
      │                                               ▼
Collateral valuations ◄──────────────────────── Property prices

Counterweights: income limits, regulation, interest rates,
new construction, household preferences and financial stress.
```

This is a possible reinforcing loop, not a complete causal account of Spain's market.

### 🏚️ B. Distressed assets after the crash

```text
Developer / borrower distress
            ↓
      Lender exposure
            ↓
 Restructuring / transfer / enforcement
            ↓
 Loan holder OR property owner
            ↓
 Management / sale / rehabilitation / social use
```

The fork between a **loan** and an **owned dwelling** is essential.

### 🏢 C. Ownership and management layers

```text
Beneficial investor(s)
         ↓
  Holding company
         ↓
 Property-owning entity ──► Asset manager
         │                        │
         ▼                        ▼
  Building / dwelling       Property manager
         │                        │
         └────────► Tenant ◄──────┘
```

Relationships vary; verify the corporate chain before applying it to a named property.

### 🕸️ D. One building, different questions

| Observer | Principal question | Data privileged | Potentially missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tenant | Can I remain and live adequately? | Income, contract, accessibility, community | Investor strategy |
| Landlord | Can the property be maintained and let? | Costs, rent, legal duties | Full displacement costs |
| Lender | Is the loan secure? | Debt, collateral, default | Lived continuity |
| Investor | What return and risk? | Yield, price, financing, exit | Non-monetised social effects |
| Municipality | What does the area need? | Stock, services, planning, demand | Individual contract detail |
| Court | What rights and remedies apply? | Law, evidence, procedure | Ability to create new housing |

### 🕯️ E. Different timescales

A portfolio may be bought and sold within years; a mortgage lasts decades; a protected tenancy may cross generations; a community may develop relationships over a lifetime. The diagram should place these horizons alongside construction and administrative delays rather than pretending that a financial transaction resets the human clock.
