# 🗝️ El Movimiento Okupa
**First created:** 2026-10-08 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-09  
*Who gets to decide what a building is for? Spanish squatting, autonomous social centres, possession, and the people who make urban space politically inconvenient.*

---

## 🧭 Orientation — A key is not an explanation

*Okupa* is a small word that has been asked to do a frankly unreasonable amount of political work. It can evoke a punk collective putting a disused building to communal use; a family with nowhere secure to live; a frightening allegation about entry into someone's home; or a politician promising to restore order. Those are not interchangeable situations. Some are not even the same kind of legal dispute.

This node follows **the Spanish *movimiento okupa*** as a recognisable, diverse current of political and cultural activity, especially from the 1980s onwards. It examines occupied residential buildings, but devotes particular attention to **centros sociales okupados autogestionados** (CSOAs): spaces whose significance extends beyond a roof and four walls into music, mutual aid, organising, conflict, neighbourhood relationships and experiments in collective self-management.

Four questions must remain distinct: **Who owns the property? Who has the legal right to possess it? What happens there? What conditions led people to occupy or defend it?** The answers can overlap without becoming identical.

**Boundary for this cluster:** Maricarmen's reported circumstances concern a long-standing tenancy and contested eviction proceedings. That is **not** evidence that she was an *okupa*. The connection is to the broader history of housing access, urban belonging and anti-displacement politics—not an invented shared legal status. The Maricarmen-specific chronology and reported 2026 events must be verified in their own sources before being treated as established here.

This is not a manifesto declaring every occupation virtuous. Nor is it an account that allows a sensational crime story to stand in for the history of a movement. A building can be socially useful and its occupation legally contested. Both things can be true. Unfortunately for tidy political television, the world has declined to sort itself into two boxes.

## 🏚️ 1. Before the K — The city was already contested

Spain's urban housing conflicts did not begin with punk, and occupying land or buildings did not begin with the self-identified *okupa* movement. Twentieth-century urbanisation, rural-to-urban migration, industrial development, speculative construction and uneven public services generated long-running struggles over who could live in cities and under what conditions. Under Francoism, housing policy and urban planning were entangled with economic development and political authority. Residents also organised to obtain infrastructure, schools, transport and decent living conditions.

Neighbourhood associations (*asociaciones de vecinos*) became important during late Francoism and the democratic transition. Their politics were not uniform, and they should not be retroactively renamed *okupa* organisations. Their significance here is structural: they demonstrated that streets and districts could become units of collective political knowledge, not merely administrative parcels on a map. Residents knew which buildings were unsafe, which services were missing and which authorities could be pressured.

After Franco's death in 1975, the political environment changed, but disputes over redevelopment and urban control did not vanish. Younger countercultural and autonomous networks developed alongside, sometimes in tension with, older neighbourhood organisations. The scholarship of Miguel A. Martínez López explicitly situates the emergence of Spanish squatting movements in the 1980s alongside a changing neighbourhood movement, while distinguishing their participants and trajectories [S1, S2].

A necessary chronological distinction: **occupation as a tactic** predates **the *okupa* movement as a political-cultural formation**. Otherwise a history of Spanish urbanisation becomes an implausible story in which everyone who ever entered an empty building was secretly waiting for the letter K to be invented.

## 🗝️ 2. The 1980s — Occupation acquires an identity

By the 1980s, young autonomous and countercultural groups were occupying buildings not only to obtain accommodation but also to create places for meetings, performances, political activity and experiments in everyday life. The unconventional spelling *okupa* marked a recognisable break with ordinary administrative language. It became associated with radical and countercultural identity, although the exact chronology of the spelling's spread should not be reduced to a single origin myth.

Cross-border influence is not speculative in the abstract. Martínez describes direct contact with squatted social centres in Italy and Germany and the adoption of the *social centre* terminology within Madrid networks [S3]. A 2023 retrospective in *El País* recounts a December 1984 occupation by Squat Barcelona in Gràcia, drawing on participants' memories of encounters with occupied spaces elsewhere in Europe [S4]. This is useful contemporary historical reporting, though retrospective testimony should be identified as such.

The movement was never a neatly incorporated national body. Different collectives pursued different purposes, occupied different sorts of property and made different decisions about publicity, confrontation, neighbourhood engagement and negotiations with authorities. Some spaces lasted briefly; others became important local institutions. Some participants needed housing. Others prioritised cultural and political autonomy. The distinction matters because a common repertoire of action is not the same as common leadership.

A useful typology from Hans Pruijt's work, included in the edited volume *¿Dónde están las llaves?*, distinguishes deprivation-based squatting, alternative housing strategies, entrepreneurial or social-centre squatting, conservation and political squatting [S5]. These are analytical types rather than airtight categories: a single occupied building may house people, preserve an otherwise threatened structure and host public activities at the same time.

## 🏴 3. What was being demanded?

One current argued that unused urban property should be put to social use rather than withheld from people who needed housing or space to organise. Another emphasised autonomy: the ability to run cultural, political and communal activities without making them dependent on a commercial landlord, party headquarters or municipal grant. Others foregrounded opposition to speculative redevelopment and displacement. Not every collective shared the same ideological vocabulary, and labels such as anarchist, libertarian socialist or autonomist should be applied to particular groups only when supported by their own statements or reliable research.

The movement's challenge can be expressed as a series of competing claims:

- **Property claim:** Someone holds legally recognised ownership or possessory rights.
- **Use claim:** A building could accommodate activities or people who currently lack suitable space.
- **Political claim:** The distribution of urban space reflects choices about whose needs count.
- **Institutional claim:** Authorities, courts and owners have different powers and responsibilities.

The claims do not automatically defeat one another. A social argument for using an empty building is not, by itself, legal authorisation to enter it. Conversely, proving ownership does not tell us whether a building's present use serves local needs, whether its vacancy is desirable or whether a different housing policy would be better. The history occurs precisely because these questions are contested.

## 🏭 4. Centros Sociales Okupados Autogestionados — Buildings doing more than standing there

A **CSOA**, or *Centro Social Okupado Autogestionado*, is an occupied and self-managed social centre. It is not necessarily a residential squat. A centre might host meetings, concerts, workshops, exhibitions, independent publications, political discussions, community kitchens or solidarity activities. Those examples are documented across the broader movement; they are **not** a claim that every centre offered every service [S1, S3].

The word *autogestión* deserves more than an admiring nod. Self-management raises concrete questions: who has access to the space, who decides what happens there, who repairs the plumbing, who handles money, who resolves conflict and who is accountable when something goes wrong? A building does not become democratic by acquiring a radical poster. Democracy is work; maintenance is also work; and neither can be outsourced to a slogan.

The physical site nevertheless matters enormously. It can hold equipment and records, provide predictable meeting times, permit repeated encounters and help people recognise a local organising network. This creates continuity across events. Someone attending a gig may later return for a housing meeting. A workshop may introduce neighbours to legal or technical expertise. Shared space lowers some of the practical costs of collective action.

There are also potential costs: accessibility problems, noise, fire safety, building condition, informal hierarchies, exclusion and conflict with nearby residents. These should be investigated in individual cases, not imagined away. An account of the movement that never asks who cleans the toilets is missing some fairly fundamental political economy.

## 🗺️ 5. Madrid — Minuesa and the making of a network

**Minuesa** offers a particularly useful case because it complicates the picture of strangers simply discovering an empty building. The former printing works on Madrid's Ronda de Toledo became entangled with workers' resistance to the closure of their workplace. Martínez recalls entering the space in 1989 and describes a combination of former factory workers, residential rooms and an emerging self-managed social centre [S3]. Movement accounts trace the occupation and associated worker struggle to 1988 [S6].

The site hosted cultural and political activity, and its story connects labour struggle, housing and the re-use of industrial premises. That is a much richer history than either 'criminals take over a building' or 'activists save the city'. It is also a reminder that participants can arrive through different pathways: employment disputes, neighbourhood relationships, housing needs and political networks.

Movement histories report that Minuesa was evicted in 1994 and that the eviction generated solidarity demonstrations and attempts to sustain the organising network elsewhere [S6]. Those accounts are valuable primary movement perspectives; their descriptions of police conduct and attendance estimates should be compared with contemporary independent reporting before being repeated as settled fact.

The scholarly literature places Minuesa within longer Madrid protest cycles and studies how occupied social centres served as nodes for feminist, anti-militarist, environmental, anti-racist and other organising [S1]. The causal point is not that every visitor belonged to one political organisation. It is that a shared place could enable otherwise separate networks to encounter one another.

Other Madrid projects—including **El Patio Maravillas** and **La Ingobernable**—are candidates for separate case studies on institutional negotiation, public cultural provision and closure. Their precise addresses, dates, ownership histories, agreements and legal trajectories should be reconstructed from dedicated primary and independent sources rather than squeezed into an unsupported list of heroic buildings.

## 🧱 6. Barcelona, the Basque Country and different urban histories

Barcelona provides a different set of urban pressures: redevelopment, tourist demand, contested public space and a dense history of neighbourhood mobilisation. The December 1984 Gràcia action described in *El País* is a useful early reference point [S4]. **Can Vies** and **Kasa de la Muntanya** are important research leads for later case studies, but each requires a documented chronology of occupation, use, legal status and conflict before claims about its outcomes can be made.

In the Basque Country, **gaztetxe** generally means a youth centre. Some gaztetxes are occupied and self-managed, but the term itself does **not** mean that a particular centre is unlawfully occupied. Linguistic shorthand should not be allowed to do the work of legal investigation.

The wider Spanish history includes Seville, Valencia, Zaragoza, Granada and other cities. Local conditions shape the movement's form: building stock, property values, municipal policy, police practice, political networks and relationships with neighbours differ. Martínez's edited comparative volume analyses trajectories in Madrid, Barcelona and Seville alongside other European cities, explicitly attending to variation rather than treating squatting as a single invariant practice [S7].

**Case-study rule:** For each centre, record the opening date, building and owner, participants' stated aims, activities, neighbourhood relationships, court or municipal actions, and the outcome. 'Famous squat' is not an evidential category.

## ⚖️ 7. The law — Morada, usurpación and possession

Spanish public debate often uses *okupación* as though it were a single legal offence. It is not. The **Spanish Criminal Code** distinguishes, among other things, unlawful entry into or remaining in another person's legally protected dwelling (*allanamiento de morada*, Article 202) from specified forms of unlawful occupation of property not constituting a *morada* (*usurpación*, Article 245). Article 245 itself distinguishes occupation involving violence or intimidation from the non-violent offence described in its second paragraph [L1].

*Morada* is a legal concept tied to the protected sphere of domestic life; it should not be mechanically equated with whichever address someone has registered as their principal home. Whether a particular property is a *morada*, and whether an offence's elements are satisfied, depend on facts and applicable law. A headline does not settle them.

Civil law also provides routes for recovering possession. Ownership and the immediate right to possess are not identical: a tenant may lawfully occupy property owned by someone else. Disputes over a lease, rent arrears, expiry or succession require attention to the tenancy and procedural framework. The presence of an occupant does not make all proceedings criminal, and an owner cannot assume every route to recovery is identical.

| Situation | Core question | Why the distinction matters |
|---|---|---|
| Alleged entry into someone's *morada* | Does Article 202 apply on the facts? | Domestic protection is central; not simply a question of vacancy. |
| Unauthorised occupation of property not constituting a *morada* | Do the elements of Article 245 apply? | Different statutory category and elements. |
| Disputed tenancy termination | What contractual/statutory possessory rights remain? | A tenancy dispute is not automatically an *okupa* case. |
| Owner seeks civil recovery | Which civil procedure and safeguards apply? | Criminal and civil remedies are not interchangeable. |
| Occupation with permission | What permission or agreement exists? | A self-managed space need not remain unauthorised. |

**Temporal caution:** The official BOE provides consolidated legislation and historical versions. Any discussion of a specific case must use the law in force at the relevant date, including later amendments and procedural changes. This node explains distinctions; it is not a determination of liability in an individual case.

## 📰 8. The politics of the label

A political identity can become a mass-media category, and a mass-media category can then swallow circumstances it was never designed to describe. In coverage of *okupación*, residential need, activist occupation, burglary-like fears, tenancy disputes and disused social centres can be pushed into one frame. This is an information problem as much as a rhetorical one: different legal facts become harder to see when the same emotionally charged word is used for all of them.

The term **inquiokupación** appears in political and media discussion of tenants alleged to have entered under a contract and then failed to pay or leave. It should **not** be presented as a self-explanatory, separate statutory offence. The relevant facts may involve civil tenancy procedures and, in some circumstances, other legal questions. Labels do not relieve us of identifying the applicable law.

Statistics require equal discipline. A figure might count police-recorded incidents, complaints, prosecutions, convictions, civil claims or completed recoveries. Those are different units. A claim that 'occupations are soaring' is not evaluable without knowing which category, geography, period and recording practice produced the figure. Nor does a prominent incident prove prevalence.

There are competing legitimate interests. Residents may fear interference with their homes; owners may seek lawful recovery of property; people without secure accommodation may face urgent hardship; and organisers may object to buildings remaining unused amid housing need. Fair analysis does not require pretending these interests are always compatible. It requires describing them accurately before judging policies intended to reconcile them.

## 🏦 9. Vacancy, ownership and the problem of social use

The movement's criticism of vacant buildings intersects with housing financialisation but cannot be substituted for housing statistics. A building may be empty because of inheritance disputes, disrepair, redevelopment plans, weak local demand, pending sale or investment choices. The fact of vacancy does not establish a single motive, and 'empty' does not mean ownerless, habitable or legally available.

An occupied social centre can nevertheless expose a political question: what kinds of social use are encouraged or discouraged by the institutions controlling urban property? Publicly owned buildings create a particularly direct version of that question, because their allocation and stewardship can be debated through democratic and administrative processes. Private property introduces different rights and obligations.

Occupation may temporarily provide space, create visibility, preserve a network or open negotiations. It may also lead to legal proceedings, unsafe conditions, conflict or eviction. The outcomes are empirical questions. This node does not treat occupying a building as a practical substitute for social housing policy, accessible housing provision or secure tenancies at scale.

## ✊ 10. Okupas, PAH and Stop Desahucios — Cousins are not clones

The *okupa* movement and the **Plataforma de Afectados por la Hipoteca** (PAH) belong to overlapping histories of urban contention, but they did not arise at the same time or with identical purposes. PAH emerged in 2009 amid mortgage distress and the financial crisis. Its repertoire includes collective advice, organising, legislative campaigning and action around evictions. The earlier *okupa* movement includes residential occupations and autonomous social centres, with its own histories of counterculture and urban autonomy.

Some activists and spaces may have supported multiple campaigns. Shared language about housing rights, speculation or eviction can indicate affinity. It does **not** establish that one organisation directed another or that all participants endorsed identical tactics. Where cooperation is claimed, name the groups, dates, actions and sources.

The distinction matters especially for the Maricarmen cluster. Opposition to eviction does not convert a long-standing tenant into an unauthorised occupant. Nor does the presence of a tenant union at a demonstration prove that a CSOA organised it. The connective tissue is a political question about access to and control of urban space; the legal and organisational biographies remain separate.

## 🕸️ 11. The social centre as an information ecology

A self-managed centre is a useful cybernetic case study because it is simultaneously **material infrastructure** and **a possible site of information exchange**. Its walls and roof make activities possible; its repeated gatherings allow relationships and expertise to accumulate. A neighbourhood resident may know a building's history, a lawyer may understand an eviction notice, a sound technician may make an event possible and an organiser may know how to contact local groups. No single person has to possess all the knowledge.

A possible feedback sequence looks like this:

> Local need → proposal → assembly or organising meeting → activity → participant response → adaptation.

Another is:

> Threat of closure → legal assessment → collective decision → public response → institutional action → revised strategy.

These are **analytical models**, not documentary proof that every centre followed them. Their usefulness lies in making questions visible: who receives information, who can challenge it, whose preferences determine action and what happens when a decision fails?

Decentralisation can spread expertise and reduce dependence on a single leader. It can also conceal informal authority. A person with the keys, the money, the mailing list or the deepest institutional memory may exercise disproportionate influence even when everyone calls the space horizontal. An account of self-management that refuses to inspect power inside the collective is not particularly self-aware.

The closure of a building raises another question: does the network die with the address? Sometimes records, equipment, relationships and practices move elsewhere. Sometimes they are lost. The history of a centre should therefore follow the people and organisational memory after eviction, not stop at the photograph of a locked door.

## 🚧 12. Conflict, policing and the limits of the tactic

Conflicts around occupied spaces can involve owners, courts, police, municipal authorities, nearby residents, cultural participants and organisers. Each has different powers and interests. Eviction may follow judicial proceedings; negotiations may produce temporary permission or relocation; some conflicts produce demonstrations and allegations of excessive force or unlawful conduct. These events require case-specific evidence.

A collective's stated political goals do not establish that every participant acted lawfully. Equally, an unlawful act by a participant does not establish that everyone attending a centre was responsible for it. The basic unit of evidence remains the specific event, person, organisation and legal finding—not the emotional convenience of assigning collective guilt.

There are limits to occupation as a housing response. Legal insecurity can be acute. Buildings may be unsuitable for safe residence or inaccessible to disabled people. Long-term maintenance is costly. A national shortage of affordable, accessible and secure housing cannot be resolved through scattered unauthorised use of buildings. Public construction, rehabilitation, regulation and social-rental provision address different scales of the problem.

But a tactic does not have to be a complete national policy to have historical consequences. Occupied centres have been sites of cultural production, political experimentation and urban conflict. The relevant questions are **what they did, for whom, at whose expense, and with what durable effects**.

## 🕯️ 13. Maricarmen was not an okupa

The placement of this node within *Por Todas Las Maricarmen* is a deliberate comparison, not a reclassification of the woman at its centre. A historical tenant asserting a right to remain under tenancy law occupies a different legal position from people entering a disused building without the owner's consent. Her particular facts, reported legal succession and enforcement chronology belong in `🕯️_la_historia_de_maricarmen.md` and require direct verification there.

The histories meet in a broader argument about the city. A home can be a site of personal continuity and care; a social centre can be a site of public activity and organisational continuity. Neither experience is adequately described by the building's market value alone. That does not erase ownership or legal process. It explains why disputes about property can become disputes about belonging, social use and the distribution of urban power.

The cleanest intellectual move is also the least flashy: **preserve the distinctions, then study the connections**. We can do that without turning Maricarmen into an honorary punk, which seems a fairly substantial administrative liberty to take with someone else's life.

## 🗝️ 14. Conclusion — Who holds the key, and what does it open?

A key may signify title, permission, practical access, security or exclusion. The *okupa* movement challenged the idea that legal ownership exhausted every political question about the use of buildings. It produced autonomous cultural spaces and forms of collective organisation; it also generated legal disputes, conflicts and criticisms that cannot be dismissed as misunderstandings.

Its history is neither a single criminal category nor a single romantic experiment. It is a history of different people trying to secure accommodation, create common space, contest redevelopment and test forms of autonomy in cities whose land and buildings were already embedded in power.

The distinction that matters most is this: **occupying a building, making a community space, holding a secure tenancy and asserting a political claim about urban life are not the same act**. Sometimes they intersect. Sometimes they collide. Understanding the movement means being able to tell which is happening.

## 🧿 16. Evidence discipline and open research

**Supported in the present source set:** the existence and diversity of Spain's *okupa* movement; the role of occupied social centres; historical ties to European autonomous networks; Minuesa as an important Madrid example; the basic criminal-law distinction between Articles 202 and 245.

**Not established here:** a comprehensive national count of occupied buildings; the present legal status of each named social centre; any claim that particular 2026 Maricarmen demonstrations were organised by *okupa* collectives; a definitive national incidence rate for offences labelled *okupación* in public debate; case-specific court holdings not examined directly.

**Research tasks for a future verification pass:**

1. Obtain municipal and archival records for Minuesa, Can Vies, Kasa de la Muntanya, El Patio Maravillas and La Ingobernable.
2. Cross-check movement accounts of evictions with contemporary journalism and court or administrative records.
3. Read the current BOE text alongside the relevant historical versions and civil procedure rules before making date-specific legal claims.
4. Obtain official Ministry of the Interior and CGPJ series, keeping complaints, offences, proceedings, judgments and enforcement distinct.
5. Check original statements from organisers of any 2026 Maricarmen demonstrations before identifying a collective as a participant.
6. Investigate who benefited from specific centres, who opposed them and what happened to their activities after closure.
