# 🏘️ Ni Casas Sin Gente
**First created:** 2026-10-08 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-09  
*Ni casas sin gente, ni gente sin casas: what Spain's housing stock, prices, laws and geography tell us about the distance between possessing buildings and providing homes.*

---

## 🧭 Orientation — The houses and the people

**Ni casas sin gente, ni gente sin casas.** No houses without people; no people without houses. A slogan with the admirable administrative simplicity of asking whether we could perhaps arrange for the people and the houses to meet.

Unfortunately, somebody has built an entire economic, legal and political system between them. The house may be in the wrong town, in poor repair, held for occasional use, subject to litigation, too expensive to rent, inaccessible to a disabled resident, or already occupied by someone whose right to stay is insecure. And a person who has a roof tonight may still have no dependable home next month.

This node investigates the **material conditions of housing access in Spain**. It does not presume that vacant dwellings are immediately available homes, or that building additional dwellings alone resolves every form of insecurity. Equally, it does not allow the practical complexities to become an excuse for refusing to ask who the housing system actually serves.

Maricarmen's reported experience is the route into this investigation, not a representative statistical sample. Her detailed chronology belongs in [🕯️ La Historia de Maricarmen](./🕯️_la_historia_de_maricarmen.md), and the wider narrative in [🌹 Por Todas Las Maricarmen](./🌹_por_todas_las_maricarmen.md). The statistics here describe the surrounding system; they do not prove the facts of her particular case.

**Evidence status:** This is a substantive analytical draft with a source and verification register. Figures marked **CHECK** are leads from earlier planning, not freshly verified primary-source extracts. Definitions, methodological cautions and analytical distinctions are kept separate from measured findings. The sources listed at the end are research destinations, not a claim that every underlying table was checked in this drafting pass.

## 🏗️ 1. The long road to the Spanish housing crisis

### Housing under dictatorship and urbanisation

Spain's present housing system cannot be explained by beginning with a bank crash in 2008. Industrialisation and internal migration reshaped cities in the mid-twentieth century. Rapid urban growth created housing demand alongside demands for sanitation, transport, schools, health facilities and adequate public space. Neighbourhood associations became sites of political participation precisely because the material requirements of ordinary life did not conveniently divide themselves into separate ministerial portfolios.

Housing policy also helped establish homeownership as a central aspiration and a major form of household wealth. This was not simply a cultural preference floating above economics: credit, public programmes, land policy, fiscal treatment and the design of protected housing all influenced the tenure choices realistically available. The precise balance changed over time and differed by region.

It matters that Spain's *vivienda protegida* tradition has included forms of subsidised ownership as well as rental accommodation. A dwelling sold into private ownership under protected conditions is not the same long-term public resource as a dwelling retained permanently in a social-rental stock. **How long a home remains affordable is part of what the public subsidy actually buys.**

### The boom, the crash and the afterlife

The late-1990s and 2000s property expansion connected construction, mortgage credit, land development, local government finance and household expectations. When the property and financial crisis arrived, it did not produce one uniform outcome. Some households faced mortgage distress. Some financial institutions held troubled assets. Some developments stood incomplete. Some owners saw asset values fall. Some renters experienced insecurity through entirely different contractual mechanisms.

The distinctions are essential:

- **Housing starts and completions** are flows of construction, not the total stock.
- **Unsold newly built dwellings** are not the same category as all vacant dwellings.
- **Mortgage foreclosure** is not synonymous with a rental eviction.
- **Bank-owned or transferred property** is not identical to property owned by an investment fund.
- **A fall in house prices** need not produce affordable access for households without savings, stable income or credit.

The Plataforma de Afectados por la Hipoteca emerged in 2009 amid mortgage-related hardship. Its history belongs in [✊ Stop Desahucios](./✊_stop_desahucios.md). The point here is structural: a system designed to expand access through ownership can expose households to serious risk when the conditions supporting that ownership collapse.

## 🏠 2. How many dwellings, and what does empty mean?

A housing count sounds simple until we ask what has actually been counted. The Spanish statistical system distinguishes dwellings used as principal residences, secondary residences and vacant dwellings. The categories depend on the census methodology and reference period. An address without a usual resident is not necessarily a habitable flat being deliberately withheld from a local rental market.

**CHECK — 2021 census:** Earlier planning identified roughly **3.8 million vacant dwellings**. Before publication, retrieve the exact INE total, its definition, geographic distribution and methodological notes. Do not reuse the rounded number as an independently verified statistic merely because it appeared in our previous research notes.

A vacant dwelling might be awaiting sale, inheritance resolution or refurbishment. It might be located in a municipality with shrinking population, limited employment or poor transport. It might also be in a high-demand neighbourhood and withheld for financial reasons. These are materially different cases, and the national aggregate does not tell us their relative prevalence.

### Geography is not a footnote

A vacant dwelling in a rural town cannot be teleported to Madrid. Nor can a resident whose job, care, school or community is in Madrid be treated as an interchangeable unit of demand that ought simply to relocate to the empty building. Housing supply is spatial. It sits inside labour markets, transport systems, care networks and personal histories.

The meaningful questions are therefore local:

1. Where are the vacant properties?
2. What is their condition and legal availability?
3. Who owns them and what use is being made of them?
4. Where are households seeking accommodation?
5. What tenure, size, price and accessibility do those households require?

A national vacancy figure can motivate investigation. It cannot finish it.

### Second homes and tourist accommodation

Secondary residences, seasonal accommodation and tourist-use properties also need separate treatment. A second home is not automatically a permanently vacant property; a room let to visitors is not equivalent to an entire dwelling removed from long-term rental. Short-term letting can matter greatly in particular neighbourhoods without explaining the whole country's housing costs.

A sound analysis compares **local registered tourist accommodation**, entire-property listings where measurable, changes in residential supply, neighbourhood demand and the applicable regulations. National totals alone are insufficient to attribute a particular rent increase to holiday letting.

## 📊 3. Five shortages hiding inside one word

**Physical shortage:** There are too few appropriate dwellings in a particular place relative to household needs.

**Affordability shortage:** Accommodation exists but its cost is beyond what households can sustain.

**Tenure shortage:** Accommodation exists, but not under the rental, ownership or protected arrangements a household can realistically access.

**Accessibility shortage:** Available dwellings do not meet mobility, disability, care or household-composition requirements.

**Security shortage:** A person occupies accommodation but cannot depend on remaining there.

These shortages overlap but are not interchangeable. New construction might ease pressure in a high-demand area, but expensive new homes do not automatically create secure, accessible social renting. Rent assistance may help a household pay today while leaving underlying supply constraints intact. Stronger tenancy rights may improve continuity without creating another dwelling. Accessibility adaptation may transform an existing property into a usable home without changing the stock count.

**CHECK — Banco de España:** Earlier planning cited a cumulative **400,000–450,000** gap between completed dwellings and net household formation during **2022–2024**, attributed to the Bank of Spain's 2024 annual report. Retrieve the exact passage and methodology before publishing the number. A gap of that kind is neither a count of people sleeping rough nor an estimate of the number of vacant dwellings.

A market can therefore contain empty properties and severe housing shortages simultaneously. This is not a paradox once the categories are defined. It remains a political problem.

## 💶 4. Prices, incomes and the cost of getting through the door

Rent is not merely a number on a property listing. It is a claim on a household's income, made repeatedly, while the household must still eat, travel, heat the dwelling and manage everything else life has the nerve to require.

### Asking rent versus paid rent

Advertised rents describe offers on a particular segment of the market. They may differ sharply from the rent paid by households in longstanding contracts. A median advertised rent in Madrid cannot be substituted for the rent paid by all Madrid tenants. Neither figure alone establishes what a pensioner, a young worker or a single-parent household can afford.

A serious comparison records the source, date, geographic area, dwelling type and whether the rent is **advertised, newly contracted or actually paid**. Income measures also need to match the relevant households and period. National average wages paired with central-city asking rents can produce a striking ratio without producing a valid account of the households actually searching there.

### The entrance fee

Housing access also has upfront costs: deposit, advance rent where permitted, transport, moving, utility setup, accessibility works and the time needed to search. Requirements for guarantees or evidence of income may exclude someone who could otherwise manage the monthly rent. The rules governing fees and deposits must be checked against the applicable tenancy legislation and date.

### Affordability is not a single percentage

Housing-cost overburden indicators, rent-to-income ratios, residual-income measures and self-reported difficulty making ends meet answer different questions. The same household can appear differently under each measure. A figure should always state whether it includes utilities, which income concept it uses and how the threshold is defined.

**CHECK — INE 2025 Living Conditions Survey:** Earlier research supplied an AROPE rate of **25.7% overall** and **19.2% for people aged 65+**. Confirm directly from the INE release and do not relabel AROPE as a housing-cost measure: it is a broader risk-of-poverty-or-social-exclusion indicator.

## 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 5. The households the averages conceal

### Younger adults

The housing-access problem can appear as delayed household formation. An adult living with parents may be making a positive family choice, facing economic constraints, or both. Treating all co-residence as failure erases preference; treating all of it as preference erases barriers.

**CHECK — INE 2025 housing-access module:** Earlier planning reported **44.3% of people aged 26–34 living with parents**. Verify the precise population and wording before citation. Pair it with evidence on unsuccessful housing searches and reasons given, rather than declaring every person in the category unable to leave.

### Older renters

Older people are not one tenure category. Some own their homes outright; others rent, live with family, occupy protected housing or depend on care arrangements. A fixed pension may make an abrupt rent increase especially difficult to absorb. Established transport routes, trusted neighbours, medical services and the accessibility of a familiar home can make relocation much more consequential than a change of postcode suggests.

A low rate of unsuccessful housing searches among older people cannot establish that they have secure homes. People who cannot move, do not expect to qualify, or are determined to remain where they are may never appear among active searchers. This is a **limitation of interpretation**, not an empirical claim that we know how many such people there are.

### Disability and independent living

An available dwelling may have stairs, narrow doors, an inaccessible bathroom or poor access to transport. Accessibility is not a luxury extra attached after the housing question has been solved. It is part of whether the accommodation is usable.

The relevant evidence includes the accessibility of existing stock, costs of adaptation, housing-allocation criteria and the rights and preferences of disabled residents. We should not assume that residential care is the appropriate default merely because someone needs support.

### Families, migrants and people without secure status

Single-parent households, people with insecure work, migrants facing discrimination, and people with incomplete documentation may encounter distinct barriers. These groups are not homogeneous. Discrimination, overcrowding, informal tenancy and unstable income require separate evidence rather than being rolled into a single category of 'the vulnerable'.

### Homelessness and displacement

Rough sleeping is only one visible form of homelessness. Emergency accommodation, temporary stays with others and insecure living arrangements may fall under different definitions. Judicial eviction statistics do not count every person who moves because a tenancy is not renewed, a rent becomes unaffordable or informal pressure makes staying untenable. We should acknowledge that gap without inventing a number to fill it.

## 🏦 6. Who owns the housing, and why does that matter?

Ownership influences incentives, but the word *landlord* covers very different arrangements. A person renting out one inherited flat, a public housing agency, a bank disposing of distressed assets, a corporate property owner and a listed property investment company do not necessarily have the same objectives or obligations.

Spain's **SOCIMI** regime provides a particular corporate and tax structure for listed real-estate investment. **SAREB**, created in 2012, originated in the restructuring of financial-sector property assets. Neither term should be used as a generic synonym for any company that owns housing. Corporate classification must be established from records, not inferred from a journalist's shorthand.

Ownership concentration, speculative incentives, financing conditions and rental returns are related questions, but each requires evidence. The full mechanisms belong in [🏦 Casas Para Vivir, No Para Especular](./🏦_casas_para_vivir_no_para_especular.md).

The material question for this node is how the ownership structure affects the **availability, price, condition and security** of homes. It is possible for a legal arrangement to produce housing while leaving households exposed to insecurity; it is also possible for a public scheme to exist on paper while failing to deliver usable homes where they are needed.

## 🏛️ 7. Public housing: what remains public, and for how long?

Spain's public and protected housing arrangements span multiple levels of government and several tenure models. The category *vivienda de protección oficial* has not always meant a permanently publicly owned rental home. Some protected housing has been sold subject to restrictions that may expire or be altered under applicable rules.

That difference is consequential. If public money helps create a dwelling that later becomes ordinary market property, the public sector may not retain a lasting unit with which to house another eligible household. A permanently held social-rental dwelling, by contrast, can continue serving successive households, provided it is maintained and allocated effectively.

A proper evaluation needs to ask:

- How many dwellings are **publicly owned and rented**?
- How many are privately owned but **subject to affordability restrictions**?
- How long do those restrictions last?
- Who allocates the homes, and on what eligibility criteria?
- How long are waiting lists, and what do they actually count?
- Are homes accessible and located near employment, schools and care?
- How are repairs, turnover and long-term maintenance funded?

National, autonomous-community and municipal powers should be mapped before blaming a single administration for every shortage. Responsibility can be fragmented; the household still experiences the result as one problem.

Public provision can involve construction, purchase, rehabilitation, leasing arrangements or transfer of existing stock. The merits depend on cost, speed, location, quality, long-term tenure and administrative capacity. 'Build more' and 'make existing housing usable' are not mutually exclusive programmes.

## ⚖️ 8. Eviction is the visible end of a longer process

The Spanish term *lanzamiento* refers to judicial enforcement of possession in contexts covered by the relevant statistics. It must not be casually translated into 'one newly homeless family'. The count may include rental, mortgage-related and other proceedings, and published series can differ by period and category.

For every figure from the **Consejo General del Poder Judicial (CGPJ)**, record whether it concerns enforcement carried out, the procedural basis, the geography and the reporting period. A scheduled eviction postponed before execution is not the same event as a completed enforcement. A single judicial event does not tell us the subsequent housing outcome for every occupant.

There is also a substantial area of insecurity that the judicial series cannot measure directly: households leaving before proceedings, moving after a non-renewal, doubling up with relatives or enduring unaffordable costs because they cannot find somewhere else. These possibilities are analytically important, but their scale requires separate research.

The legal safeguards, statutory categories and international housing-rights framework belong in [⚖️ El Derecho a la Vivienda](./⚖️_el_derecho_a_la_vivienda.md). Maricarmen's individual orders must be reconstructed from actual records in [🕯️ La Historia de Maricarmen](./🕯️_la_historia_de_maricarmen.md).

## 🗺️ 9. Spain is not one housing market

Madrid's metropolitan demand, Barcelona's regulatory history, Mediterranean tourist economies, island geography and depopulating rural municipalities are not one uniform market. National averages can conceal severe local scarcity alongside substantial vacancy elsewhere.

A meaningful regional comparison should place at least four kinds of information beside one another: **household growth, usable supply, housing costs relative to local incomes, and tenure/accessibility needs**. Vacancy can be added only with a clear definition and an understanding of the condition and location of the stock.

The most useful proposed map is not a dramatic red-and-blue image of 'empty Spain' versus 'full Spain'. It is a carefully labelled comparison of **where accommodation exists and where suitable accommodation is demanded**. Employment, transport and care are part of the map's explanation, even if they cannot all be plotted on it.

## 🔁 10. Why doesn't the market simply fix it?

A market responds to effective demand: what people can and will pay under the prevailing rules. Human need is not automatically effective demand. Someone can urgently need housing without having the income, deposit or credit needed to compete for it.

Construction is also slow. Land, permissions, labour, materials, financing, infrastructure and developer expectations shape what gets built and when. High prices may encourage development while simultaneously making it harder for lower-income households to enter the market. An increase in expensive supply is not the same as an immediate increase in suitable social housing.

Several feedback loops can operate together:

**Demand pressure → higher prices → investment incentives → potential new construction**, subject to time and planning constraints.

**Higher housing costs → reduced disposable income → delayed household formation or relocation**, changing the composition of observed demand.

**Higher asset values → gains for some existing owners → wider disparities in access**, particularly between those with property wealth and those without it.

**Tenure insecurity → pressure to move or accept unsuitable conditions → disrupted routines and networks**, even without a completed judicial eviction.

These are analytical mechanisms, not proof that every arrow has the same magnitude in every Spanish region. Research should test the links against the relevant empirical literature. Rent regulation, taxation, planning reform, social housing and housing allowances may change different parts of the system, sometimes with contested trade-offs.

A housing policy that claims to solve everything with one lever should be asked which specific shortage it is solving. Preferably before it commissions the commemorative plaque.

## 🧩 11. What would materially improve access?

| Measure | Intended mechanism | Evidence question or trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| More construction | Increase local physical supply | Where, how quickly, and affordable to whom? |
| Permanent social renting | Provide long-term below-market access | How is stock retained, allocated and maintained? |
| Rehabilitation | Return unsuitable or unused stock to use | Are buildings habitable and where demand exists? |
| Tourist-let regulation | Preserve or recover residential use | What is the demonstrated local impact? |
| Tenancy protections | Improve security and predictability | How are rules enforced and exemptions handled? |
| Rent regulation | Constrain defined rental charges | What happens to access, maintenance and available stock? |
| Housing allowances | Increase ability to pay | Are benefits captured partly through higher rents? |
| Public acquisition | Add existing dwellings to controlled stock | At what cost, condition and location? |
| Accessibility adaptation | Make homes usable for disabled or older residents | Who pays, qualifies and has decision-making power? |
| Better coordination | Reduce gaps between public authorities | Which institution can actually act? |

These are not equally effective in every setting. Nor are they necessarily alternatives. A city may need both additional housing and stronger protections for residents already there. A rural municipality may need refurbishment and transport more urgently than another estate. The correct policy package depends on the diagnosed failure.

The most useful evaluation criteria are **affordability, adequacy, accessibility, security, location, durability and distribution**. A measure that increases unit count while worsening displacement risk should not be credited as an unqualified success.

## 🕯️ 12. A home is not merely a unit of supply

A statistical dwelling has an address, floor area and occupancy status. A lived home may also contain the infrastructure of a person's independence: a known route to the bathroom, neighbours who notice an absence, familiar transport, nearby care, a lifetime of routines and the ability to decide who enters one's private space.

This does not mean that every person wishes to stay in the same dwelling forever. It means that **meaningful choice about moving, remaining, receiving support or changing care arrangements** depends on the available alternatives. For some people, relocation is welcome. For others, an apparently adequate replacement address may dismantle practical arrangements that made independent living possible.

The research tradition on *ageing in place* provides a way to study these questions without turning every older person into an identical object of concern. Housing security, accessibility, autonomy and community support are related dimensions of wellbeing. A safe place to sleep is necessary; it is not always sufficient.

Maricarmen's reported case makes this distinction politically visible. But the facts of her individual tenancy, expressed preferences and health circumstances require their own documentary standard. We should not use her life as a shortcut around the evidence.

## 🏘️ 13. Conclusion — The machinery between houses and people

The slogan **ni casas sin gente, ni gente sin casas** sounds simple because its moral question is simple. Why should a society tolerate people lacking dependable homes while some residential space remains unused or inaccessible?

The answer is not one tidy number. Housing stock is distributed unevenly. Dwellings vary in condition, price and suitability. Ownership and tenancy rules govern access. Incomes and wealth shape who can compete. Public institutions divide responsibilities. Care networks and local lives cannot be shifted around like stock in a warehouse.

Explaining those mechanisms does not dissolve the political demand. It tells us what fulfilling it would actually require.

**A country can count its houses without knowing whether its people have homes.** The relevant test is not merely how many units exist or what they are worth, but whether people can obtain and retain accommodation that is safe, suitable, accessible and dependable.
