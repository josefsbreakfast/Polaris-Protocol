# 🕸️ Cómo Se Organiza Un Barrio
**First created:** 2026-10-08 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-09  
*How a neighbourhood becomes an information network, a system of mutual support, and occasionally a formidable administrative inconvenience to people who assumed nobody was comparing notes.*

---

## 🧭 Orientation — A barrio is not merely a location

A *barrio* is a neighbourhood. But geography does not, by itself, produce solidarity, organisation or a functioning communications network. People can live on the same landing for twenty years and know almost nothing about one another. They can also live streets apart and know exactly whom to contact when the lift breaks, a neighbour needs translation, or a landlord sends a letter nobody can make sense of.

The distinction is **proximity versus connection**. A second distinction follows: connection is not yet coordination. And coordination is not automatically care. A densely connected neighbourhood can distribute meals, organise a legal referral, circulate malicious gossip or exert pressure on someone who would rather be left alone. The architecture is not virtuous merely because it is local. Its purposes, safeguards and distribution of power matter.

Imagine an older tenant facing eviction. One neighbour knows the history of the tenancy. Another knows the housing office. Somebody can read the legal paperwork; somebody can bring food; somebody can call a meeting; somebody can check what the tenant actually wants. None has the whole answer. The practical question is how their partial knowledge becomes usable without turning the tenant into an exhibit in somebody else's campaign.

This node investigates three overlapping systems: **the material neighbourhood** (streets, homes, public facilities), **the relational neighbourhood** (people and their ties), and **the organising neighbourhood** (shared procedures, decisions, records, responsibilities and feedback). It uses Spain's neighbourhood movements as historical terrain and cybernetics as an analytical instrument. The model is ours; it must not be retroactively attributed to every Spanish organiser.

The reported Maricarmen case supplies a reason to ask these questions, not a licence to invent participants or actions. Specific claims about her eviction and subsequent mobilisation require a separately verified chronology. Hypothetical examples here are labelled as such.

## 🏘️ 1. Before the banner — Spain's neighbourhood movement

### 1.1 Urbanisation creates shared problems, not automatic communities

During the twentieth century, Spanish cities underwent major demographic and spatial change. Internal migration, industrial employment and rapid residential development produced new districts whose services and infrastructure did not always keep pace with their populations. Residents confronted concrete questions: where was the school, the bus, the sewer, the health facility, the safe road? A demand for a pedestrian crossing might appear modest beside a demand for democracy, but the capacity to organise around either rests on recognisable procedures: gathering evidence, locating decision-makers, agreeing demands and sustaining participation.

Under late Francoism, neighbourhood associations (*asociaciones de vecinos*) became significant vehicles for local demands and, in some settings, broader political participation. Their development was uneven, conditioned by restrictions on association and by local social, religious, labour and political networks. Their history should not be told as though every association was clandestine, uniformly radical or destined to become a later housing platform. The differences between districts, organisations and periods are part of the evidence.

### 1.2 The democratic transition changed the institutional interface

After 1975 and during the democratic transition, the environment for municipal politics and civic participation changed. Neighbourhood activists sometimes gained formal access to institutions, while associations faced new choices about negotiation, independence and party politics. Being invited into a committee can be a genuine route to influence; it can also change whom an association answers to, what information it receives and how much energy it spends translating community concerns into administrative categories.

The analytical question is not whether cooperation or confrontation is inherently purer. It is **what changed in decision-making power**, and who could check the results. Did residents gain services, information and enforceable commitments? Did a small number of representatives become gatekeepers? Did the organisation retain the ability to challenge the municipality after the photograph was taken?

### 1.3 Continuity without imaginary family trees

Modern anti-eviction organising, autonomous social centres and tenants' unions exist within a wider Spanish history of local mobilisation. That does not make them the same movement. Three different relationships must be documented separately: a **shared repertoire** (for example assemblies), **documented cooperation** between organisations, and **direct organisational inheritance**. Similar methods are not proof of common command or descent.

**Research priority:** build case-specific timelines from municipal archives, neighbourhood federation records, oral histories and scholarship, distinguishing participants' recollections from contemporary documents. The neighbourhood is not one organism with one continuous memory. Sometimes it has three competing committees and somebody's auntie who remembers what actually happened.

## 🧱 2. The physical neighbourhood is part of the machinery

A network cannot be reduced to dots connected by lines. People have bodies. Meetings have locations. Documents must be printed, translated, stored and retrieved. Transport takes time; stairs exclude people; childcare costs money. Information is experienced through material conditions, not beamed into identical abstract brains.

### 2.1 The humble meeting place

A community hall, school room, library, café, occupied social centre, courtyard or kitchen table can provide the continuity required for repeated interaction. Its characteristics shape participation: opening hours, cost, noise, heating, accessible toilets, seating, transport links and whether people feel welcome. An evening meeting may suit workers with daytime hours but exclude others with care responsibilities or fatigue. A digital meeting removes some travel barriers and creates others.

An accessible room is not decorative inclusivity. It determines whose knowledge reaches the collective. If the person with the most relevant experience cannot enter the building, the group's understanding is structurally incomplete before anyone opens their mouth.

### 2.2 Ordinary equipment, extraordinary consequences

A printer, noticeboard, locked cabinet, shared calendar, reliable internet connection and a table large enough for documents can be organisational infrastructure. So can a bus route. This matters when evaluating community capacity: participant counts alone do not show whether people can coordinate, preserve records or meet safely.

### 2.3 What closure destroys

When a community venue closes or residents are displaced, the loss is not only square metres. The group may lose a recognisable meeting point, storage, routines, intergenerational encounters and the unplanned conversations through which small problems are noticed early. Online channels may preserve some relationships, but they cannot always replace transport, meals, local presence or an accessible room. Conversely, some groups successfully relocate and develop new ties. **Network disruption is a hypothesis to investigate, not an inevitable consequence to declare.**

## 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 3. How one person becomes several people

People join collective activity through overlapping routes: friendships, kinship, schools, workplaces, existing associations, local businesses, religious or cultural spaces, chance encounters and direct requests for help. No single route reaches everybody.

### 3.1 Strong ties and weak ties

The distinction associated with sociologist Mark Granovetter is useful here. Close relationships may provide trust and sustained practical assistance; more distant ties can bridge otherwise separated circles and bring unfamiliar information into view. Neither category guarantees accuracy or kindness. A friend may know the situation intimately but misunderstand the law; a weak tie may connect someone to a competent adviser without knowing anything about the household.

### 3.2 Recognition is not recruitment

A person hearing that neighbours have encountered similar rent increases may begin to identify a pattern. That recognition can support collective inquiry, but it must not be treated as consent to public action. Some residents want a campaign. Others want a confidential referral. Others want to observe. A functional network needs several participation levels, not a binary distinction between heroic organisers and the insufficiently enthusiastic.

### 3.3 Different contributions, different limits

One person can attend every assembly; another can translate a letter once; another can provide an accessible lift; another can check a date in a court document. The system becomes stronger when those contributions are routed well, not when everybody is pressured to become the same sort of activist. The organisational problem is to match **capacity, competence, consent and need**.

## 📡 4. Information travels — and changes on the way

Communication is often described as sending a message. For neighbourhood organisation, it is also an act of classification: is this urgent, confirmed, private, relevant, actionable, outdated? Every channel imposes constraints on what can be said and who will see it.

### 4.1 Multiple channels are not redundancy by default

Word of mouth can reach people who avoid online platforms, but may distort dates and exclude newcomers. Posters are visible to passers-by but cannot update themselves. Assemblies permit questions and corrections but require attendance. Telephone calls can convey urgency and nuance but are hard to audit. Messaging applications distribute notices rapidly but may blur private and public audiences, bury corrections and create exhausting volumes of chatter. Social media expands visibility while also attracting attention from people with no stake in the outcome.

The sensible question is not 'Which app does the barrio use?' It is **which channel is appropriate for which information and which audience?** A public meeting time belongs in a different information environment from a tenant's medical details or legal correspondence.

### 4.2 The rumour problem

Suppose someone forwards: 'Eviction tomorrow, eight o'clock.' Which address? Which date? Is the enforcement actually scheduled? Is the tenant asking for public attendance? Was the notice superseded? An urgent-sounding message without provenance can waste scarce capacity or cause direct harm.

A minimal evidence discipline asks for the original document or accountable source, date of last verification, scope of permission to share, and a named person responsible for corrections. It also requires **retiring stale information**. A network that circulates everything but corrects nothing is not well informed; it is simply busy.

### 4.3 Communication changes behaviour

An announcement can produce attendance, alter institutional expectations, prompt a journalist to investigate, or persuade someone not to participate. These effects feed back into later communication. The same observation can mean different things to a resident, organiser, lawyer and official because each possesses different context, incentives and questions. That is an information ecology, not merely a megaphone.

## 🧠 5. Distributed knowledge — nobody has the whole map

A neighbourhood contains knowledge in different locations: residents' memories, tenancy documents, council records, professional expertise, local histories and practical familiarity with buildings and services. It is not enough for that knowledge to exist. Someone must be able to find it, understand its relevance and determine whether it can be used.

### 5.1 The routing problem

Imagine a neighbour asking whether a notice is enforceable. The person answering may know the local landlord but not the legal procedure. Good routing means recognising the boundary of one's competence and connecting the question to someone appropriately qualified. Bad routing means confident improvisation, followed by the entire group learning the hard way that 'my cousin reckons' is not a statutory provision.

### 5.2 Translation between kinds of expertise

Legal terminology, administrative forms and institutional responsibilities can be difficult to navigate. The task is not merely translating Spanish into another language, but translating a specialised system into actionable understanding without distorting it. The organiser should distinguish the document's contents, the professional interpretation, the affected person's account and any remaining uncertainty.

### 5.3 Documentation and memory

Written notes can preserve chronology, decisions, responsibilities and corrections across personnel changes. But records also create risks. Sensitive information should be collected only for a defined purpose, stored appropriately, shared with permission and deleted when no longer needed. An archive is not a licence to retain somebody else's distress forever.

### 5.4 The actual formula

**Collective intelligence is not the sum of everything everybody knows. It depends on whether useful information can move between people without losing its meaning, provenance or necessary context.**

That is the node's principal cybernetic claim. It is an analytical proposition, not an assertion that historical neighbourhood groups consciously used this vocabulary.

## 🏛️ 6. Knowing who can actually do what

A housing crisis can involve a landlord, managing agent, legal representative, court, judicial enforcement, social services, municipal housing office, autonomous community and national legislation. These actors do not have interchangeable powers. The wrong referral may consume the very time a household cannot spare.

A court can determine questions within its jurisdiction; a municipality may administer particular assistance or housing programmes; a landlord may negotiate contractual terms; a regional authority may control other housing resources; parliament can amend law but cannot ordinarily resolve a particular case on the spot. Each power must be checked against the jurisdiction, legal route and date of the case.

| Actor | Possible role | What must be checked |
|---|---|---|
| Tenant or affected household | Provides instructions, documents and consent | Their wishes; actual legal position |
| Landlord or creditor | Contractual decisions, negotiation | Identity, authority, relevant agreement |
| Lawyer or adviser | Professional legal assessment | Qualifications, instructions, deadlines |
| Court | Determines legally submitted questions | Case type, order, procedural stage |
| Enforcement authorities | Implement relevant orders | Current status and lawful procedure |
| Municipality | Housing or social support within its powers | Eligibility, available programmes |
| Autonomous community | Regional housing responsibilities | Applicable regional rules |
| Legislature | Changes general legal framework | Enacted text and commencement |

**Knowing that an institution exists is not the same as knowing what it can actually do.** The useful neighbourhood map identifies powers, deadlines, referral routes and escalation options. It also records when a person has no authority to give the answer they are confidently offering.

## 🗳️ 7. Assemblies — who decides, and for whom?

Assemblies can provide an accessible forum for discussion, shared learning and collective decisions. Their actual practices vary: consensus, voting, delegated tasks, rotating facilitation or informal agreement. No single method guarantees equal voice. An organisation can reject formal hierarchy while acquiring very powerful informal gatekeepers.

### 7.1 Participation is a design problem

Who receives notice? Can people enter the room? Are meetings interpreted? Is childcare possible? Do minutes exist? Can somebody contribute privately? Does the loudest speaker repeatedly define the agenda? A nominally open meeting may remain practically closed to those who cannot afford its demands.

### 7.2 Accountability is more than minutes

Decisions need named responsibilities, realistic deadlines and a means of reporting back. Minutes should distinguish proposals from decisions, and decisions from completed actions. A collective may need to revise its approach when facts change. That is not betrayal; it is the purpose of feedback.

### 7.3 The non-negotiable boundary

**Collective decision-making is not collective ownership of somebody else's life.** An assembly can decide what support it is willing to offer. It cannot assume the right to publicise a resident's circumstances, negotiate on their behalf or expose their personal information without appropriate authority and consent. This distinction is especially important when a vulnerable person becomes the focus of a widely supported campaign.

## ✊ 8. From information to action

A conceptual organising cycle is useful, provided it is not presented as the universal procedure of Spanish neighbourhood movements:

```text
Concern raised
      ↓
Facts and deadlines checked
      ↓
Affected person's wishes established
      ↓
Relevant expertise and institutions identified
      ↓
Options and risks discussed
      ↓
Tasks allocated with consent
      ↓
Action and institutional response
      ↓
Outcome checked; record corrected; approach revised
```

At each stage, there are possible failure points. A false date contaminates the plan. An inaccessible meeting excludes crucial testimony. An adviser may exceed their competence. A public announcement may outrun the affected person's consent. An apparent victory may be only a postponement. The cycle is therefore not a triumphant march from grievance to justice; it is a mechanism for recognising and correcting errors before they compound.

Practical responses might include obtaining professional advice, preparing an administrative application, negotiating, arranging transport, finding translation, accompanying a resident or organising a lawful public demonstration. The appropriate mix depends on the actual case. **The person affected is not a variable to be optimised out of the system.**

## 🌱 9. Mutual aid — the work outside the photograph

Public campaigns are often remembered through crowds and banners. Their less photogenic infrastructure may include meals, transport, childcare, accessible communication, accompaniment, interpretation and helping someone keep track of paperwork. Those contributions can determine whether participation is possible at all.

Mutual aid is not simply a fashionable synonym for charity. In many political traditions it describes reciprocal or collectively organised support, though reciprocity need not mean that two individuals exchange equal resources. A person who needs sustained assistance is not thereby disqualified from belonging. Nor does every act of kindness establish the existence of a formal mutual-aid network.

### 9.1 Who does the invisible work?

Research on care and community organising should examine gendered expectations without assuming women are naturally more suited to administration, emotional labour or keeping everyone fed. When one person becomes the default keeper of records, meeting reminders, tea bags and everybody else's feelings, the group has developed a critical single point of failure and perhaps an unpaid job description nobody agreed to.

### 9.2 Mutual aid and public responsibility

Community support may fill immediate gaps. It does not absolve institutions of statutory duties or turn volunteer exhaustion into a sustainable housing policy. Ask whether assistance is supplementary, temporary, or effectively compensating for missing public provision. These have different political implications.

## 🚪 10. When an eviction becomes a neighbourhood event

The most visible moment may be a scheduled enforcement, but the useful history starts earlier. What tenancy or mortgage arrangement existed? What notices were served? What decisions had been made? Who had been consulted? Which legal routes remained available? What did the affected person ask supporters to do?

A neighbourhood network may contribute information, practical help, specialist referrals, public communication and lawful collective advocacy. It may also misread a document, amplify a rumour or attract attention the household did not want. Evaluation must therefore track **process and outcome**, not just attendance.

| Reported outcome | What it actually establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Large public turnout | People mobilised | Legal success or the tenant's wishes |
| Eviction postponed | Enforcement did not occur then | Permanent security of tenure |
| Negotiation announced | Parties communicated or proposed terms | A concluded, implemented agreement |
| Alternative accommodation offered | A possible housing route exists | Suitability or acceptance |
| Court order changed | A particular legal development occurred | Cause of that development without evidence |
| Media coverage expanded | Public visibility increased | Durable resolution |

Follow the person after the cameras leave. Did they remain housed? Did the network continue supporting them? Did the group learn anything, and was that learning recorded without violating privacy? These questions are more useful than counting placards and declaring the machinery defeated.

## 🔄 11. Feedback, bottlenecks and resilience

Cybernetics becomes useful when it makes mechanisms clearer rather than merely giving ordinary social life a collection of impressive arrows.

### 11.1 Positive feedback can help or harm

A successful collaboration may increase trust, participation and future coordination. But rapid mobilisation can also create expectations the group cannot meet. More cases may mean more demands on a handful of experienced volunteers, reducing the time available for each case. The same network can develop reinforcing loops of confidence or exhaustion.

### 11.2 Delays matter

Courts, housing offices, volunteer groups and households operate on different timescales. An urgent deadline may collide with a monthly committee meeting. A response received after enforcement may be technically correct and practically useless. Time is therefore a material resource, not just a line on the case chronology.

### 11.3 Bottlenecks and redundancy

If one person holds the passwords, knows the lawyer, stores the minutes and remembers the last five negotiations, they are both invaluable and a vulnerability. Shared documentation, appropriate handovers and clear responsibilities can reduce dependence on one exhausted organiser. Redundancy does not mean everybody should possess everybody's private files; it means essential functions should survive a person's absence **within proper access controls**.

### 11.4 Adaptation and review

Useful questions after an action: What was known at the start? What changed? Which assumptions failed? Were affected people heard? Were promises fulfilled? Did the communication channel work? What must be corrected in the archive? Learning requires permission to acknowledge mistakes. A group unable to say 'we got that wrong' cannot become reliably better informed.

## 🏚️ 12. When displacement breaks the network

Housing displacement may move a person away from familiar services, neighbours, transport routes and informal support. A neighbourhood experiencing repeated displacement may lose institutions, historical knowledge and opportunities for routine interaction. The mechanism is plausible but must be investigated in particular places rather than treated as an automatic law of gentrification.

One possible reinforcing loop is:

```text
Housing pressure → displacement → weakened local ties
       ↑                              ↓
Reduced capacity to contest policy ← lost coordination
```

The loop is a hypothesis. People can preserve relationships across distance, new residents can contribute new networks, and some organisations adapt successfully. A serious analysis must ask **under what conditions** the loop operates, for whom, and with what countervailing resources.

This is also why a home is not simply a housing unit. It locates someone within an ecology of relationships and services. For an older or disabled resident, changing address may alter access to care, familiar routes and practical support in ways not captured by a simple count of available dwellings.

## 📰 13. Who gets to tell the barrio's story?

The same event may appear differently in a tenant's account, a landlord's statement, a judicial record, a campaign leaflet and a news report. Each has a different purpose and evidential status. A legal order can establish what the court decided, but not necessarily how the affected person experienced the process. A resident's testimony can establish their account, but does not by itself verify every legal claim made on their behalf.

### 13.1 Provenance before amplification

Separate **event**, **account**, **interpretation** and **inference**. Record when a claim was made, by whom, on what basis and whether it was later corrected. This is especially important when a campaign becomes a national story and local participants lose control of how their words circulate.

### 13.2 Visibility is a trade-off

Publicity may attract resources, pressure and scrutiny. It can also expose financial circumstances, health information, relationships or location. Consent to one interview is not consent to unlimited republication. A neighbourhood organisation must not confuse its need for a compelling story with the resident's right to control their own.

### 13.3 The problem with heroic simplification

'Everyone in the barrio united' makes a splendid headline. It may erase dissent, hesitation, invisible labour and the people excluded from the assembly. The fuller story is often more interesting: several groups, competing priorities, a few practical successes, an institutional dead end, and someone who has kept the documents in a biscuit tin since 1987. History is not improved by removing the inconvenient people who made it possible.

## 🏛️ 14. Cooperation, confrontation and the state

Neighbourhood groups may petition, negotiate, litigate, collaborate with public services, occupy public space, or campaign against an institutional decision. These methods should be analysed in context rather than ranked by theatrical intensity. An agreement may produce a durable service; a demonstration may change the public agenda; litigation may clarify a right; a consultation may provide access while also consuming enormous volunteer time.

Institutional relationships create asymmetries. Officials may control documents and deadlines; organisers may possess local knowledge and public legitimacy; elected representatives may influence policy without controlling individual proceedings. Personal familiarity can ease communication but can also obscure accountability if decisions depend on who knows whom. The test is whether the process remains transparent, challengeable and useful to affected residents.

A group should be able to work with an institution on one matter and challenge it on another. A municipality should not mistake consultation for consent, nor should campaigners assume that refusing every conversation is proof of independence. What matters is **who retains the power to decide, explain, appeal and withdraw**.

## 🧩 15. Who is missing from the assembly?

The word *community* can conceal unequal access. A meeting may be formally open and practically inaccessible to people with mobility impairments, sensory differences, fatigue, care responsibilities, insecure work, language barriers or limited digital access. Residents without stable housing may not see local notices. Migrants may reasonably worry about exposure. Older people may prefer a conversation in person; younger residents may be excluded by a system that assumes everyone has time to attend meetings.

### 15.1 Representation requires evidence

A dozen articulate attendees cannot automatically speak for every household in a district. A campaign's claim to represent 'the barrio' should be assessed against participation, outreach, decision rules and the affected person's consent. Support can be real and substantial without being unanimous.

### 15.2 Accessibility improves the knowledge base

Providing interpretation, varied meeting times, accessible venues, clear written summaries and confidential channels does more than satisfy a procedural nicety. It changes what the group can learn. Excluding people with direct experience produces an information deficit disguised as consensus.

### 15.3 Dissent is data, not sabotage

Disagreement may reveal a genuine conflict of interests or a failure of communication. It should not automatically be coded as disloyalty. A network that cannot hear unwelcome information may remain tightly connected while becoming less accurate.

## 🧭 16. A neighbourhood is not automatically benevolent

Close networks can sustain care; they can also enable surveillance, gossip, exclusion and coercion. The person who knows everybody may be the most helpful neighbour in the district or the person from whom somebody most urgently needs privacy. High connectivity is not a moral achievement in itself.

A useful governance checklist asks: Can people decline participation without penalty? Is sensitive information restricted? Can errors be challenged? Who controls public statements? Is there a complaints route? Are financial decisions recorded? Can newcomers influence the group? What happens when an organiser behaves badly? These are not hostile questions. They are how solidarity avoids becoming another unaccountable institution.

**Efficient information circulation is not inherently prosocial.** The system's incentives, boundaries, consent arrangements and opportunities for correction determine what that efficiency serves.

## 🌍 17. Spanish case studies — evidence before illustration

A full comparative account should examine distinct local traditions rather than declaring one model 'the Spanish barrio'. The following are **research routes**, not fully verified case reconstructions.

### 17.1 Madrid and the neighbourhood associations

Investigate the history of residents' associations in districts such as Vallecas, using contemporary local records and historical scholarship. Which material demands organised people? Which associations persisted? How did their relationship with municipal government change? What evidence exists for actual decisions and outcomes?

### 17.2 Barcelona and contested urban space

Select a documented neighbourhood struggle involving redevelopment, housing or a community facility. Establish the relevant organisations, property status, dates, municipal decisions and local disagreements. Avoid treating the entire city as a single movement with a single position.

### 17.3 A local PAH assembly

Choose one platform with sufficient primary records and independent research to reconstruct casework, decision-making and shared materials. Distinguish PAH's national public identity from the procedures of that local assembly. Its methods can illuminate the network model without becoming an invented universal rule.

## 🕯️ 18. Maricarmen — what did the barrio actually do?

The analytical temptation is obvious: take the familiar story of a threatened older tenant, supply an assembly, a group chat, a lawyer, a crowd and a victorious ending, and call it neighbourhood cybernetics. That would be an excellent fictional demonstration and a dreadful evidential method.

For the actual case, reconstruct the sequence from primary legal documentation where available and contemporary reporting: who the tenant was, what her legal relationship to the property involved, when decisions occurred, who organised support, what she authorised, which institutions were approached, what public actions happened and what outcomes followed. Identify whether organisers were established neighbourhood associations, tenants' groups, PAH platforms, informal networks or a coalition. **Do not infer affiliation from shared slogans.**

A source register should distinguish (a) direct documentation, (b) participant accounts, (c) institutional statements, (d) independent reporting and (e) analytical interpretation. Conflicting accounts should remain visible until resolved. A reported agreement is not the same as an implemented agreement; a postponement is not permanent tenure; a public tribute is not evidence of what the person wanted during her lifetime.

The case belongs in this node because it asks what neighbourhood organising makes possible. But **Maricarmen remains a person, not an organising diagram**. Her legal status, wishes and agency must not be flattened to make the theory look tidy.

## 🕸️ 19. Conclusion — Somebody knows something

The neighbourhood's intelligence is not located in a committee room, an app or the loudest person holding the microphone. It is distributed across bodies, relationships, records, memories, skills and institutions. Making it useful requires places to meet, reliable channels, practical support, consent, accessible decisions, accountable leadership and the willingness to revise a mistaken account.

Spain's neighbourhood movements provide historical examples of residents organising around material needs and institutional decisions. The precise forms differ across decades, districts and political traditions. The common analytical question is how separate experiences become shared understanding and how shared understanding becomes action without dispossessing the people whose lives are at stake.

A network can be large and ineffective, small and resilient, caring and intrusive, democratic in name and exclusive in practice. Its quality lies in the mechanisms, not the slogan. It should help people reach the right knowledge and resources, preserve the context of their circumstances and remain able to correct itself.

**A neighbourhood becomes organised when people develop reliable ways to turn separate knowledge and separate capacities into collective action — while preserving the rights, wishes and agency of those affected.**

*The barrio does not need everybody to know everything. It needs people to know how to find one another.*

- **Contemporary case:** left unresolved where independently verified evidence is not available.

## 🗺️ 22. Diagram specifications

These are specifications for future illustrations, **not completed graphics**.

### 🕸️ A. Network and relationship types

Depict residents, local associations, social centres, advisers and institutions. Use different edges for trust, information, material support and formal authority. Do not depict a thematic similarity as an established organisational tie.

### 📡 B. Routing and verification

```text
Resident identifies problem
           ↓
Consent and immediate safety checked
           ↓
Source documents / dates verified
           ↓
   ┌───────┼─────────┐
   ↓       ↓         ↓
 Legal   Practical  Institutional
 advice  support    contact
   └───────┼─────────┘
           ↓
 Response / follow-up / corrections
```

### 🧠 C. Knowledge distribution

Show separate stores of local memory, legal expertise, lived experience and administrative knowledge; depict translation and routing rather than one omniscient central node.

### 🔄 D. Two feedback loops

**Reinforcing:** reliable support → increased trust → more usable connections → improved coordination.  
**Depleting:** increased caseload → volunteer overload → reduced capacity → heavier burden on remaining volunteers.

Both are **hypotheses**, not measured Spanish case results.

### 🏚️ E. Displacement and continuity

Show possible loss of physical meeting spaces and local ties, alongside countervailing paths through relocation, new membership and digital continuity.

## ✨ 24. Stardust

- 🕸️ **Proximity is not organisation.** Geography provides opportunities, not guarantees.
- 🏘️ **Community is not automatic.** Nor is community necessarily inclusive.
- 🧱 **Infrastructure is political capacity.** The accessible room, printer and bus route matter.
- 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 **Different contributions are useful.** Participation need not look identical.
- 📡 **Information needs routing and correction.** A fast rumour is still a rumour.
- 🧠 **Context travels with meaning.** Facts stripped of provenance become unreliable.
- 🏛️ **Institutions have different powers.** A referral must reach somebody able to act.
- 🗳️ **An assembly is not automatically representative.** Check access and decision rules.
- ✊ **Consent is not transferable to the crowd.** Support must preserve agency.
- 🌱 **The unseen work is still work.** Transport and translation belong in the account.
- 🔄 **Feedback can build or exhaust a network.** Count bottlenecks, not only members.
- 🏚️ **Displacement can disrupt relationships.** But outcomes depend on context.
- 📰 **Visibility is not always protection.** Publicity can help and harm.
- 🧩 **Exclusion creates informational blindness.** Accessibility changes what is known.
- 🧭 **Connectivity is not benevolence.** Privacy and accountability matter.
- 🕯️ **Maricarmen is a person, not a systems diagram.** Establish her actual wishes and the actual facts.
