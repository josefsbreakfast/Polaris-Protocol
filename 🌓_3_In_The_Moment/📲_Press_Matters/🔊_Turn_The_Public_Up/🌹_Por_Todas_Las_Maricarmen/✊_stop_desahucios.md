# ✊ Stop Desahucios
**First created:** 2026-10-08 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-09  
*When an eviction becomes everybody's business: Spain's mortgage crisis, PAH, collective legal knowledge, and the people who learnt to read the machinery before it reached the door.*

---

## 🧭 Orientation — Before the door

The camera tends to arrive for the door. By then the mortgage has been signed, the payments have become impossible, the letters have accumulated, the bank has instructed somebody, and the court has been asked to do something that may change a family's life. The photograph captures the last few metres of a process that began years earlier. **Stop Desahucios** — *Stop Evictions* — is associated with attempts to intervene before and during that final stage. Its history is also a history of what people learnt to do with the paperwork.

Spain's **Plataforma de Afectados por la Hipoteca** (PAH, Platform for People Affected by Mortgages) emerged in Barcelona in 2009 amid the collapse of the property boom. The organisation developed collective advice, negotiations, public mobilisation, campaigns for legal reform and housing-related direct action. Human Rights Watch's 2014 investigation describes the organisation's role in advice, bank negotiations and anti-eviction mobilisation; that investigation is an independent contemporary source for the broad historical account, though it also reports PAH's own outcome figures as *PAH claims*, not independently audited totals. [1]

This node distinguishes **PAH as an organisation and network**, **Stop Desahucios as a campaign name and wider rallying cry**, and the many local tenants' unions, neighbourhood groups and other organisations which have resisted evictions without necessarily belonging to PAH. It also distinguishes **mortgage foreclosure**, **rental possession disputes**, **the physical enforcement of an order**, and **the subsequent problem of where someone can live**. Those distinctions are not pedantry. They tell us which institution holds which lever.

The connection to the Maricarmen cluster is historical and methodological. A disputed long-standing tenancy is not a mortgage default, and participation by a named organisation in her reported case cannot be assumed from the shared anti-eviction objective. The contemporary Maricarmen chronology requires its own corroboration. This node concentrates on the independently documented development of the anti-eviction movement and the mechanisms that later organisers could draw upon.

## 🏦 1. The mortgage economy that came before the movement

Spain's housing model strongly favoured homeownership for decades. Public policy, the tax treatment of housing, mortgage finance, household expectations and the comparatively limited social-rental sector interacted to make purchase an important route to housing security. The expansion of credit and residential construction in the years before the 2008 financial crisis made that arrangement seem durable. It was not equally durable for everyone.

When unemployment rose and incomes fell, some households could no longer maintain repayments. A falling property price did not necessarily reduce the debt secured against that property. Nor did the repossession or sale of a home necessarily extinguish the borrower's remaining liability. That last point is central to understanding PAH's demand for *dación en pago*: surrendering the home in satisfaction of the relevant mortgage debt, rather than losing the home and continuing to owe money.

The stages must be separated. **Arrears** are missed payments. **Foreclosure** is a legal route by which a secured creditor enforces its claim. **Adjudication** concerns who acquires the property following enforcement. **Lanzamiento** is the judicially enforced removal or recovery of possession in the relevant proceeding. **Homelessness** is a possible consequence, not a synonym for any of the preceding stages. The number of court enforcement actions cannot automatically be translated into a number of people sleeping rough.

The asymmetry was not only financial. Creditors could draw on legal departments, established forms, internal records and repeat experience. A household might have one mortgage, one crisis, and very little idea what a procedural deadline meant. A person can be legally responsible for responding to a document while lacking any realistic familiarity with the system that produced it. That informational asymmetry is where collective organisation became especially important.

There is a political temptation to say the problem was simply that people borrowed irresponsibly, or simply that banks behaved badly. Both explanations can conceal the actual interactions among credit standards, wages, unemployment, property valuations, consumer protection, state policy and contractual enforcement. The historical question is how those conditions exposed households to risks they could not individually manage — and how responsibility for the losses was distributed.

## ✊ 2. Barcelona, 2009 — PAH appears

Human Rights Watch records that PAH was founded in Barcelona in 2009 by people associated with the social-rights organisation Observatori DESC. [1] Its formation belongs to a period when mortgage distress was no longer an exceptional private event but a recurring public problem. People confronting similar proceedings began to compare cases and develop shared responses.

The word *afectados* matters: **people affected**. It does not name a professional charity dispensing assistance to a passive population. The movement's organising model placed people facing mortgage problems among those who discussed strategy, shared experience and supported later participants. That principle should not be turned into a claim that every assembly was perfectly equal, accessible or free of informal hierarchies. The difference between a declared organisational principle and its practical implementation is a proper subject of research.

PAH spread beyond Barcelona through local platforms, relationships among organisers, public campaigning and the circulation of methods. Its recognisability did not require each local action to be issued from one national command centre. Local courts, creditors, housing markets and municipal services differed; so did the knowledge needed to act in each place. National demands could coexist with local adaptation.

A movement history needs to ask who built that infrastructure, including people whose names never became attached to a press conference. Founders, spokespersons, legal volunteers, people confronting foreclosure and those who maintained meeting spaces performed different kinds of work. Their roles should not be collapsed into a single heroic biography.

## 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 3. The assembly as an information environment

Imagine the difference between receiving a formal notice alone and bringing it to a room where several people have already encountered similar notices. The paper has not changed. The environment in which it can be interpreted has.

Collective advice can make unfamiliar procedures intelligible. Someone may recognise a deadline; another may understand the bank's proposed terms; another may know which social-service office can assess urgent need; a lawyer may identify a legal argument requiring professional representation. These are distinct contributions. **Lived experience is not identical to legal qualification**, but neither is procedural experience worthless merely because it was acquired outside a profession.

The assembly also changes the unit of analysis. One person may describe the situation as an individual failure. Repeated cases can reveal patterns: similar contractual clauses, common creditor practices, recurrent administrative obstacles or consistent gaps in public provision. Identifying a pattern does not prove that every case has the same legal merits. It gives participants better questions to ask.

A useful conceptual sequence is:

> Individual problem → shared description → document review → available options → collective decision → institutional response → learning.

That is an **analytical model**, not a claim that PAH had one mandatory workflow everywhere. A local group may offer advice, organise a public campaign, refer someone to a qualified lawyer, or decide that an intervention is not appropriate. Consent matters: a person's debt, health, family and immigration circumstances do not become public property merely because a movement could use their story.

There are limits. Assemblies depend on time, translation, physical access, care arrangements and volunteers. People who are exhausted, disabled, working irregular hours or frightened of publicity may find participation difficult. A collective that wishes to speak for people experiencing housing insecurity has to reckon with those who cannot readily attend the meeting. The movement's social importance does not exempt its own procedures from examination.

## 🚪 4. Stop Desahucios — The visible intervention

A public anti-eviction gathering may appear to be the entire campaign because it is what the press can photograph. It is usually better understood as one possible stage of a longer intervention. Earlier work may involve document review, legal applications, conversations with creditors, communication with social services, requests for accommodation, and negotiations over the timing or terms of enforcement.

On the day itself, supporters may assemble, communicate publicly, negotiate or undertake peaceful protest. Historical descriptions should not turn into a universal instruction manual: legal routes, the wishes of the household and the conduct of authorities vary. Nor should the presence of supporters be treated as proof that an enforcement action was legally invalid.

**What counts as stopping an eviction?** That question matters more than a headline total. A scheduled enforcement may be postponed; a proceeding may be suspended; the parties may negotiate; the creditor may agree to new terms; the household may obtain a social tenancy; or the original eviction may go ahead while alternative accommodation is arranged. These are materially different outcomes. A postponed date is time gained, not necessarily security won.

The movement's own counts require the same care. Human Rights Watch reported that, by March 2014, PAH said it had prevented 1,011 evictions and rehoused 1,049 people through empty properties. Those are **figures attributed to PAH**, as recorded by Human Rights Watch, not an independently established census of durable outcomes. [1] Their importance lies in showing the scale PAH claimed for its work; their limitations matter when comparing outcomes or drawing causal conclusions.

Where a court postponed an enforcement after mobilisation, the next question is **why**. Was there a legal defect? A newly available protection? An agreement? An administrative problem? A decision connected explicitly to the protest? Chronology alone cannot supply the answer. A movement may be effective in creating time, leverage or visibility without being the sole legal cause of every resulting order.

## ⚖️ 5. Dación en pago — A home and a debt are not the same thing

*Dación en pago* describes an arrangement in which property is transferred to a creditor in satisfaction of a debt. The concept predates PAH. What PAH contested was the extent to which distressed mortgage borrowers could obtain such an outcome, especially when foreclosure would otherwise leave them with residual debt.

The campaign's argument was that losing a family home while remaining indebted could turn a housing crisis into long-term economic exclusion. Creditor arguments concerned the allocation of contractual risk, repayment obligations, lending incentives and the stability of the mortgage system. Both sides were discussing who should bear losses after the underlying property and income assumptions failed.

Spain's **Royal Decree-Law 6/2012** introduced measures for certain mortgage debtors without resources, including a Code of Good Practice. Later legislation amended that framework. Under specified eligibility conditions, the Code included debt restructuring, possible reductions and *dación en pago*. This is **not** the same as a universal, unconditional statutory right for every mortgage borrower to extinguish all debts by handing over keys. [2]

That distinction is important when evaluating reform. A voluntary settlement, a lender's commitment under a qualifying code, and a general legal entitlement have different effects. The movement's proposed rights must be compared with the text actually enacted, not with the government's announcement that it had done something about housing.

## 🏛️ 6. The 2013 popular legislative initiative

PAH and allied campaigners sought to move beyond case-by-case intervention by using Spain's **Iniciativa Legislativa Popular** (ILP), a mechanism through which citizens can bring a legislative proposal before parliament subject to constitutional and procedural requirements. The initiative connected mortgage-debt reform, eviction protection and social-rental demands.

A contemporary *El País* retrospective reports **1,402,854 signatures** collected for the initiative and describes the parliamentary admission for consideration in February 2013. [3] The figure should be attributed to that account unless checked against the formal parliamentary file. The distinction between **accepting an initiative for consideration** and **enacting its proposed text** is indispensable. Parliament can debate a popular initiative without adopting its provisions as submitted.

The campaign illustrates several kinds of political work operating at once. Volunteers gathered signatures and explained proposals; affected people testified publicly; legal specialists drafted and criticised provisions; elected representatives made procedural and legislative decisions; organisers responded when the resulting law differed from their demands. A petition does not itself become a statute. The democratic machinery has several more rooms, and a remarkable number of doors.

**Law 1/2013**, adopted on 14 May and published in the BOE on 15 May, strengthened certain protections for mortgage debtors, altered aspects of mortgage enforcement, and addressed social renting. Its enacted terms were not simply PAH's ILP reproduced verbatim. The law's preamble also acknowledges the Court of Justice's *Aziz* judgment and its significance for unfair terms in enforcement proceedings. [4]

A proper evaluation therefore needs three columns: **what campaigners proposed; what parliament enacted; what the measures actually covered**. An official title invoking debtor protection is evidence of legislative purpose, not evidence that every person at risk became protected.

## ⚖️ 7. Aziz — Consumer law enters the enforcement machinery

On **14 March 2013**, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled in **Case C-415/11, Mohamed Aziz v Catalunyacaixa**. The issue concerned the relationship between Spanish mortgage-enforcement proceedings and judicial scrutiny of potentially unfair consumer-contract terms under EU law. The Court found the Spanish framework incompatible with effective consumer protection insofar as the court considering unfair terms could not halt enforcement in circumstances where later compensation would not adequately remedy the loss of the home. [5]

This is a crucial distinction. The judgment did not say that mortgage debt was unenforceable as a category. It addressed whether legal procedures gave consumers an effective opportunity to challenge unfair contractual terms before irreversible enforcement consequences. The timing of legal review can determine whether a remedy is meaningful.

The judgment also demonstrates why an anti-eviction movement cannot be understood exclusively through street protest. Legal doctrines, national procedural rules, EU consumer law and individual litigation can change the conditions under which creditors and courts operate. **An effective right is not merely a right written down; it must be usable before the harm it is intended to prevent has already happened.**

## 🟢 8. Obra Social PAH — When delay is not a home

A postponed eviction does not create an affordable tenancy. That gap helps explain **Obra Social PAH**, a campaign strand associated with bringing empty properties into use for households lacking secure accommodation. Human Rights Watch documented PAH's involvement in collective occupation of vacant buildings and its reported rehousing activity. [1]

The legal and political distinctions matter. Occupying a vacant bank-owned property, negotiating a social lease, receiving a municipal housing allocation and establishing an autonomous social centre are not the same act. The ownership, possession and contractual arrangements differ; so do the long-term prospects for residents. Some cases may move between statuses through negotiation or enforcement.

The argument for social use does not make a property owner legally disappear. Nor does legal ownership answer every question about the adequacy of housing provision. The historical work is to reconstruct what was occupied, by whom, under what circumstances, and with what outcome — including failed negotiations and further evictions.

This is also the point of contact with the *okupa* history. There may be overlapping tactics and shared arguments about unused buildings. **PAH is not simply another name for the okupa movement.** The organisations' origins, constituencies and purposes must be traced separately.

## 📣 9. Escraches — Taking a demand to a decision-maker

The word *escrache* has a history beyond Spain, notably in Argentine protest. PAH-associated *escraches* brought housing-policy demands into direct public confrontation with elected representatives. The tactic generated controversy over democratic accountability, personal privacy, intimidation and the appropriate locations for political protest.

For supporters, the point was to make a consequential legislative choice harder to ignore. Critics argued that pressure directed at identifiable individuals, particularly near private or family settings, could cross legitimate boundaries. Neither position should be replaced by the claim that every such protest was peaceful and justified, or that all such protest was inherently criminal. The specific location, conduct, threats if any, and applicable legal rulings matter.

Analytically, the tactic alters **where information is delivered**. Instead of waiting for a parliamentary office to process a petition, organisers seek to make the political decision publicly visible in another environment. Whether that intervention is lawful, proportionate or effective is a separate question. A mechanism can be described without endorsing every use of it.

## 🏘️ 10. The housing problem changes shape

The mortgage crisis gave PAH its initial institutional target: debt, foreclosure and the distribution of losses after the property boom. Later housing pressure increasingly involved tenants facing high rents, insecure renewals, constrained supply and disputes over continued possession. These are connected by housing insecurity but not governed by identical contracts or remedies.

Tenants' unions and neighbourhood organisations developed their own histories, campaigns and organisational cultures. Some shared methods with PAH: assemblies, peer advice, public legal education, demonstrations and negotiations. Shared methods do not establish that the groups are subsidiaries of one another. Nor does a banner saying *Stop Desahucios* prove that PAH coordinated the event.

A mortgage borrower, a private renter, an older tenant with historical succession rights and an unauthorised occupant may all face removal from a dwelling. Their legal situations remain distinct. The court's task, the landlord's or lender's powers, and the available protections depend on those differences. A serious housing movement must understand them; a serious history of the movement must preserve them.

## 🕸️ 11. How distributed knowledge becomes organisational capacity

PAH is especially useful to Polaris because it illustrates how people can pool knowledge about systems designed around specialised expertise. One person has a bank letter; another knows a procedural route; another understands a local authority's housing register; another has seen the same clause before. Together they can identify patterns and options which may be invisible to a person acting alone.

There are several layers to this process:

1. **Intake:** A person brings a specific problem and relevant documents.
2. **Interpretation:** Participants identify the legal, financial and administrative questions.
3. **Routing:** The problem is directed towards negotiation, specialist advice, social services or campaigning, as appropriate.
4. **Coordination:** People decide who can do what, and by when.
5. **Feedback:** An institution responds, a deadline changes, or an option closes.
6. **Learning:** Experience informs later cases, subject to confidentiality and the risk of overgeneralisation.

This is a model for understanding distributed collective problem-solving, **not a verified PAH operations manual**. Actual practices differ among local groups and over time. Its value is to make the informational mechanism legible.

A decentralised organisation is not necessarily structureless. Repeated meetings, shared vocabulary, trusted relationships, written materials and recognised roles create structure even without a single chain of command. That can be a strength: local groups adapt to different courts and creditors. It can also create problems: inconsistent advice, uneven resources, difficulty representing the movement publicly, and volunteer exhaustion.

The movement's memory is an asset. A case resolved in one municipality may teach another group what questions to ask. But transferring a tactic without its legal and institutional context can be dangerous. The lesson is not 'copy what worked'; it is **understand why it worked, for whom, under which conditions, and whether those conditions still exist**.

## 🧠 12. Shame, testimony and the right not to become a symbol

Financial distress is frequently individualised. A person who cannot pay a mortgage may feel responsible for every aspect of a crisis whose causes also include employment, lending practice, market conditions and state policy. Public testimony can reveal common patterns and challenge the idea that every household is an isolated exception.

Yet participation has costs. A person may have to disclose private debts, family circumstances or health information. Public exposure can invite scrutiny, abuse or stigma. People experiencing housing insecurity are not obliged to become articulate campaign representatives. The right to support should not depend on the willingness to provide a moving story for an audience.

The phrase **affected people as political participants** should therefore be understood alongside **affected people retaining agency over their own information**. Consent is contextual. Somebody may agree to legal help but not publicity, to a photograph but not a permanent online archive, or to collective attendance at a hearing but not the publication of intimate family details.

This is not a peripheral ethical matter. Information environments can either increase a person's practical control or take that control away in the name of helping them. A movement concerned with housing security has reason to care about both.

## 🏛️ 13. Who can actually change the outcome?

An eviction-related crisis may involve institutions whose powers do not line up neatly with the household's needs. A **creditor** can negotiate repayment or settlement within the law. A **landlord** can agree to tenancy terms, subject to legal constraints. A **court** can decide legal claims and procedural questions. **Enforcement authorities** implement relevant orders. **Municipal services** may assess urgent accommodation or welfare needs. **Autonomous communities** exercise significant housing responsibilities. **National legislators** can change general legal rules.

One actor may acknowledge vulnerability without possessing the power to cancel another institution's decision. A municipality might offer temporary accommodation but not the original home. A court might decide a tenancy has ended while being unable to create a social tenancy elsewhere. A campaign can therefore be asking several institutions to solve different parts of the same human problem.

| Institutional actor | Possible role | What must not be assumed |
|---|---|---|
| Creditor or landlord | Negotiate terms, make contractual decisions | That all legal enforcement is discretionary |
| Court | Determine rights and order or suspend enforcement where law permits | That it can allocate public housing |
| Judicial enforcement | Implement court decisions | That it sets housing policy |
| Municipality | Social assessment, emergency support, local housing services | That it can overturn any judgment |
| Autonomous community | Regional housing policy and programmes | That powers are uniform across Spain |
| National government and parliament | Legislation, budgets, national programmes | That announcing a proposal makes it operative law |

The organisational problem is partly one of **routing**. Who must be approached for a postponement? Who for a debt settlement? Who for emergency shelter? Who for reform of a general rule? A protest that correctly identifies the human harm can still fail if the demand is addressed to an institution unable to deliver the requested remedy.

## ⚖️ 14. Law changed — but how much, and for whom?

Several legal developments are indispensable to the history. **Royal Decree-Law 6/2012** introduced an emergency framework for qualifying mortgage debtors without resources. **Law 1/2013** amended mortgage and enforcement rules and provided particular protections for vulnerable households, alongside measures connected to social renting. The **Aziz** judgment supplied a major EU-law intervention on unfair contractual terms and effective remedies. [2][4][5]

The chronology should not flatten these into 'the government banned evictions'. Eligibility rules, household circumstances, the kind of proceeding and the date of the applicable law determine what protection is available. Some protections are temporary; some concern only specific mortgage enforcement situations; some require applications and documentary proof. A legal headline cannot do the work of reading the provision.

For later periods, Spain's housing legislation, pandemic-related measures, rental protections and constitutional litigation require their own date-specific review. The previously proposed September–October **2026** emergency-decree sequence in the wider Maricarmen project has **not been independently verified for this node** and is deliberately not asserted as fact here. Any such claims must be checked against the BOE, Congress records and the operative text at the relevant date.

A useful comparison for each reform is: **movement demand → draft or proposal → enacted text → entry into force → eligibility → enforcement → observed effect**. Missing stages should remain missing, not be supplied by political enthusiasm.

## 📊 15. What would count as success?

There is no single valid success metric for anti-eviction organising. Consider the following outcomes:

| Outcome | Evidence required | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Scheduled eviction postponed | Court record or corroborated report | The household may still face a later date |
| Proceeding suspended | Relevant legal order | Suspension may be conditional or temporary |
| Mortgage debt discharged | Agreement or judicial disposition | Does not automatically establish housing security |
| Social rental agreed | Contract or reliable confirmation | Terms, duration and accessibility matter |
| Alternative housing obtained | Verified accommodation outcome | Location and suitability may be contested |
| Statute changed | BOE and parliamentary record | Scope may be narrower than demands |
| Wider public support | Comparable polling or documented participation | Attention is not identical to durable reform |
| Collective capacity sustained | Records of continuing assemblies and work | Survival alone is not proof of effectiveness |

Counts also need denominators and definitions. How many households sought help? How many cases were eligible for particular remedies? How many postponements became durable resolutions? Are the same households counted more than once? A movement's published figures may be valuable evidence of its activities while remaining unsuitable for precise causal claims.

The counterfactual is difficult. If a bank agrees to a settlement after a campaign, would it have agreed anyway? If a court postpones enforcement, was the decision triggered by legal submissions, social-service evidence, public pressure or several factors together? The answer requires case records. **Sequence is evidence of timing, not automatically of cause.**

## 🌍 16. Beyond Spain — A transferable method, not a universal template

The wider importance of PAH is not restricted to a particular slogan or confrontation. The movement showed how people confronting specialised institutions might create collective spaces for interpretation, shared experience and action. That model can inform the study of welfare appeals, debt advice, disability rights, labour organising and other settings where procedural knowledge is unevenly distributed.

But the Spanish legal environment matters. Mortgage liability, civil enforcement, consumer protection, social housing and municipal responsibilities differ across countries. A technique that changes a negotiation in Barcelona cannot simply be exported to Britain without understanding the relevant law and institutions. The transferable element may be the **question** — how do people obtain usable knowledge and collective capacity? — rather than any one tactic.

International attention also risks simplifying the movement into a few charismatic figures or memorable photographs. Organisational history should follow the assemblies, the less visible volunteers, the households whose cases did not reach the news and the people who kept the work going after the cameras left.

## 🕯️ 17. Maricarmen — Related history, different legal circumstances

The reported Maricarmen case in this Polaris cluster concerns a long-established tenancy and contested continuation of occupancy. It should **not** be described as a mortgage foreclosure, and the presence of anti-eviction protesters should **not** be treated as proof that PAH directed the campaign. Named organisational participation, dates, judicial decisions and negotiated outcomes must be verified in the dedicated biographical and mobilisation nodes.

What can responsibly be compared is the organising problem: a person confronts a specialised legal process, supporters seek information, different institutions hold different powers, and public mobilisation interacts with legal proceedings and negotiation. Those mechanisms resemble questions encountered in the history of Stop Desahucios. Similarity is not proof of direct descent.

The individual remains more important than the neatness of the comparison. A movement's history can explain why a community possesses methods for responding to eviction. It cannot replace the affected person's own words, legal position or wishes. **Maricarmen does not become a PAH case merely because her story belongs in a history of housing resistance.**

## ✊ 18. Conclusion — The paperwork, the people and the door

The history of Stop Desahucios begins before the door and continues after it. It includes mortgage debt, legal reform, court procedures, meetings, negotiations, collective advice, public demonstration, disputed tactics and the search for secure accommodation. It also includes failures, unequal access to support, and the difference between winning time and winning a home.

PAH's historical contribution was not that it magically eliminated institutional power. It was that people directly affected by housing insecurity built ways to understand and contest that power together. They made procedures discussable, assembled practical knowledge and insisted that housing loss had consequences beyond the private contract. They also pursued legislative change through formal democratic channels, while confronting the limits of what legislation actually delivered.

**The door is where the public often first sees the conflict. The organising history is everything that happened before someone reached for the handle.**
