🌕 README.md

Purpose

* Orient the reader to the full-moon phase.
* Explain that the movement has become visible enough for the surrounding machinery to become visible.
* Establish that maximum visibility does not mean complete knowledge.
* Map the systems the movement confronted.
* Set evidentiary rules for the entire folder.
* Transition into 🌘_Did_The_System_Become_Tolerable/.

Central claim

* The movement did not confront:
    * one minister;
    * one examination authority;
    * one contractor;
    * one broken test;
    * one media outlet;
    * one platform;
    * one surveillance product.
* It confronted an institutional environment able to:
    * centralise authority;
    * fragment delivery;
    * outsource responsibility;
    * gate access to the future;
    * expose people unequally;
    * identify participants;
    * narrow public narration;
    * acquire capability through procurement;
    * connect with wider systems of repression;
    * absorb pressure without necessarily redistributing power.

        ↓

and harder to assign

Lunar correspondence

* 🌕 as pūrṇimā.
* Full illumination as the point where the machinery becomes most visible.
* Exposure rather than omniscience.
* The public becomes visible to institutions that may remain opaque to the public.
* The movement may illuminate connections without proving every suspected relationship.

Main orientation points

* Formal authority, operational delivery and actual control may sit with different actors.
* Outsourcing does not remove public responsibility.
* Uniform rules can generate unequal consequences.
* Students may already exist as administrative data before becoming protesters.
* Information narrowing does not require total censorship.
* Procurement creates capability but does not prove case-specific deployment.
* Connected oppressions are not identical oppressions.
* Similar techniques do not prove a documented transfer pathway.
* The government was confronting a living archive of Indian resistance, not a politically rootless internet public.
* That archive includes:
    * anticolonial struggle;
    * anti-caste struggle;
    * labour and peasant struggle;
    * women’s organising;
    * post-independence democratic resistance;
    * the Emergency;
    * student movements;
    * recent regional mobilisation;
    * song, dance, theatre, satire and alternative communication.

Important contradiction

* The government draws legitimacy from:
    * postcolonial recovery;
    * resistance to foreign rule;
    * civilisational confidence;
    * refusal of Western domination.
* But the same history teaches publics:
    * how to identify arbitrary power;
    * how rulers criminalise resistance;
    * how information control accompanies coercion;
    * how culture becomes resistance infrastructure.
* Key formulation:

The state could invoke India’s history of liberation, but it could not reserve that history’s political agency for itself.

* 🏫 — who controlled authorised knowledge and access to institutions.
* ☔️ — how common rules produced unequal visibility and consequence.

* 💰 — how capability was purchased, owned, integrated and maintained.
* 🍉 — how one population’s repression became another institution’s capability, precedent or political cover.
* 🌍 — how techniques moved across political systems and outlived their original justification.

Evidence rules

* Distinguish:
    * documented fact;
    * reported claim;
    * participant interpretation;
    * analytical inference;
    * plausible but unresolved connection.
* Distinguish:
    * capability;
    * access;
    * implementation;
    * demonstrated use;
    * attribution;
    * causation.
* Distinguish:
    * explicit participant reference;
    * recognisable cultural adaptation;
    * analytical resemblance.
* Do not convert historical or cultural continuity into a claim of conscious imitation without evidence.

🏫 gating_the_curriculum.md

Purpose

* Connect authorised knowledge, national identity and institutional entry.
* Explain why the examination gate carried political and ideological meaning.
* Show the tension between postcolonial recovery and postcolonial standardisation.

Central question

Who determines what counts as legitimate knowledge, and who gets through the institutional gate built around it?

Central claim

* The examination did not merely measure performance.
* It formed part of a system through which institutions:
    * authorised knowledge;
    * claimed to identify merit;
    * allocated education and professional futures;
    * reproduced an account of the nation.
* The legitimacy of the gate depended on the credibility of the system operating it.

Corruption did not disappear; it moved

* corruption may move into:
    * procurement;
    * contracting;
    * data;
    * access;

Young people can see the contradiction

* paper leaks;

Postcolonial legitimacy

* government use of postcolonial language;
* civilisational recovery;
* refusal of Western domination;

* national pride;

Postcolonial is not automatically decolonial

* changing the identity at the centre does not dismantle centralisation;
* Hindu identity may be elevated as the authentic centre;
* Hindi may be treated as the natural national language;
* other identities become conditional, regional or secondary;
* plurality may be arranged around one preferred national subject;
* selected resistance histories may be absorbed into government legitimacy.

Key formulation:

Replacing the coloniser’s preferred identity with one formerly subordinated identity does not, by itself, dismantle the colonial machinery of standardisation.

The government cannot monopolise resistance history

* national resistance is celebrated;
* current dissent is disciplined;
* historical resistance is treated as heroic only when safely concluded;
* students can claim that history too;
* the state may embody independence without owning every meaning of liberation.
