Each part does different work.

Humour

Humour lowers the emotional and social cost of first participation.

A person does not initially have to sign a manifesto. They can:

* share a joke;
* draw the mascot;
* wear sunglasses;
* repeat a phrase;
* join a song;
* repost an image.

The symbol

The cockroach compresses a complicated claim:

We are the people treated as disposable by a system that depends on us.

That creates recognition faster than a long policy explanation.

Distributed reproduction

A symbol that can be redrawn, remixed, performed and translated cannot easily be contained by removing one account or leader.

Mutual aid

Care and logistics convert symbolic affinity into sustained participation.

Without:

* food;
* water;
* medical support;
* legal information;
* transport;
* communication;
* risk management;

the meme remains attention rather than movement capacity.

Permission

Visible participation reduces perceived isolation.

The movement tells people:

You are not the only one who thinks this.
You will not necessarily be standing alone.
There is now a recognised form in which this can be said.

The central theory-of-change finding for Act II is:

Humour did not cause political change by itself. It reduced the cost of entry into a movement whose organisational infrastructure could convert participation into pressure.

Change ladder

1. Outputs

Things the movement directly produces:

2. Immediate outcomes

Changes in behaviour or political conditions:

* public mockery becomes normal;
* journalists gain permission to report more directly;
* repression becomes more costly;

3. Intermediate outcomes

Changes in institutional behaviour:

* cases withdrawn;
* compensation paid;
* examination procedures changed;
* contractor accountability imposed;
* independent review established;
* procurement transparency increased;
* student representation introduced;
* safeguards against retaliation created.

4. Structural outcomes

Changes in the underlying distribution of power:

* examination bodies become independently accountable;
* candidates gain enforceable remedies;
* institutional failure carries automatic consequences;
* vendor chains become transparent;
* surveillance and data use are constrained;
* public-order powers receive judicial oversight;
* caste and class inequalities in access are addressed;
* the state cannot simply move responsibility through contractors.

That distinction prevents us from treating every visible win as equivalent.

The demands as a theory-of-change test

The three-demand structure is particularly useful because each demand touches a different layer of power.

Resignation
→ symbolic and political accountability
Dropping cases
→ reduction of coercive power
Compensation
→ redistribution of material cost
Institutional reform
→ redistribution of future authority

So the movement’s theory of change can be assessed by asking:

Did it change the face of authority?
Did it reduce the state’s capacity to punish?
Did it force the institution to bear the cost of its failure?
Did it alter how future decisions will be made?

That gives us a clean evaluation matrix:

Level	Question	Evidence of change
Narrative	Did the public meaning of the crisis change?	Government forced to respond; ridicule and grievance become widely legible
Political	Did an office-holder bear responsibility?	Resignation or removal
Coercive	Did protesters become safer?	Cases dropped; retaliation protections
Material	Did the institution bear the harm it caused?	Compensation and repair
Administrative	Did procedures change?	New safeguards, redundancy, oversight
Structural	Did power move?	Independent accountability and enforceable rights

The whole case in one theory-of-change sentence

When a widely shared institutional failure is translated into a culturally portable symbol, supported by distributed organisation and mutual aid, it can lower the cost of participation, create visible public alignment and raise the reputational cost of inaction; however, durable justice occurs only when that pressure moves beyond symbolic concession to reduce coercion, redistribute material cost and alter institutional authority.

And the more Polaris version:

The cockroach made the grievance visible. The organisers made visibility durable. The public made dismissal expensive. The government conceded the cheapest demand first. The remaining question is whether the movement can force change into the machinery.

Likely compact findings

1. The examination did not merely measure students; it allocated scarce futures.
2. The system demanded near-perfect compliance from candidates while failing
   to impose equivalent discipline on its own institutions and suppliers.
3. The cockroach was not merely a mascot. It became a distributed political
   interface supported by logistics, care, humour and operational competence.
4. CJP did not emerge from an empty digital field. It concentrated a pre-existing
   repertoire of coded, vernacular and low-visibility resistance.
5. The movement created permission for a wider public to express dissatisfaction
   that had become expensive or dangerous to state directly.
6. Government concession was real but asymmetrical: it surrendered the most
   visible symbol of responsibility before redistributing money, legal protection
   or institutional authority.
7. Digital authoritarianism does not act on a flat public. Caste, class, gender,
   language and regional power shape who becomes visible and who bears the risk.
8. India illustrates how authoritarian capabilities can accumulate inside
   democratic procedure through regulation, procurement, surveillance and
   state–corporate convergence.
9. India’s political information environment is regional and global. Control
   techniques travel, but so do evidence, jokes, organising practices and criticism.
10. The procurement chain must be traced beyond the Indian prime contractor to
    technology ownership, licensing, maintenance and geopolitical dependency.
