# 🧾 Claim, Source, Confidence And Limit  
**First created:** 2026-07-30 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-30  
*How to trace a significant claim from its exact wording through its earliest available source, assess what the evidence supports and preserve the limit instead of editing uncertainty out of the story.*  

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

A fast-moving political case produces claims faster than it produces settled evidence.

The Cockroach case contained:

- official statements;
- participant accounts;
- images;
- videos;
- slogans;
- legal reporting;
- live blogs;
- later summaries;
- social-media interpretation;
- newsroom synthesis;
- analytical inference.

These materials did not all carry the same evidentiary weight.

They also changed over time.

A claim that was responsibly described as **reported** on one date may later become:

- confirmed;
- contradicted;
- narrowed;
- superseded;
- impossible to verify;
- shown to have originated from one derivative source.

The central reporting problem is:

> What exactly is being claimed, which source supports it, how strong is that support, and what remains outside the evidence?

The newsroom should preserve four fields together:

```text
claim
        +
source
        +
confidence
        +
limit
```

Removing the limit makes the claim sound cleaner.

It also makes the reporting less accurate.

---

## 🧾 Start With The Exact Claim  

Do not begin with the article.

Begin with the sentence the newsroom intends to publish.

For example:

- Dharmendra Pradhan resigned.
- The government accepted every CJP demand.
- All protest cases were withdrawn.
- Forty-seven NTA officials were dismissed.
- The cockroach meme caused the movement to grow.
- Regional coverage increased political pressure.
- Compensation was granted to bereaved families.

These claims are not equally precise.

Some contain several hidden questions.

Take:

> All protest cases were withdrawn.

The newsroom must ask:

- Which cases?
- In which jurisdictions?
- At what procedural stage?
- Withdrawn by police, prosecutors or court?
- Was only non-coercive action promised?
- Were investigations still active?
- Were records corrected?
- What was the assessment date?

The exact claim should be written before searching for support.

Otherwise, a nearby source may be accepted because it feels relevant.

---

## 🔬 Break Compound Claims Apart  

A sentence may contain several claims disguised as one.

Example:

> The government accepted CJP’s demands, withdrew the cases and implemented examination reform.

This contains at least three separate propositions:

1. the government accepted a defined demand set;
2. covered legal proceedings were ended;
3. operational reform was implemented.

Each requires different evidence.

```text
one fluent sentence
        ≠
one evidentiary claim
```

Break compound claims into units that can be independently:

- sourced;
- dated;
- assessed;
- corrected.

If one component fails, the newsroom should not have to retract the entire account.

---

## 🗣️ Preserve Exact Wording  

Record:

- the exact words;
- who used them;
- where;
- when;
- in which language;
- whether they were spoken, written or paraphrased.

Terms such as:

- accepted;
- agreed;
- considered;
- promised;
- initiated;
- completed;
- withdrew;
- protected;
- compensated;
- reformed;

are not interchangeable.

For example:

```text
government agreed to study a charter
        ≠
government accepted every proposal

state announced withdrawal action
        ≠
every proceeding was finally closed

compensation acknowledged in principle
        ≠
money reached recipients
```

The original wording should remain accessible even where the newsroom uses a natural-language summary.

---

## 🔗 Trace It To The Earliest Available Source  

The newsroom should locate the earliest available source supporting the claim.

This does not always mean the earliest publication is the most reliable.

It means the newsroom can see where the wording entered the record.

Trace:

- original statement;
- original document;
- original recording;
- first participant account;
- first published report;
- later derivative reports.

A useful pathway is:

```text
primary document
        ↓
press release
        ↓
news report
        ↓
commentary
        ↓
social post
        ↓
later citation
```

At every stage, wording may strengthen.

A report may say:

> CJP said the government had agreed to consider its charter.

A later article may say:

> The government accepted CJP’s reform demands.

A social post may say:

> All reforms were agreed.

A later writer may cite the social wording as though it appeared in the original settlement.

The newsroom must reverse the chain.

---

## 🗂️ Record The Source Properly  

For every significant claim, record:

| Field | What to preserve |
|---|---|
| Exact wording | The words being relied upon |
| Publication date | Including time where relevant |
| Author | Named author, desk or organisation |
| Outlet | Publication or platform |
| Source class | Official, participant, legal, journalistic, visual or analytical |
| Original language | Language and script |
| Translation | Literal and natural rendering where needed |
| Primary status | Primary, secondary or derivative |
| Earliest located source | First source presently found |
| Archive | Stable copy or capture |
| Access date | When the newsroom reviewed it |
| Correction status | Corrected, updated or unchanged |
| Known limit | What the source does not establish |

This turns the citation into a record rather than a decorative link.

## 🪜 Source Classes  

### Primary source  

A source directly produced by an actor or system involved.

Examples:

- official order;
- resignation letter;
- court filing;
- police notice;
- CJP statement;
- participant recording;
- original image;
- original charter;
- tender document.

Primary does not mean neutral.

It means close to the event, actor or decision.

### Secondary source  

A source that independently reports or analyses primary material.

Examples:

- reported article;
- legal summary;
- investigative report;
- local reporting based on several interviews.

### Derivative source  

A source that mainly repeats another source.

Examples:

- aggregator;
- rewritten article;
- social summary;
- commentary relying on one report;
- later article citing earlier coverage without new verification.

```text
primary
≠ automatically true

secondary
≠ automatically weaker

derivative
≠ independent corroboration
```

The source class describes the relationship to the evidence.

It does not decide credibility by itself.

---

## 🧠 Source Position And Source Quality  

A source may be:

- official;
- prestigious;
- fluent;
- polished;
- widely quoted;

and still be evidentially weak for a specific claim.

A participant account may be:

- fragmented;
- emotional;
- locally published;
- difficult to translate;

and still be the strongest evidence of participant meaning or immediate experience.

Assess:

- proximity;
- access;
- incentives;
- corroboration;
- internal consistency;
- documentary support;
- risk;
- subject-matter competence.

Keep separate:

```text
social prestige
        ≠
evidentiary quality
```

The newsroom should not demand detailed proof from marginalised participants while allowing officials to generalise unsupported.

---

## 🪜 Evidence And Confidence Categories  

### Confirmed  

Supported by strong, direct evidence and, where appropriate, independent confirmation.

Examples may include:

- formally accepted resignation;
- published court order;
- official payment record confirmed by recipient;
- final case-disposal order.

### Reported  

Published by a credible source, but not yet fully verified through direct records or independent confirmation.

### Participant interpretation  

A participant’s account of:

- meaning;
- motive;
- movement experience;
- settlement understanding;
- risk.

This is primary evidence of participant interpretation.

It is not automatically proof of external causation.

### Observer interpretation  

A journalist, researcher, lawyer or external observer explains the event.

### Analytical inference  

A conclusion drawn from several pieces of evidence.

It should identify:

- the supporting record;
- rival explanations;
- the degree of confidence.

### Plausible But Unresolved  

Consistent with the known record, but lacking enough evidence for firmer attribution or causation.

### Unsupported  

The located source does not provide adequate support.

Unsupported does not necessarily mean false.

It means the claim should not presently be published as established.

---

## 📊 Confidence Labels  

Confidence should attach to the claim, not to the whole person, outlet or institution.

### High confidence  

- direct record;
- clear wording;
- appropriate authority;
- consistent independent evidence;
- little material contradiction.

### Medium confidence  

- credible reporting;
- partial documentation;
- some independent support;
- remaining scope or implementation questions.

### Low confidence  

- one source;
- derivative reporting;
- ambiguous wording;
- unresolved contradiction;
- incomplete provenance.

### No responsible confidence  

- source cannot be located;
- wording has materially changed;
- evidence does not address the claim;
- attribution depends on speculation.

Example:

> **High confidence:** the minister’s resignation was formally accepted.  
> **Medium confidence:** some state-level withdrawal action was initiated.  
> **Low confidence:** every protest-related record was corrected.  
> **No responsible confidence:** the cockroach symbol alone caused the resignation.

---

## 🧵 Repetition Is Not Independent Corroboration  

Ten outlets may appear to corroborate a claim.

All ten may rely on:

- one agency report;
- one live blog;
- one spokesperson;
- one press release;
- one translated article;
- one social post.

The newsroom should map source dependency.

```text
one source
        ↓
ten rewrites
        ≠
ten confirmations
```

Look for:

- identical phrasing;
- identical errors;
- same unnamed source;
- same quotation order;
- same omitted details;
- links back to one article.

Independent corroboration requires an independent evidentiary route.

---

## 🧬 Source Dependency Map  

For a significant claim, record:

```text
Claim A
├── Outlet 1 → official statement
├── Outlet 2 → Outlet 1
├── Outlet 3 → agency copy of Outlet 1
├── Social post → Outlet 2
└── Later article → social post
```

This record may show that apparent consensus is one statement travelling through several publication layers.

That does not make the statement false.

It changes the confidence assessment.

---

## ⚖️ Unsupported Is Not The Same As Disproven  

Suppose a report is cited for the claim:

> The cockroach meme directly caused the government to accept the settlement.

The article may describe:

- the symbol;
- the movement;
- the settlement.

It may not establish causation.

The correct conclusion is:

> The source does not support the causal claim.

Not:

> The symbol had no causal effect.

```text
source does not support claim
        ≠
claim has been disproven
```

A responsible newsroom preserves the distinction between:

- lack of evidence;
- evidence of absence;
- contradictory evidence;
- disproven claim.

This is especially important where records remain incomplete.

---

## 📰 Headline Strength Versus Source Strength  

A headline may be stronger than the evidence beneath it.

The source may say:

- may;
- could;
- appears;
- is consistent with;
- was described by participants as;
- remains under investigation.

The headline may convert this into:

- caused;
- proved;
- targeted;
- used;
- admitted;
- exposed.

The headline should not exceed the strongest reliable source.

```text
source says possibility
        ↓
headline says proof
        =
evidentiary inflation
```

Record the headline separately from the article body.

A correction to the body does not fix:

- search results;
- social cards;
- aggregator text;
- screenshots;
- AI summaries.

---

## 🔥 Precision Is Not Political Timidity  

A claim can be politically serious without being rhetorically inflated.

Use language that matches the evidence:

- documented;
- reported;
- alleged;
- participant-described;
- consistent with;
- capable of;
- appears to;
- cannot yet be verified;
- requires further evidence.

Do not collapse:

```text
capability
≠ access
≠ implementation
≠ demonstrated use
≠ attribution
≠ causation
```

A technically capable system may never have been deployed.

A deployed system may not have been used in the alleged incident.

A pattern may be real while the selected attribution is wrong.

An exact account can still be angry.

It simply directs the anger at what can be shown.

---

## 🧭 Analytically Plausible, Attributionally Weak  

Some claims may be analytically useful but attributionally weak.

Example:

> The cockroach helped reduce the recognition cost of joining the movement.

That may be a defensible analytical inference based on:

- repetition;
- recognisability;
- participant explanation;
- visible adaptation.

But a stronger claim such as:

> The CJP leadership deliberately designed the cockroach to produce national mobilisation.

would require evidence of:

- intent;
- planning;
- decision-making;
- strategy.

Keep separate:

```text
mechanism appears plausible
        ≠
specific actor intended the mechanism
```

Do not discard the analysis.

Label the attribution limit.

---

## 🗣️ Participant Meaning Needs Its Own Label  

Participant testimony may establish:

- what a slogan meant to them;
- why they joined;
- what risk they experienced;
- what they thought had been conceded;
- how they understood the outcome.

It may not establish:

- every participant’s meaning;
- national scale;
- hidden institutional motive;
- complete chronology;
- legal status.

A participant should not be forced to prove the whole case alone.

Report:

> One organiser described the cockroach as…

rather than:

> The cockroach meant…

unless broader evidence supports the generalisation.

---

## 🌐 Translation Can Change The Claim  

The source record should preserve:

- original script;
- transliteration;
- literal translation;
- natural translation;
- uncertainty;
- translator or cultural explainer.

Translation may change evidentiary force.

A term translated as:

- accepted;
- heard;
- agreed;
- considered;
- acknowledged;

may materially change the settlement record.

A joke may appear hostile in literal English while functioning as reclamation in context.

The translated claim should not silently replace the original.

---

## 🖼️ Visual Sources Need Provenance  

For images and video, record:

- original file;
- earliest located upload;
- date;
- time;
- location;
- caption;
- uploader;
- known edits;
- crop;
- audio changes;
- metadata where safe;
- exposure risk.

Visual evidence can support:

- presence;
- chronology;
- visible action;
- public performance;
- signage.

It may not establish:

- motive;
- what occurred outside frame;
- representativeness;
- who organised the event.

The image is a source.

The caption is another claim.

---

## 📅 Date The Assessment  

Every assessment should state its date.

This matters where:

- cases remain active;
- compensation remains pending;
- reform is future-facing;
- documents appear later;
- participant accounts change;
- official wording is corrected;
- dead links remove evidence.

Use:

> **Assessment as of 30 July 2026:** …

A date does not weaken the finding.

It makes the finding honest.

```text
true on assessment date
        ≠
permanently settled
```

---

## 🚧 Source Limits  

Every source note should identify what it cannot establish.

Examples:

### Official statement  

May establish:

- what the institution publicly said;
- formal policy;
- stated intent.

May not establish:

- actual implementation;
- participant outcome;
- independent truth.

### Participant account  

May establish:

- lived experience;
- participant meaning;
- local chronology.

May not establish:

- movement-wide representativeness;
- hidden government motive.

### News report  

May establish:

- what was reported;
- sourced chronology;
- attributed quotation.

May not establish:

- independent corroboration where it relies on one source.

### Image  

May establish:

- visible content within the frame.

May not establish:

- complete context;
- causation;
- unseen events.

A source limit should appear beside the claim, not buried in a later disclaimer.

---

## ❓ Missing Evidence  

Record the evidence that would strengthen or resolve the claim.

Examples:

- original five-point charter;
- written government response;
- complete case list;
- final withdrawal orders;
- compensation scheme;
- payment records;
- meeting minutes;
- NTA personnel orders;
- contractor audits;
- original meme file;
- participant chronology;
- internal government decision record.

Missing evidence is itself part of the assessment.

It should not be rhetorically filled by confidence.

---

## ⚔️ Contradictions  

Where sources conflict, record:

- exact wording;
- date;
- source position;
- scope;
- possible reason for difference;
- which evidence is stronger;
- whether the contradiction is resolved.

Do not silently blend incompatible accounts.

Example:

```text
movement:
all demands accepted

government:
charter will be considered

record:
resignation complete;
other commitments future-facing
```

The contradiction may be:

- political framing;
- different definitions;
- different moments in negotiation;
- genuine dispute.

Preserve it until evidence resolves it.

---

## 🛠️ Corrections And Stronger Evidence  

When stronger evidence appears:

1. preserve the earlier assessment;
2. record the new source;
3. explain what changed;
4. update the confidence label;
5. correct headline, body and metadata;
6. identify whether the earlier claim was reasonable at the time;
7. retain a visible correction trail.

Do not make the record look as though the newsroom always knew the later answer.

```text
earlier assessment
        +
new evidence
        ↓
traceable update
```

A correction is not an embarrassment to hide.

It is evidence that the method works.

---

## 🔗 Dead Links And Archival Fragility  

Digital evidence disappears through:

- deleted posts;
- changed URLs;
- paywalls;
- altered pages;
- platform suspension;
- unannounced corrections;
- link rot.

Preserve where lawful and safe:

- archive copy;
- screenshot;
- PDF;
- timestamp;
- metadata;
- quoted passage;
- access date.

Do not preserve sensitive participant information merely because it may later disappear.

Archival value must be balanced against exposure risk.

## ✅ A Reusable Claim Card  

For each major newsroom claim:

```text
CLAIM:
Exact sentence being assessed.

DATE:
Assessment date.

SOURCE:
Earliest available source and strongest supporting source.

SOURCE CLASS:
Primary / secondary / derivative / participant / official / visual.

ORIGINAL LANGUAGE:
Language, script and translation status.

WHAT IT ESTABLISHES:
The narrow supported finding.

CONFIDENCE:
High / medium / low / no responsible confidence.

LIMIT:
What remains outside the evidence.

CONTRADICTIONS:
Conflicting source or wording.

MISSING EVIDENCE:
What would strengthen or resolve the claim.

UPDATE:
Later correction, confirmation or narrowing.
```

Inside this folder, the card should remain anchored to Cockroach-specific claims.

The fully generic protocol belongs later in `🧭_Reusable_Findings/`.

## 🚧 What This Node Does Not Claim  

This node does not claim that:

- primary sources are always true;
- official sources are always weak;
- participant accounts are merely subjective;
- repetition has no evidentiary value;
- an unsupported claim is false;
- every claim needs certainty before publication;
- confidence labels eliminate judgement;
- translation can produce one perfect meaning;
- archives should preserve sensitive material without limit;
- corrections erase the value of earlier reporting;
- exact attribution requires political neutrality;
- analytical inference should be excluded.

It does claim that:

- every significant claim needs an identifiable source;
- wording, date and source class matter;
- derivative repetition is not independent corroboration;
- confidence must attach to the claim;
- source limits and missing evidence should remain visible;
- unsupported and disproven are different;
- precise criticism can remain politically forceful;
- corrections should be traceable.

## 🧷 Compact Finding  

A claim does not become stronger merely because it has travelled farther.

The newsroom must preserve:

- the exact wording;
- the earliest available source;
- the source class;
- the confidence;
- the limit;
- the assessment date.

Precision is not political timidity.

It is how a newsroom keeps a real pattern from being dismissed because one attribution outran the evidence.
