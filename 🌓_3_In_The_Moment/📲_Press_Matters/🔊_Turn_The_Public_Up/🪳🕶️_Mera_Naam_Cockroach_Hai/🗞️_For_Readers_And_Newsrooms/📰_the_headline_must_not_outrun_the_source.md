# 📰 The Headline Must Not Outrun The Source  
**First created:** 2026-07-30 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-30  
*Why the headline is not merely packaging: it is an evidentiary claim that must remain inside the strongest reliable source, preserve uncertainty and survive later correction across every place the story travels.*  

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

The headline is often the most widely read sentence in the article.

It may appear in:

- search results;
- social cards;
- newsletters;
- aggregators;
- screenshots;
- push notifications;
- AI summaries;
- database entries;
- later citations.

Many readers will never reach the paragraph where the newsroom restores the uncertainty removed from the headline.

That means the headline is not merely:

- marketing;
- compression;
- tone;
- editorial style.

It is a claim.

The central newsroom problem is:

> Does the headline state only what the strongest reliable source supports, or has compression quietly converted possibility, attribution or participant interpretation into fact?

Keep separate:

```text
source finding
≠ attributed quotation
≠ newsroom synthesis
≠ headline claim
```

Each layer can strengthen the wording.

That strengthening must be controlled.

---

## 📰 The Headline Is An Evidentiary Claim  

A headline may assert:

- who acted;
- what happened;
- why it happened;
- who caused it;
- whether something was proved;
- whether a system was used;
- whether a movement won;
- whether a government admitted wrongdoing.

Those are substantive claims.

The newsroom should be able to answer:

- Which source supports this wording?
- Is the claim direct or inferred?
- Is the verb stronger than the source?
- Is uncertainty preserved?
- Is attribution visible?
- Does the body actually establish the headline?

A headline that outruns the evidence can distort the entire article even where the body is careful.

---

## 🔬 Compression Creates Risk  

Headlines compress.

Compression may remove:

- date;
- jurisdiction;
- attribution;
- confidence;
- scope;
- exception;
- source class;
- rival explanation;
- implementation limit.

Example:

> Government agrees to review CJP demands.

may become:

> Government accepts CJP demands.

Then:

> CJP forces government reform.

Each version says more.

The last version adds:

- full acceptance;
- causation;
- implementation;
- movement responsibility.

```text
shorter
        can become
stronger
```

The newsroom must compress without inventing.

---

## 🧾 The Headline Should Match The Claim Card  

Before publication, compare the headline to the claim record.

If the claim card says:

- **confidence:** medium;
- **source:** participant statement;
- **limit:** no final government order located;

the headline should not say:

> Government confirms all demands implemented.

The headline should reflect:

- source class;
- evidentiary strength;
- scope;
- assessment date where necessary.

Possible alternatives:

- “CJP says government accepted key demands”
- “Government announces review after CJP mobilisation”
- “Resignation confirmed; implementation of wider settlement remains unclear”

The best headline is not always the boldest one.

It is the one the evidence can continue to carry.

---

## 🪜 Match The Verb To The Evidence  

Causal and evidentiary verbs carry different weights.

### Lower-strength verbs  

- says;
- reports;
- describes;
- alleges;
- claims;
- announces;
- proposes;
- considers;
- appears;
- may;
- could;
- is consistent with.

### Medium-strength verbs  

- confirms;
- finds;
- documents;
- establishes;
- verifies;
- records.

### High-strength verbs  

- proves;
- caused;
- forced;
- targeted;
- used;
- exposed;
- admitted;
- covered up;
- orchestrated.

High-strength verbs require high-strength evidence.

```text
source says “may”
        ≠
headline says “proved”
```

The newsroom should not use a stronger verb merely because the underlying pattern feels politically obvious.

---

## ⚖️ Causal Verbs Require Causal Evidence  

A headline such as:

> Cockroach meme forced minister to resign

requires more than:

- the meme existed;
- the movement mobilised;
- the minister resigned.

The newsroom would need evidence linking:

- symbol;
- mobilisation;
- decision-making;
- resignation.

Without that, a more defensible headline might be:

> Cockroach symbol became a public face of the movement before minister’s resignation

or:

> CJP mobilisation preceded minister’s resignation as wider demands remained unresolved

Keep separate:

```text
sequence
≠ causation

association
≠ mechanism

political pressure
≠ sole cause
```

---

## 🗣️ Quotes Must Exist In The Source  

A headline may place a phrase in quotation marks.

Before publication, check:

- Did the source use those exact words?
- Was the quote translated?
- Was it shortened?
- Were words combined from separate sentences?
- Was it a paraphrase?
- Was the speaker describing fact, belief or fear?
- Did the source later clarify?

A translated phrase should not be presented as an exact English quotation without explanation where wording matters.

A composite quote is not a quote.

```text
accurate paraphrase
≠ verbatim quotation
```

Where the words are not exact, remove quotation marks.

---

## 🧩 Do Not Combine Separate Claims Into One Headline  

Example:

> CJP victory ends cases and reforms examination system

This may combine:

- resignation;
- withdrawal promise;
- compensation;
- reform announcement;
- future implementation.

Each may have a different status.

The headline should not create one completed event from several unfinished processes.

```text
several related developments
        ≠
one settled outcome
```

If needed, use a headline and subheading that preserve the distinctions.

---

## 📊 Report Finding, Quote, Synthesis And Headline  

A useful editorial test separates four layers.

### Report finding  

What the source or investigation directly establishes.

### Attributed quotation  

What an actor says happened.

### Newsroom synthesis  

The newsroom’s summary across several sources.

### Headline  

The compressed public claim.

Example:

```text
report finding:
minister resigned;
other commitments remain under review

participant quote:
“we won all five demands”

newsroom synthesis:
movement achieved a major concession,
but implementation remains incomplete

headline:
minister resigns after CJP mobilisation;
wider settlement still unresolved
```

The headline should reflect the synthesis without converting the quote into established fact.

---

## 🧠 Participant Language Needs Attribution  

Movement language may be:

- triumphant;
- strategic;
- motivational;
- emotionally true;
- politically useful.

Examples:

- historic victory;
- all demands accepted;
- government defeated;
- justice delivered.

These may be valid participant interpretations.

They should not automatically become newsroom findings.

Use:

> CJP declared a historic victory

not:

> Historic victory delivers justice

unless the newsroom independently supports that conclusion.

```text
participant declaration
≠ newsroom verification
```

---

## 🏛️ Official Language Needs Attribution Too  

Official language can sound neutral while remaining strategic.

Examples:

- normalcy restored;
- isolated incident;
- necessary force;
- reform initiated;
- misinformation contained;
- cases reviewed.

These phrases should not become unexamined headline language.

Use:

> Government says normalcy restored

rather than:

> Normalcy restored

where the factual condition remains disputed.

Official wording is still attributed wording.

---

## 🔥 The Structural Pattern May Be Real While Attribution Is Wrong  

Sometimes a newsroom correctly identifies a structural pattern but attaches it to the wrong actor, mechanism or event.

For example:

- the system may be coercive;
- the protest may have faced suppression;
- information may have been narrowed;
- contractors may have created risk.

But the headline may wrongly state:

- a named actor ordered it;
- a specific tool was used;
- one office caused it;
- a particular event proves the whole structure.

The correct response is not to abandon the structural analysis.

It is to correct the attribution.

```text
pattern may be real
        while
specific attribution is wrong
```

This is why precision protects serious criticism.

One overstated attribution can be used to dismiss the wider documented pattern.

---

## 🔥 Keep The Anger, Make The Attribution Exact  

A headline can remain politically forceful without outrunning the evidence.

Examples:

Instead of:

> Government lies about every CJP concession

use:

> Government record does not yet show full implementation of CJP settlement

Instead of:

> Cockroach meme brought down the minister

use:

> Minister resigns after movement turns cockroach insult into national political symbol

Instead of:

> State used terrorism law to crush peaceful Kashmir protest

where the full record remains disputed, use:

> Terrorism designation deepens crackdown as deaths and arrests in Pakistan-administered Kashmir remain under investigation

Precision does not require emotional neutrality.

It requires that each accusation lands on what can be shown.

---

## 🧪 Do Not Hide Uncertainty In The Body  

A common structure is:

### Headline  

> Government implements all reforms

### First paragraph  

> The movement says all reforms were accepted.

### Later paragraph  

> No final implementation document has been published.

The uncertainty is present.

But the headline has already overwritten it.

The headline, standfirst and first paragraph should agree on:

- who says what;
- what is established;
- what remains unresolved.

Do not use the body as a legal disclaimer for a stronger promotional claim.

---

## 🪧 Subheadings Can Also Outrun The Source  

Subheadings, pull quotes and section titles may be reused separately.

Audit:

- standfirst;
- deck;
- pull quote;
- image caption;
- sidebar title;
- social copy;
- newsletter subject;
- push notification.

A careful headline can be undermined by:

> How students finally defeated the government

inside the article.

Every compressed text object should pass the same evidentiary test.

---

## 🖼️ Dramatic Images Can Inflate The Headline  

An image may make a modest source feel conclusive.

Examples:

- police line;
- injured participant;
- huge placard;
- empty classroom;
- burning object.

The image may be authentic.

The headline may still overstate:

- scale;
- cause;
- representativeness;
- chronology.

The newsroom should not let visual intensity substitute for evidentiary strength.

```text
dramatic image
≠ stronger claim
```

The headline and image caption must be audited separately.

---

## 🌐 Social Cards Harden The Strongest Wording  

Social cards may preserve:

- headline;
- image;
- short description;
- publication date.

They may continue circulating after the article is corrected.

The newsroom should update:

- Open Graph title;
- social description;
- thumbnail;
- platform-specific card;
- pinned post;
- scheduled post;
- newsletter archive.

A correction that changes only the article body leaves the strongest false wording alive.

---

## 🔎 Search Results Create A Second Headline  

Search systems may display:

- title tag;
- meta description;
- cached text;
- old snippet;
- structured data.

The visible search result may differ from the current article headline.

Audit:

- page title;
- metadata;
- schema markup;
- URL slug;
- indexed description.

A misleading URL slug may survive every visible correction.

Example:

```text
/article/cockroach-meme-forced-resignation
```

may remain after the headline is corrected.

The correction process should include the publication infrastructure.

---

## 🤖 AI Summaries Amplify Compression Errors  

AI systems may summarise:

- headline;
- standfirst;
- metadata;
- frequently repeated claims;
- derivative articles.

A nuanced body cannot reliably repair an inflated headline once the claim enters:

- search summaries;
- answer engines;
- automated newsletters;
- synthetic timelines;
- later generated reporting.

The newsroom should assume:

> The strongest compressed wording may become the permanent machine-readable version of the story.

This makes headline precision an archival responsibility.

---

## 🔁 Derivative Reporting Strengthens Claims  

A derivative outlet may copy:

> CJP says all demands accepted

as:

> All CJP demands accepted

A later commentator may write:

> Government implemented every demand

A later timeline may cite the commentator.

The original attribution disappears.

```text
attributed claim
        ↓
unattributed summary
        ↓
settled fact
```

The newsroom should make attribution hard to remove.

Useful techniques include:

- naming the source in the headline;
- preserving uncertainty in the first clause;
- linking the primary material;
- avoiding ambiguous passive voice.

---

## 🧱 Passive Voice Can Hide Attribution  

Example:

> Protest cases withdrawn after settlement

Questions:

- by whom?
- which cases?
- in which jurisdiction?
- finally closed or only reviewed?
- according to which source?

Passive voice may be useful where the actor is unknown.

It should not conceal an unresolved actor.

A better headline may be:

> State government announces review of protest cases after settlement

The actor, action and limit remain visible.

---

## 🧭 Scope Words Matter  

Watch terms such as:

- all;
- every;
- nationwide;
- historic;
- unprecedented;
- complete;
- permanent;
- national;
- universal;
- final.

These words often outrun the source.

Ask:

- all of what?
- nationwide by which measure?
- historic according to whom?
- unprecedented within what period?
- complete at which stage?
- permanent under which order?

A small scope word can turn a defensible claim into an indefensible one.

---

## 🗺️ Territorial And Community Labels Matter  

Headlines may flatten:

- India;
- Pakistan;
- Kashmir;
- students;
- Brahmins;
- diaspora;
- Hindus;
- protesters;
- government.

Use the narrowest accurate label.

For example:

- Pakistan-administered Kashmir;
- JKJAAC organisers;
- CJP participants;
- one state government;
- several student groups reviewed by the newsroom.

Do not use a national or communal label where the evidence is local, organisational or partial.

---

## 🕰️ Date Boundaries Belong In The Headline When Needed  

A story may be true only as of a particular point.

Examples:

- cases remain under review;
- compensation had not yet been paid;
- reform process had begun but was incomplete;
- casualty count remained disputed.

Where the story is likely to be read later, the headline or standfirst may need:

- “as of July 2026”;
- “initial findings”;
- “provisional count”;
- “settlement announced, implementation pending”.

Date-bounded language prevents a live process from becoming a false permanent fact.

---

## 🛠️ Corrections Must Follow The Headline Everywhere  

When a headline is wrong or overstated, correct:

- article headline;
- page title;
- standfirst;
- first paragraph;
- image caption;
- alt text;
- social cards;
- social posts;
- push notification archive;
- newsletter;
- metadata;
- URL where feasible;
- syndication partners;
- database entry.

Add a visible correction note stating:

- what was wrong;
- what changed;
- why;
- when.

Do not silently replace the headline.

A silent correction removes evidence of the newsroom’s own error.

---

## 🧾 Correction Language Should Be Specific  

Weak:

> This article has been updated.

Strong:

> An earlier headline said the government had implemented all five CJP demands. The available record shows that the minister resigned and several commitments were announced, but full implementation had not been established. The headline and metadata were corrected on 30 July 2026.

The correction should identify:

- the original claim;
- the corrected claim;
- the evidentiary reason.

---

## 🧪 Pre-Publication Headline Test  

Before publication, ask:

1. What exact factual claim does the headline make?
2. Which source supports each verb?
3. Is the source direct, secondary or derivative?
4. Is attribution visible?
5. Has possibility become proof?
6. Has sequence become causation?
7. Has one participant become the whole movement?
8. Has a local event become national?
9. Has a promise become implementation?
10. Has a quote been altered or translated?
11. Does the body preserve the same level of certainty?
12. Could the headline stand alone without misleading?
13. Would the social card still be accurate?
14. Would an AI summary based only on the headline be defensible?
15. Can the newsroom correct every place the wording will travel?

If the answer to twelve or fourteen is no, rewrite.

## 📋 Headline Audit Record  

For each significant story:

```text
HEADLINE:
Exact proposed wording.

CLAIMS:
Each factual proposition contained in the headline.

VERBS:
Says / reports / confirms / proves / caused / forced / other.

SOURCE:
Strongest source supporting each claim.

SOURCE CLASS:
Primary / secondary / derivative / participant / official.

CONFIDENCE:
High / medium / low / unresolved.

ATTRIBUTION:
Visible / implied / absent.

SCOPE:
Who, where, when and how much.

LIMIT:
What the headline cannot establish.

BODY MATCH:
Does the article support the same certainty?

SOCIAL CARD:
Exact wording and image.

METADATA:
Title tag, description, structured data and URL.

CORRECTION ROUTE:
Where and how the headline can be updated.
```

Within this folder, the audit should remain anchored to Cockroach-specific reporting.

## 🚧 What This Node Does Not Claim  

This node does not claim that:

- headlines should be dull;
- strong verbs are always wrong;
- uncertainty prevents publication;
- participant language is unreliable;
- official language is always deceptive;
- causal claims can never be made;
- every headline must contain every caveat;
- subheadings and social copy are secondary;
- AI systems are the newsroom’s only audience;
- URL changes are always practical;
- visible corrections eliminate all downstream error;
- anger should be removed from political reporting.

It does claim that:

- the headline is an evidentiary claim;
- compression can strengthen wording;
- causal verbs require causal evidence;
- quotes must be exact or clearly paraphrased;
- participant and official language both require attribution;
- the structural pattern can remain valid after a specific attribution is corrected;
- uncertainty should not be hidden in the body;
- social cards, metadata and AI summaries can preserve an error;
- corrections must follow the claim through the publication pathway;
- precision can remain politically forceful.

## 🧷 Compact Finding  

The headline must not outrun the source.

It must not turn:

- a quote into a finding;
- possibility into proof;
- sequence into causation;
- a promise into implementation;
- one spokesperson into the whole movement;
- one image into a complete theory.

Keep the anger.

Make the attribution exact.

The headline should be able to travel alone through search, social cards, screenshots, databases and AI summaries without becoming more certain than the evidence.
