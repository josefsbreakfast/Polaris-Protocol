# 🎶 The Pressure Valve Had A Soundtrack  
**First created:** 2026-07-28 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-04  
*How chants, songs, rhythm, costume, humour and replay helped participants regulate pressure, recognise one another and turn spectatorship into public participation.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

A movement is not only:

- an argument;
- a list of demands;
- a crowd;
- a sequence of official responses.

It is also:

- sound;
- rhythm;
- repetition;
- gesture;
- costume;
- timing;
- laughter;
- collective memory.

The Cockroach Janta Party did not become publicly legible through policy language alone.

Its identity travelled through:

- chants;
- songs;
- placards;
- visual jokes;
- remixed footage;
- clothing;
- movement;
- platform-native performance.

The central argument is:

> The soundtrack did not trivialise the pressure. It helped people remain inside it together.

Humour and performance could:

- reduce isolation;
- regulate fear;
- synchronise participants;
- preserve political language;
- make mockery socially reproducible;
- create low-barrier forms of participation.

The causal chain should remain careful:

```text
shared pressure
        ↓
humour, sound and performance
        ↓
emotional regulation and recognition
        ↓
greater participation durability
        ↓
possible public pressure
```

This does not mean:

```text
song
=
organisation
=
policy change
```

Culture was part of the movement’s capacity.

It was not a single sufficient cause.

Nor did the internet create the political imagination that made the soundtrack legible.

It helped:

- compress it;
- remix it;
- circulate it;
- place old references inside new public forms.

India already possessed an immense multilingual archive through which people had argued about:

- colonial rule;
- revolutionary struggle;
- caste;
- Partition;
- plural citizenship;
- corruption;
- education;
- policing;
- patriotism;
- resistance.

The meme supplied a packet.

Cinema, literature, theatre, song and historical memory supplied much of the compressed content.

## 🎶 The Pressure Valve Had A Soundtrack  

A pressure valve does not remove the system producing pressure.

It creates a route through which some of that pressure can be released without immediate collapse.

Humour, music and collective performance may help participants process:

- fear;
- rage;
- shame;
- grief;
- humiliation;
- uncertainty;
- exhaustion.

```text
pressure remains real
        +
collective expression changes how it is carried
```

The soundtrack therefore should not be read as evidence that:

- the movement was unserious;
- the grievance was minor;
- people were merely having fun.

It may be evidence that people needed forms capable of holding:

- pain;
- defiance;
- sociability;
- endurance;

at the same time.

## 🫁 Humour As Emotional Regulation  

Humour can alter the body’s experience of participation.

It may:

- interrupt panic;
- release tension;
- reduce shame;
- make strangers feel less strange;
- allow people to remain present;
- create moments of recovery.

This is emotional regulation.

It is not the same as:

- denial;
- trivialisation;
- lack of political understanding.

A person may laugh because:

- the situation is absurd;
- the joke restores agency;
- laughter is socially safer than fear;
- the crowd needs relief.

```text
laughter
≠ absence of injury

laughter
→ one way of surviving injury together
```

## 🥁 Synchronising The Public  

Rhythm helps a crowd coordinate without requiring detailed instruction.

A chant supplies:

- timing;
- repetition;
- entry points;
- shared breath;
- predictable response.

People can join after hearing only part of it.

```text
one voice
        ↓
repeated phrase
        ↓
many voices
        ↓
audible public
```

Synchronisation matters because a crowd may initially be:

- fragmented;
- anxious;
- uncertain;
- physically dispersed.

Rhythm helps participants experience themselves as:

- present;
- numerous;
- responsive;
- capable of acting together.

The public becomes audible to itself.

## 🗣️ Call And Response  

Call-and-response structures distribute participation.

One person does not need to deliver a full speech.

They need only know:

- the cue;
- the response;
- the rhythm.

This lowers the expressive threshold.

```text
leader or caller
        ↓
short recognisable cue
        ↓
collective response
```

Call-and-response can:

- amplify demands;
- maintain focus;
- test crowd recognition;
- create repetition useful to recording.

The archive should not invent a canonical call-and-response where reporting preserves only a slogan fragment.

That is particularly important for:

> **Paper leak se azadi**

The slogan family is attested.

The exact full performance structure remains less certain.

## 🧠 Rhythm Is A Form Of Memory  

Rhythm helps language survive.

People may forget:

- a speech;
- a briefing;
- a detailed explanation.

They are more likely to remember:

- rhyme;
- repetition;
- cadence;
- a familiar melodic frame.

The early chant:

> **Cockroach aa raha hai, Dharmendra Pradhan ja raha hai**

worked partly because it paired:

- arrival;
- departure;
- mascot;
- accountable office-holder.

```text
cockroach comes
        ↔
minister goes
```

The chant compressed:

- movement identity;
- confidence;
- political demand;
- prediction.

Its rhythm made the argument portable.

## 🪧 The Four Authorised Slogans  

On 22 July, a CJP spokesperson asked participants at Jantar Mantar to restrict demonstration language to four slogans, as preserved in [contemporary live reporting from *The New Indian Express*](https://www.newindianexpress.com/live/2026/Jul/22/cjp-protest-live-updates-2):

1. **Bharat Mata ki Jai**;
2. **Inquilab Zindabad**;
3. **Jai Bhim**;
4. **Dharmendra Pradhan, istifa do**.

He reportedly explained that others were looking to “make a narrative” and asked participants to avoid negative slogans.

This matters because it documents deliberate management of the public soundtrack under hostile narrative conditions.

The authorised set combined:

```text
patriotic language
        +
revolutionary or anti-colonial inheritance
        +
Ambedkarite anti-caste language
        +
specific ministerial accountability
```

The sequence can also be read as a compact political grammar:

```text
national belonging
        ↓
revolutionary legitimacy
        ↓
anti-caste and constitutional justice
        ↓
specific accountable demand
```

The slogans did not originate in one political tradition.

Their combination held several traditions inside one repeatable protocol.

### Bharat Mata Ki Jai  

> **Bharat Mata ki Jai**  
> **भारत माता की जय**  
> **بھارت ماتا کی جے**  
> *Victory to Mother India.*

### Inquilab Zindabad  

> **Inquilab Zindabad**  
> **इंक़लाब ज़िंदाबाद**  
> **انقلاب زندہ باد**  
> *Long live the revolution.*

### Jai Bhim  

> **Jai Bhim**  
> **जय भीम**  
> **جے بھیم**  
> *Victory to Bhim; a salute associated with B. R. Ambedkar and anti-caste politics.*

`Jai Bhim` long predates the 2021 Tamil film of the same name.

It is an Ambedkarite salutation rooted in the struggle against caste oppression.

The film provides a contemporary cultural anchor for readers approaching that political world from elsewhere.

T. J. Gnanavel’s *Jai Bhim* dramatises a real legal struggle following the custodial killing of Rajakannu, an Irular man, and the efforts of his wife — renamed Sengeni in the film — to force police and courts to account for what happened. Justice K. Chandru’s own account of the case and the film is preserved in [his interview with *The News Minute*](https://www.thenewsminute.com/tamil-nadu/cops-tried-bribe-me-i-threw-out-suitcase-justice-chandru-interview-jai-bhim-157222); a [Government of India discussion of the film](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1879532) provides a second institutional reference point.

It gives narrative form to:

- caste vulnerability;
- Irular and Adivasi marginalisation;
- custodial violence;
- fabricated official accounts;
- evidential persistence;
- women forcing institutions to see what they would prefer not to see;
- constitutional law used from below.

This does not prove that every participant chanting `Jai Bhim` intended to invoke the film.

It shows what political and cultural memory the words can carry.

> **Cultural anchor:** Watch *Jai Bhim* (2021) for one narrative entry point into caste, policing, evidence, constitutional law and resistance from below.

### Dharmendra Pradhan, Istifa Do  

> **Dharmendra Pradhan, istifa do**  
> *Dharmendra Pradhan, resign.*

The authorised set narrowed a much wider cultural field into language less easily reframed as indiscriminate hostility.

This was not merely message discipline.

It was risk management.

It was also an argument about belonging.

The protocol refused a choice between:

- patriotism;
- revolution;
- anti-caste justice;
- immediate democratic accountability.

It placed them in the same chorus.

## 🎬 The Internet Did Not Invent Indian Resistance  

The movement did not emerge into a culturally empty public.

India has had nearly eight decades of post-independence cinema, literature, theatre and popular song through which successive generations have interpreted:

- the freedom struggle;
- Partition and its aftermath;
- multifaith and multilingual citizenship;
- caste and class;
- workers, farmers and Adivasi communities;
- corruption and state violence;
- educational pressure;
- communalism;
- the unfinished meaning of independence.

This archive is not politically uniform.

It contains:

- state-building stories;
- revolutionary stories;
- reformist stories;
- majoritarian stories;
- anti-caste stories;
- regional and linguistic histories;
- critiques of the postcolonial settlement itself.

The works below should therefore not be collapsed into one message.

They demonstrate the density of the cultural material available to a public trying to explain itself.

### *Shaheed* — Revolutionary Memory In Popular Form  

S. Ram Sharma’s [*Shaheed* (1965)](https://indiancine.ma/LTY) brought Bhagat Singh’s revolutionary life into popular Hindi cinema less than two decades after independence.

Its songs — including `Aye Watan, Aye Watan` — carried sacrifice, anti-colonial struggle and the demand that independent India remember those who resisted British rule.

The film matters here because it shows that patriotic sound and resistance to authority were never clean opposites.

Bhagat Singh’s public memory makes a continuing claim upon the nation:

> freedom was produced through resistance;
>
> patriotic duty may therefore include resisting power again.

This is one reason `Inquilab Zindabad` cannot be translated as though it were merely a generic statement of youthful anger.

The phrase arrives with history already attached.

> **Cultural anchor:** Watch *Shaheed* (1965) for an early post-independence cinematic encounter with Bhagat Singh, revolutionary sacrifice and the sound of anti-colonial memory.

### Plural Nationhood Was Rehearsed For Decades  

Post-independence cinema did not only remember the British leaving.

It repeatedly asked what sort of country independence was meant to produce.

Among the answers was a vision of India as:

- multifaith;
- multilingual;
- regionally diverse;
- constitutionally shared;
- held together through common citizenship rather than religious sameness.

Films such as:

- *Garm Hava* (1973), through Partition, Muslim citizenship and the decision to remain;
- *Amar Akbar Anthony* (1977), through a spectacular popular fable of Hindu, Muslim and Christian belonging;

placed plural nationhood inside forms consumed far beyond specialist political writing.

These films did not resolve India’s inequalities.

They made particular ideas of India emotionally available, repeatable and difficult to pretend had never existed.

The BJP and RSS may reject, revise or attempt to displace parts of this inheritance.

They cannot engage Indian cultural memory as though plural and resistant accounts of the nation were imported yesterday.

They are embedded in:

- films;
- songs;
- school and family memory;
- dialogue;
- television repetition;
- the reputations of Bhagat Singh and Ambedkar;
- regional histories of struggle.

This is a cultural installed base.

### Different Films Open Different Doors  

Other useful reader-facing anchors include:

- *Rang De Basanti* (2006) — young people translating revolutionary memory into resistance to contemporary corruption;
- *3 Idiots* (2009) — educational pressure, institutional authority, student suffering and the possibility that pressure can end in more than one way;
- *Jai Bhim* (2021) — caste, custodial violence, evidence and constitutional law forced to work from below;
- *RRR* (2022) — anti-colonial resistance translated into spectacular popular mythology.

These films come from different languages, genres, periods and political traditions.

Their value here is not that they form one approved viewing list.

It is that they provide human-sized entry points into arguments too large to carry through abstract exposition alone.

```text
short chant or clip
        +
historical and cultural memory
        ↓
compressed political meaning
```

A fast-moving meme culture can therefore be culturally deep.

The internet helped assemble and circulate the public.

It did not invent the public’s political imagination.

## 🕯️ There Will Always Be Indian Histories Of Resistance  

Colonialism, Partition, caste struggle, linguistic organisation, labour politics, peasant movements, the Emergency, communal violence and state repression are not marginal additions to modern Indian history.

They are constitutive parts of it.

No government can permanently monopolise patriotism while those histories remain available.

Attempts to define criticism as disloyalty will repeatedly encounter older stories in which:

- authority was unjust;
- official law protected harm;
- ordinary people resisted;
- resistance became part of the nation’s honour.

This does not mean every invocation of resistance is emancipatory.

It means there will always be Indian histories capable of portraying resistance as a patriotic act.

The political struggle is partly over:

- which histories are remembered;
- who is permitted to inherit them;
- whose resistance counts as national;
- whose suffering is edited out.

Soundtracks matter because they let those histories return in forms people can carry together.

## 🎭 Performance And Collective Deniability  

A joke or song allows different degrees of political explicitness.

A participant may be able to say:

> I was singing.

> I was filming.

> I was wearing the joke.

The shared meaning can still be obvious.

This creates collective deniability:

```text
individual intention remains partly contestable
        +
the crowd’s shared political meaning becomes legible
```

Performance can therefore widen participation among people who are:

- unwilling;
- unable;
- not yet ready;

to make a complete public declaration.

This does not make performance apolitical.

It makes political participation available in more than one register.

## 👕 Costume And Embodied Humour  

Clothing and costume can turn the body into a public text.

The cockroach could be:

- worn;
- carried;
- painted;
- performed;
- attached to a profile or placard.

Costume changes participation because the person does not need to speak continuously.

The sunglasses were especially useful because they gave the cockroach:

- composure;
- style;
- attitude.

The visual instruction was:

Embodied humour can therefore resist demands that harmed people appear only as:

- solemn;

## 👀 Spectatorship Becoming Participation  

A person may begin as a spectator.

They:

- watch;
- film;
- laugh;
- repeat a line;
- send a clip.

At some point, spectatorship may become participation.

```text
watching
        ↓
recognising
        ↓
recording or sharing
        ↓
joining the public language
```

The threshold is not always clear.

A person filming may be:

- a participant;
- a journalist;
- an observer;
- a hostile recorder;
- all of these at different moments.

The archive should not infer full movement membership from spectatorship alone.

It should recognise that replay can turn observation into circulation.

## 🎥 While We Watched  

Filming changes the event.

Participants may perform partly for:

- the immediate crowd;
- later viewers;
- journalists;
- absent supporters;
- institutions expected to see the footage.

A chant is no longer only a sound in one place.

It becomes:

- a clip;
- a caption;
- an archive;
- a remix source;
- a future reference.

```text
live performance
        ↓
recorded object
        ↓
discoverability and replay
        ↓
new audience and new interpretation
```

Replay can:

- extend participation;
- preserve memory;
- create evidentiary cover;
- expose participants.

The camera increases both reach and risk.

It can extend the chorus while isolating one face from it.

A clip may remain politically active through:

- mirrors;
- screenshots;
- private forwarding;
- cross-platform reposting;
- multilingual captioning;

even after the original becomes difficult to find.

The archive should still distinguish:

```text
original removed
≠ original downranked
≠ searchability reduced
≠ clip encountered less often
```

Discoverability affects who can enter the soundtrack.

Retention affects who can remain after being identified through it.

## 😂 When Mocking The Leader Becomes Normal  

Authority often depends partly on emotional control.

The official decides:

- what is serious;
- what is respectful;
- when the public should listen;
- which tone is legitimate.

Mockery interrupts that control.

Once ridicule becomes socially normal:

- solemnity is no longer guaranteed;
- official language can be answered immediately;
- fear may decline;
- participation may feel less exceptional.

```text
untouchable authority
        ↓
repeatable joke
        ↓
reduced emotional distance
```

This does not prove:

- institutional power has disappeared;
- public mockery is representative;
- ridicule produces reform.

It changes the emotional terms on which power is encountered.

## 🌐 Translation, Rhythm And Loss  

A slogan may travel across languages while losing:

- rhyme;
- metre;
- double meaning;
- political inheritance;
- emotional register.

For example:

> **Inquilab Zindabad**

can be translated as:

> *Long live the revolution.*

The translation preserves the basic meaning.

It does not fully preserve:

- historical resonance;
- sound;
- inherited political use;
- crowd cadence.

The archive should retain:

- original language;
- script;
- transliteration;
- literal translation;
- idiomatic explanation.

Where a song or chant is only partially recorded, the archive should not reconstruct missing lyrics as fact.

## 🎼 Unofficial Songs And Participatory Authorship  

The movement reportedly produced multiple Hindi and regional-language songs.

That suggests participatory authorship rather than one central anthem process.

The widely reported line:

> **Unhone humein cockroach kaha, par cockroach kabhi marte nahi**  
> *They called us cockroaches, but cockroaches never die.*

connects:

- insult;
- survival;
- repetition.

Other reported English-language poetic formulations included ideas such as:

- being knocked down and rising again;
- cockroaches as children of a burning city.

These belong to:

- song;
- poetry;
- movement culture.

They should not automatically be classified as:

- official chants;
- organiser-approved slogans;
- language repeated by all participants.

Multiple songs indicate a cultural field.

They do not establish one authoritative anthem.

## 📜 Copyright, Quotation And Attribution Care  

Songs and film material require care.

The archive should:

- quote only what is necessary;
- identify the creator where known;
- distinguish reported excerpts from verified full lyrics;
- preserve language and context;
- avoid reproducing complete copyrighted songs without permission.

Where the source provides only a short reported line, the archive should not silently expand it.

```text
reported lyric fragment
≠ verified complete song
```

Cultural analysis can proceed through:

- description;
- limited quotation;
- contextual interpretation.

## 🧾 Consent And Identifiable Performance Footage  

Performance footage may expose:

- faces;
- voices;
- clothing;
- location;
- associates;
- political affiliation.

Archiving should consider:

- whether the footage was intended for broad circulation;
- whether republishing increases risk;
- whether minors appear;
- whether usernames or metadata should be removed;
- whether the same analytical point can be made without identification.

```text
publicly posted
≠ consequence-free to reproduce
```

The crowd may create collective cover in person.

A close-up recording can remove that cover later.

## ⚠️ Culture Is Evidence, Not A Single Cause  

Songs, chants and memes help explain:

- emotional tone;
- public recognition;
- participation;
- circulation;
- memory.

They do not prove by themselves:

- crowd size;
- representativeness;
- organisation;
- public opinion;
- causal effect on government.

The careful hierarchy is:

```text
cultural object observed
        ↓
circulation or use established
        ↓
participant meaning may be inferred cautiously
        ↓
organisational or political effect requires further evidence
```

Culture should neither be:

- dismissed as superficial;
- inflated into a complete causal theory.

## 🪜 Evidence And Provenance  

For each chant, song or performance object, preserve:

- date;
- location;
- platform;
- performer or creator where known;
- original language;
- script;
- transliteration;
- translation;
- whether heard live or reported;
- whether official, authorised, participant-generated or retrospective;
- whether the original remains available;
- whether a copy is a screenshot, mirror, edit or re-encode;
- whether preservation would increase participant exposure;
- confidence.

Distinguish:

```text
heard once
≠ routinely used

reported lyric
≠ complete verified song

placard text
≠ chant

online branding
≠ street language
```

## 🔬 Disproof Conditions  

The analysis should change if evidence shows that:

- music and chants were peripheral to mobilisation;
- widely circulated footage came from few events;
- the unofficial anthem was mainly retrospective;
- reported slogan wording was inaccurate;
- the four-slogan restriction was not implemented or was misreported;
- later remixes were produced mainly by external observers;
- participants did not experience humour as emotionally useful;
- cultural material travelled without meaningful connection to movement activity.

These findings would narrow the claim.

They would not make the soundtrack irrelevant as cultural evidence.

## 💫 Compact Finding  

The pressure valve had a soundtrack.

Humour, rhythm and collective performance did not replace grievance or organisation. They helped participants carry pressure, synchronise, remember and remain present. Songs and chants turned private recognition into shared timing, while filming and replay carried the public beyond the immediate demonstration.

The movement’s cultural language was plural: core slogans, authorised chants, placards, unofficial songs and meme-era performance should remain distinct. Together, they made the public audible to itself and harder for official narration to contain.

That language did not arrive without history. The internet circulated and recombined a political imagination cultivated through decades of cinema, literature, theatre, song and public memory. A short chant could therefore carry national belonging, revolutionary legitimacy, anti-caste justice and immediate accountability at once.
