# 👁️ Capability Is Not The Same As Use  
**First created:** 2026-07-31 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-31  
*How to distinguish technical possibility from access, integration, deployment, demonstrated case-specific use, attribution and causation—while still reporting the structural risks created by procurement and uncertainty.*  

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

Technology reporting often jumps too quickly from:

> This tool can do something.

to:

> This institution used it here.

That leap is analytically dangerous.

The core distinction is:

```text
capability
≠ access
≠ implementation
≠ deployment
≠ demonstrated use
≠ attribution
≠ causation
```

Each step requires different evidence.

A government, university, police force, platform or contractor may:

- know that a capability exists;
- purchase access;
- license software;
- integrate it into a system;
- deploy it operationally;
- use it in a particular case;
- contribute to a particular outcome.

These are not one claim.

The central transferable question is:

> What exact level of the capability ladder is supported, what remains unproved, and how can the structural risk be reported without converting possibility into allegation?

---

## 🧾 The Capability Ladder  

The ladder has seven distinct levels.

### 1. Capability  

The technology can perform a function.

### 2. Access  

An actor can obtain or use the technology.

### 3. Implementation  

The technology has been integrated into the actor’s systems or workflow.

### 4. Deployment  

The implemented system has been placed into operational use.

### 5. Demonstrated Use  

Evidence shows the tool was used in a relevant operational setting.

### 6. Case-Specific Attribution  

Evidence links the tool or actor to the event being investigated.

### 7. Causal Contribution  

Evidence supports a claim that the use materially contributed to the outcome.

The ladder is cumulative.

Evidence at a lower level does not automatically establish the levels above it.

---

## 🧠 Why The Distinction Matters  

Collapsing the ladder can cause:

- false accusation;
- weak legal analysis;
- misleading headlines;
- dismissal of a real structural pattern;
- source exposure;
- vendor misidentification;
- confusion between fear and demonstrated action.

It may also help powerful institutions escape scrutiny.

Once one overstated allegation is corrected, they may imply that:

- no capability existed;
- no access existed;
- no chilling effect existed;
- no procurement risk existed;
- no oversight question remains.

That is also wrong.

```text
specific allegation unproved
        ≠
structural concern disproved
```

Precision protects the wider analysis.

---

## 💻 Capability Evidence  

Capability evidence asks:

> What can the technology do?

Possible evidence includes:

- official technical documentation;
- research papers;
- patents;
- product manuals;
- supplier demonstrations;
- engineering specifications;
- verified prior deployments;
- independent technical testing.

Capability evidence may establish that a system can:

- identify faces;
- classify text;
- correlate records;
- score risk;
- monitor networks;
- infer relationships;
- filter content;
- generate alerts.

It does not establish:

- that a particular institution bought it;
- that it was integrated;
- that it was active;
- that it was used against a named person.

Marketing language should be treated cautiously.

A supplier’s claim that a product can perform a function is not the same as independent proof that it performs reliably in real conditions.

---

## 💰 Procurement Creates Access  

Procurement evidence may include:

- contract award;
- framework agreement;
- purchase order;
- licence;
- grant;
- subscription;
- memorandum of understanding;
- reseller relationship;
- budget line;
- tender result.

Procurement may establish access.

It may not establish implementation.

```text
purchase
        ≠
integration

licence
        ≠
active operational use
```

The institution may have:

- bought a pilot;
- retained dormant licences;
- purchased a bundle;
- cancelled integration;
- used the product in another department;
- used only one feature.

The contract must be read for:

- scope;
- dates;
- users;
- modules;
- jurisdiction;
- implementation obligations;
- maintenance;
- training;
- termination.

---

## 🧩 Technology Ownership Matters  

Ownership and control may be divided.

Relevant actors may include:

- developer;
- intellectual-property owner;
- reseller;
- prime contractor;
- cloud provider;
- systems integrator;
- public authority;
- local operator.

Ask:

- Who owns the code?
- Who controls updates?
- Who holds the data?
- Who configures the system?
- Who can view outputs?
- Who can suspend access?
- Who audits use?

```text
product owner
≠ system operator
≠ data controller
≠ decision-maker
```

These distinctions matter for both attribution and remedy.

---

## 🔑 Licences, Maintenance And Training  

A tool may exist inside an institution without being operationally meaningful.

Implementation may depend on:

- active licences;
- specialist staff;
- maintenance;
- updates;
- API access;
- data pipelines;
- hardware;
- training;
- policy approval;
- user permissions;
- legal authority.

Evidence that an institution once purchased a tool may become stale.

Ask:

- Was the licence active at the relevant time?
- Was the system maintained?
- Were staff trained?
- Was the relevant module enabled?
- Was the necessary data available?
- Did policy permit its use?

Capability and access can decay.

---

## 🧑‍💻 Implementation Requires Integration  

Implementation evidence asks:

> Was the technology connected to the institution’s actual systems and processes?

Possible evidence includes:

- integration contracts;
- technical architecture;
- data-flow diagrams;
- implementation reports;
- user manuals;
- staff training;
- change records;
- internal policy;
- audit documentation;
- testing records.

Implementation may involve:

- connecting databases;
- defining thresholds;
- assigning users;
- configuring alerts;
- creating review workflows;
- integrating outputs into decisions.

A system can be technically installed but institutionally marginal.

Ask whether outputs affected:

- investigation;
- moderation;
- eligibility;
- risk assessment;
- allocation;
- enforcement.

---

## 🧪 Pilot, Trial And Production Are Different  

A technology may be present as:

- demonstration;
- sandbox;
- pilot;
- limited trial;
- test environment;
- production system.

These stages should not be collapsed.

```text
pilot
≠ routine deployment

test data
≠ operational data

demonstration
≠ case use
```

A pilot may still create risk.

But the headline should reflect the stage accurately.

---

## 👁️ Deployment Requires Evidence  

Deployment means the system was placed into operational use.

Evidence may include:

- operational policy;
- live user accounts;
- production logs;
- procurement milestones;
- staff instructions;
- performance reports;
- incident records;
- audit findings;
- official admission.

Deployment evidence should identify:

- department;
- location;
- time period;
- user group;
- purpose;
- limits.

A technology may be deployed for one purpose and prohibited for another.

Example:

```text
deployed for fraud detection
        ≠
used for political monitoring
```

Purpose matters.

---

## 🎯 Demonstrated Use Requires A Relevant Record  

Demonstrated use asks:

> Is there evidence that the tool was actually used in a relevant operation?

Possible evidence includes:

- audit logs;
- access records;
- query history;
- case notes;
- operator testimony;
- disclosed screenshots;
- internal emails;
- official findings;
- court records;
- confirmed output.

The record should connect:

- user;
- time;
- system;
- action;
- operational purpose.

Without that connection, the correct claim may remain:

> The institution had access to the capability.

Not:

> The institution used it in this case.

---

## 🪪 Case-Specific Attribution  

Case-specific attribution requires evidence linking the demonstrated use to:

- a person;
- group;
- protest;
- investigation;
- decision;
- outcome.

Relevant questions include:

- Was the person’s data queried?
- Was an alert generated?
- Did an operator review it?
- Did the output enter a case file?
- Did a decision-maker rely on it?
- Is the identifier accurate?
- Could another system have produced the same result?

Similar outcomes do not establish the same tool.

```text
same pattern
        ≠
same supplier

same result
        ≠
same mechanism
```

---

## 🔗 Attribution Is Not Causation  

Even where case-specific use is established, causation remains separate.

A tool may have:

- informed;
- contributed;
- accelerated;
- prioritised;
- corroborated;
- duplicated;
- been ignored.

To claim causation, ask:

- Did the output change the decision?
- Would the outcome likely have occurred without it?
- What human review followed?
- What other evidence existed?
- Was the system decisive or peripheral?
- Did the institution rely on it formally or informally?

```text
used in case
        ≠
caused outcome
```

Causal wording should reflect contribution strength.

---

## 🧰 Evidence By Level  

| Level | Core Question | Useful Evidence | What It Does Not Prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capability | Can the tool do this? | Technical documents, testing | Access or use |
| Access | Could the actor obtain it? | Contracts, licences | Integration |
| Implementation | Was it integrated? | Architecture, training, policy | Live operation |
| Deployment | Was it operational? | Logs, instructions, reports | Case-specific use |
| Demonstrated use | Was it used in relevant operations? | Query logs, case notes | Use against this subject |
| Attribution | Was it used in this case? | Identifiers, records, testimony | Causal effect |
| Causation | Did it materially affect the outcome? | Decision record, counterfactual evidence | Sole cause |

The table is a reporting aid.

It is not a mechanical proof system.

Evidence quality still matters.

---

## 📣 Marketing Claims Are Not Deployment Records  

Suppliers may market systems through:

- hypothetical scenarios;
- selected demonstrations;
- broad capability language;
- client logos;
- case studies from other jurisdictions.

A client logo may establish a relationship.

It does not establish:

- which product;
- which module;
- which department;
- which dates;
- which purpose;
- which case.

A prior use elsewhere may support plausibility.

It does not prove present use.

```text
previous deployment
        ≠
current case attribution
```

---

## 🌍 Similar Outcomes Do Not Prove The Same Tool  

Institutions may produce similar outcomes through:

- manual review;
- shared databases;
- different vendors;
- local software;
- human reporting;
- policy rules;
- ordinary bureaucracy.

For example, a person may experience:

- increased scrutiny;
- content removal;
- risk labelling;
- repeated questioning.

Those effects do not identify the system that caused them.

The outcome can still be real.

The mechanism may remain unresolved.

This is why analysts should preserve:

```text
documented effect
        +
unresolved mechanism
```

rather than forcing a tool attribution.

---

## ➖ Negative Evidence And Its Limits  

Negative evidence may include:

- no matching contract found;
- official denial;
- no log produced;
- audit found no use;
- system not active in that period;
- module unavailable.

Negative evidence can lower confidence.

It may not settle the question because:

- records may be incomplete;
- subcontracting may obscure access;
- retention periods may have expired;
- the search may use the wrong product name;
- classified or confidential systems may not appear publicly;
- use may have occurred through another tool.

Keep separate:

```text
no evidence found
≠ evidence of no use
```

But do not reverse the burden:

```text
records could be hidden
≠ use should be presumed
```

The correct result may remain unresolved.

---

## 🧊 Uncertainty Can Still Chill  

A person does not need proof of surveillance to change behaviour.

Uncertainty may affect:

- speech;
- association;
- protest;
- study;
- professional activity;
- online participation.

The chilling mechanism may be:

```text
known capability
        +
uncertain boundaries
        +
high future dependency
        ↓
self-limitation
```

This is a real structural effect.

It should be reported separately from claims of demonstrated use.

A defensible statement may be:

> The institution’s access to poorly bounded monitoring capabilities may create a chilling effect even where case-specific use has not been established.

---

## 🏷️ “Capability, Not Allegation”  

The correct frame may be:

> This section documents capability and access. It does not allege demonstrated use in the present case.

Use this when:

- procurement is documented;
- technical ability is relevant;
- integration or deployment is unresolved;
- the structural risk matters;
- a direct accusation would outrun evidence.

This frame is not a retreat.

It is a precise description of the evidentiary level.

It allows the analysis to examine:

- safeguards;
- oversight;
- chilling effect;
- procurement dependency;
- future risk.

---

## ✍️ How To Write Cautiously Without Becoming Useless  

Weak caution:

> Something may possibly have happened.

Useful caution:

> The authority purchased access to the platform in 2025. The available documents do not establish whether the relevant module was integrated or used in this case.

Good cautious writing identifies:

- what is known;
- what level it reaches;
- what is missing;
- why the missing evidence matters.

Use verbs deliberately:

### Capability  

- can;
- is designed to;
- is marketed as able to.

### Access  

- purchased;
- licensed;
- contracted for;
- obtained access to.

### Implementation  

- integrated;
- configured;
- connected;
- trained staff to use.

### Deployment  

- placed into operational use;
- activated;
- used by the department.

### Attribution  

- records show use in;
- logs link the system to;
- the inquiry found that.

### Causation  

- contributed to;
- materially informed;
- was a decisive factor in.

## 🛠️ Correcting Overclaiming  

When a specific allegation is too strong:

1. identify the unsupported step;
2. return to the highest supported level;
3. correct the headline and metadata;
4. preserve the structural evidence;
5. explain what remains unresolved;
6. state what would be needed to advance the claim.

Example:

```text
unsupported:
supplier X targeted the protesters

supported:
the authority held a contract granting access
to capabilities relevant to protest monitoring

unresolved:
whether the system was integrated,
deployed or used in the case
```

The correction should not erase:

- the contract;
- the capability;
- the oversight failure;
- the chilling risk.

---

## 🧭 Structural Argument Without False Attribution  

A structural argument may remain strong even when the named tool is wrong.

The analyst may still show:

- broad procurement;
- weak oversight;
- opaque supply chains;
- poor audit;
- insufficient safeguards;
- high dependence;
- unclear appeal;
- anticipatory chilling.

```text
specific supplier attribution corrected
        ≠
procurement structure disappears
```

Precision prevents one error from becoming an excuse to dismiss the whole system.

## 🚨 Why This Matters In Surveillance Reporting  

Surveillance reporting is especially vulnerable because:

- tools are opaque;
- vendors over-market;
- governments use secrecy;
- effects may be indirect;
- multiple systems overlap;
- records may be unavailable;
- fear itself alters behaviour.

The capability ladder prevents:

- sensationalism;
- vendor-by-association accusations;
- unsupported claims about named individuals;
- confusion between lawful capability and actual misconduct.

It also prevents excessive deference.

Documented capability and access may justify scrutiny even before misuse is proved.

---

## 👮 Why This Matters In Policing  

Policing systems may include:

- facial recognition;
- risk scoring;
- intelligence databases;
- social-media monitoring;
- link analysis;
- automated triage.

The reporting questions remain:

- Was the system procured?
- Was it integrated?
- Which unit used it?
- Under what policy?
- Was the subject queried?
- Did it affect an operational decision?
- What review existed?

A force-wide capability does not prove use by every unit.

A manual decision does not become automated merely because software existed.

---

## 📱 Why This Matters In Platform Reporting  

Platforms possess capabilities to:

- rank;
- recommend;
- moderate;
- identify networks;
- limit reach;
- suspend accounts.

A harmful outcome may result from:

- policy;
- automation;
- human moderation;
- reporting campaigns;
- technical error;
- ordinary ranking changes.

Keep separate:

```text
platform capability
≠ deliberate targeting

reduced reach
≠ proven suppression

similar content outcome
≠ same moderation mechanism
```

The experience may still require investigation.

The mechanism should not be guessed.

---

## 🏛️ Why This Matters In Procurement Reporting  

Procurement reporting often reveals:

- contracts;
- relationships;
- capabilities;
- dependencies.

It may not reveal:

- activation;
- user behaviour;
- case-specific queries;
- causal decisions.

This makes procurement reporting valuable but bounded.

It can establish:

- opportunity;
- access;
- institutional choice;
- oversight obligation.

It cannot automatically establish use.

## 🧪 Rival Explanations  

Where a suspected technical system appears to explain an outcome, alternatives may include:

- human reporting;
- ordinary administrative process;
- another vendor;
- local software;
- manual moderation;
- unrelated platform change;
- coincidence;
- institutional policy;
- operator error.

A strong analysis should record these alternatives.

The mechanism-level claim should survive them:

> The capability ladder is necessary because similar outcomes can arise through different technical and human pathways.

## 🌉 What Transfers And What Does Not  

### What may transfer  

- capability and use are distinct;
- procurement usually establishes access, not deployment;
- implementation requires integration;
- licences, maintenance and training matter;
- prior use elsewhere does not prove present use;
- similar outcomes do not identify a supplier;
- uncertainty can create chilling effects;
- overclaiming can be corrected without abandoning structural analysis.

### What may not transfer cleanly  

- exact CJP technical allegations;
- exact suppliers;
- exact contracts;
- exact product modules;
- exact data flows;
- exact operational use;
- exact causal role.

The transferable mechanism is evidentiary discipline.

The case-specific allegation remains elsewhere.

## 🧪 Transfer Test  

Before applying this finding elsewhere, ask:

1. What capability is documented?
2. Who had access?
3. Was the relevant licence active?
4. What implementation evidence exists?
5. Was the system deployed operationally?
6. What demonstrates use in a relevant context?
7. What links it to the specific case?
8. What shows causal contribution?
9. What rival mechanisms remain?
10. What negative evidence exists?
11. Could uncertainty itself create harm?
12. What evidence would move the claim one level higher?

## 🧷 Compact Finding  

Capability is not use.

The analyst must move step by step through:

- capability;
- access;
- implementation;
- deployment;
- demonstrated use;
- case-specific attribution;
- causation.

A lower rung may still justify scrutiny.

It does not justify pretending the higher rung has been reached.

The strongest reporting says exactly what is documented, exactly what remains possible and exactly what evidence would be needed next.
