# 🏛️ Centralised Power Can Hide Behind Fragmented Delivery  
**First created:** 2026-07-31 | **Last updated:** 2026-07-31  
*How institutions retain central authority while dispersing delivery across contractors, software suppliers, operators and regulators—and how to stop administrative complexity from becoming an accountability shield.*  

---

## 🛰️ Orientation  

A system may have one powerful centre and many operational hands.

The centre may decide:

- the policy;
- the eligibility rule;
- the examination standard;
- the enforcement threshold;
- the procurement model;
- the appeal route;
- the acceptable level of risk.

Delivery may then be divided among:

- prime contractors;
- subcontractors;
- software suppliers;
- data processors;
- local operators;
- call centres;
- testing bodies;
- regulators;
- consultants;
- public agencies.

When the system works, centralisation may be presented as:

- consistency;
- scale;
- efficiency;
- national coordination.

When it fails, delivery may suddenly be described as fragmented.

The central transferable question is:

> Who held the authority to design and continue the system, who performed each operational function, and who remained responsible when fragmented delivery caused public harm?

Keep separate:

```text
authority
≠ delivery
≠ ownership
≠ operational control
≠ legal liability
≠ political responsibility
```

Fragmentation may divide tasks.

It does not automatically divide responsibility.

---

## 🏛️ Centralised Authority  

Centralised authority may determine:

- what the system is for;
- who must use it;
- which standards apply;
- what counts as compliance;
- which evidence is accepted;
- how errors are challenged;
- which contractor is appointed;
- whether the system continues after warning signs.

This authority may belong to:

- a ministry;
- regulator;
- examination body;
- university;
- employer;
- police force;
- health system;
- welfare authority;
- central procurement body.

The institution may not write every line of code or operate every call centre.

It may still control the conditions under which those systems exist.

```text
did not perform every task
        ≠
did not govern the system
```

The first responsibility question is:

> Who had the power to authorise, alter, suspend or replace the arrangement?

---

## 📦 Fragmented Delivery  

Operational delivery may be dispersed through a chain.

```text
public authority
        ↓
prime contractor
        ↓
subcontractor
        ↓
software or data supplier
        ↓
local operator
        ↓
affected person
```

Each layer may control only part of the process.

One organisation may:

- design the portal.

Another may:

- host the data.

Another may:

- administer the examination.

Another may:

- operate identity verification.

Another may:

- handle appeals.

Another may:

- investigate complaints.

This fragmentation can create genuine technical complexity.

It can also make it difficult for the affected person to find anyone able and willing to repair the whole failure.

---

## 🕳️ The Hollow Centre  

A hollow centre appears when an institution retains power but lacks enough internal capability to understand or control delivery.

The centre may still:

- set rules;
- compel participation;
- impose deadlines;
- defend outcomes;
- reject appeals.

But it may be unable to explain:

- how a decision was produced;
- which supplier caused an error;
- who can reverse it;
- whether the system was tested;
- how data moved;
- what contingency existed.

```text
central authority
        +
outsourced understanding
        =
hollow centre
```

The institution remains powerful toward the public.

It becomes weak toward its own suppliers.

That asymmetry matters.

---

## ⚖️ Strict Compliance Downward, Weak Accountability Upward  

Fragmented systems often impose precise obligations on the public.

The affected person may face:

- exact deadlines;
- strict identification rules;
- narrow appeal windows;
- automatic exclusion;
- proof requirements;
- penalties for minor mistakes.

The delivery chain may face:

- unclear ownership;
- delayed investigation;
- contractual ambiguity;
- blame transfer;
- no single repair route.

```text
strict individual compliance
        versus
diffuse institutional accountability
```

This contradiction can become a legitimacy rupture.

The system demands reliability from people while tolerating uncertainty about its own operation.

---

## 🔗 Every Handoff Is A Point Of Failure  

Each handoff may create risk.

Possible failures include:

- incomplete specifications;
- incompatible systems;
- corrupted data;
- delayed updates;
- unclear escalation;
- loss of context;
- duplicated records;
- gaps in testing;
- inaccessible interfaces;
- unresolved ownership.

A handoff may occur between:

- policy and procurement;
- contractor and subcontractor;
- software and operator;
- central body and local office;
- automated result and human review;
- complaint and remedy.

The relevant map is not only organisational.

It is functional.

Ask:

- What information crossed the boundary?
- Who checked it?
- Who could correct it?
- What happened when the next actor rejected responsibility?

---

## 🌐 One Gateway Can Create Systemic Scale  

A central gateway can make access easier.

It can also convert one design failure into a system-wide problem.

A gateway may control access to:

- examinations;
- healthcare;
- employment;
- public benefits;
- immigration;
- professional registration;
- housing;
- legal status.

```text
single gateway
        +
large dependent population
        =
systemic failure potential
```

Centralisation amplifies:

- efficiency when the system works;
- exclusion when it fails;
- the consequences of incorrect data;
- dependence on one supplier or architecture.

The scale of the gateway should increase the standard of:

- testing;
- redundancy;
- appeal;
- audit;
- remedy.

It should not be used to justify fewer human routes.

---

## 🧑‍💻 Technology Is Not Institutional Competence  

An institution may purchase:

- software;
- automation;
- analytics;
- identity systems;
- scoring tools;
- monitoring platforms.

That does not prove it possesses:

- technical understanding;
- integration capacity;
- testing capacity;
- independent oversight;
- contingency planning;
- meaningful human review.

```text
technology acquisition
≠ institutional competence
```

Technology can become a substitute for rebuilding weakened administrative capability.

The institution may then depend on suppliers to explain:

- what the system does;
- whether it failed;
- how it should be repaired.

A public authority should not outsource the ability to understand its own exercise of power.

---

## 🧾 Procurement Does Not End At Contract Award  

The responsibility chain continues after purchase.

Relevant questions include:

- Who specified the requirement?
- Who evaluated bids?
- Who assessed risk?
- Who tested integration?
- Who approved launch?
- Who monitored performance?
- Who received incident reports?
- Who renewed the contract?
- Who decided not to suspend the system?
- Who designed the remedy?

A contract is not a transfer of political responsibility.

It is one instrument through which authority is exercised.

```text
procurement
        ≠
delegation of public accountability
```

---

## 🏢 Prime Contractors And Subcontractors  

A prime contractor may hold the formal relationship with the institution while relying on other suppliers for:

- software;
- cloud hosting;
- identity verification;
- support;
- security;
- data processing;
- specialist assessment.

Subcontracting can provide expertise.

It can also create:

- opaque dependencies;
- diluted oversight;
- unclear incident ownership;
- contractual gaps;
- weak public visibility.

The institution should know:

- which subcontractors perform critical functions;
- where data travels;
- what happens if one supplier fails;
- whether substitutions require approval;
- how audit rights pass down the chain.

The public should not need to reconstruct the supply chain before receiving a remedy.

---

## 🧱 The Contractor-Blame Reflex  

When failure becomes public, the institution may say:

- the contractor made an error;
- the supplier misunderstood the specification;
- the operator failed to follow procedure;
- the problem was technical;
- the incident was isolated.

These statements may be true.

They do not settle responsibility.

The institution may still have:

- chosen the supplier;
- written the specification;
- accepted the risk;
- failed to monitor;
- ignored warnings;
- continued the system;
- designed an inadequate appeal route.

```text
contractor fault
        can coexist with
institutional responsibility
```

A complete account should identify both.

---

## ⚖️ Non-Delegable Responsibility  

Some responsibilities should remain with the authority even when delivery is outsourced.

These may include responsibility for:

- lawful decision-making;
- equality;
- accessibility;
- safeguarding;
- data protection;
- procedural fairness;
- effective appeal;
- remedy;
- continuity of essential service;
- public explanation.

Non-delegable responsibility means:

> The institution may purchase assistance, but it cannot purchase an escape from accountability.

This does not make the institution legally liable for every contractor act in every jurisdiction.

It means the governance analysis should ask which duties remain attached to the public power being exercised.

---

## 🧮 Six Different Responsibility Questions  

A useful analysis separates six questions.

### 1. Authority  

Who had the power to set the rule?

### 2. Delivery  

Who performed the relevant task?

### 3. Ownership  

Who owned the system, data, code or asset?

### 4. Operational Control  

Who could alter, stop or correct the process at the relevant time?

### 5. Liability  

Who may bear contractual, regulatory or legal responsibility?

### 6. Political Responsibility  

Who must answer for imposing the system on the public?

These answers may identify different actors.

They should not be collapsed into one vague question:

> Who was responsible?

---

## 🧭 The Responsibility Matrix  

A practical matrix may look like:

| Function | Actor | Power Held | Evidence | Failure | Repair Authority |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Policy design | Ministry | Sets rules | Policy documents | Unfair threshold | Minister or department |
| Procurement | Central body | Selects supplier | Tender and contract | Weak specification | Contracting authority |
| Software | Supplier | Builds system | Technical records | Defect or limitation | Supplier under contract |
| Operation | Local body | Applies process | Logs and manuals | Human or procedural error | Operator and authority |
| Oversight | Regulator | Audits compliance | Reports and notices | Missed warning | Regulator |
| Remedy | Public authority | Corrects public harm | Appeals and orders | Delay or refusal | Non-delegable owner |

The purpose is not to simplify the system falsely.

It is to prevent complexity from dissolving ownership.

## 🧾 Documentary Trails That Matter  

Important records may include:

- policy documents;
- ministerial directions;
- business cases;
- procurement notices;
- tender specifications;
- contracts;
- subcontractor lists;
- data-processing agreements;
- impact assessments;
- equality assessments;
- risk registers;
- testing reports;
- incident logs;
- service-level reports;
- audit findings;
- complaint records;
- change-control documents;
- renewal decisions;
- correspondence;
- board minutes.

Different records answer different questions.

```text
contract
        may show
formal allocation

incident log
        may show
operational knowledge

renewal record
        may show
continued institutional choice
```

The responsibility chain should be reconstructed from documents and function, not from branding.

## 🧪 Marketing Claims Are Not Governance Evidence  

Suppliers may describe products as:

- seamless;
- intelligent;
- accurate;
- secure;
- automated;
- transformative;
- compliant.

These claims do not establish:

- successful integration;
- lawful deployment;
- adequate testing;
- effective human review;
- case-specific use;
- absence of harm.

Similarly, an institution’s claim that it uses a “best-in-class” supplier does not establish institutional competence.

The evidentiary question is:

> What did the system actually do in this delivery environment?

---

## 🧯 Administrative Complexity Can Absorb Blame  

Complexity may create a loop:

```text
public authority:
ask the contractor

contractor:
ask the operator

operator:
the system generated it

supplier:
we followed the specification

regulator:
use the complaints process

complaints process:
no actor accepts the whole failure
```

This is administrative absorption.

The person’s effort is consumed by the system’s internal fragmentation.

The harm may continue even when every actor performs a narrow contractual role correctly.

That is why the whole system must be assessed.

---

## 👤 The Public Should Not Carry Integration Risk  

Where the institution requires use of a system, it should not make the affected person carry the cost of poor integration.

The public should not have to:

- identify the faulty vendor;
- reconcile conflicting databases;
- prove which handoff failed;
- repeat the same evidence to every actor;
- wait while contracts are interpreted;
- accept exclusion because responsibility is disputed.

```text
institution chose fragmented delivery
        ↓
institution retains integration responsibility
```

The authority may seek recovery from suppliers later.

The public remedy should not depend on that dispute finishing first.

---

## 🔄 Centralisation Amplifies Efficiency And Failure  

Centralisation is not inherently bad.

It may support:

- consistent standards;
- shared infrastructure;
- pooled expertise;
- lower duplication;
- national remedies.

But it also creates:

- systemic dependence;
- large-scale data errors;
- common points of failure;
- uniform exclusion;
- reduced local discretion;
- high switching costs.

A central system needs:

- redundancy;
- local escalation;
- human review;
- independent audit;
- clear suspension powers;
- contingency delivery.

The greater the reach, the less acceptable it is to treat failure as an isolated inconvenience.

---

## 🧽 Vendor Replacement Is Not Structural Repair  

Changing vendors may be necessary.

It may not repair:

- poor policy;
- unrealistic specification;
- weak oversight;
- fragmented appeals;
- inadequate internal expertise;
- excessive centralisation;
- missing remedies;
- coercive deadlines.

```text
new supplier
        ≠
new governance
```

If the institution retains the same:

- incentives;
- authority structure;
- procurement assumptions;
- accountability gaps;

the failure may recur through a different vendor.

The repair question is:

> What changed in the institution’s ability and duty to govern delivery?

---

## 🪜 Fragmentation Can Hide Unequal Harm  

A system-wide failure may not affect everyone equally.

The greatest harm may fall on people who have less:

- money;
- time;
- English fluency;
- digital access;
- legal knowledge;
- professional security;
- mobility;
- family support.

A fragmented appeal structure rewards people able to:

- contact several bodies;
- preserve records;
- understand contracts;
- wait;
- escalate publicly.

Equal access to the same broken process does not produce equal outcomes.

The responsibility map should therefore include:

- who absorbed delay;
- who lost access;
- who could appeal;
- who disappeared from the record.

---

## 🔍 How To Map Who Owns A Failure  

Use this sequence:

### Step 1: Identify The Public Decision  

What decision, exclusion or allocation affected the person?

### Step 2: Identify The Governing Rule  

Who authorised the standard or process?

### Step 3: Map The Delivery Chain  

Which actors performed each function?

### Step 4: Locate Operational Control  

Who could correct or suspend the process at the relevant time?

### Step 5: Trace Knowledge  

Who knew about the risk, and when?

### Step 6: Trace Continuation  

Who allowed the system to continue after warnings?

### Step 7: Locate Remedy  

Who had power to repair the public harm?

### Step 8: Separate Liability From Political Responsibility  

A contractual dispute may continue.

The public authority still has to answer for the system it imposed.

## 🪳 Grounding In The Cockroach Case  

The Cockroach case supports examination of a broader institutional pattern:

- high-stakes central rules;
- delivery through multiple administrative and technical actors;
- strict consequences for students;
- uncertainty about where operational failure sat;
- public difficulty locating complete responsibility;
- pressure for visible accountability and structural correction.

The case-derived inference is not:

> Every identified contractor caused the harm.

It is:

> A centrally governed high-stakes system can become difficult to challenge when delivery is fragmented and no actor accepts responsibility for the whole outcome.

Exact procurement relationships, supplier roles and product-specific claims remain in the case-specific nodes.

They should not be settled here.

## 🧪 Rival Explanations  

Fragmented delivery may not be the only explanation for institutional failure.

Other explanations may include:

- poor policy design;
- inadequate staffing;
- corruption;
- political interference;
- local incompetence;
- weak regulation;
- sudden scale;
- deliberate coercion;
- ordinary administrative error.

The mechanism-based claim is narrower:

> Fragmentation can make failure harder to understand, repair and attribute, especially where central authority remains strong toward the public.

That claim can coexist with other causes.

## 🌉 What Transfers And What Does Not  

### What may transfer  

- formal authority can remain central while delivery fragments;
- one gateway can create systemic scale;
- each handoff creates risk;
- technology does not substitute for institutional competence;
- contractor fault and institutional responsibility can coexist;
- public authorities retain integration and remedy responsibilities;
- complexity can absorb blame;
- changing vendors alone may not repair governance.

### What may not transfer cleanly  

- exact CJP institutional arrangements;
- exact contracts;
- exact suppliers;
- exact product capabilities;
- exact liability under a particular law;
- exact chronology of technical failure;
- exact responsibility of any named actor.

The transferable mechanism concerns the structure of authority and delivery.

The case-specific attribution remains elsewhere.

## 🧪 Transfer Test  

Before applying this finding elsewhere, ask:

1. Who set the governing rule?
2. Who compelled the public to use the system?
3. Who procured delivery?
4. Which actors performed each function?
5. Who owned the technology or data?
6. Who had operational control?
7. Who knew about failure?
8. Who could suspend or repair it?
9. Which duties remained non-delegable?
10. Did fragmentation delay remedy or dilute accountability?
11. Would changing the vendor alter the governing structure?
12. What evidence would falsify the responsibility map?

## 🚧 What This Node Does Not Claim  

This node does not claim that:

- outsourcing is inherently illegitimate;
- centralisation is always harmful;
- every contractor error creates public-authority liability;
- all fragmented systems are badly governed;
- every technology failure reflects deliberate policy;
- one actor must control every operational detail;
- local discretion always improves delivery;
- vendor replacement is never useful;
- every documentary trail will be public;
- exact supplier responsibility in the Cockroach case is settled here;
- complexity can always be reduced to one cause.

It does claim that:

- central authority and fragmented delivery can coexist;
- fragmentation can weaken accountability without weakening coercion toward the public;
- institutions need internal capability to govern what they purchase;
- contractor blame does not automatically remove institutional responsibility;
- critical public duties may remain non-delegable;
- affected people should not carry integration risk;
- responsibility mapping requires functional and documentary analysis;
- governance repair requires more than changing vendors.

## 🧷 Compact Finding  

Centralised power can hide behind fragmented delivery.

The institution may retain the power to:

- set the rule;
- compel use;
- impose consequences;
- continue the system;

while dispersing implementation across enough actors that nobody accepts the whole failure.

The reusable task is to map:

- authority;
- delivery;
- ownership;
- operational control;
- liability;
- political responsibility.

Complexity may explain how the failure happened.

It must not become a reason that nobody repairs it.
