**First created:** 2026-10-08 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-08  
*🍊☘️ Seas an Fód — Drumcree and Garvaghy Road: a dated record of law, policing, diplomacy, institutional handovers and the information moving between them.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The dispute over the Orange Order's return procession from Drumcree Church along Garvaghy Road is not one dispute heard by one institution. It is a series of connected decisions made by institutions with different powers, legal tests, records and responsibilities. The distinction is especially important in 2026, when an earlier Parades Commission determination was quashed for a procedural defect, a replacement determination permitted a tightly restricted procession, residents challenged that determination, the PSNI declined to force a passage through a large counter-protest, and ministers attempted negotiations without securing agreement.

This is the **master chronology** for the *Seas an Fód* cluster. Its job is to keep dates, people, authority, evidence and uncertainty in the same frame. Detailed legal argument belongs in the sibling nodes. A person changing office is not automatically evidence that the change caused an institutional decision; a court refusing to intervene is not automatically an instruction to police to use force; a diplomatic conversation is not automatically a formal treaty mechanism.

**Cut-off: 8 October 2026.** Sources were checked for this draft, but not every underlying record is public or has been obtained. The 5 October appellate judgment was **reserved** in the most recent verified court reporting used here; its subsequent publication requires checking. The original PSNI submission, the full August judgment, and the complete 1 October Policing Board video have **not** been reviewed for this node. Do not upgrade a reported claim to a judicial finding.

**Reading key:** **PRIMARY** = official statute, government, regulator or oversight record; **REPORTED** = attributed contemporary reporting; **OPEN** = documentary question not answered here; **DISPUTED** = competing claims, not resolved by this chronology. Sources are listed at the end, with stable labels [S01]–[S24].

## 1. 🗺️ Historical foundations: the road and the institutions

### 1.1 The route is not merely a line on a map

The annual Orange Order procession associated with Drumcree Church and its return through Garvaghy Road became a major point of conflict in Portadown during the 1990s. Procession participants have described the return route in terms of tradition and religious/cultural expression. Residents of the predominantly nationalist Garvaghy Road have described the march through their neighbourhood in the context of sectarian intimidation, community safety, unequal power and the memory of violent confrontations. Neither description can be substituted for the other's lived history; the state has legal responsibilities toward both.

**1995–1997:** A sequence of Drumcree confrontations, policing decisions, negotiations, protests and disorder made clear the limitations of treating contested parades as a purely police-operational problem. **1996** is particularly important for the police decision initially to restrict the route and the subsequent reversal amid serious disorder. **1998:** The murder of the three Quinn brothers in Ballymoney amid the wider parading crisis remains a grave historical reference point; it must be treated as the murder of children, not a decorative entry in a chronology. For the detailed day-by-day history, consult CAIN's contemporary archives [S04].

### 1.2 From police discretion to a separate regulator

- **January 1997 — North Review:** Proposed an independent framework for handling contentious public processions, including mediation and decisions informed by competing rights and community relations. Retrieve the original report before quoting its recommendations verbatim [S04].
- **February 1998 — Public Processions (Northern Ireland) Act 1998:** Created the statutory framework for the Parades Commission, including its determination functions [S01]. **It predates the Belfast/Good Friday Agreement.**
- **10 April 1998 — Belfast/Good Friday Agreement:** Established the three-strand political and constitutional architecture and the principle of consent concerning Northern Ireland's constitutional status. It did **not** create the Parades Commission [S02–S03].
- **1999–2001 — Policing reform:** The Patten Report, Police (Northern Ireland) Act 2000 and establishment of the PSNI reshaped police governance and accountability. This is a related but separate institutional history [S05].

### 1.3 The bilateral diplomatic foundations

- **15 November 1985 — Anglo-Irish Agreement:** Formalised British–Irish consultation while addressing constitutional consent [S06].
- **15 December 1993 — Downing Street Declaration:** A key step in the diplomatic pathway to inclusive negotiations [S07].
- **1998 — Belfast/Good Friday Agreement:** Strand One concerns Northern Ireland's devolved institutions; Strand Two North–South cooperation; Strand Three East–West relationships, including the **British–Irish Intergovernmental Conference**. The Conference is not the same institution as the British–Irish Council [S02].

**Interpretive boundary:** The Irish government has recognised treaty-based and diplomatic roles, but it does not command the PSNI or exercise the Commission's statutory powers. Nor is the existence of the BIIGC proof that it was convened over Drumcree in 2026.

## 3. 🔄 Personnel and sign-off chronology: the question that cannot be skipped

A cluster of changes across key posts may matter. But it needs to be **measured**, not narrated into existence. Record **announcement date**, **legal effective date**, **first day exercising responsibility**, **vacancy/interim period**, **appointment authority**, **stated reason**, and **pending decisions** separately. A ministerial reshuffle, statutory term expiry and police promotion can coincide without sharing a cause.

### 3.1 One confirmed and particularly relevant turnover signal

**2 September 2026 — PRIMARY:** Secretary of State **Chris Bryant** told Parliament that **one Parades Commissioner had recently resigned** and that **the remaining commissioners were approaching the end of their terms**. He said officials had been instructed to launch a public appointments process and characterised this as standard practice. The same statement said the existing commissioners retained his backing while a judge-led governance review proceeded. This is not merely a hypothetical turnover question: there was a documented membership transition issue at the regulator at the time of the litigation [S11].

**What the statement does not tell us:** The departing commissioner's identity; exact resignation date; date of the original appointment; whether the resignation related to the July/August proceedings; whether any commissioner was absent from particular approval stages; or whether the impending term expiries changed the September decision process. These must remain **OPEN**, not implied findings.

The statement also identified **Baroness Anderson** as the junior minister monitoring the review's progress and subsequent implementation. Record the exact portfolio and tenure before attributing any additional operational role to her [S11].

### 3.3 How to test whether turnover was unusual

1. Build the **January–October 2026** list of changes in genuinely relevant posts.
2. Compare the same nine-month period in **2021–2025**, separating statutory terms, reshuffles, promotions and diplomatic rotations.
3. Count changes by **effective date**, not press-announcement date; report 30-, 60- and 90-day clusters.
4. Identify which offices actually exchanged information or approved decisions.
5. Seek documented handover failures or changes in assessment before asserting institutional consequences.

**A temporal cluster is a finding to investigate, not proof of a coordinated personnel operation.** Equally, the Commission resignation and imminent term expiries are documented facts and should not be waved away as merely hypothetical.

## 4. 📅 January–July 2026: establish the pre-judgment baseline

**July 2026 — PRIMARY:** A Portadown Orange Lodge member sought judicial review of the Commission's determination restricting the return leg of the annual Drumcree parade along Garvaghy Road. The Secretary of State's later statement records the July proceedings and the August outcome [S11].

**OPEN — original regulatory record:** Retrieve the July notification, determination, exact conditions, final approved text, circulation history, commissioner attendance/approval records and legal papers. Determine which members held office at every approval stage. The available ministerial statement establishes a later procedural defect; it does not supply the full internal approval chronology.

**OPEN — personnel baseline:** Identify Commission membership on 1 January, on the date of the July decision, and immediately before the August hearing. Also obtain relevant NIO and police command lists, rather than starting the register after a dispute had already become public.

**Why this matters:** A resignation announced or noted in September might have been planned much earlier. Without the pre-August baseline, a researcher can accidentally manufacture causation from proximity.

## 5. ⚖️ 25 August–3 September: quashing and governance response

### 25 August — High Court quashes earlier determination **[PRIMARY: ministerial account]**

The High Court in Belfast quashed the earlier Parades Commission determination. The Commission did not contest the proceedings because of a procedural problem in how the decision was recorded: **not every commissioner had approved the final text**. This is the explanation formally given to Parliament on 2 September [S11]. Contemporary reporting identifies **Mr Justice McAlinden** in connection with the ruling [S17].

**Do not confuse procedural invalidity with the substantive conclusion that the route must be allowed.** The ruling removed the earlier decision; it did not itself direct police to facilitate an unrestricted march.

**Outstanding:** Complete judgment, court order, identity and approval status of commissioners, the Commission's own contemporaneous account, and any earlier warning of the defect.

### 1 September — Commissioners meet Secretary of State **[PRIMARY]**

Chris Bryant reported meeting commissioners on 1 September to discuss their response to the judgment [S11]. Establish attendance and whether written action points or revised approval procedures followed.

### 1–2 September — independent governance review announced and explained **[PRIMARY]**

The NIO commissioned a judge-led review into the Commission's decision-making procedures and organisational governance. The **2 September written statement** said the 1998 statutory framework itself was outside the review's scope, that Baroness Anderson would monitor progress, and that the existing commissioners retained ministerial backing. It also disclosed the commissioner resignation and approaching term expiries [S11].

**Journalistic hinge:** A governance review and a new public appointments process were being set in motion while the Commission continued exercising its statutory functions. What procedural assurance applied to any replacement determination in the meantime?

### 3 September — new notification **[PRIMARY: later Commission record]**

A new Portadown District LOL No. 1 notification concerned the **27 September** return procession. The official Commission record gives the proposed route and participant numbers [S08]. The precise notification submission date should be checked against the underlying form before final publication; **3 September is retained as a working chronology lead**, not independently established by the visible summary page.

## 6. 📜 23–25 September: replacement determination

### 23 September — consideration date **[PRIMARY]**

The Parades Commission's official record identifies **23 September** as the date on which the 27 September procession was to be considered [S08]. The reported appointment of **Sir John O'Hara KC** to lead the governance review also belongs in this period; obtain the dated NIO appointment release and terms of reference before treating the precise announcement date and review milestones as verified.

### 25 September — new determination **[PRIMARY]**

Official reference: **`PAR\111838`**; official parade database identifier: **96413**. Organisation: **Portadown District LOL No. 1**. The notification proposed **60 participants**, **two accordion bands**, a **13:00–14:30** return route including Garvaghy Road. The Commission recorded **conditions imposed**, including restrictions categorised as music, time, other and Code of Practice [S08]. Contemporary reporting describes the permitted version as **35 participants**, **no band**, **no supporters**, and a morning departure around **08:00** [S15–S16].

**Source distinction:** The online summary reliably shows the **proposed** route and the existence/categories of conditions. The precise **permitted** conditions and reasoning should be quoted from the linked determination PDF itself. Do not mistake proposed afternoon timings for permitted timings.

**OPEN:** Download and preserve the full determination; check all paragraphs, commissioner approval, police-evidence characterisation, community-relations reasoning, any mediation references, and exact time/banners/supporter provisions. Preserve its issue time as well as its date if available.

**Core question:** Did the Commission and PSNI assess the same facts differently, did the information change, or did the Commission misunderstand the advice? The answer requires the original submission, not merely later statements.

## 7. 🏛️ 26–27 September: emergency court sequence

### 26 September — interim High Court restraint **[REPORTED]**

Residents obtained a temporary injunction preventing the procession. Contemporary reporting records an urgent High Court hearing [S18]. Identify the precise order, parties, judge, reasons and operative terms from the judicial record.

### 26 September — Court of Appeal overturns interim order **[REPORTED]**

The Court of Appeal allowed an appeal against the temporary injunction. RTÉ reported Lady Chief Justice **Siobhan Keegan** saying the High Court judge had erred in law [S18]. **This did not settle every subsequent judicial-review question.**

### Early 27 September — judicial-review permission refused **[REPORTED]**

The residents were refused leave to seek judicial review in further urgent proceedings. That decision became the subject of the **5 October appeal** [S15–S16]. The High Court decisions are identified in the working source register as *MacCionnaith* **[2026] NIKB 55** and **[2026] NIKB 56** [S09–S10]; check exact case titles, judgments and order sequence directly before quoting their reasoning.

**Court-event discipline:** Interim injunction, appellate intervention, refusal of permission, and appeal against that refusal are **separate events**. A court's disposition of a legal challenge is not, without more, an operational order specifying the police tactics to be used on Garvaghy Road.

## 8. 🚨 27–28 September: the route, the protest and the police decision

### 27 September — large counter-protest prevents procession **[REPORTED; PSNI subsequent account PRIMARY]**

Residents and supporters assembled on Garvaghy Road and the notified procession did not proceed along the route. Public reporting described a large protest, including participation by prominent Sinn Féin politicians [S19]. Record attendance estimates as estimates attributed to their sources, not a verified census.

### 28 September — PSNI public account **[PRIMARY]**

Assistant Chief Constable **Ryan Henderson** said the police were balancing the human rights of all parties and that, despite the Commission's determination, the procession had not been facilitated because of an **unnotified and unlawful protest**. He said the police continued considering their response and would act appropriately and proportionately. He also said there had been **no violence or disorder on Garvaghy Road over the weekend** [S12].

**Distinguish:** The police description of the protest's legal status; the decision not to force clearance; the police assessment of proportionality; and the separate question of whether particular offences should subsequently be investigated. A determination permitting a procession does not itself authorise every possible use of force.

**OPEN:** Who signed off the operational plan? Who authored the advice to the Commission? Did the command chain change between submission and 27 September? What information was newly available? Which tactical alternatives were assessed? Was a request made under section 9 of the Public Processions Act?

## 12. ⚖️ 5 October: Court of Appeal hears residents' challenge

**5 October — REPORTED:** A three-judge Court of Appeal heard the residents' appeal against refusal of permission for judicial review. Counsel for the residents argued that the Commission had failed properly to weigh or understand police advice and had reached an irrational conclusion. Counsel for the Commission disputed that account and maintained that commissioners had been briefed about police resources and their ability to contain disorder [S15–S16].

Lady Chief Justice **Siobhan Keegan** said the court would need time to consider submissions. **Judgment was reserved**; no specific delivery date was given in the reporting checked for this draft [S15].

**This is an unresolved legal question in the record as of the verified reporting**, not a finding that police evidence was misrepresented. Check the Northern Ireland Judiciary publication database before any subsequent update.

**Further legal question:** The originally determined parade date had passed. Reporting indicates that the continuing legal significance of the determination was discussed. The final judgment, not an inference from the calendar, must settle what relief was available.

## 15. 🔬 Testing institutional continuity rather than assuming a plot

The **2 September parliamentary statement** is a useful anchor because it documents an actual commissioner resignation and impending end of other commissioners' terms. It also records a procedural approval failure, a governance review and an appointments process. These facts justify questions about continuity. They **do not** establish that the resignation caused the approval defect, that any appointment was politically engineered, or that unrelated posts changed in concert.

### Research tests

**A. Appointment timing.** Compare announced, effective and handover dates. Identify whether a commissioner resigned *before* or *after* the decision challenged in court.

**B. Approval chain.** Who saw the draft, who approved the final text, who certified that approval, and who published the determination? Did the process change before 25 September?

**C. Police handover.** Who prepared and approved police advice? Who received it? Who commanded on 27 September? Did any relevant personnel change between those stages?

**D. Diplomatic continuity.** Who represented each government during negotiations? What records of prior proposals passed to any new minister or official?

**E. Comparison baseline.** Count comparable office changes in equivalent periods from 2021–2025. An unusual cluster must be shown against an ordinary rate, not judged by how striking a chart looks.

**F. Evidence of causation.** Seek written reasons, handover failures, procedural findings, meeting minutes or official explanations linking a personnel transition to an institutional consequence. Dates alone cannot do that work.

The question is not simply **“Why did everyone change jobs?”** It is **“Which jobs actually changed, under whose authority, at what point in the decision chain, and with what demonstrable consequences?”**

## 18. 🧬 Information ecology: decisions survive their decision-makers

The dispute reveals at least **three clocks** running at once: the clock of statutory decisions, the clock of events on the road, and the clock of appointments and institutional handovers. A fourth clock belongs to courts and publication: a judgment can be reserved after the parade date has passed, and a public account of evidence can appear after operational decisions have already been made.

```text
NOTIFICATION ──► PSNI ADVICE ──► COMMISSION DETERMINATION
                       │                    │
             [Who prepared it?]     [Who approved final text?]
                       │                    │
                       └────────► COURT REVIEW
                                      │
                                      ▼
                            POLICE OPERATION
                            [What changed by then?]
                                      │
                    ┌─────────────────┼──────────────────┐
                    ▼                 ▼                  ▼
              POLICING BOARD     NEGOTIATIONS       FURTHER COURTS
                    │                 │                  │
                    └─────────────────┴──────────────────┘
                                      ▼
                            PUBLIC ACCOUNTABILITY

AT EACH TRANSITION:
Who held office? Who had authority? What was recorded?
What was known at that time? What changed? Who can answer now?
```

A regulator can make a lawful decision that police cannot safely implement by a particular tactic. A court can uphold a legal decision without choosing a policing plan. A foreign minister can discuss the dispute without acquiring domestic police powers. And an officeholder can leave without the institution's responsibilities disappearing. None of these distinctions makes accountability optional. They tell us **where to look for it**.
