# 🕊️ Российское антивоенное движение
**First created:** 2026-10-10 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-10  
*The Russian Anti-War Movement — opposition to Russia’s war against Ukraine, its competing political traditions, the risks of resistance and the information that disappears when disagreement becomes dangerous.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation — Apparently, Not Everybody Agreed to the War

The Russian government launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022, following Russia's aggression against Ukraine beginning in 2014. Russian people have also opposed that war. These statements belong in the same account without one cancelling the other.

Some protested in the streets. Some organised feminist and socialist networks. Some published investigations or helped people facing prosecution. Some left the country; some remained. Some criticised mobilisation without rejecting the invasion itself. Their positions, risks and political projects cannot be treated as interchangeable.

> The government would prefer the world to understand that Russia supports the war. This is administratively convenient. Unfortunately, Russia contains Russians. And Russians, like other populations, have a regrettable tendency to disagree with one another.

**The task is not to invent a secret majority or a unified movement. It is to establish who resisted, what they demanded, what happened to them, and what the evidence can actually tell us.** Ukrainian sovereignty is not a side issue in that inquiry.

**Evidence boundary (10 October 2026):** This node develops the agreed investigative framework and established historical context. It is **not** a newly completed primary-source audit. Named events, legal amendments, organisational statements and individual outcomes requiring original-document verification are identified as research tasks; no invented citations or case outcomes are supplied.

---

## 1. 🗓️ Before February 2022 — The War Did Not Begin There

The full-scale invasion began on 24 February 2022. Russia’s war against Ukraine began in 2014. Treating 2022 as the beginning of the entire conflict erases eight years of Ukrainian experience and the history of Russian opposition to the annexation of Crimea and intervention in Donbas. This investigation therefore begins with the political argument made in 2014: could Russian citizens reject a war presented to them as national restoration?

Peace marches in Moscow and elsewhere in 2014, statements from opposition figures, independent reporting on Russian military involvement and the response to the annexation of Crimea form a necessary documentary prehistory. The chronology needs the dates of each march, contemporary organisers’ statements, police estimates and independently reported attendance kept separate. Neither a large crowd nor a famous photograph proves the movement's social representativeness.

A further continuity question matters: which activists, media outlets and networks survived until 2022, which were already under pressure, and which positions changed? Political continuity should be demonstrated with records, not inferred from the recurrence of the word *peace*.

---

## 2. 🪧 February–March 2022 — The First Public Resistance

The invasion was followed by anti-war demonstrations in Russian cities and detentions reported by independent monitors. The first phase deserves a day-by-day chronology, not a montage of dramatic images. Establish the city, date, original footage or contemporary report, what protesters actually said, who documented detentions, and whether a later prosecution can be linked to the same event.

A detention total measures reported detentions under a particular monitoring method. It does not measure unique protesters, all people who attended, or everyone privately opposed to the invasion. Nor should every participant be retroactively enrolled in a unified organisation. Some actions were coordinated; others were spontaneous.

The visual record must be treated as evidence with provenance: original uploader, upload time, location clues, visible slogans and any reason to suspect an image has been reused. A placard reading **«Нет войне» — “No to war”** is strong evidence of the message on that placard, not of the political programme of everyone standing nearby.

---

## 3. 🕊️ Who Actually Makes Up the Movement?

There is no single membership roll. The relevant actors include independent demonstrators, feminist organisers, socialists, liberals, artists, journalists, researchers, religious dissenters, families affected by mobilisation, and organisations operating in exile. Some networks overlap; some emphatically do not.

For each named organisation the final source register must record the original Russian name, date of formation, whether the organisation itself described its activity as anti-war, its position on Ukrainian sovereignty, and whether its public presence was domestic, exiled or mixed. Do not assume that criticism of the military command amounts to rejection of the invasion. Do not treat assistance to displaced people as proof of a particular party affiliation.

**The movement is a network of acts and claims, not a census category.** That distinction preserves political diversity and avoids conferring an invented mandate on the most internationally visible spokespeople.

---

## 4. 🌸 Feminist Anti-War Resistance

**Феминистское антивоенное сопротивление — Feminist Anti-War Resistance (FAR)** is a key research case. Its public formation in response to the 2022 invasion, published manifestos, decentralised communications and forms of feminist organising should be traced through its own statements and corroborating reporting. The final investigation must distinguish what FAR publicly claimed from what outside observers infer about its internal organisation.

Feminist resistance makes visible political labour often excluded from the cinematic image of protest: translating information, distributing texts, supporting people facing penalties, maintaining contacts and connecting militarism to the distribution of care, fear and bodily risk. These are possible mechanisms to investigate, not a licence to attribute every form of mutual aid to one organisation.

The difficulty with imagining all political resistance as a man standing at a podium is that the women may already have organised the meeting, preserved the documents, supported the detainees and gone home to do the second shift. One would hate for the historical record to miss the administration again.

---

## 5. 🚩 The Anti-War Left

Russian socialist and communist positions on the invasion are not uniform. The established **КПРФ — Communist Party of the Russian Federation** and independent left-wing organisations must not be collapsed into a single political voice. Some communist actors supported the Kremlin's position; others rejected the invasion through anti-imperialist and internationalist arguments. Specific names, documents and dates are required before attributing either position.

The central conceptual disagreement concerns whether solidarity is owed primarily to the Russian state or to people subjected to military aggression, including Ukrainian workers and civilians. Arguments about NATO, capitalism or Western power may be part of a group's analysis, but **criticism of NATO does not itself constitute opposition to Russia's invasion**.

Cross-reference [🧨 Opposition Within the Opposition](./🧨_оппозиция_внутри_оппозиции.md). The political work is to show exactly where the factions disagree: territorial integrity, military withdrawal, political prisoners, sanctions, labour interests, and the legitimacy of the state that launched the war.

---

## 6. 🪆 Liberal and Democratic Opposition

Opposition to Putin and opposition to the invasion overlap, but they are not identical political propositions. The liberal and democratic opposition includes people with different histories of commenting on Crimea, Russian nationalism, federalism and the rights of neighbouring states. A profile written only from a figure's most recent international interview risks flattening that history.

Build dated position histories: original statement before 2014 where relevant; position in 2014; response in February 2022; later position on withdrawal, Ukrainian sovereignty, reparations and accountability. Distinguish an organisation's platform from a member's remarks. Where positions changed, describe the change rather than quietly harmonising contradictory statements.

Political imprisonment and exile change who can speak publicly, but neither automatically makes one figure the authorised representative of all Russian opponents of the war.

---

## 7. 👩‍👦 Families of Mobilised Soldiers

The relatives of mobilised personnel occupy a distinct political space. Campaigns may demand demobilisation, rotation, information about missing soldiers, medical treatment, compensation or an end to deployment. Those demands can be urgent and politically consequential without necessarily rejecting the invasion's objectives.

A wife demanding her husband's return is not required to produce a complete foreign-policy manifesto before her grievance can be documented. Equally, the investigator must not convert her words into an anti-war declaration she did not make. Read the original petition or speech, preserve its terms and distinguish demands about military administration from demands to end the war.

Mobilisation is experienced in households: absence, income disruption, care work, uncertainty, injury and grief. These are not decorative details attached to a defence-budget table. They are part of the social system through which war is sustained or contested.

---

## 8. 🫀 The Human Cost of Saying No

Political speech can entail detention, fines, prosecution, loss of employment, family disruption, emigration and imprisonment. The scale and legal outcome of each consequence must be established case by case. A prosecution is not a conviction; a conviction is not independent proof that a political statement was false.

Individual accounts should preserve the person's own words and chronology: what they did, what the authorities alleged, the statute cited, what the court decided, whether an appeal occurred and what is known about their present circumstances. First-person testimony is evidence of reported experience; official court records are evidence of legal procedure and outcome; neither should silently substitute for the other.

The aim is not to make a gallery of brave faces for a theory chapter. **Information is experienced**: a person may pay for a public statement with years of their life. They remain an actor with a position and a history, not a prop in our argument.

---

## 9. ⚖️ The Law Against the War’s Critics

Three frequently relevant provisions require precise legal treatment: **КоАП РФ, статья 20.3.3** (administrative liability relating to alleged discrediting of the armed forces), **УК РФ, статья 280.3** (criminal liability relating to alleged discrediting in specified circumstances), and **УК РФ, статья 207.3** (criminal liability for certain alleged knowingly false information about the armed forces). Their exact current wording, amendments, commencement dates and judicial interpretation require checking against official texts.

The investigation should separate the alleged act from the official description of the act. A placard, online post or interview may be classified under a statutory provision; that classification does not settle the moral or factual dispute about the speech. Record the court, case number, date, disposition and appeal rather than writing only “arrested for opposing the war”.

For the wider enforcement architecture see [⛓️‍💥 Repression and Resistance](./⛓️‍💥_репрессии_и_сопротивление.md).

## 10. 📲 Censorship and the Disappearance of Visible Dissent

Blocking a publication, restricting a platform, pressuring a publisher and criminalising certain statements operate at different points in the information system. They may reinforce one another, but evidence of one is not evidence of all four. Roskomnadzor's powers should be distinguished from decisions by prosecutors, courts, security bodies and private platforms.

Declining visibility may result from deterrence, exhaustion, changed tactics, emigration, censorship or genuine changes in belief. These explanations can coexist. We cannot infer a hidden anti-war majority from silence; neither can we infer unanimity from an absence of demonstrations.

See [📲 Roskomnadzor and the Control of Information](./📲_роскомнадзор_и_контроль_информации.md) for the legal and network-level distinction between a regulatory order, a measured disruption and a user's experience of being unable to access a service.

## 11. 🧠 The Cybernetics of Anti-War Resistance

The system contains several different things: an event (war), people's experience of it, their political interpretation, their capacity to organise, the risks of speaking, the visibility of their acts and the state's response. Treating visible protest as a direct output of private opinion erases most of the mechanism.

```text
War and its consequences
          ↓
Experience, information and interpretation
          ↓
Private positions and political disagreements
          ↓
Resources, networks, risks and opportunities
          ↓
Protest / journalism / mutual aid / silence / exile
          ↓
State response and media visibility
          ↓
Observed activity — not a census of belief
          ↺
Changed risks, networks and future decisions
```

Repression changes the measurement conditions. People who speak may be unusually willing or able to accept risk; people who do not speak may be frightened, indifferent, supportive of the war or occupied with survival. These are analytically different states that can produce the same outward silence.

The state's intervention is not outside the system being measured. It changes the system, and therefore changes the evidence later used to describe it.

## 12. 👁️ What Can Polling Tell Us?

Polling can illuminate attitudes, but politically sensitive survey responses require methodological interpretation. Question wording, sampling frames, mode of interview, non-response, perceived surveillance and social desirability can alter the answers obtained. Different questions about supporting troops, supporting the invasion, favouring negotiations or accepting territorial concessions do not measure identical preferences.

The proper response is not to declare every survey worthless. It is to preserve the question in Russian, the fieldwork dates, sample, recruitment method, response information where available and the organisation that conducted it. Compare changes within genuinely comparable series.

**A poll response is a response under conditions.** It is neither a confession of private belief nor automatically a lie. Both certainty and blanket dismissal make the analysis worse.

---

## 13. 🕊️ What Does Peace Actually Mean?

A ceasefire, withdrawal, negotiations, return of prisoners, reparations, accountability and guarantees against renewed invasion are different proposals. A movement's claim to support **мир — peace** does not tell us which of these it supports. The final directory should record each organisation's actual position on occupied territory and Ukraine's sovereign political agency.

A proposal for peace cannot be assessed only by asking what would be convenient for Russian domestic politics. Ukraine is the invaded state. Its people, territory, institutions and demands are not a subcommittee of the Russian opposition.

“We want peace” is an excellent opening sentence. It is not yet the treaty. One must still establish who withdraws, from where, what happens to prisoners and whether Ukraine is permitted to participate in deciding Ukraine's future. **The invaded country is not a procedural inconvenience.**

---

## 14. 🌍 Exile and the Geography of Resistance

Leaving Russia can alter the immediate risks of publication, but exile also changes access to communities, domestic networks, funding and sources. People inside Russia may have knowledge that is difficult to share safely; those outside may have easier access to international media without having a mandate to speak for people who stayed.

Trace the routes by which information is corroborated across borders, the gaps introduced by security and communication restrictions, and the disagreements between domestic and exiled organisations. International attention is an observable distribution of visibility, not a referendum on representativeness.

An ethical investigation should not expose private contacts or identify people whose safety depends on anonymity. Public attribution is appropriate only when it is already established and necessary to the account.

---

## 15. 🇺🇦 Ukrainian Perspectives on Russian Anti-War Activism

Ukrainian criticism of Russian opposition politics is not a nuisance to be smoothed out for a satisfying narrative about resistance. It can concern Crimea, imperial assumptions, accountability, territorial concessions, the centring of Russian suffering and the difference between opposing Putin and rejecting Russia's imperial claims.

The final source pass should include Ukrainian journalists, scholars, organisers and survivors in their own words, without inventing a single Ukrainian consensus. The question is not whether a Russian activist has earned a universal certificate of virtue; it is whether their political proposals take Ukrainian rights and experiences seriously.

**Russian resistance is worth documenting. It does not displace Ukrainian agency or Russian state responsibility.** Both statements can be true without competing for the same paragraph.

## 16. 🚩 Anti-War Resistance and Communist History

The historical inheritance of socialism is contested. Some groups appeal to Soviet victory and Russian state continuity; others emphasise internationalism, anti-militarism and opposition to imperial domination. Identical imagery can carry incompatible contemporary political messages.

An archival photograph of a red flag tells us that a flag was present. A manifesto, speech, banner text and organisational affiliation are needed to establish the claim being made. The presence of Soviet symbolism does not prove that a gathering is anti-war, pro-war or affiliated with the КПРФ.

🚩 One faction has brought the flags. Another has brought the manifestos. A third would like to know why everybody appears to have forgotten the internationalism. The committee is experiencing historical disagreement. See [🚩 Communists Are Revolting](./🚩_коммунисты_бунтуют.md).

## 17. 📣 Moscow, October 2026 — Is There an Anti-War Connection?

The companion [📣 Moscow Protests of October 2026](./📣_московские_протесты_октября_2026.md) contains unresolved event-verification questions. At the drafting cut-off, this node does **not** independently establish dates, organisers, slogans, arrests or an anti-war affiliation for those reported events.

The correct next step is to identify an original dated source for each event, confirm the location, record the organisers' own demands, and look for anti-war statements rather than assuming them from communist symbolism or criticism of Putin. Economic protest, electoral protest, internal party conflict and anti-war mobilisation can overlap; they are not interchangeable.

Until then, the October events remain a research junction, **not a case study demonstrating an anti-war mobilisation**.

## 18. 💰 The War Economy and Anti-War Politics

War spending, recruitment incentives, inflation, household incomes, regional public services and labour shortages can shape political grievances. Yet economic hardship is not itself evidence of opposition to the invasion. A person can oppose mobilisation while supporting the war; another may oppose the war on moral grounds without citing personal financial harm.

Trace economic arguments through original anti-war statements rather than projecting macroeconomic figures onto activists. If a movement says resources should go to schools, hospitals or wages, quote and date that claim. If it demands an end to the invasion because of Ukrainian civilian suffering, do not translate that into a household-cost complaint.

The companion [🍊 Лох — не мамонт, не вымрет](./🍊_Лох_не_мамонт_не_вымрет.md) explores wartime pressures and negotiating incentives; economic proximity does not establish causal influence on a particular protest or diplomatic decision.

---

## 19. 🛟 Can Resistance Change State Policy?

Political effects come in several forms: changed public knowledge, support for detainees, durable networks, international documentation, legal challenges, shifts in official language, and actual policy change. These outcomes must not be collapsed into one measure of “success”.

A protest can fail to change a government decision while still producing evidence, solidarity and a historical record. Equally, international publicity does not establish domestic influence. To claim a policy consequence, establish the sequence, the relevant decision-maker, contemporaneous statements and plausible alternative explanations.

The question is not whether resistance deserves a triumphant ending. It is what the evidence permits us to say it accomplished, what it could not accomplish, and what people risked in the attempt.

## 21. 🧾 Organisations, Events and Case Registers

The research architecture should preserve three kinds of evidence without confusing them. An organisation's statement proves a position was published; a protest record documents an event; a judicial record documents a legal outcome. All three may concern the same people but require different source fields.

```yaml

  name_ru: null
  name_en: null
  formation_date: null
  stated_position_on_invasion: null
  position_on_ukrainian_sovereignty: null
  primary_statements: []
  corroboration: []
  evidence_status: unverified

Do not convert this queue into a bibliography of sources supposedly consulted.

## 22. ⚖️ What We Can and Cannot Conclude

Documented acts of resistance establish that particular people resisted. They do not establish that most Russians share their beliefs. Detentions establish enforcement actions, not the total number of protesters. Polling establishes reported answers under identifiable conditions, not unmediated private thought.

An organisation may call itself anti-war while offering a peace proposal that leaves territorial claims unresolved. A mobilisation campaign may seek the return of soldiers without opposing the invasion. A liberal opposition figure may criticise Putin while retaining positions that Ukrainian critics regard as imperial. These are not details to edit out for narrative tidiness.

The strongest conclusions are specific: **who spoke, what they said, what they did, when they did it, and what followed**. The movement's representativeness, causal influence and likely future trajectory require further evidence. Silence is not consent; neither is it proof of a hidden majority.

---

## 23. 🏁 Conclusion — Russia Is Not One Person

The Russian state invaded Ukraine. Russian citizens have also opposed that invasion. Some protested; some organised; some published; some left; some stayed; some were prosecuted or imprisoned. They did not all agree with one another, and they did not all make the same demands.

The disagreement does not make the resistance imaginary. Nor does the resistance absolve the state of responsibility. Ukraine's sovereignty and the experiences of Ukrainians remain central to the political and moral question.

A serious history must resist two temptations: treating an entire population as the government that rules it, and treating visible dissidents as proof that the population secretly agrees with them. **The Russian government is not the Russian population. The Russian anti-war movement is not the Russian population either.**

> 🦊: “We've established that not everybody agrees with the government.”  
> 🏛️: “How many?”  
> 🦊: “Difficult to determine.”  
> 🏛️: “Why?”  
> 🦊: “Because we've made it dangerous for them to tell us.”  
> 🏛️: “Ah.”
>
> *Fictional satire. An unfortunately genuine measurement problem.*
