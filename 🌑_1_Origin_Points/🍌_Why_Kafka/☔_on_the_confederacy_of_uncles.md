# ☔ On The Confederacy Of Uncles
**First created:** 2026-09-01 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-01  
*A Kafka bridge for private courts, informal access, hidden information layers, survivor data, behavioural proxies, and the peculiar constitutional problem of needing somebody who knows a chap.*

---

## 🧭 Purpose

This node uses Chapter Six of Kafka’s *The Trial* — “K.’s Uncle — Leni” — as a small origin lens for Polaris.

Chapter One begins with a missing breakfast.

Chapter Two hides the court inside an apartment block.

Chapter Three shows that the court has offices.

Chapter Four shows K. reproducing some of the behaviour the court has taught him.

Chapter Five asks what happens when punishment reaches the visible actor without reaching the system that produced the harm.

Chapter Six introduces an uncle.

This turns out to be more important than it sounds.

K. has spent much of the novel trying to understand the court as though it were an institution with a location, hierarchy, procedure, and route of access.

His uncle responds differently.

His uncle knows a chap.

The chap is a lawyer.

The lawyer knows people at the court.

One of those people is already sitting quietly in the lawyer’s bedroom.

This is funny.

It is also an extremely serious description of what happens when public procedure becomes dependent upon private legibility.

The court is not merely hidden inside private spaces.

Its effective access routes run through private relationships.

---

## ☔ The confederacy does not have to know it is confederated

A confederacy of uncles is not necessarily a conspiracy.

That distinction matters.

There does not need to be a secret committee.

There does not need to be one central plan.

There does not need to be a single malicious actor coordinating everybody else.

There does not even need to be agreement about what is happening.

You only need people who know people.

People who have heard things.

People who know which door opens.

People whose calls are answered.

People who went to school together.

People who know the lawyer.

Lawyers who know officials.

Officials who privately visit lawyers.

Carers who know judges.

Family members who hear things through workplaces.

Colleagues who know that a case is serious without knowing what the case is about.

People who can interpret signals unavailable to the person formally subject to the process.

The confederacy can emerge from ordinary relationships.

That is partly what makes it difficult to see.

Every individual connection may be perfectly explicable.

Together, they can form an alternative map of the institution.

## 👨‍🦳 Uncle Karl knows a chap

Karl’s response to learning about K.’s trial is immediate.

He does not know what the allegation is.

He does not know how the court works.

He does not understand the procedure.

He has spent twenty years living largely outside the city and admits that he is out of practice with this kind of thing.

But Karl possesses something K. does not.

Social capital.

He knows Dr. Huld.

They went to school together.

So the route becomes:

**K. → Uncle Karl → Huld → court**

This is Karl’s practical answer to institutional opacity.

Not:

Where are the published rules?

Not:

Who is formally responsible?

Not:

What is the statutory route?

Not:

Where can Josef inspect the allegation?

But:

**I know someone.**

And Kafka does not reveal Karl to be foolish for thinking this way.

Quite the opposite.

It works.

## 🛏️ The court is literally in somebody’s bedroom

Chapter Two already makes the court difficult to separate from domestic space.

The courtroom is inside an apartment block.

Chapter Three goes further.

The court has offices in the attic of the tenement.

By Chapter Six, another possibility becomes difficult to ignore.

Perhaps the problem is not simply that the court has been placed in inappropriate private buildings.

Perhaps the court has no stable institutional perimeter.

Karl takes K. to Huld’s home.

Huld is lying ill in bed.

Leni is caring for him.

Karl is visiting an old schoolfriend.

They are discussing K.’s criminal case.

And sitting silently in a dark corner of the bedroom is an office director from the court.

K. does not initially know he is there.

That spatial arrangement is almost absurdly exact.

K. cannot see the senior layers of the court.

But they can be present.

K. cannot easily reach them.

But his lawyer can privately socialise with them.

K. does not know who knows about his case.

But people around the court apparently do.

The court is not merely invading private space.

**Private space has become part of the court’s operating environment.**

---

## 🕸️ The court is a social graph

K. keeps looking for an institution.

Kafka keeps showing him relationships.

The practical topology of Chapter Six looks something like this:

**bank employee → Erna → Karl → Huld → office director → court**

There is another route:

**K. → Leni → Huld → judges / clients / court knowledge**

And another:

**Karl → old school friendship → Huld**

The court therefore does not operate only through an organisational chart.

It also operates through a social graph.

Who knows whom?

Who trusts whom?

Who can introduce whom?

Who can obtain a private conversation?

Who hears about which case?

Who knows what somebody inside the institution thinks?

Who knows which official is important?

Who knows how that official prefers to be approached?

Who can get into the room?

Who already belongs there?

This is not necessarily corruption.

Humans necessarily have relationships.

Professionals necessarily know other professionals.

Expertise necessarily produces networks.

But a public-governance problem appears when the social graph becomes substantially better at making the institution work than the institution’s public route.

At that point, formal equality and practical equality begin to separate.

---

## 🚪 Who you know becomes part of the procedure

Two people can possess exactly the same formal rights and radically different practical access to them.

One receives the form.

The other knows the person who understands what happens after the form arrives.

One receives the published email address.

The other knows who actually answers.

One receives the complaints process.

The other knows which person has authority to resolve the underlying problem.

One is told that decisions are independent.

The other knows the people making them.

One sees the face layer.

The other sees the map underneath.

This is the Chapter Six problem.

**The formal system determines K.’s fate.**

**The informal system increasingly determines K.’s access to the formal system.**

That is why Karl becomes furious when K. leaves Huld’s bedroom.

From Karl’s perspective, something extraordinarily valuable has happened.

The route has opened.

Karl knows Huld.

Huld knows the office director.

The office director has some relationship to K.’s case.

All the right people are temporarily in one room.

And K. walks out.

He has not merely been rude.

He has broken the path through which Karl believes the system can actually be acted upon.

## 🎭 The face layer

A modern public institution usually has a face layer.

This is the layer the citizen is supposed to encounter.

Published procedure.

Guidance.

Professional standards.

Evidential tests.

Complaints routes.

Safeguarding language.

Regulatory structures.

Review mechanisms.

Official contact details.

Formal decision-making.

Rights.

Responsibilities.

Reasons.

Appeals.

The face layer matters.

It is not necessarily fake.

But the existence of a public procedure does not establish that the public procedure contains every variable acting upon the outcome.

Behind it may exist another informational environment.

Reputation.

Institutional memory.

Professional relationships.

Previous interactions.

Informal warnings.

Old records.

Private assessments.

Risk classifications.

Social familiarity.

Who trusts whom.

Who has heard what.

Who knows which history.

Who can infer something from apparently unrelated information.

Some of this may be legitimate.

Some may be necessary.

Some may improve decisions.

Some may prevent harm.

The problem begins when this hidden informational layer substantially determines whether the face layer works for the person while remaining inaccessible to them.

## 🟥 The scarlet letter does not need to be visible

A person can be marked by information that most people cannot see.

That marker does not need to be maliciously created.

It may arise from legitimate contact with a legitimate institution.

A previous report.

A safeguarding record.

A police interaction.

A medical note.

A previous complaint.

A withdrawn allegation.

An NFA decision.

A vulnerability assessment.

A credibility judgement.

A risk classification.

A record that something happened without sufficient context explaining why.

Conceptually, the marker may become:

**PREVIOUS_VICTIM**

Most people walking past the person cannot see it.

But particular systems may be able to see or infer it.

And different actors can read the same marker differently.

A protective institution might incorrectly read:

**PREVIOUS_VICTIM → difficult history → credibility complication**

A predatory or manipulative actor might read:

**PREVIOUS_VICTIM → known vulnerability → perhaps easier to harm, destabilise, discredit, or isolate**

Nobody has to coordinate these interpretations.

That is the important part.

The danger can emerge from the system itself.

---

## 🧟 Zombie data can become a targeting variable

Polaris already uses **zombie data** for information that continues acting after its original context has disappeared.

A stale note.

A partial disclosure.

An old risk marker.

A safeguarding referral.

A credibility judgement.

A copied phrase.

A classification created for one purpose and later reused for another.

Chapter Six adds another dimension.

Persistent information does not only affect institutions.

It can alter the person’s attack surface.

A piece of information collected because somebody was harmed may itself become harmful if it is:

accessed by the wrong person,

interpreted without context,

shared beyond its intended purpose,

combined with other information,

used to predict vulnerability,

or allowed to shape future credibility assessments without proper scrutiny.

A system designed partly to recognise vulnerability can therefore accidentally construct a variable useful for identifying vulnerability.

That is a data-governance problem even if nobody intended to create it.

---

## 👻 The marker may no longer need to exist

Modern information systems make the problem harder.

You may not need a field saying:

**PREVIOUS_VICTIM = TRUE**

You may only need behavioural proxies.

Patterns of contact.

Changes in communication.

Repeated interaction with particular services.

Location patterns.

Employment disruption.

Housing disruption.

Safeguarding activity.

Complaint behaviour.

Security behaviour.

Changes in mobility.

Changes in social participation.

Clusters of otherwise ordinary data.

The sensitive fact does not necessarily have to be stored explicitly if it can be inferred from everything surrounding it.

The scarlet letter becomes latent.

The person may therefore correct one record while leaving the inference intact.

That produces a different information-governance problem.

It is not enough to ask:

**Did we store the sensitive fact?**

We also need to ask:

**Can the sensitive fact be reconstructed from what we retained?**

And:

**Who can perform that reconstruction?**

---

## 🚨 Vulnerability is dual-use information

Information about vulnerability is inherently difficult to govern because the same knowledge can support opposite purposes.

A safeguarding worker may need to know that somebody is vulnerable in order to provide protection.

A clinician may need contextual information to provide appropriate care.

A prosecutor may need to understand previous victimisation to interpret behaviour accurately.

A support service may need to recognise repeated harm.

But vulnerability information can also be valuable to somebody looking for a person who is easier to exploit.

The information itself therefore has dual-use properties.

**Protective interpretation:**

This person may require additional support.

**Predatory interpretation:**

This person may be easier to target.

The same underlying signal can support both readings.

That does not mean protective systems should stop collecting necessary information.

It means survivor data requires threat modelling.

Who can see it?

Who can infer it?

How long does it persist?

What context travels with it?

What happens when the context disappears?

Can it be challenged?

Can downstream users tell whether an old judgement remains reliable?

Can the person know what is acting upon them?

What happens if somebody hostile obtains the same information?

---

## 🪡 Rape cases make this especially dangerous

Sexual violence exposes this problem particularly sharply.

A survivor may enter the public system expecting each allegation to be assessed through evidence, law, context, and an understanding of coercion.

But previous contact with the system can itself generate information.

If earlier records are subsequently treated as credibility signals rather than contextual information, the person can acquire an invisible burden they cannot easily inspect.

This is especially dangerous because repeated victimisation is real.

Previous victimisation is not evidence that later victimisation did not happen.

Indeed, vulnerability can itself increase exposure to further harm.

A system that mistakes repeated victimisation for reduced credibility can therefore create a grotesque inversion.

The protective institution becomes less confident.

The predatory actor becomes more confident.

The same history is being read in opposite directions.

That is horrendous.

And it does not require a network of abusers.

It only requires a vulnerability signal and different incentives for interpreting it.

---

## 🕯️ Savile and the danger of institutional memory facing the wrong way

Britain does not need Kafka to know that institutions can accommodate abuse for long periods.

Repeated public scandals have demonstrated that.

Savile matters not simply because one extremely prolific abuser existed.

He matters because the conditions around him included status, access, institutional relationships, reputation, disbelief, fragmented knowledge, failures to join information, and people or organisations responding inadequately to what was known.

Later inquiries into institutional sexual abuse have repeatedly demonstrated that abuse cannot always be understood as an isolated transaction between perpetrator and victim.

Rooms matter.

Organisations matter.

Reputations matter.

Information matters.

Who knew what matters.

Who could safely say something matters.

What happened to earlier reports matters.

What institutions learned — and failed to learn — matters.

The lesson should not be that every modern institution secretly contains another Savile network.

The lesson is narrower.

**Do not design systems on the assumption that powerful or sophisticated abusers will behave like ordinary users of the system.**

Some people study affordances.

Some people learn which accusations are believed.

Some people learn which institutions protect their reputation.

Some people learn what makes a target appear unreliable.

Some people learn which behaviours produce records.

Some people learn where responsibility fragments.

Some people become highly skilled at operating in the spaces between formal rules.

---

## 🧠 Systems literacy can be used offensively

A sophisticated manipulator does not need to control an institution.

They may only need to understand it better than the person they are targeting.

Which complaints are taken seriously?

Which behaviour is interpreted as instability?

Which records persist?

Which professionals defer to which other professionals?

Which allegation will trigger safeguarding?

Which allegation will trigger suspicion?

What happens if the target complains repeatedly?

What happens if they become angry?

What happens if they withdraw?

What happens if they seek help from many places?

What happens if the system sees the effects of targeting without seeing the targeting itself?

This is systems literacy used offensively.

The manipulator does not necessarily need privileged database access.

They may simply understand the behavioural consequences of the environment they are creating.

That matters for abuse which is not primarily sexual or physical.

Coercion can be informational.

Reputational.

Administrative.

Legal.

Financial.

Social.

Digital.

A person can be attacked through the systems by which other people come to understand them.

---

## 💾 Data abuse is still under-characterised

We have relatively mature language for some forms of abuse.

Physical violence.

Sexual violence.

Financial abuse.

Coercive control.

Harassment.

Stalking.

We have less settled language for deliberate manipulation of information architecture around another person.

Who sees what?

Who receives which version?

Which true facts are stripped of context?

Which records appear first?

Which classifications become sticky?

Which audiences receive which fragments?

Which behaviours are provoked and then presented as evidence about the person?

Which systems become harder for the person to access because of information generated elsewhere?

Importantly, informational abuse does not necessarily require false information.

A manipulative actor can sometimes build a damaging picture from:

selective truths,

missing context,

sequencing,

audience selection,

privileged access,

repetition,

and strategic interpretation.

The abuse can lie partly in the architecture of disclosure.

Polaris should therefore treat **data abuse** as something requiring much more explicit characterisation.

## 🔁 When the reaction becomes new evidence

One particularly dangerous loop occurs when targeting changes behaviour.

A person subjected to sustained pressure may:

complain more often,

seek help repeatedly,

become security-conscious,

change communication habits,

withdraw,

become distressed,

change work patterns,

change travel patterns,

seek legal advice,

contact multiple institutions,

document obsessively,

or become angry when they are not understood.

Those behaviours can then become additional data.

If downstream systems observe the behavioural effects without seeing the cause, the loop becomes:

**TARGETING**

↓

**BEHAVIOURAL CHANGE**

↓

**NEW DATA**

↓

**VULNERABILITY OR CREDIBILITY INFERENCE**

↓

**DIFFERENTIAL TREATMENT**

↓

**GREATER VULNERABILITY**

The target can therefore become increasingly legible as a problem while the thing producing the problem remains illegible.

That is a dangerous feedback loop.

It also gives manipulative actors an incentive to provoke precisely the behaviour that later institutions will misread.

## 🌍 Foreign actors do not need to control the court

This has national-security implications.

A foreign actor does not necessarily need to infiltrate or control a British institution in order to affect the informational environment that institution later encounters.

A hostile state.

A proxy.

A commercially motivated intelligence actor.

A wealthy private actor.

A political organisation.

A coordinated harassment network.

Any of these may attempt to change what is publicly or institutionally knowable about a target.

Digital harassment.

Strategic complaints.

Legal intimidation.

Threatened litigation.

Selective publication.

Reputation attacks.

Impersonation.

Information laundering.

Mass attention.

Provocation.

The important systems point is that the downstream British decision-maker can remain genuinely independent.

The court can remain independent.

The regulator can remain independent.

The employer can remain independent.

The university can remain independent.

The journalist can remain independent.

The police force can remain independent.

But independent decision-making does not guarantee independent inputs.

If somebody can manipulate the information environment from which independent actors make decisions, they may be able to influence outcomes without controlling those actors.

---

## ⚖️ Why SLAPP protection belongs here

This is one reason SLAPP protections matter beyond journalism.

A strategic lawsuit does not only seek a courtroom outcome.

Legal pressure can alter behaviour before judgment.

It can consume money.

Consume time.

Create fear.

Produce records.

Change search results.

Generate correspondence.

Discourage publication.

Alter who will speak to whom.

Create reputational ambiguity.

Force organisations into defensive positions.

Make other people reluctant to associate with the target.

The legal process itself becomes an informational intervention.

That is why anti-SLAPP architecture can also be understood as information-system hardening.

It reduces the ability of powerful actors to cheaply convert legal process into silence, exhaustion, distorted public information, or reputational signals that then propagate through other systems.

This becomes especially important when foreign or transnational actors can use domestic legal and digital infrastructure against people or groups inside another country.

The national-security question is therefore not only:

**Can a foreign actor control one of our institutions?**

It is also:

**Can a foreign actor write information onto people which our institutions will subsequently read?**

---

## 👑 Elite abuse is a legitimate systems question

Recent and historical scandals make public concern about elite abuse legitimate.

That does not mean every powerful person accused, arrested, investigated, associated with another person, or present in the same social environment is guilty.

It does not mean every elite abuse case is networked.

It does not mean association proves participation.

Those distinctions matter.

But elite abuse creates particular governance problems because wealth, status, professional networks, institutional familiarity, lawyers, reputation management, social access, and information asymmetry can change the environment in which allegations are made and assessed.

The relevant systems questions therefore extend beyond:

Did this individual commit this specific offence?

They include:

Who knew what?

What information moved?

What failed to move?

Whose warning was believed?

Whose warning was discounted?

Which relationships altered access?

Did institutional reputation affect response?

Were earlier complaints joined together?

Were they improperly treated as evidence against the complainant?

Did anybody understand the system well enough to exploit its fragmentation?

Was information about vulnerable people adequately protected?

None of those questions requires guilt by association.

They are questions about whether the architecture itself is safe.

## 🎭 Innocence has a dress code

Karl provides another important clue.

He looks at K.’s behaviour and says, essentially:

**That is not how an innocent man behaves.**

This is extraordinary because Karl does not know what K. has allegedly done.

He nevertheless possesses an idea of how innocence should look.

K. is too calm.

Too indifferent.

Not sufficiently urgent.

Not sufficiently defensive.

Not behaving as though the family reputation is in danger.

So K. must perform innocence before people who cannot tell him the charge.

This is another hidden layer.

Formal justice asks whether the allegation can be proved.

Social justice asks whether the accused looks like the kind of person everyone expects an innocent person to look like.

The same mechanism can operate against victims.

Too calm.

Too distressed.

Too organised.

Too disorganised.

Reported immediately.

Reported late.

Stayed.

Left.

Returned.

Complained repeatedly.

Stopped cooperating.

Sought help everywhere.

Did not seek help enough.

Once behavioural interpretation becomes detached from a serious model of coercion, trauma, context, and individual variation, almost any behaviour can become evidence of the story an observer already prefers.

That is why procedural systems require disciplined evidential reasoning.

Otherwise the face layer says:

**we assess evidence**

while the hidden layer quietly asks:

**does this person look right to me?**

## 🖼️ The tiny judge on the enormous throne

Leni shows K. a portrait of an examining judge.

In reality, she tells him, the man is tiny.

He sits on a kitchen chair with an old horse blanket.

In the portrait, he sits enormously upon a gilded throne.

This is not incidental decoration.

Chapter Six is full of the difference between formal appearance and operational reality.

The portrait gives that difference a visual form.

The authority displayed publicly is grand.

The material reality underneath it is shabby.

But the shabbiness does not mean the power is unreal.

The tiny man can still be a judge.

The kitchen chair can still sit inside a functioning system.

The inflated portrait can still influence how authority is perceived.

The problem is therefore not merely hypocrisy.

It is interface design.

**What does the institution show you?**

**What actually makes it work?**

## 🔑 Leni has another door

K. leaves one informal access network and enters another.

Leni knows the court.

She knows judges.

She knows what people are saying about K.

She knows the supposed strategy.

She tells him that his problem is his unyieldingness.

She tells him to confess.

She offers to help him.

Then she gives him a key.

The symbolism is almost painfully obvious.

Huld offers professional social capital.

Karl offers familial social capital.

The office director embodies institutional social capital.

Leni offers intimate social capital.

Erna provides informational social capital.

Everyone has a route.

Everyone has knowledge.

Everyone knows something.

Everyone can potentially help.

And none of them gives K. the thing a legitimate criminal process should have given him at the beginning.

**The allegation and a comprehensible route for answering it.**

The system has become extraordinarily rich in access to people while remaining catastrophically poor in access to procedure.

## 🧨 Finding the control surface is not enough

Chapter Five asks where the actual control surface is.

Chapter Six briefly gives K. something that looks remarkably close to one.

His uncle.

A lawyer familiar with the court.

A senior court official.

All physically assembled around his case.

And K. leaves.

This should not simply be read as:

K. is stupid.

Something more interesting has happened.

The process has already changed his attention.

Sex.

Curiosity.

Court gossip.

Leni.

The judge’s portrait.

Another promise of help.

Another private route.

Another person who seems to know more than he does.

K. has spent so long trying to locate the system that when a potentially useful access point appears, he struggles to remain oriented toward it.

This gives us an important addition to the control-surface problem.

**Finding the correct control surface is insufficient if the system has already degraded the person’s capacity to use it.**

Orientation is therefore not merely about locating power.

It is also about preserving enough attention, stability, evidence, restraint, and agency to act when a meaningful route finally becomes available.

## 🇬🇧 A peculiarly British danger

Britain has always had informal networks.

Clubs.

Schools.

Universities.

Chambers.

Professions.

The civil service.

Political parties.

Family connections.

Old colleagues.

Friends of friends.

People who know a chap.

That history alone does not establish corruption.

But the stakes change when old relational forms become attached to a modern administrative and technological state.

Databases.

Digital identity.

Platform infrastructure.

Security classifications.

Procurement systems.

Outsourced contractors.

Consultancies.

Regulatory discretion.

Automated inference.

Instant private communications.

Large-scale data joining.

The old British:

**I know a chap**

can now potentially intersect with systems capable of affecting far more than a private introduction.

That creates a democratic question.

How much of a public institution can migrate into private networks before equal formal access stops meaning equal practical access?

And how much hidden information can influence public procedure before the person subject to that procedure loses meaningful ability to understand what is acting upon them?

## 🧭 The Polaris move

The confederacy-of-uncles problem does not ask us to assume conspiracy.

It asks us to map access.

What is the published procedure?

What actually happens when somebody uses it?

Who possesses discretion?

Who possesses information?

Who possesses relationships?

Which information is formally recorded?

Which information is socially transmitted?

Which information can be inferred?

Which classifications persist?

What context travels with them?

What context falls away?

Who can inspect the hidden layer?

Who cannot?

Does somebody need a personal introduction to obtain something the public procedure says should already be available?

Is vulnerability information adequately threat-modelled?

Could a protective marker become a targeting variable?

Could behavioural proxies reconstruct something supposedly protected?

Could an external actor manipulate the inputs consumed by an otherwise independent institution?

Is the person responding to the actual procedure or to a version of themselves assembled somewhere they cannot see?

And, critically:

**Who knows a chap?**

That question should be funny.

Sometimes it is.

It should not determine whether a person receives justice.

## 🧯 Guardrail

This node is not permission to treat every professional relationship as corruption.

It is not permission to infer conspiracy from association.

It is not permission to treat every private meeting as improper.

It is not permission to assume that every institution contains a hidden network controlling formal decisions.

It is not permission to assume that every old record remains operational.

It is not permission to infer that a specific abuser has accessed survivor data without evidence.

It is not permission to treat arrests, investigations, social connections, or allegations as proof of guilt.

It is not permission to collapse all elite abuse into one network.

It is not permission to treat every behavioural pattern as evidence that somebody has been classified or targeted.

It is not permission to use uncertainty as accusation.

The discipline is narrower and stronger:

Map formal access.

Map practical access.

Separate relationships from corruption.

Separate association from participation.

Track information movement.

Threat-model vulnerability data.

Look for context collapse.

Distinguish explicit markers from inferred proxies.

Ask whether behavioural effects are being mistaken for causes.

Protect the integrity of independent decision-making by protecting the integrity of its inputs.

Do not require a conspiracy where ordinary system dynamics are sufficient.

Do not assume that ordinary system dynamics make the resulting harm trivial.

---

## 🧩 Working definition

The **confederacy-of-uncles problem** is what happens when the effective operation of a public system depends partly upon private relationships, informal knowledge, persistent classifications, or inferred information that are not equally visible or accessible to the people formally subject to the system.

The confederacy does not need to be organised.

Its members do not need to share intentions.

They do not need to know that their actions form a larger pattern.

The resulting hidden layer can emerge from ordinary social and informational processes.

The danger becomes greater when vulnerability information persists or can be inferred, because the same signal may be interpreted protectively by one actor and opportunistically by another.

The formal institution may remain independent.

The people inside it may remain individually well-intentioned.

The face layer may remain real.

But if consequential information and practical access travel through routes the citizen cannot see, then equality on paper may coexist with profound inequality of orientation.

K. keeps trying to find the court.

His uncle knows a chap.

The chap knows the court.

The court is sitting in the chap’s bedroom.
