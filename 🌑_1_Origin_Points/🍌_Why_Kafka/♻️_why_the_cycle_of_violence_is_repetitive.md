# ♻️ Why The Cycle Of Violence Is Repetitive
**First created:** 2026-08-21 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-21  
*A Kafka bridge for learned violence, embodied repetition, displaced control, and the possibility of practising something else.*

---

## 🧭 Purpose

This node uses Chapter Four of Kafka’s *The Trial* — “Miss Bürstner’s Friend” — as a small origin lens for Polaris.

Chapter One begins with a missing breakfast.

Chapter Two hides the court inside an apartment block.

Chapter Three shows that the court has offices.

Chapter Four shows something more uncomfortable.

K. has learned from the court.

Not its law.

Its behaviour.

The intrusion, watching, uncertainty, compelled participation, inaccessible explanation, control of access, and interpretation of ordinary movements as part of a process do not remain outside him.

He begins to reproduce them.

Onto Miss Bürstner.

That matters because violence is repetitive partly because violence becomes familiar.

It becomes what the body expects.

What the mind knows how to interpret.

What power looks like.

What conflict feels like.

What a person reaches for when they are frightened, humiliated, uncertain, or desperate to recover control.

The cycle is not inevitable.

But it is learnable.

And if violence can be learned from one another, alternatives can be learned from one another too.

---

## ♻️ Everything that happened to him, he mimics onto her

The correspondence is difficult to miss.

K.’s ordinary domestic space is invaded.

Then he occupies Miss Bürstner’s room while she is absent.

K. is watched.

Then he sits in darkness watching the hallway for her.

Other people determine when and how K. can encounter the process.

Then K. changes his morning schedule in the hope of intercepting Miss Bürstner alone.

The court intrudes into K.’s ordinary working life.

Then K. writes to Miss Bürstner both at home and at her office.

K. cannot obtain the explanation he wants.

Then he insists that Miss Bürstner should explain herself to him.

K. is forced to organise his time around an unexplained process.

Then he tells Miss Bürstner that he will remain in his room all Sunday waiting for a sign from her.

K. encounters representatives and intermediaries who speak for processes he cannot directly access.

Then Miss Bürstner communicates through Miss Montag, and K. resents the mediation.

K. becomes suspicious of people who appear to know more than he does or coordinate outside his view.

Then he begins reading Miss Montag, Captain Lanz, and Miss Bürstner as a group arranged around his exclusion.

And the novel began with officials entering K.’s bedroom.

Chapter Four ends its central movement with K. opening Miss Bürstner’s closed door without permission.

The direction has changed.

The grammar has not.

## 🕳️ The smaller machine comes online

None of this requires K. consciously to decide:

I will do to her what was done to me.

That is precisely why the chapter matters.

K. thinks he is trying to resolve something.

He offers amends.

He promises to respect whatever boundary Miss Bürstner sets.

He asks for a conversation.

He tells himself that a conversation is the reasonable way to clear things up.

But Miss Bürstner is already setting boundaries.

She avoids him.

She does not answer his letters.

She changes the physical arrangement of her room by accepting Miss Montag into it.

She sends Miss Montag to communicate that a meeting would serve no useful purpose.

K. receives the answer.

Then he goes to her door.

He knocks.

There is no answer.

He knocks again.

There is no answer.

He knocks harder.

There is no answer.

Then he opens the door.

Boundary.

Boundary.

Boundary.

Explicit boundary.

Door.

The repetition is almost brutally clean.

The machine does not need to announce itself as violence in order to reproduce.

It can call itself clarification.

Reconciliation.

Closure.

Reasonableness.

A final conversation.

The wish to put things right.

The wish to be understood.

The wish not to be left uncertain.

Those wishes can be real.

They still do not create a right of access to another person.

## 🧠 Violence teaches methods

“Cycle of violence” can become a vague phrase if it is treated as though harm automatically turns harmed people into people who harm.

That is not the claim here.

Victimhood does not mechanically produce perpetration.

Trauma does not erase agency.

Being harmed does not make later harm inevitable.

And people who have experienced violence are not dangerous merely because violence happened to them.

The narrower claim is more useful:

**Violence can teach methods.**

Being watched can teach vigilance.

Being controlled can make control feel like the obvious route back to safety.

Being denied explanation can create a compulsive demand that somebody else explain themselves.

Having one’s boundaries disregarded does not automatically teach respect for boundaries.

Being made uncertain can make another person’s ambiguity feel intolerable.

Being compelled to participate can make compelled conversation feel less strange.

Being humiliated can create an appetite to recover status somewhere easier.

A person can therefore reject the people who harmed them while reproducing parts of the relational structure they learned from them.

The content changes.

The justification changes.

The scale changes.

The power changes hands.

The pattern survives.

## 🪿 The body learns the room

This is where the cycle belongs with [[../🪿_Embodied_Information_Ecology/README|🪿 Embodied Information Ecology]].

K.’s repetition is not only an intellectual error.

His body has changed its relationship to ordinary space.

He waits.

He watches.

He listens.

He gets up earlier.

He sits in an unlit room.

He tracks footsteps in the hallway.

He becomes preoccupied with doors.

He reads movement around the lodging house as information.

He anticipates encounters before they happen.

The process has entered his sensorium.

The environment trains the body.

The trained body then participates in producing the next environment.

That is one way a cycle becomes repetitive.

The person does not merely remember violence.

They may begin organising attention, expectation, movement, timing, proximity, threat, and control around what violence taught them.

Then other people enter the environment produced by those adaptations.

Miss Bürstner is no longer simply living in a lodging house.

She is living in a lodging house where K. is watching for her.

Mrs. Grubach is no longer simply serving breakfast.

She is managing K.’s displeasure.

Miss Montag is no longer simply moving rooms.

Her footsteps become part of K.’s interpretation of what is happening to him.

One nervous system changes the informational weather inhabited by another.

That is why embodied information ecology matters.

## 🌸 Containment can become contagious

This also belongs with [[../🌸_Containment_Studies/README|🌸 Containment Studies]].

Containment is not only something an institution does from above.

Its techniques can migrate.

Watch.

Classify.

Narrow access.

Demand explanation.

Interpret silence.

Control the route.

Decide which answer counts.

Treat uncertainty as threat.

Make somebody else legible enough to manage.

A person subjected to containment can begin reproducing containment at a smaller scale without becoming identical to the institution that contained them.

That distinction matters.

The court has institutional authority over K.

K. does not possess equivalent authority over Miss Bürstner.

The harms are not symmetrical.

The acts are not interchangeable.

The point is not equivalence.

The point is recurrence.

**The direction of power can change while a relational pattern survives.**

That is one of the ways systems reproduce through people.

## 🍳 Breakfast changes sides

Breakfast returns in Chapter Four.

That matters.

At the beginning of *The Trial*, breakfast does not arrive.

The failure of an ordinary sustaining ritual is the first indication that K.’s morning no longer belongs entirely to him.

By Chapter Four, Mrs. Grubach brings K. his breakfast herself.

She no longer trusts the maid with even this small task because she is frightened of upsetting him after their quarrel.

She cries.

She searches for reconciliation.

She interprets even K.’s sharp questions as a possible sign of forgiveness.

K. is thinking about Miss Bürstner.

When Miss Montag’s invitation arrives, he reads it through his grievance.

Then Mrs. Grubach observes that he has barely touched breakfast.

K. shouts at her to take it away.

The breakfast motif has changed sides.

The man whose breakfast was disrupted now produces the emotional weather around somebody else’s breakfast service.

That does not make the two situations equal.

It shows how quickly disruption can become relational habit.

The question after [[🍳_why_josefs_breakfast]] is therefore not only:

What do we do when breakfast stops arriving?

It is also:

**What do we do so that, after our own breakfast has been ruined, we do not spend the rest of the morning ruining everybody else’s?**

## 🧩 An opaque process is not the same as an unwanted answer

Miss Montag creates another strange echo of the court.

K. wants direct access to Miss Bürstner.

Instead, a representative appears.

She speaks on Miss Bürstner’s behalf.

She reformulates the issue.

She presents herself as comparatively impartial.

She says that the requested meeting is unnecessary.

She does not provide every underlying reason.

K. immediately begins reading the arrangement as another process happening around him.

There is, however, a crucial difference.

This time K. has an answer.

No.

He may dislike the route.

He may distrust the intermediary.

He may believe Miss Montag is exaggerating the importance of the situation.

He may suspect social manoeuvring.

He may feel embarrassed.

He may still want to hear the answer directly from Miss Bürstner.

None of those things convert the answer into yes.

This distinction matters for Polaris.

A person inside an illegible process deserves routes that are accessible, reviewable, proportionate, and sufficiently clear.

But legibility does not guarantee the outcome the person wants.

Sometimes orientation means continuing to challenge an inaccessible process.

Sometimes orientation means recognising that an answer has become sufficiently clear.

And stopping.

---

## 🧨 Distress does not make the method reasonable

K.’s distress matters.

The court really has intruded into his life.

His domestic environment really has been disrupted.

He really has been subjected to an unexplained process.

His sense of ordinary social footing really has changed.

None of that means every method he reaches for in response is reasonable.

This is an important guardrail for survivor-centred work.

A person can be right about what happened to them and wrong about what they are entitled to demand from somebody else.

A person can need explanation from an institution without being entitled to explanation from every individual connected to their distress.

A person can deserve procedural access without possessing interpersonal access.

A person can be frightened without another person becoming responsible for removing all uncertainty.

A person can need repair without being entitled to compel somebody else into the role of repairer.

The reality of distress does not automatically validate the method chosen to relieve it.

That is not a withdrawal of compassion.

It is part of making compassion safe.

## 🧄 What kind of power does not need domination?

This is where the node turns toward [[../🧄_Exousiología/README|🧄 Exousiología]].

If violence teaches particular practices of power, then interrupting violence requires more than telling people not to be violent.

We need alternatives for what power is for.

Who gets to approach?

Who gets to refuse?

Who controls the room?

Who defines the meaning of silence?

Who is entitled to an explanation?

Who can require participation?

What does authority owe the person subject to it?

What does one person owe another when there is no legitimate authority between them?

What does strength look like when it does not require another person’s compliance?

K. repeatedly tries to recover power through access.

Access to the room.

Access to the conversation.

Access to the explanation.

Access to Miss Bürstner.

But another practice of power is possible.

Power can include the capacity to stop.

To tolerate an unanswered question.

To refuse to convert distress into entitlement.

To leave a door closed.

To protect another person’s right to say no even when their no hurts.

To challenge institutions strongly while refusing to reproduce institutional domination in intimate life.

The alternative to domination is not powerlessness.

It is another practice of power.

## ☁️ We have to imagine what comes after repetition

This is also why the cycle belongs beside [[../☁️_Kabbalist_Futurism/README|☁️ Kabbalist Futurism]].

Diagnosis is not enough.

If broken relations reproduce broken relations, then the task is not merely to identify the break.

It is to ask what practices make another future more likely.

What do we repeat until care becomes ordinary?

What do we practise until consent becomes easier to recognise than entitlement?

What kinds of institutions teach restraint rather than domination?

What kinds of communities make repair possible without demanding access to the person harmed?

What kinds of authority remain legible without becoming total?

What kinds of strength can survive refusal?

What does a repaired informational environment feel like in the body?

A future is not built only by removing bad machinery.

People need other relational forms available to them.

Otherwise the old methods remain the methods everyone already knows.

## 🌌 We learn from each other

There is a hopeful fact underneath the frightening one.

Human beings are permeable to one another.

That permeability is part of how violence travels.

It is also part of how violence stops.

The author has learned a great deal from Black futurism imagined, shared, argued, made beautiful, made strange, and made possible in online space.

That intellectual debt is worth naming.

Black futurist traditions are not interchangeable with Kabbalist Futurism, and learning from them does not make them the author’s to absorb or rename.

Their histories remain their histories.

Their traditions remain distinct.

But encounter matters.

Online space can allow people to encounter traditions, aesthetics, survival practices, political thought, speculative worlds, humour, scholarship, art, and ways of imagining life beyond structures that once presented themselves as permanent.

People learn.

They carry something away.

They make something with what they have learned.

Other people encounter that.

The thread continues.

We all learn something from each other, and weave something greater than ourselves.

The threads do not have to become indistinguishable for the weaving to matter.

---

## 🧶 The other cycle

Violence is repetitive.

But repetition itself does not belong to violence.

There is another cycle available.

We inherit.

We encounter.

We learn.

We transform.

We contribute.

Someone else encounters what we made.

Care can propagate.

Consent can become practised.

Restraint can become ordinary.

Good authority can teach expectations of good authority.

People who have experienced domination can learn forms of power that do not require domination.

Communities can transmit repair.

Art can transmit possibility.

Theory can give language to something another person had felt but could not yet name.

Someone else’s imagined future can make our own future easier to imagine.

If an information environment can train a body toward vigilance and control, another environment can help train bodies toward safety, curiosity, reciprocity, and refusal without catastrophe.

We do not escape repetition by becoming untouched by one another.

We escape violent repetition by becoming more deliberate about what we pass on.

## 🧭 The Polaris move

Polaris cannot promise that a harmed person will never reproduce harm.

It can build interruption points.

What has happened to me?

What did it teach me to expect?

What am I doing because it is useful?

What am I doing because it is familiar?

Am I asking an institution for accountability, or an individual for emotional regulation?

Has somebody actually failed to answer me, or have they given an answer I do not want?

Am I documenting movement, or watching a person?

Am I seeking lawful access to a process, or treating another person’s boundary as an obstruction?

Am I trying to restore my own agency by reducing somebody else’s?

What would stopping look like?

What would another practice of power look like?

Who has already imagined something better that I can learn from?

What do I want the next person to learn from me?

The protocol is not there to make a person morally perfect.

It is there to create enough orientation for choice to re-enter the cycle.

## 🧯 Guardrail

This node is not permission to claim that people who experience violence inevitably become violent.

It is not permission to stigmatise survivors as dangerous.

It is not permission to equate institutional violence with every interpersonal boundary violation.

It is not permission to flatten differences in power, scale, intent, consequence, or responsibility.

It is not permission to excuse harmful behaviour because the person causing harm was previously harmed.

It is not permission to diagnose trauma from behaviour.

It is not permission to demand forgiveness, reconciliation, access, conversation, or emotional labour from a person who has said no.

It is not permission to appropriate Black futurist traditions, collapse them into other traditions, or erase the particular histories from which they emerged.

The discipline is narrower and stronger:

Notice repetition.

Ask what has been learned.

Ask what has become embodied.

Separate explanation from excuse.

Separate institutional accountability from interpersonal entitlement.

Respect refusal.

Interrupt the familiar method.

Learn alternatives.

Practise them.

Pass something better on.

---

## 🧩 Working definition

The repetitive-cycle problem is what happens when violence becomes familiar enough to supply the methods through which a person later manages uncertainty, fear, humiliation, conflict, access, or power.

The repetition does not require identical acts.

It does not require equal power.

It does not require conscious imitation.

The pattern can survive while changing direction, scale, justification, and setting.

But human permeability is not only a vulnerability.

People can also learn care, consent, restraint, repair, imagination, and non-dominating forms of power from one another.

Systems reproduce through people.

People can also become the place where reproduction stops.
