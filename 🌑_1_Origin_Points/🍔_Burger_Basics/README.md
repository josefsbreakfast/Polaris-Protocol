# 🍔 Burger Basics  
**First created:** 2026-08-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-18  
*Cross-disciplinary systems literacy for understanding the technical, legal, institutional, and human stacks that make modern governance work — and the Frankenburgers that emerge when they don't.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Modern government is built out of stacks.

Some of them look like technology:

**databases → APIs → identity systems → permissions → models → interfaces → applications**

Some of them look like bureaucracy:

**law → regulation → guidance → policy → procedure → staff interpretation → decision**

Some look like markets:

**government → prime contractor → subcontractor → supplier → platform → infrastructure**

And increasingly, these are not separate systems.

They are **one sociotechnical environment**.

A legal power becomes a technical permission.  
A database output becomes an administrative judgement.  
A procurement decision determines which infrastructure can be used.  
A security classification determines who can see what.  
A staff member interprets information produced several systems away.  
That decision creates another record, which becomes somebody else's input.

Eventually everyone can be doing their individual job correctly while the **aggregate system is doing something catastrophically stupid**.

Welcome to Burger Basics. 🍔

---

## 🍔 Why Burgers?

Because stacks are easier to understand when you can see the layers.

A burger can contain perfectly respectable ingredients and still become horrifying when assembled badly.

The same is true of information systems.

The important unit of analysis is therefore not always the ingredient.

Sometimes it is **the burger**.

A lawful power does not guarantee a lawful or sensible aggregate outcome.

A technically functioning database does not guarantee that its information is being interpreted appropriately downstream.

A competent member of staff cannot compensate for information they cannot see.

A sensible security restriction can create a governance failure somewhere else.

A good commercial product can create an undesirable strategic dependency.

A perfectly functioning interface can efficiently transport the wrong information.

**A good ingredient does not guarantee a good burger.**

Burger Basics provides a deliberately accessible language for taking these systems apart, examining their layers, and putting them back together conceptually before somebody does it for real.

## ⚡️ The Frankenburger

Technology and information governance already have a useful monster: the **Frankenstack**.

Frankenstacks emerge when systems, products, databases, suppliers, workarounds, interfaces, and legacy infrastructure accumulate across time without forming one coherent architecture.

Burger Basics takes the idea further.

The **Frankenburger** is a deliberately toxic teaching model.

Instead of waiting for a terrible stack to emerge accidentally, we can construct one on paper.

Take:

- a questionable data source;
- a probabilistic identity match;
- a risk classification;
- an automated information-sharing mechanism;
- an institution that trusts the upstream classification;
- a security restriction preventing somebody downstream from seeing its provenance;
- an outsourced technical component;
- an unclear correction process;
- several different legal regimes;
- and a human being expected to make a sensible decision at the end.

Stack them.

Then ask what happens.

The point is not that every ingredient is inherently harmful.

The point is to expose **interaction risk, emergent behaviour, attack surface, propagation, and missing ownership**.

The Frankenburger lets us make the invisible architecture visible.

## 🇬🇧 Britain Has Stacks

The United Kingdom is an especially useful place to learn this way of thinking.

British bureaucracy is itself an enormous collection of stacks.

Law sits on law.  
Policy sits on law.  
Guidance sits on policy.  
Departments interact with agencies.  
Local government interacts with central government.  
Public bodies contract private suppliers.  
Old infrastructure interacts with new infrastructure.  
Information moves between institutions operating under different duties, cultures, technical systems, and levels of secrecy.

Britain also has a substantial legacy technology estate.

That should not be mistaken for evidence that Britain is inherently bad at technology.

Britain was an early and important computing environment. Early adoption itself creates technological strata: systems are built, extended, connected, partially replaced, outsourced, patched, modernised, and connected again.

Later commercial consolidation, international supply chains, procurement decisions, vendor dependency, and the enormous growth of US technology companies have added further layers.

The result is not simply **old computers**.

The real UK Frankenstack can include:

**law + policy + institutions + humans + databases + vendors + contracts + security classifications + procurement + algorithms + old infrastructure + new infrastructure + information-sharing + institutional culture**

That is the system we need to learn to see.

## 🌉 Policy Stack ↔ Technology Stack

One purpose of Burger Basics is to bring people from **technology, law, policy, governance, and civil society** into the same conceptual room.

Policy people do not need to become software engineers.

Technologists do not need to become lawyers.

They need enough shared systems literacy to recognise when they need each other.

Many apparently technical concepts already have familiar bureaucratic cousins:

| Technology | Policy / bureaucracy |
| --- | --- |
| API | statutory information gateway / formal handoff |
| permissions | delegated authority |
| database record | administrative record |
| identifier | case/reference identity |
| dependency | consequential policy reliance |
| logging | audit trail |
| legacy code | inherited procedure |
| technical debt | accumulated administrative workaround |
| patch | emergency policy fix |
| deprecated system | technically superseded procedure everyone inexplicably still uses |
| garbage in, garbage out | a procedurally immaculate decision based on shit information remains a shit decision |

The analogy is not exact.

It does not need to be.

It provides a bridge.

A policy-maker asking why an engineer cannot simply remove one legacy component may understand the answer considerably faster when asked:

> **Why can't we just delete this paragraph from the statutory guidance?**

Dependencies.

Welcome to tech.

Likewise, an engineer who thinks law is simply text wrapped around a technical system may discover something familiar.

Law has architecture.

It has permissions, inheritance, interfaces, dependencies, precedence, handoffs, edge cases, legacy structures, and failure propagation too.

Welcome to bureaucracy.

## 💻 Tech Basics

Burger Basics will include a dedicated **Tech Basics** layer.

This is not Computer Science 101.

Its purpose is to teach enough technology for someone working in law, policy, Parliament, regulation, journalism, civil society, or public administration to ask **competent questions about systems they are responsible for governing**.

You do not necessarily need to know how to build an API.

You may need to know enough to ask:

- what information crosses it;
- who requested that information;
- what authentication occurred;
- whether the interaction was logged;
- what the recipient retains;
- whether provenance travels with the information;
- whether uncertainty travels with it;
- and what happens downstream when the originating record changes.

The same principle applies to identifiers, databases, caching, permissions, models, classifiers, cloud infrastructure, logs, interoperability, legacy systems, and other technical concepts.

The objective is not mastery.

It is **governance literacy**.

## ⚖️ Legal And Policy Basics

The translation has to work in the other direction too.

Technical staff need enough legal and institutional literacy to recognise when something that looks like an engineering decision has acquired a different significance.

Moving information from one database to another is technically ordinary.

But the second environment may have:

- a different statutory purpose;
- a different controller;
- different access rights;
- different retention requirements;
- different evidential consequences;
- different correction mechanisms;
- or the power to make decisions with substantial effects on somebody's life.

A duplicated record is therefore not always merely **duplicate data**.

Sometimes it is a new governance event.

Technologists do not need to become barristers.

They need enough literacy to recognise **when to call one**.

## 🔐 Security Is Not The Same As Governance

Defence, intelligence, and national-security readers are welcome here.

They are not, however, the assumed audience.

Cybersecurity frequently operates across environments where some information genuinely cannot be publicly disclosed.

Civilian governance cannot therefore depend upon everyone receiving unrestricted access to everything.

But the opposite approach does not work either.

If every difficult question touching national security disappears behind classification, the people responsible for healthcare, law, regulation, Parliament, or civilian administration may become unable to govern the systems for which they remain responsible.

Burger Basics therefore works from a simple distinction:

> **Secret capability does not require secret governance.**

Someone may legitimately be unable to know precisely how a sensitive capability works.

They can still learn to ask whether:

- it has been independently tested;
- errors can be detected;
- outputs enter civilian systems;
- uncertainty and provenance are preserved;
- consequential decisions receive human review;
- records expire;
- corrections propagate;
- appropriate audit exists;
- and somebody is accountable for the aggregate outcome.

You do not always need access to the protected answer to know whether **the right question has been answered**.

## 🕸️ The Stack Is Also An Attack Surface

Frankenstacks are not merely inefficient.

They are exploitable.

A seam between two systems can become a pathway for:

- error;
- manipulation;
- inappropriate access;
- poisoned information;
- misplaced institutional trust;
- privilege escalation;
- cascading failure;
- or deliberate adversarial intervention.

An attacker does not necessarily need to compromise the entire architecture.

Sometimes it is enough to discover:

**A influences B.  
B triggers C.  
C produces the desired real-world consequence.**

Repeat the interaction.

Observe the result.

Learn the system.

This is why information governance, cybersecurity, institutional design, and national security cannot be treated as completely separate disciplines.

If information can propagate through the stack, **influence can propagate through the stack**.

## 🎯 Find The Leverage, Not Just The Logo

Stacks also matter when trying to change systems.

The most visible organisation is not necessarily the most useful intervention point.

The largest company is not necessarily the easiest company to influence.

The most morally objectionable actor is not necessarily the node where intervention produces the greatest downstream effect.

A supply chain can contain dozens of firms, subsidiaries, contractors, financiers, platforms, component manufacturers, insurers, logistics providers, and institutional customers.

A campaign aimed at changing the system therefore needs to ask:

**What outcome are we actually trying to produce?**

Then:

- where does the relevant money travel?
- where does the relevant information travel?
- where does authority travel?
- which relationships are replaceable?
- which nodes are vulnerable to legal, financial, commercial, democratic, or reputational pressure?
- which intervention has the clearest evidence?
- which has the broadest defensible coalition?
- what is easiest to change?
- what produces the greatest material effect?
- and what else changes if we succeed?

**Visibility is not leverage.**

A new, highly salient target should not automatically displace an unresolved, lower-contestability, higher-leverage intervention.

New problem?

Add it to the map.

**Do not overwrite the fucking map.**

## 💷 Follow The Risk

Sometimes the strongest intervention is the one that requires the least ideological agreement.

A lawyer, investor, engineer, regulator, activist, MP, and accountant may disagree profoundly about the political meaning of an issue while still recognising the same governance vulnerability.

That matters.

A strong systems intervention can sometimes be justified simultaneously through:

- legal risk;
- financial risk;
- operational risk;
- cyber risk;
- reputational risk;
- human-rights risk;
- procurement risk;
- strategic dependency;
- and institutional resilience.

Burger Basics therefore distinguishes between:

**moral culpability** — how objectionable is the activity?

**causal importance** — how much does this node contribute to the outcome?

**intervention leverage** — how feasible is changing it, and what changes if we do?

**contestability** — how much additional disagreement must be resolved before action becomes possible?

These are related.

They are not the same thing.

Good intervention design knows the difference.

## 🌐 Dependency Is A Choice

Using foreign technology is not inherently a loss of sovereignty.

International cooperation, commercial procurement, alliances, specialist suppliers, and global technology markets can create enormous benefits.

The relevant question is whether **meaningful choice survives the dependency**.

Can an institution change supplier?

Can data be moved?

Can systems interoperate without permanent lock-in?

Can government enforce its own governance requirements?

Can Britain disagree with an allied government or foreign corporation without disabling essential infrastructure?

Does domestic expertise still exist?

Could missing capability be rebuilt?

Is there an exit plan?

Formal authority is not the same as practical control.

A government may retain the legal power to say **no** while constructing an architecture in which exercising that power becomes prohibitively expensive.

Burger Basics therefore treats technological sovereignty not as:

> **BUILD EVERYTHING HERE.**

but as:

> **Understand your systems well enough that you retain meaningful choices.**

## 🧿 What Burger Basics Is For

This cluster exists to build **cross-disciplinary systems literacy for civilian governance**.

It is for:

- policy-makers who need to interrogate technical systems;
- technologists who need to understand the institutional environments their systems enter;
- lawyers who need to trace technical causation;
- regulators who need to see beyond individual organisations;
- MPs who need to ask useful questions even when some answers are classified;
- journalists who need to distinguish a company from a supply chain;
- civil society trying to identify effective intervention points;
- and anyone who has ever been told that something is far too complicated for them to question.

You do not need to understand every ingredient before you are allowed to ask what the burger is doing.

Sometimes the first useful question is simply:

> **Can somebody show me the whole fucking burger?**
