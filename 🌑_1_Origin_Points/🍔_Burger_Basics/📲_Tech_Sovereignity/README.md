# 📲 Tech Sovereignty
**First created:** 2026-08-23 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-23  
*Understanding sovereignty in technological systems as the preservation of meaningful agency, control, choice, and exit — from the device in your hand to the infrastructure of a state.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Technology has made ownership weird.

You can own a phone without controlling every process running on it.

A government can own infrastructure while depending upon a foreign supplier to maintain it.

An institution can be legally responsible for data stored inside systems it does not meaningfully understand.

A country can retain the formal authority to reject a technology while becoming so dependent upon it that saying **no** would impose enormous operational costs.

None of these situations automatically means sovereignty has disappeared.

They do mean we need to be considerably more precise about what sovereignty actually is.

**Tech sovereignty is not technological isolation.**

It does not mean:

> BUILD EVERYTHING YOURSELF.

It means retaining enough knowledge, authority, capability, interoperability, accountability, and practical freedom that **meaningful choices remain possible**.

This applies at several scales:

**person → institution → state**

The details change.

The underlying question does not.

> **Who actually gets to decide what this technology does?**

---

## 🍔 Where This Fits In Burger Basics

Burger Basics treats modern governance as a sociotechnical stack:

**law + policy + institutions + humans + databases + vendors + contracts + security classifications + procurement + algorithms + infrastructure + information-sharing**

Tech sovereignty asks what happens when **control is distributed across that stack**.

A government department may be legally sovereign over a decision while depending upon:

- information collected elsewhere;
- software supplied by a contractor;
- infrastructure operated by another company;
- technical expertise held outside government;
- proprietary systems it cannot inspect;
- security information it cannot fully access;
- contractual relationships expensive to unwind;
- and upstream classifications it has little ability to challenge.

Likewise, an individual may own the physical device through which they encounter an enormous number of systems they neither operate nor control.

The interesting question is therefore rarely:

> **Who owns the thing?**

It is:

> **Who can make the thing behave differently?**

That is a much better sovereignty question.

## 📲 Your Phone Is An Endpoint, But It Is Also Your Room

A personal device is technically an endpoint.

It is also where somebody lives a substantial portion of their modern life.

Phones can contain or mediate:

- private conversations;
- photographs;
- medical information;
- financial information;
- legal communications;
- intimate relationships;
- location information;
- authentication;
- work;
- political activity;
- reading;
- music;
- memories;
- identity;
- access to public services;
- and increasingly the mechanisms required to participate in ordinary society.

That makes the device unusual.

It is simultaneously:

**hardware + software + communications infrastructure + identity token + sensor platform + personal archive + social environment**

Calling it merely an endpoint can therefore obscure the human significance of what happens there.

A technically minor intervention can be experientially enormous.

A notification changes.

A message disappears.

A recommendation appears.

An account becomes inaccessible.

A location is inferred incorrectly.

A security decision prevents an action.

A platform modifies what information reaches the user.

Some of these behaviours may be mundane consequences of ordinary technical systems.

Others may arise from legitimate security, fraud, moderation, legal, administrative, or operational processes.

The sovereignty question remains useful regardless:

> **What authority permits the intervention, what exactly may that authority do, and what protects the person at the endpoint from inappropriate use?**

## 🗝️ Ownership, Access, Control And Authority Are Different Things

These concepts are frequently collapsed.

They should not be.

### Ownership

Who legally possesses the device, system, infrastructure, intellectual property, or other asset?

### Access

Who can enter, query, modify, administer, observe, or otherwise interact with it?

### Control

Who can materially determine how it behaves?

### Authority

Who is legitimately permitted to exercise that access or control, for what purpose, and under what conditions?

These categories can belong to different actors.

You can own a phone while a manufacturer controls parts of its operating system.

A company can operate infrastructure while another company owns the physical data centre.

A public authority can have lawful access to information without owning the system containing it.

An administrator can possess technical capability without possessing legitimate authority to use that capability however they like.

That final distinction matters enormously.

> **Capability is not permission.**

And permission for one purpose is not automatically permission for another.

---

## 🔐 Security Access Is Not General Sovereignty

Some technological environments require privileged access.

Security teams need administrative capabilities.

Fraud systems need to identify suspicious activity.

Platforms need mechanisms for enforcing rules.

Law enforcement may obtain lawful powers of access.

Intelligence and national-security systems may operate under authorities that cannot be publicly described in full.

None of this produces a universal licence to control the environment.

The existence of legitimate privileged access therefore creates a governance requirement rather than eliminating one.

Questions still exist:

- Who authorised the access?
- What purpose permits it?
- What technical capability was exercised?
- Was the action necessary and proportionate?
- What was logged?
- Who can audit the action?
- How long is resulting information retained?
- Can it propagate elsewhere?
- Can the purpose change downstream?
- What happens if the information is wrong?
- How does correction travel?
- What remedy exists if authority is exceeded?

The more powerful the technical capability, the more important these questions become.

**Secret capability does not require secret governance.**

---

## 👁️ Security Monitoring Is Not Behavioural Ownership

Security systems frequently need to observe behaviour.

That does not mean they should own it.

There is an important difference between:

**detecting risk**

and

**controlling somebody's informational environment because they have been classified as risky.**

The distinction can become particularly important when systems are interconnected.

A risk flag created for one purpose may travel into another environment.

A security classification may affect permissions.

A fraud score may alter access.

A moderation decision may affect discoverability.

An identity mismatch may create administrative friction elsewhere.

A legitimate upstream process can therefore generate downstream consequences that nobody explicitly intended.

This is Burger Basics territory.

**Good ingredient. Bad burger.**

The relevant governance question is not merely whether the original process was permitted.

It is whether somebody remains responsible for understanding what happens **after the output leaves that process**.

## 🏛️ Institutional Sovereignty

Institutions face the same problem at a larger scale.

A public body can formally control a system while having remarkably little practical ability to change it.

Consider an organisation that depends upon:

- proprietary software;
- specialist contractors;
- external cloud infrastructure;
- undocumented legacy systems;
- outsourced expertise;
- bespoke integrations;
- expensive migration;
- restrictive licensing;
- inaccessible historical data;
- or suppliers whose products have become embedded across essential workflows.

On paper, the organisation remains in charge.

In practice:

> **Can it leave?**

If the answer is **not without catastrophic cost**, the dependency has acquired governance significance.

Institutional sovereignty therefore requires more than procurement authority.

It requires enough internal understanding to know:

- what the system does;
- what information enters it;
- where information goes;
- who can access it;
- what other systems depend upon it;
- what happens when it fails;
- what contractual rights exist;
- what technical alternatives exist;
- and how the organisation would exit if necessary.

You cannot govern a dependency you cannot map.

## 🌐 National Technological Sovereignty

At national scale, the same principle becomes strategically important.

Modern states depend upon international technology ecosystems.

That is normal.

Semiconductors, cloud infrastructure, telecommunications, software, cybersecurity products, satellites, undersea cables, industrial control systems, financial infrastructure, AI systems, operating systems, and specialist components exist inside international markets and alliances.

Attempting complete technological autarky would often be extraordinarily expensive, inefficient, or impossible.

Tech sovereignty therefore asks a different question:

> **Does dependency preserve meaningful national choice?**

Can the state:

- regulate the technology?
- inspect what matters?
- impose governance requirements?
- move its data?
- change supplier?
- maintain interoperability?
- continue essential services during political disagreement?
- understand strategic dependencies?
- retain domestic technical expertise?
- rebuild missing capability if necessary?
- resist unacceptable contractual conditions?
- and say **no** without disabling itself?

Formal sovereignty is insufficient if practical dependency makes exercising it impossible.

## 🤝 Sovereignty Is Compatible With Cooperation

Tech sovereignty is not anti-alliance.

It is not anti-trade.

It is not anti-American, anti-European, anti-Chinese, anti-cloud, anti-commercial, or anti-technology.

International cooperation can increase sovereignty by giving states access to capabilities they could not efficiently maintain alone.

Shared standards can increase choice.

Interoperability can reduce lock-in.

Allied technical cooperation can improve resilience.

Commercial suppliers can provide expertise governments would struggle to reproduce internally.

The problem begins when cooperation quietly becomes **irreversibility**.

Healthy dependency allows participants to retain agency.

Unhealthy dependency makes disagreement operationally impossible.

A good alliance should therefore tolerate:

**yes**

and

**no**

and occasionally:

**you were wrong about that.**

If disagreement itself threatens the functioning of essential infrastructure, the architecture has acquired a political property that should be visible to the people governing it.

## 🚪 Exit Is Infrastructure

One of the simplest tests of technological sovereignty is therefore:

> **Where is the exit?**

Exit does not mean that somebody intends to leave.

It means leaving remains technically and institutionally possible.

For a personal service, that might mean:

- data portability;
- account deletion;
- interoperable formats;
- alternative providers.

For an institution:

- migration plans;
- documented systems;
- transferable data;
- retained internal expertise;
- replacement suppliers;
- contractual exit provisions.

For a state:

- diversified suppliers;
- strategic reserves;
- domestic capability;
- allied alternatives;
- interoperability;
- resilient infrastructure;
- technical education;
- independent governance capacity.

An exit that exists only theoretically is not much of an exit.

**Exit capability is part of the architecture.**

## 🧠 Knowledge Is Sovereignty

There is another dependency that is easier to miss.

Expertise.

An institution can outsource technology without outsourcing responsibility.

But if it outsources enough technical knowledge, eventually it may lose the ability to determine whether its supplier is giving it good advice.

That creates an uncomfortable situation:

> **The organisation responsible for governing the system must ask the organisation selling the system to explain whether the system is governable.**

This does not require malicious suppliers.

The structural problem exists even when everybody is competent and acting in good faith.

Different organisations have different incentives, information, obligations, and fields of vision.

Maintaining internal expertise therefore provides more than technical capacity.

It preserves the ability to ask independent questions.

**Knowing how your burger works is part of owning the burger.**

---

## ⚖️ Sovereignty Requires Accountability

Control without accountability is not a particularly attractive form of sovereignty.

Neither is accountability without control.

A useful technological governance environment therefore needs both:

**agency + constraint**

People and institutions need enough authority to make meaningful decisions.

Those exercising powerful technical capabilities need enough constraint that those decisions remain legitimate.

That means building systems where consequential actions can, where appropriate, be:

- attributed;
- logged;
- reviewed;
- challenged;
- corrected;
- reversed;
- explained;
- and remedied.

The precise mechanism will differ according to the system.

The principle does not.

If nobody can determine who changed something, why it changed, what authority permitted the change, or how to put it back, the architecture has a sovereignty problem.

---

## 🕸️ Sovereignty Is A Stack Property

There is no single sovereignty button.

A country can have excellent domestic infrastructure and terrible procurement governance.

An institution can have excellent contracts and no internal expertise.

A person can own their hardware while having little practical control over the services required to use it.

A state can have legal authority while depending upon infrastructure it cannot replace.

A technically sovereign system can still be democratically unaccountable.

A democratically accountable system can still be technologically dependent.

This is why sovereignty has to be examined across the whole stack:

**law → authority → procurement → infrastructure → software → permissions → information → expertise → operations → accountability → exit**

A weakness in one layer can constrain choices elsewhere.

Tech sovereignty is therefore not a product.

It is an **architectural property**.

---

## 🧿 The Basic Questions

When assessing technological sovereignty, start here:

1. **Who owns it?**
2. **Who operates it?**
3. **Who can access it?**
4. **Who can change it?**
5. **Who is authorised to change it?**
6. **Who knows how it works?**
7. **Who can see what happened?**
8. **Who bears the consequences when it fails?**
9. **Who can correct an error?**
10. **What dependencies sit underneath it?**
11. **What happens if the parties disagree?**
12. **Can we leave?**

You do not need to understand every technical component before asking these questions.

You are allowed to ask whether the burger belongs to you **before somebody explains the molecular structure of the cheese**.

---

## 🍔 The Burger Basics Rule

Tech sovereignty can ultimately be reduced to one Burger Basics principle:

> **If somebody formally has the right to choose but the architecture makes meaningful choice impossible, look at the architecture.**

The problem may be technical.

It may be contractual.

It may be informational.

It may be institutional.

It may be financial.

It may be legal.

It may be strategic.

Usually, it is several of them at once.

That is why we map the whole burger.
