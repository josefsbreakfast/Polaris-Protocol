# ⏰ Get Better Timekeepers
**First created:** 2026-10-25 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-25  
*On Jewish plurality, public timing, safeguarding, and the relationships a united antifascist front requires.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation — Chaps. Perhaps Thursday.

I have a modest proposal for institutions that wish to speak on behalf of Jewish communities: **get better timekeepers**.

This is not a request to stop speaking about antisemitism. Antisemitism is real, and Jewish children deserve protection from it. Nor is it a suggestion that Jewish organisations should need permission from other communities before making a public statement.

It is a request to remember that public speech happens *in time*, among other people, whose histories, emergencies, commemorations and organising efforts do not disappear simply because an institution has reached the publication date on its own calendar.

Some recent coverage has hurt me deeply. I am not pretending otherwise. But I am also **refusing to believe that every person involved meant to be harmful in this way**. If the outcomes were not what the people involved intended, then we ought to be able to discuss how to prevent them recurring.

Presumably, what you need are better schedulers?

The proposition sounds small because it is supposed to. It is an invitation to fix something without first requiring every participant to be declared malicious. Yet there is a serious political reason to care about the apparently small things.

**Fascism is best met with a united front.**

And united fronts are not assembled out of nowhere on the morning they become necessary.

---

## 🦁 Fascism Is Best Met With a United Front

A united front is not a single voice. It is not a single religious position, a single theory of Jewish identity, or a demand that everybody agree about Israel and Palestine before they may stand together against fascism.

It is a practical arrangement among people who recognise a shared threat and retain enough mutual trust to act against it. That trust is built through ordinary work: defending one another, turning up, sharing expertise, taking other people's concerns seriously, and not treating every disagreement as grounds for expulsion.

Jewish antifascist traditions have never been politically uniform. Bundist, socialist, religious, secular, liberal and other Jewish organising histories have differed, sometimes sharply, in their priorities and strategies. They are not interchangeable. But the history is sufficient to disprove the idea that Jewish political life must be channelled through one authorised institutional account of what Jewish people believe.

Coalition does not mean surrendering Jewish particularity. It means that Jewish people can be particular *and* act with others. Nor does it mean overlooking antisemitism in movements that claim to oppose fascism. Solidarity has to work in both directions, or it is not much of a front.

The practical question for any institution concerned about Jewish safety is therefore not merely, *Did our statement achieve attention?* It is also, *What did our statement do to the relationships on which Jewish safety may depend?*

---

## 🪞 Whose Jewishness Gets Recognised?

A government department, school system or media organisation may become more willing to recognise antisemitism, while becoming no better at recognising the diversity of Jewish people. These are distinct outcomes.

If the public account of Jewishness becomes too closely associated with one institutional or political position, the recognition achieved may be remarkably narrow. It may benefit some Jewish people and leave others having to explain why their Jewishness does not resemble the version that has just been presented as representative.

Jewish lives include religious and secular practices, different relationships to Zionism and anti-Zionism, different experiences of racism, disability, sexuality, class and migration, and different histories of institutional protection or failure. These are overlapping lives, not tidy administrative categories.

Recognition of antisemitism is valuable. But **recognising antisemitism and recognising Jewish plurality are not the same administrative task**. A school can improve its response to antisemitic harassment while still struggling to understand a Jewish pupil who disagrees with a prominent communal organisation. That is a possibility worth designing against, not an inevitable result of any particular guidance.

A representative organisation may speak for its members, its trustees or the constituency its mandate actually covers. It does not thereby acquire authority over every Jewish person's identity, political associations or understanding of Jewish safety.

If the intended good is protection for Jewish people, then we should ask which Jewish people the institution has learned to see.

---

## 🧶 The Jews You Leave Outside

There is a further consequence to narrow representation, and it is not merely a matter of hurt feelings.

Jewish people already participate in Palestinian solidarity, disability advocacy, anti-racist organising, child-protection work, survivor-led initiatives, LGBTQ+ organising, interfaith relationships and local mutual aid. Some do this explicitly as Jews. Others do not lead with Jewish identity every time they enter a room. Neither choice makes their Jewishness conditional.

When prominent institutions present a narrow account of Jewish interests, those other Jews may find themselves spending additional time disentangling their own identities and work from positions they did not choose and may not share. Relationships can become harder to establish or sustain. People who were not consulted may bear the practical cost of the public representation.

Call these **institutional representation externalities**: costs that fall beyond the organisation making the decision, including upon people it claims to protect.

These consequences should be investigated rather than assumed to be uniform or universal. But institutions ought to have some way to hear about them.

And here is the political point: **if you cannot be part of a particular antifascist unity, do not be surprised when the Jews you have isolated find unity with others in order to defend themselves against fascism.**

They will be doing so as Jews.

This is not a threat, nor a request for permission. It is what political agency looks like. An institution may decline a coalition; it cannot decide on behalf of every Jewish person that the coalition is therefore closed to them.

## ⏰ Not Today, Perhaps

The immediate occasion for this reflection is October 2026 coverage of proposals or guidance concerning antisemitism in schools. I have been struck by how the public presentation appeared, from where I was standing, to intersect with other profoundly serious matters: the anniversary of the Aberfan disaster, reporting and discussion about institutional responses to child sexual abuse, violence affecting Palestinians in Hebron and the wider West Bank, and political controversy involving the Green Party.

**This is an account of the author's concern, not a verified chronology of coordinated announcements.** The precise dates, texts, institutional roles and media framing need to be documented before any stronger factual conclusion is drawn. In particular, I do not know whether the timing was chosen freely, whether anybody advised against it, or whether the relevant organisations anticipated the coverage that followed.

Aberfan matters here not as an interchangeable comparison to any contemporary event, but as a terrible reminder of what institutional failure can mean for children and families. Contemporary child-abuse reporting raises different questions, which deserve their own evidential treatment. Violence against Palestinian civilians is not a backdrop against which Jewish advocacy becomes illegitimate; it is human suffering that public institutions should be capable of noticing without being instructed to look away from antisemitism.

The concern is about **how an intervention lands in a shared public world**. Something an organisation experiences as an important contribution to safeguarding may be received as competing for attention with other people's grief or vulnerability. The appearance can be damaging even when the intent was entirely different.

If an announcement is urgent, say why it is urgent. If it is discretionary, ask whether a different day, a different framing or an act of collaboration would better serve the underlying purpose.

## 🕰️ Timing Is Information

The same announcement does not carry identical information for every observer.

Inside an institution, it may represent months of careful policy work and a long-awaited opportunity to be heard. To a parent, survivor, Palestinian neighbour, Jewish dissenter or person attending a memorial, it may arrive alongside events that change its apparent meaning.

This is not a licence to invent intent. Four different mechanisms should be kept separate:

| Possible mechanism | What it would mean | What is needed to establish it |
|---|---|---|
| Coincidence | Events overlap without relevant coordination. | Reliable chronology. |
| Institutional tunnel vision | Decision-makers fail to account for important outside context. | Evidence about process, consultation or omissions. |
| Opportunistic timing | Attention is deliberately sought because of another event. | Evidence supporting that decision or strategy. |
| Strategic displacement | An intervention is deliberately intended to redirect attention. | Stronger evidence of purpose and coordination. |

**Intent is not appearance. Appearance is not consequence. Consequence is not proof of intent.**

The point of better timekeeping is not to learn how to conceal indifference through clever scheduling. It is to ensure that other people's lives and vulnerabilities are actually included in the decision-making process.

## 📰 The Media Will Not Preserve Your Nuance

An institution may believe it is making a broad contribution to education, discrimination policy or safeguarding. A headline may narrow that intervention to a dispute about Israel and Palestine. Readers may then encounter an account of Jewish communal interests far smaller than the institution intended.

Journalists have responsibilities here. Organisations also have choices about what they put on the public record, whom they consult, what they foreground and whether they correct misleading impressions.

A sensible review would compare the original guidance, accompanying statements and resulting coverage. What was actually proposed? What was omitted? Which Jewish voices were heard? Which were not? Did a safeguarding proposal become a proxy for a political argument? We should not answer these questions from headlines alone.

The durable remedy is not simply better press management. It is wider participation in civic life, so that Jewish communal concern for other people is visible because it is practised, not because a communications team has found an attractive sentence about it.

---

## 🧒 Whose Children Are We Protecting?

Protection from antisemitic harassment matters. So do children's privacy, freedom of expression, opportunities to learn, and protection from failures by adults and institutions.

I am concerned by the possibility of a safeguarding conversation in which institutions become more attentive to what children say, while remaining insufficiently accountable for what adults do. But whether the particular October guidance would produce increased monitoring, data collection or disciplinary action **must be established from its actual provisions**. It should not be asserted merely because those outcomes are conceivable.

Any serious assessment should ask:

- What conduct is being identified, and how is antisemitic harassment distinguished from legitimate political disagreement?
- What information about children would be recorded, shared or retained, and under what safeguards?
- Who reviews errors, contested classifications and disproportionate effects?
- How are children and families able to challenge institutional decisions?
- What corresponding duties fall upon schools, police and other adult institutions?
- Were Jewish children with differing political and cultural experiences consulted?

**Surveillance is not synonymous with safeguarding.** Nor is the absence of surveillance automatically sufficient protection. The issue is whether the proposed intervention is proportionate, accountable and capable of protecting the children it names.

## 🇵🇸 If You Care About Israel, Pay Attention to Hebron

For organisations that place Israel's security and future at the centre of their advocacy, violence against Palestinians in the West Bank is not an unrelated concern. It is relevant to the human and political conditions in which that future will be decided.

Recognising Palestinian civilian suffering does not require indifference to antisemitism. Criticising settler violence or the conduct of Israeli authorities does not make British Jews collectively responsible for those actions. Nor should Jewish organisations be treated as though they all hold identical views about Israel.

The relevant October events, including any statements by the UN Secretary-General, require exact sourcing before being presented as a documented sequence. The ethical question does not depend on guessing those details: can institutions maintain concern for Jewish safety while also demonstrating that Palestinian lives are not invisible to them?

For Jewish people already working in Palestinian solidarity, that question is not theoretical. It can shape whether their Jewishness is welcomed, doubted or treated as an inconvenience in either community.

## 🧠 Who Is Advising You?

I do not think most people working in Jewish communal advocacy set out to make Jewish people feel alienated, make cross-community initiatives harder, or create the impression that Jewish safety is a rival claim to everybody else's safety. Such outcomes would often undermine their own stated aims.

Which brings us, with some relief, to administration.

Who checks the wider calendar? Who knows which communities are mourning? Who has spoken to Jewish people outside the organisation's usual circles? Who understands the difference between a legal safeguarding requirement and a discretionary publicity opportunity? Who is allowed to say that the work is important but the launch date is unwise?

I do not know what advice was given in any particular case. It would be unfair to invent a meeting and then criticise the imaginary people in it.

But the governance question remains useful: **does somebody have the knowledge, standing and permission to recommend waiting?**

If not, that is a fixable design problem.

---

## 🔍 Transparency Without Spilling Everything

Nobody needs to publish confidential conversations, identify vulnerable consultees or expose safeguarding-sensitive material to demonstrate that a decision-making process exists.

An organisation can explain, at a general level, who authorises major statements, how competing obligations are considered, when external perspectives are sought, how scheduling decisions are reviewed and how feedback reaches decision-makers.

Such transparency does not guarantee agreement. It does make it easier to distinguish an organisation's stated process from speculation about its motives.

If publishing a process feels difficult, begin by observing other organisations. How do survivor-led groups handle commemorative dates? How do disability organisations coordinate around major policy announcements? How do local interfaith initiatives share attention rather than compete for it? There is no shame in learning from somebody else's good administration.

---

## 📅 Borrow Somebody Else's Calendar

A better calendar is not a list of dates on which Jews must remain silent. It is a working map of a shared civic environment.

It should include Jewish religious and cultural dates in their diversity; commemorations of atrocities and institutional failures; dates important to Palestinian, Black British, migrant, disabled, queer and survivor-led communities; major inquiries and safeguarding developments; and opportunities for constructive collaboration.

It should also be maintained by people who understand that these communities overlap. A queer Jewish survivor is not required to select a single calendar before being allowed to matter.

The deeper recommendation is relational. Attend other people's commemorations when invited. Support their campaigns where there is a genuine shared objective. Listen without immediately seeking the microphone. Ask what would be useful. Sometimes the best contribution is practical help that does not require a photograph or a press release.

**Learn when other people are hurting, not merely when other people will be looking.**

## 🧰 A Better Timekeeping Protocol

Before a significant public intervention, a small review could ask:

| Check | Practical question | Possible action |
|---|---|---|
| Urgency | Must this happen now? | Distinguish an urgent warning from a discretionary launch. |
| Calendar | What anniversaries and commemorations coincide? | Adjust timing or acknowledge context. |
| Current events | What serious events are affecting other communities? | Review likely reception without assuming universal agreement. |
| Safeguarding | Are children and institutions both accounted for? | Seek specialist review. |
| Jewish plurality | Who within Jewish life has not been considered? | Broaden consultation. |
| Coalition effects | Could this create avoidable difficulties for existing relationships? | Speak to people doing that work. |
| Collaboration | Could the intervention support an existing initiative? | Cooperate rather than compete for attention. |
| Decision | Publish, amend, collaborate or postpone? | Record a proportionate rationale. |
| Learning | What happened after publication? | Listen, correct and improve the next decision. |

This is not a veto system. A genuine emergency cannot always wait for the perfect moment. Nor should every statement be expected to address every injustice.

It is simply a mechanism for noticing foreseeable consequences before other people have to carry them.

## 🤝 A United Front Is Not a Single Voice

Here is the reason all of this matters beyond one news cycle.

Fascism is best met with a united front. A united front requires relationships, and relationships are affected by how institutions behave when no emergency has yet forced them to cooperate.

Jewish people may disagree profoundly about religion, politics, Zionism, Palestine, institutions and strategy. They may nevertheless share an interest in resisting antisemitism and fascism. They may find allies among people who are themselves confronting racism, state violence, exclusion and authoritarianism.

A communal organisation may choose not to participate in a particular coalition. That is a decision it can make for itself. It is not a decision it can make for every Jew.

**If you cannot be part of that unity, do not be surprised if the Jews you isolate end up in unity with others in order to defend against fascism.**

We have not ceased to be Jewish by doing so. We have not necessarily abandoned Jewish communal interests. We may understand ourselves to be protecting those interests through relationships that an institution has failed to recognise.

And those relationships need to be reciprocal. Jewish people must be able to name and challenge antisemitism within antifascist movements. No coalition deserves the name of solidarity if participation requires Jewish people to accept hostility towards Jews.

The aim is not one authorised Jewish voice. It is the capacity to stand together where standing together matters.

---

## ⏰ Get Better Timekeepers

I am hurt by some of what I have seen. I think the consequences may extend well beyond the people whose names appear in the coverage. A narrow public account of Jewish identity can make life more difficult for Jewish people already engaged in wider civic and antifascist work. Poorly judged timing can make serious safeguarding advocacy appear indifferent to the safeguarding concerns of others.

But I am refusing to believe that everyone involved wanted those outcomes.

That leaves room for a very practical proposition.

Get better timekeepers. Give them a wider calendar and the authority to use it. Listen to Jewish people whose lives do not fit the familiar institutional template. Learn the histories and priorities of the people with whom we may need to stand. Be transparent about the process without pretending that every private conversation belongs in public. Remember that being heard is not the same as representing everybody.

You do not have to agree with everybody to stand alongside them.

You do not have to lead every initiative to support it.

And you do not have to turn every opportunity for recognition into a competition for whose suffering matters most.

**Fascism is best met with a united front.**

**Solidarity is strength, my friend.**

Chaps. Perhaps Thursday.

## 📎 Evidence and verification notes

This node is a **survivor-authored political and ethical argument**, not a completed investigative chronology. Before using the October 2026 case study to make specific claims about named institutions, the following should be checked against primary documents and contemporaneous reporting:

1. The precise school antisemitism guidance, its issuing bodies, publication date, legal status and provisions.
2. The original statements and the media coverage that followed, including the distinction between institutional wording and editorial framing.
3. The relevant October reporting about child sexual abuse and the Metropolitan Police, with careful attention to allegations, findings and procedural status.
4. The precise dates and nature of violence reported in Hebron and the wider West Bank, and the exact UN statements.
5. The Green Party controversy and whether it was substantively connected to the guidance or merely adjacent in the news cycle.
6. Any publicly documented scheduling constraints, consultation procedures or governance arrangements.

The Aberfan disaster occurred on **21 October 1966**, killing **116 children and 28 adults**. Its commemorative significance does not establish any motive behind a contemporary publication decision.

No private intent, deliberate coordination or causal effect on particular community initiatives is asserted without evidence. The author's experience of alienation is presented as her experience; the wider institutional mechanisms are proposed for examination.
