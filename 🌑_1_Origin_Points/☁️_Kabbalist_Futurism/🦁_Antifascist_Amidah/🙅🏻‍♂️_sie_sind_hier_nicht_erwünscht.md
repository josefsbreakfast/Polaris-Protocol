# 🙅🏻‍♂️ Sie Sind Hier Nicht Erwünscht
**First created:** 2026-10-10 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-10  
*Doikayt at the doorway: Jewish students, the politics of remembrance, and solidarity that does not wait for ideological consensus.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

**„Sie sind hier nicht erwünscht.“**

*You are not welcome here.*

On 9 October 2026, seven years after the antisemitic terrorist attack in Halle (Saale), members of the Jüdische Studierendenunion Deutschland (JSUD, Jewish Students’ Union of Germany) linked arms outside the synagogue and blocked Hans-Thomas Tillschneider, an AfD politician and newly elected vice-president of the Saxony-Anhalt state parliament, from entering the grounds for the commemoration. JSUD president Ron Dekel addressed him directly. Tillschneider eventually left. The confrontation was filmed and reported by German Jewish and national media. [1–5]

This is a story about young Jewish people making a decision in public, with their faces visible, at a site where a terrorist tried to murder Jewish worshippers. It is not necessary to know whether these students are Bundists, Zionists, neither, or somewhere in between to recognise what they did. Their own political positions are theirs to state. Their courage does not need to be borrowed by anybody else's faction.

There is a word for one particularly illuminating way of reading the action: **doikayt** (דאָיִקייט), the Bundist language of *hereness*. We are here. We have lives here. We can organise here. And our presence has political consequences.

There is another demand running through this account: **solidarity cannot stop at the synagogue door.** The 2019 attack did not stop there either.

This node draws on the German-language reporting listed below (the external links have not been independently rechecked during this revision) for the 2026 incident and the documented 2019 attack, while distinguishing the students’ stated reasons from the author's Bundist-inflected interpretation. It does not pretend to possess a full independently transcribed video.

## 1. 🙅🏻‍♂️ The Doorway

The scene is striking precisely because it is so uncomplicated. Young Jewish people link arms. A politician approaches a commemoration. They tell him he is not welcome. He questions them; they explain their refusal. He leaves. [1–5]

There is a great deal of German politics behind this moment. There is also a basic practical fact: **a parliamentary title is not a universal synagogue-entry pass.** Institutional office, appropriate participation in remembrance, and the consent of the community concerned are three different things.

The students were not waiting for the political establishment to decide whether Tillschneider's new office should make him acceptable at their memorial. They made a decision about the event they had come to defend.

That is an unusually clear example of living Jewish political agency at the very moment the surrounding institutional environment is changing.

The physicality matters. These students were not anonymous accounts arguing online. Their faces were visible. They took a position that could expose them to identification and harassment, although no particular subsequent targeting is asserted here. And they did it on the anniversary of a violent attack meant to terrorise Jewish people.

The location was not incidental. Seven years earlier, a man had tried to force his way through that synagogue's defences. The resonance of a line of Jewish people standing at its entrance now is powerful. **That is an interpretation of the scene, not a claim that every student consciously intended this symbolism.**

### 🛡️ Visibility is not a cost-free aesthetic

The human chain is easy to celebrate from the safety of a screen. Its participants did not have that distance. The public footage shows identifiable people standing outside a site of antisemitic violence, not an anonymous crowd protected by retrospective historical certainty. Visibility can expose people to identification, hostile circulation of images and harassment; that is a risk, **not** an assertion that specific reprisals have occurred.

Nor should the bravery be made conditional on learning their politics. I do not need their views on Jewish nationalism, the current Israeli government or the Bund to recognise a young person publicly defending Jewish remembrance. The insistence that Jews pass an ideological examination before other Jews may acknowledge their courage is itself a way of delaying solidarity. Their courage belongs to them, and they need not be converted into representatives of my preferred tradition to be recognised.

## 2. 🕯️ 9 October 2019: The Violence Did Not Stop at the Synagogue

On Yom Kippur in 2019, a far-right antisemitic attacker attempted to enter Halle's synagogue while worshippers were inside. He failed to breach the entrance. He murdered **Jana Lange** outside, then attacked a nearby kebab shop, where he murdered **Kevin Schwarze**. Others were endangered and injured. He was later convicted and sentenced to life imprisonment. [6–9]

Jana and Kevin were people, not illustrative counters in an argument about extremism. Remembering their names must come before using the attack to teach anybody a theory.

The attacker's antisemitism is not conjecture. Reporting on his own recorded statements and the criminal proceedings establishes his Holocaust denial, antisemitic conspiracy beliefs and far-right ideological framework. Reporting on material attributed to him also indicates that a mosque or left-wing centre had been contemplated as a target before he selected the synagogue. [7–9]

This does **not** mean we know every private calculation behind his subsequent selection of the kebab shop. Nor does the venue establish the ethnicity or religion of every person present. But it does mean that the attack cannot responsibly be narrated as though its danger was neatly confined to Jewish people.

A racist and antisemitic worldview may construct several populations as outsiders simultaneously. Its categories need not be consistent, historically informed or even internally coherent to be lethal.

The 2019 attack also belongs in the history of networked far-right terrorism: the attacker livestreamed the violence and referenced conspiratorial ideas circulating internationally. The informational environment was part of the violence, not an irrelevant digital afterthought. [7, 9]

### 🧾 The chronology must keep people, targets and evidence distinct

The attempted mass murder inside the synagogue, the murder of Jana Lange outside it, and the subsequent attack at Kiez-Döner in which Kevin Schwarze was murdered are connected episodes of one attack, not interchangeable locations or identities. The entrance's resistance was consequential: the attacker could not reach the worshippers inside. Yet a failed attempt to enter did not end the violence. The danger moved into surrounding public life. [6–9]

Three questions require different evidence: **What did the perpetrator publicly declare? What did investigators and the court establish? What can historians reasonably infer from his choice of targets?** His recorded ideological material and the trial provide evidence for the first two. The social meaning of a kebab shop in Germany helps frame the third, but does not give us direct access to his thoughts at each instant. This is why the analysis should neither describe the shop as a random footnote nor confidently attribute an unrecorded calculation to him.

The people inside the synagogue, the people at the kebab shop, Jana Lange and Kevin Schwarze should not disappear beneath the elegance of our framework. An expanded archival edition needs survivor testimony and trial documentation in German, with careful consent and quotation handling. The perpetrator's livestream should be discussed as an element of the attack's networked ideology, not reproduced as spectacle.

## 3. 🧿 Who Gets to Count as German?

For English-speaking readers, the kebab shop needs some German political context. It is not just a generic takeaway in the narrative.

West Germany recruited workers from Turkey from 1961 under the *Gastarbeiter* system. Generations of Turkish-German families have since built lives, businesses, neighbourhoods and political institutions in Germany. Yet the language of *migration background* and the social habit of identifying people as *Turkish* or *foreign* can persist long after someone has been born and raised in Germany.

Turkish, Turkish-German, Muslim, migrant and **racialised as Turkish** are not interchangeable categories. Nor is anti-Turkish racism identical to anti-Muslim racism. They can, however, overlap in the ways people are marked as not fully belonging.

This history is not the same as the history of Jewish migration from the Russian Empire and the Pale of Settlement, or the development of German racial antisemitism. One cannot paste the histories on top of each other. The useful comparison is in the **mechanisms**: who is imagined as an outsider; who is asked to justify their presence; whose neighbours are told to regard them as alien; and whose ordinary life becomes available for political suspicion.

The Turkish kebab shop is therefore legible in a particular German social and political landscape. The attacker's documented ideological statements make the connection between antisemitic conspiracy thinking and hostility to immigration especially important. [7–9]

A crucial correction to the tidy way such attacks are sometimes retold: the attacker does not have to restrict violence to the one group commentators have identified as the ‘main’ target. An exclusionary worldview can keep a whole list of imagined enemies.

**Solidarity across communities is not an optional decorative extra when the threats themselves cross communal boundaries.**

### 🧱 The German history English-language readers need

The 1961 West German–Turkish recruitment agreement matters because *Gastarbeiter* was an administrative description of labour recruitment that implied temporary presence, even as families made permanent lives. The later growth of Turkish-German neighbourhoods, businesses and generations born in Germany exposed the gap between **living here** and being recognised as belonging here. Citizenship rules, including the historical importance of descent-based nationality and later reforms, are part of this story. These are research directions requiring dedicated historical references in the next source pass, not propositions proven by the Halle news reports.

The history of racist violence against people identified as Turkish or migrant is not merely rhetorical. **Mölln (1992)** and **Solingen (1993)** belong in the chronology of racist arson attacks; the **National Socialist Underground (NSU)** murders and failures in their investigation belong in the history of racist terrorism and institutional response. These events are not offered as proof of the Halle attacker's private reasoning. They explain why a German reader may immediately recognise a kebab shop as socially legible within a longer history of exclusion and violence, while an English-speaking reader may miss that context. Dedicated German scholarship, official inquiry records and survivor-centred reporting must be added before these histories are treated in detail.

The analogy with Jews arriving from the Russian Empire and the Pale of Settlement is about the **production of an outsider category**, not a claim of historical equivalence. Germany already had entrenched antisemitic traditions; Jewish migration did not create them, and the Nazi genocide cannot be reduced to an automatic consequence of immigration politics. Nor are Turkish-German people an undifferentiated bloc: citizenship, religion, class, migration history and self-description vary. The comparison becomes useful only when those differences survive it.

There is a familiar rhetorical move here: a person can have been born in Germany, educated in Germany and have no other home, yet still be introduced in political argument as *the migrant*. That is a question about who is allowed to be understood as local. It is also why a shop associated with Turkish-German everyday life is not politically invisible, even though nobody can infer the identity of its customers from its menu.

## 4. 🤝 Arm in Arm, Without an Ideological Entrance Exam

I do not need to know whether the young men outside Halle synagogue are Bundists or Zionists. I do not need to know their position on every disagreement in Jewish politics. I can see what they did.

They stood together, visibly, and said no.

Jewish communities are living through disagreements about nationalism, diaspora, safety, self-determination, racism and political responsibility. These are real disagreements. Some are irreconcilable in particular circumstances. Solidarity does not require pretending otherwise.

But there is a difference between **recognising an act of courage** and **endorsing every political position held by the person undertaking it**. We do not need to make admiration conditional on an ideological questionnaire.

My own position also has an additional requirement: antiracism must extend beyond Jews. I want solidarity with Turkish people, Muslims and others who are racialised as outsiders. I want the politics of protection to include the neighbours who can be endangered by overlapping exclusionary ideologies. That is my argument; it is **not** a claim that these students were speaking for all those communities or that they have endorsed my programme.

Three scales of action should remain distinct:

- **Immediate solidarity:** recognising and supporting a particular community's right to resist intimidation and defend its remembrance.
- **Political coalition:** building relationships across groups facing overlapping threats, without denying differences between their histories.
- **Long-term antiracism:** arguing about the principles, institutions and practices that protect everybody's ability to live where they are.

These are connected, but they are not identical. One need not complete the third before doing the first.

**We can argue about the future. We still have to look after one another in the present.**

### 🧭 Two timescales of solidarity

I can recognise the students' refusal **now**, even while Jewish communities are arguing intensely about what political futures they want. That reckoning is not an administrative obstacle to be cleared before people are allowed to protect one another. It is precisely the setting in which solidarity becomes difficult, necessary and real.

But immediate solidarity is not the endpoint of political thinking. In progressive spaces, I still want the harder conversation: how do we make antiracism genuinely universal in its commitments, while attending to the distinct ways antisemitism, anti-Muslim racism, anti-Turkish racism and other exclusions work? A coalition that protects only the people with whom its members already agree is a fragile kind of protection. Conversely, demanding total agreement before any collective act may make cooperation impossible at the very moment it is needed.

This is not a request to put words into the students' mouths. Their refusal is theirs. The broader demand for cross-community antiracism is mine. **Solidarity does not abolish disagreement; it refuses to let every disagreement veto the possibility of standing together.**

## 5. 🦁 Doikayt — Hereness with Consequences

*Doikayt* (דאָיִקייט) is commonly rendered as **hereness**. In Bundist politics it names a commitment to Jewish life and political struggle where Jews actually live: organisation, cultural life, labour politics, collective action and claims to belonging in the societies of the diaspora.

It was not merely the passive observation that Jews happened to be somewhere. It was a challenge to the assumption that Jewish life in those places had no political future.

Bundism and Zionism emerged as different historical responses to overlapping crises of antisemitism, insecurity and Jewish self-determination. They disagreed profoundly over nationhood, territory, diaspora and political strategy; neither was a single, unchanging bloc. Those differences should be studied rather than politely erased. They also do not decide whether a Jewish student may stand beside another Jewish student at a synagogue doorway.

I am not identifying the JSUD students as Bundists. **I am using a Bundist concept to interpret an action that does not belong to Bundism.** A Zionist, non-Zionist or politically unlabelled Jewish student could participate in exactly this act.

The concept helps explain the force of the action:

*We are here. We have a community here. We have memory here. We can organise here. We can say no here.*

The students were not requesting a licence to exist in Germany. They were exercising agency within German society, at a specific site, in relation to a specific ceremony.

**Doikayt, at the doorway.**

And here is my proposed extension, not a quotation from historical Bundist doctrine: if our right to be here matters, then our neighbours' right to be here matters too. A society that habitually casts one minority as permanently foreign creates conditions that other minorities have reason to examine closely.

Hereness is not a prize granted after assimilation. It is a lived fact with political implications.

### 📍 Doikayt is not permission to remain

*Doikayt* was historically more than an uplifting synonym for belonging. The Jewish Labour Bund organised around workers, political participation, Yiddish cultural life and forms of collective organisation where Jews lived. Different periods and branches of the movement developed those commitments in different ways. We need primary Bundist writing and modern historical scholarship to distinguish *doikayt* as a historical programme from the more portable contemporary metaphor of **hereness**; the present bibliography does not yet provide that scholarly foundation.

This matters because a community can be *tolerated* while remaining politically powerless. To say **we are here** is one claim. To say **we have institutions, relationships, memory, obligations and the ability to act here** is another. The students were not requesting an abstract guarantee that Jews may inhabit Germany. They were making a decision in a particular German place, about a particular Jewish occasion, under conditions they did not choose.

Bundism and Zionism arose from overlapping conditions of Jewish insecurity, but they were not mirror-image answers with a single correct historical verdict waiting to be announced. Bundist *doikayt* and Zionist projects of Jewish national self-determination took different positions on territory, diaspora and political strategy, with substantial diversity inside each movement. A student who identifies as Zionist could still stand in this human chain; so could a Bundist, a non-Zionist, or somebody who rejects the whole taxonomy. The shared act cannot adjudicate those disagreements, and it need not.

My extension of *doikayt* towards antiracism across communities is an **authorial argument**, not a quotation from Bundist doctrine: if our presence has political consequences, then so does our neighbours' presence. A Jewish claim to hereness that never notices other people being designated perpetual outsiders has left something ethically unfinished. That is a proposition for discussion, not a condition imposed retroactively on these students.

## 6. 🪨 Stolpersteine — Who Is Allowed to Have Been Here?

The *Stolpersteine* are small brass-coloured memorial markers embedded in ordinary pavements, associated with artist Gunter Demnig's project to remember people persecuted under Nazism at locations connected to their lives. They memorialise Jewish people and other persecuted groups. Their scale is part of their force: a person lived here. At this address. In this street. Among neighbours.

**The stones insist that people were here. The students insist that Jewish people are here.**

The politics of these markers is not hypothetical. On **24 September 2026**, the town council in **Heidenau, Saxony**, voted to reject the future installation of additional Stolpersteine in public streets and pavements. The proposal was jointly supported by councillors from the **AfD, CDU and a local citizens' grouping**. The reported vote was **13 in favour, six against, one abstention**. The city administration had recommended rejecting the proposal, arguing for visible remembrance in everyday public space. [10–12]

Supporters of the restriction framed their position as a dispute over the *form* of commemoration, including the idea that names should not be stepped on and that remembrance might be located elsewhere. These are claims to examine, not automatically a confession of any one private motive. There is a genuine history of disagreement over the form of Stolpersteine, including among Jewish public figures. [10–12]

But the political setting matters. Charlotte Knobloch, who has herself long criticised the Stolpersteine format, also criticised the Heidenau decision and the political cooperation involved. Her intervention is particularly instructive: **opposition to the format of a memorial and support for a political effort to stop new memorials are not automatically the same position.** [12]

Another revealing example comes from **Buxtehude**, where an AfD councillor's published May 2026 remarks about a Stolperstein proposal combined stated support for commemorating Nazi victims with attacks on antifascist organisers and a claim that the Nazis were left-wing socialists. The councillor ultimately voted for the relevant proposal after clarification about costs. That is evidence of a particular politician's rhetoric, **not** evidence that every AfD branch voted against every stone. [13]

This is the point at which declarations of political neutrality need to be tested against actual decisions. *Erinnerungskultur* — culture of remembrance — is not neutral merely because an official calls it neutral. Who gets to place a marker? Where? Who can object? What account of the past is being promoted or displaced? These are political questions.

The fact that Stolpersteine commemorate people persecuted for different reasons also returns us to cross-community solidarity. The pavement does not belong exclusively to one group's history. Nor should the dignity of one set of victims be purchased by erasing another.

### 🪨 Remembering where people lived — and who objects

The project's ordinary scale is crucial. A stone does not require a visitor to enter a national museum or adopt a prescribed itinerary. It interrupts a pavement: **here lived a person**. Gunter Demnig's project commemorates multiple categories of people persecuted by the Nazi regime, not Jewish victims alone. An archival version should use the project's own historical documentation and distinguish the stone's inscription, placement and local sponsoring process.

This is why the September 2026 Heidenau dispute deserves documentary precision rather than an all-purpose claim that *the AfD hates Stolpersteine*. We need the original motion, administrative recommendation, minutes, recorded vote and any subsequent implementation. We must also distinguish opposition to the form of pavement memorials from opposition to Holocaust remembrance itself. Charlotte Knobloch's position makes that distinction impossible to ignore: criticism of walking over names need not imply support for a party's municipal strategy. [10–12]

But the opposite error is to let an official description of a dispute as *merely procedural* foreclose examination of its political context. A decision about whether names may appear in public space is also a decision about what kind of historical encounter everyday civic life will permit. That is a legitimate subject of political analysis. The broader AfD record on *Erinnerungskultur*, including specific politicians' statements and actual votes, requires a separate, dated German-source chronology. Björn Höcke's widely discussed 2017 remarks about Berlin's Holocaust memorial are a research lead here, **not an independently sourced finding of this revision**.

**The stones say that people were here. The students demonstrate that living Jews can still make decisions here.** Neither fact makes the other redundant.

## 7. 🏛️ Public Office Is Not Communal Consent

Hans-Thomas Tillschneider had just become a vice-president of the Saxony-Anhalt parliament. That status helps explain why he might claim a role at an official remembrance event. It does not settle whether Jewish participants regarded his attendance as appropriate. [1–5]

The JSUD's explanation was not that public remembrance should cease. It was that their remembrance should not be appropriated by a politician they regarded as belonging to the far right. In German interviews, Dekel and other JSUD representatives framed the action as a refusal to allow the commemoration to be used for political rehabilitation. [1–3]

Tillschneider disputed their characterisation and said he had come in an official capacity. His account should be reported accurately, but the description *official* does not itself settle the question of communal consent. [2–4]

The underlying distinctions are straightforward:

1. **Institutional status:** the politician holds a public office.
2. **Commemorative legitimacy:** participation is appropriate to the occasion and its historical meaning.
3. **Communal consent:** the affected community accepts the person's participation in its own commemorative space.

They overlap, but none automatically produces the others. A public street, synagogue grounds and a formally organised state ceremony also involve different legal and practical questions; this account makes no sweeping legal claim that a group can exclude any official from any public event.

The students' action exposes the gap between remembering Jewish victims and respecting living Jewish people when they express an unwelcome political judgement.

### 🏛️ An office, an invitation and a threshold are different things

The change in Tillschneider's institutional position matters to the interpretation. The argument is not simply that an unpopular politician attended an event. It is that a new or newly salient official title can be used to claim a place in ceremonies that confer symbolic legitimacy. The students did not accept that the title settled the matter. **A public appointment can establish a role in an institution without compelling a community to regard the office-holder's presence as welcome.**

This does not answer every legal question about a public memorial, synagogue property or access to a state-organised ceremony. The exact event organiser, invitation status, geography of the confrontation and powers attached to the parliamentary office need checking against primary records. The point is an analytic distinction, not a general licence to exclude officials from public places.

A related question runs through German remembrance politics: is remembering murdered Jews compatible with dismissing the political judgement of living Jews about how that remembrance is used? There may be disagreements within Jewish communities themselves. That makes listening to the local community, JSUD and organisers **more**, not less, important. No politician's claim to neutrality settles the issue automatically; neither does an outsider's preferred interpretation of the students' intentions.

## 9. 🧬 Antifascist Amidah — Standing Is an Action

*Amidah* is associated with standing in Jewish liturgical language. Here, **Antifascist Amidah** is the name of an interpretive framework, not an assertion that the students were performing a prayer or invoking this terminology themselves.

There is a temptation, when looking at brave people in a photograph, to make them into an emblem. The risk is that we stop seeing them as people. These young men are not here to prove Bundism correct, to settle Zionism, or to reassure anybody that Jewish political disagreement has ended.

They are people who stood at a synagogue doorway on a difficult anniversary and publicly refused a politician's participation.

The arms matter. A human chain makes an individual judgement collective. It distributes the visible act of refusal across bodies in space. It does not abolish fear or guarantee safety. It says that nobody in that line is standing entirely alone.

For me, that is the connection between *doikayt* and solidarity: belonging is not only a claim about identity. It is something people make consequential through relationships and action.

And it is not only a Jewish question. Halle's history includes a kebab shop because the attacker's violence reached beyond the synagogue. The broader antiracist argument must therefore be capable of recognising different forms of racialisation without treating them as identical, and of building solidarity without insisting on total ideological agreement.

We can argue about the future. We still have to look after one another in the present.

## 11. 🕯️ The People Who Are Here

A Stolperstein restores a name to a street. A memorial restores a history to public attention. Neither can substitute for listening when living people speak.

The stones tell us that people were here. They had addresses and neighbours and ordinary lives. The synagogue tells us that Jewish life is here now. And the kebab shop reminds us that the politics of exclusion does not respect the tidy boundaries between communities that commentators sometimes draw after violence.

I do not need to know what every young man in that human chain thinks about Bundism, Zionism or the future of Jewish politics. I know what I saw them do, and what their representatives said they were defending.

They stood together. Their faces were visible. They made a decision about their own remembrance.

And there is something worth learning from the simplicity of that.

**Doikayt, at the doorway.**

**„Sie sind hier nicht erwünscht.“**
