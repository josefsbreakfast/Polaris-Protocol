# 🫒 Lech Lecha 5787 — The Canaanites Were Already There
**First created:** 2026-10-24 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-24  
*Abraham, Hebron, a military operation, and the rather urgent business of letting Palestinian families harvest their own olives.*

**Parsha:** Lech Lecha, Genesis 12:1–17:27  
**Shabbat:** 24 October 2026 / 13 Cheshvan 5787  
**Evidence cutoff:** 24 October 2026. Events and figures below are dated; this is not a continuously updated situation report.

---

## 🛰️ Orientation — Welcome to Canaan

Hello. Welcome to **Violent Parshiyot**.

This week G-d tells Abram to go to Canaan. Several thousand years later, we are still having difficulties distinguishing between a religious narrative and permission to do whatever the fuck we like to people already living there.

So we are going to read the actual text. All of it. Including the bits that make the convenient political interpretation rather less convenient.

And we are going to do so while Israeli forces are conducting a major operation in Hebron, in the occupied West Bank, following the killing of two Israeli soldiers. Palestinians have been displaced; homes and other structures have reportedly been demolished; movement has been restricted. At the same time, the Palestinian olive harvest is underway amid an already documented pattern of settler attacks, restricted agricultural access, stolen crops, and damaged trees.

We should not pretend these are two disconnected realities: an ancient text in one room and living people outside. But neither should we invent a causal link. **There is no evidence presented here that the timing or planning of the Hebron operation was determined by the Torah-reading calendar.** This is a study of how people read scripture *within* a political and military environment, and what they may believe themselves authorised to do.

I am writing as a British Jewish anti-Zionist woman, not as a Palestinian farmer, an Israeli bereaved relative, a halakhic decisor, or a military lawyer. The ethical commitments here are the author's; the evidence and legal questions must still be handled properly. Palestinian people are not supporting characters in a Jewish intellectual exercise. They are entitled to freedom, safety, self-determination and ordinary lives because they are human. Full stop.

Some readers need a further pragmatic explanation. Fine. We can do that too. But the right to harvest one's olives does not depend on whether a Jewish reader finds the explanation useful.

**The harvest needs to happen.**

## 📜 1. Go Forth — But Notice What the Text Actually Says

### 🧳 Genesis 12:1–3 — A departure and a blessing

*Lech lecha* (לֶךְ־לְךָ): go forth. Abram is told to leave his country, his kindred and his father's house for a land G-d will show him. The promises include descendants, a great name, and blessing. In Genesis 12:3, the wider world is not absent: the blessing extends outward to the families of the earth.

There are many Jewish interpretations of the phrase *lech lecha*, including readings that explore movement towards oneself. We need not pretend those readings replace the plain narrative of departure and promise. But the text is about a human being moving into a place where other human beings live. It is not a standing operational order issued to a modern state.

Abram is a migrant. He experiences hunger, uncertainty, political vulnerability, wealth, conflict, and responsibilities towards people around him. This is already rather more complicated than an arrow drawn across a map with **G-D SAID SO** written above it.

### 🌍 Genesis 12:6–7 — The Canaanites were in the land

Here is the sentence we cannot skip: **the Canaanites were then in the land**.

The passage proceeds to describe G-d's promise to Abram's descendants. The promise is real *within the text*. So is the acknowledgement of existing inhabitants.

We should not erase either half to make ourselves comfortable.

This is not a claim that Genesis 12 supplies a modern political constitution, or that ancient Canaanites and contemporary Palestinians are interchangeable populations. It is a much simpler warning about reading habits: if your interpretation of a sacred territory requires you to stop seeing its inhabitants, you have managed to read *less* than the text actually gives you.

**Genesis remembered to mention the people already there. We can manage at least that much.**

### 🥖 Genesis 12:10–20 — Abram and Sarai in Egypt

There is famine. Abram goes to Egypt. He fears being killed because of Sarai's beauty, asks her to present herself as his sister, and she is taken into Pharaoh's household. The episode ends with Pharaoh rebuking Abram and sending them away.

Whatever one's reading of its complexities, this is not a story in which the central covenantal protagonist is rendered morally uncomplicated by his proximity to G-d. Sarai's exposure to danger matters. Abram's fear matters. The conduct that follows still demands scrutiny.

A sacred story can record fear without requiring us to endorse every decision made in response to it. We will need that distinction later.

### 🏕️ Genesis 13 — Abram and Lot do not have to escalate

Their herds and households have become too numerous for the same immediate resources. Their herdsmen quarrel. Abram proposes separation and offers Lot a choice of direction.

This is not a ready-made peace treaty. It is, however, a narrative in which competing claims over land and resources do not automatically require a war. Someone with standing and power can choose restraint.

After Lot departs, the land promise is restated; Abram later settles near the oaks of Mamre at Hebron. That location matters deeply to Jewish tradition. **Sacred geography matters. It does not abolish the people living in the contemporary city.**

### ⚔️ Genesis 14 — Rescue, warfare, and refusing the spoils

Lot is captured in a regional war. Abram arms his household, pursues the captors, and rescues Lot and others. Afterwards he refuses the king of Sodom's offer of enrichment, lest that king claim to have made Abram rich.

Ancient narrative is not modern international humanitarian law. We must not retrofit a Geneva Convention into Genesis 14. But we can notice that the account distinguishes rescuing people from taking advantage of violence to enrich oneself.

A reader determined to find *a biblical war* will find one. A reader prepared to read the whole episode will also find limits, motives, negotiations, and refusal of spoils. The question is not whether ancient people fought. The question is what a contemporary reader thinks that fact permits them to do to a living civilian.

### 🗺️ Genesis 15 — The promise is not imaginary; nor is it a title deed

The covenantal passage makes expansive territorial promises. We should neither pretend it says nothing about land nor treat its geographical language as a modern land registry, border treaty, or rules-of-engagement manual.

Distinguish the layers: biblical promise; Jewish historical and religious attachment; the variety of Jewish interpretations; modern sovereignty; private landholding; political rights; and the legal obligations of a state exercising control over occupied territory.

Those questions intersect. They are not the same question.

Jewish attachment to Hebron does not require denying Palestinian attachment to Hebron. Nor does the existence of Jewish sacred history grant a modern soldier permission to punish somebody who did not commit an offence.

### 🫀 Genesis 16 — Hagar is not an inconvenient footnote

Sarai gives Hagar, her Egyptian servant, to Abram. Hagar becomes pregnant. Conflict follows. Sarai deals harshly with her; Hagar flees. In the wilderness, Hagar encounters a divine messenger and names the G-d who sees her.

Here is an uncomfortable question for a covenantal household: **who is safe inside it?**

Who possesses power? Who can leave? Who is vulnerable to somebody else's fear, humiliation or frustration? Who gets to narrate the household as righteous? Who is actually being harmed?

Hagar is not a one-to-one allegory for Palestinians. Turning her into one would risk erasing her as a person within the narrative and flattening both histories. But the episode demonstrates something indispensable: a household central to a sacred story can mistreat someone vulnerable within it. Being chosen for a role in a religious narrative does not confer moral infallibility.

Nor does Hagar's suffering disappear because the household has an important destiny.

### 🕎 Genesis 17 — Ishmael receives a blessing too

The covenant is renewed. Abram becomes Abraham; Sarai becomes Sarah. Circumcision is established as its sign. The text differentiates the covenantal line through Isaac, but Ishmael is explicitly blessed, with descendants and a future.

Particularity is not the same as permission to erase everyone else. The narrative is capable of distinguishing covenantal roles while still acknowledging the life and flourishing of someone outside the line on which its principal story will focus.

If our political reading makes other people's children disposable, we should be asking whether the problem lies with the text or with what we have decided we want from it.

## 🏙️ 2. Hebron Is a City, Not an Empty Bible Illustration

Hebron/al-Khalil is both a site of profound religious significance and a contemporary Palestinian city. Its history cannot be collapsed into a single community's account of belonging. The 1997 Hebron arrangements divided the city into H1 and H2, with distinct security arrangements; movement restrictions and the presence of Israeli settlements have profoundly affected Palestinian daily life, particularly in H2.

This background matters because the events of 23–24 October 2026 did not arrive on an empty landscape.

On 24 October, [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/around-1200-palestinians-are-displaced-hebron-israel-begins-military-operation-2026-10-24/) that roughly **1,200 Palestinians had been displaced** as Israeli forces launched a large operation following an attack that killed **two Israeli soldiers** at a checkpoint. Reuters reported Israeli searches in the Al-Jaladah and Ein Deir Baha neighbourhoods, reported demolitions, and a warning from Hebron's municipal leadership that further residents faced displacement. It attributed accusations of collective punishment to Palestinian officials; those accusations are not, by themselves, a court finding.

Contemporaneous Israeli reporting carried a somewhat different displacement estimate: [The Times of Israel, 24 October](https://www.timesofisrael.com/idf-op-in-hebron-after-soldiers-killing-said-to-displace-1500-pm-vows-big-surprise-for-pa/) reported approximately **1,500** people displaced, citing local accounts. **Do not silently reconcile these estimates into an invented exact total.** They were reported at different moments and may reflect different counting methods.

In its account of the operation, [The Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/idf-launches-major-operation-in-hebron-after-soldiers-killed-in-shooting-attack/) quoted the Israeli military describing targeted searches for weapons and militant infrastructure, and describing the operation as geographically focused and limited. The military's description of its objectives must be recorded accurately; it is not evidence that every resulting measure was necessary, lawful, or non-discriminatory. Residents' accounts of displacement and destruction likewise need independent scrutiny and investigation.

The killing of the two soldiers is a real loss. Their families will grieve. We do not need to make their deaths abstract in order to recognise the Palestinian civilians whose homes and livelihoods are at risk.

**The dead will not return because somebody else's home is demolished.**

### 🧭 What this node does *not* establish

- It does **not** establish that the operation was planned to coincide with Lech Lecha.
- It does **not** establish that a biblical text was cited in the operation's orders.
- It does **not** verify an alleged operation codename circulating without a reliable primary source.
- It does **not** independently determine the legal status of each demolition, arrest or closure.
- It does **not** claim every resident affected by the operation is an olive farmer, or that every Hebron orchard was closed by this operation.

These are evidence boundaries, not invitations to ignore the harms that *are* documented.

## 🫒 3. The Harvest Was Already Under Pressure

Before the operation, there was already a harvest problem. And before this harvest, there were already years of restrictions, threats and attacks.

For the agricultural and ecological baseline, see [🫒 Treasure on Trees](../../../../🌓_3_In_The_Moment/📲_Press_Matters/🇵🇸_Palestine_Factchecking/🍉_Current_Events/🫒_treasure_on_trees.md). That node treats an orchard as living infrastructure rather than decorative scenery: trees, land, access, labour, tools, roads, presses, sales, household income, and accumulated knowledge.

An olive tree can remain alive while the household that tends it loses the harvest. An orchard can remain visible from the road while its owner cannot safely enter it. A temporary closure can cause permanent loss of a seasonal opportunity.

**The harvest needs to happen.** Not metaphorically. Agriculturally.

### 📊 What was documented before 24 October?

The [UN OCHA humanitarian report dated 15 October 2026](https://www.ochaopt.org/content/humanitarian-situation-report-15-october-2026), covering 29 September–12 October, reported **199 olive-harvest-related settler attacks from 1 September to 12 October**, with **one Palestinian killed, 124 injured, and more than 2,500 trees and saplings damaged** in those harvest-related incidents. OCHA said **166** of those incidents occurred in the report's two-week period. These are OCHA's defined incident counts for specific dates; they are not a census of every form of intimidation or a figure for the entire season.

The same report described extensive existing access constraints, including agricultural gates with limited opening days, permit restrictions and expansion of areas requiring prior military coordination. It noted that **63 per cent of 123 priority harvest-protection hotspot communities** identified by humanitarian partners were concentrated in Ramallah, Nablus and **Hebron** governorates. The 2026 harvest was officially scheduled to begin on **15 October**; the violence began earlier.

On [22 October, UN reporting](https://palestine.un.org/en/324862-%E2%80%98ominous-start%E2%80%99-west-bank-olive-harvest-amid-settler-attacks) described live ammunition, arson, tree destruction, stolen crops and severe movement restrictions. It relayed concerns from the UN human rights office about Palestinian farmers being prevented from reaching their land while settlers accessed it.

The Israeli human-rights organisation **Yesh Din** subsequently reported **672 incidents** from 27 September to 22 October, using a broader categorisation and reporting period than OCHA's earlier harvest-related attack count. [The Times of Israel summarised Yesh Din's figures on 23 October](https://www.timesofisrael.com/liveblog_entry/yesh-din-reports-672-incidents-of-settler-violence-since-start-of-olive-harvest-1-month-ago/). **These figures should not be added together.** They describe overlapping events under different definitions.

There is already enough evidence to establish that farmers were working within a dangerous and constrained system before the new Hebron operation began.

### ⏳ The additional-pressure problem

What happens when you add a military operation, further closures or new displacement to an already disrupted harvest?

The answer is not necessarily linear. A household that has lost several safe harvesting days may have only a few opportunities remaining. An additional closure might mean the difference between collecting most of the crop and missing the viable window altogether. Whether that occurred in a particular Hebron community must be established with local reporting, access records and farmers' accounts.

**Threat or restriction → missed work → reduced harvest → lost income → less capacity to maintain the orchard → greater vulnerability next season.**

The agricultural clock is not obliged to respect the military administrative calendar.

We need to ask: Which groves are affected? Which access routes? Are harvesters permitted through? Can hired labour and relatives reach the land? Can fruit reach a press? What arrangements exist for safe passage? Who verifies they work? What happens when a settler threatens a farmer? Who investigates theft? How quickly? Can the crop still be recovered?

A claim that an operation is geographically limited is not a complete account of its effects on civilian systems.

And no, the Palestinian farmer does not need to settle the meaning of G-d's promise to Abraham before being allowed to pick their own olives.

## 🗺️ 4. Biblical Geography Is Already Political Language

**Judea and Samaria** (*Yehuda veShomron*) is established Israeli administrative and political terminology for the West Bank. It invokes ancient geographical names associated with Jewish history. Its use alone is not proof of a desire to expel Palestinians, and it would be absurd to treat every mention of biblical geography in Jewish study as an extremist signal.

But vocabulary does political work. **Occupied West Bank** foregrounds a contemporary legal and territorial relationship. **Judea and Samaria** foregrounds an ancient historical-geographical frame. Where the latter accompanies claims that Palestinians possess no legitimate political rights or that settlements may expand without regard to their homes and land, we should examine the *whole* argument rather than isolating a word and declaring it innocent by dictionary definition.

The same applies, with different and potentially graver implications, to **Canaan** and **Canaanites**. Genesis describes an ancient population. A contemporary speaker may use the term in scholarship, prayer, historical analogy, territorial rhetoric, or to identify a modern population as an enemy in a biblical story of conquest.

These are not the same speech acts.

The critical transition is:

**Ancient category → contemporary people → claimed sacred authority → proposed treatment of those people.**

If a speaker explicitly advocates expelling or killing Palestinians, that is not merely a subtle dog whistle. It is an overt threat or advocacy of serious harm, whatever religious vocabulary decorates it. We should not dignify an explicit demand for violence by pretending it is an interesting ambiguity in ancient Hebrew.

**Amalek** is another biblical category with its own textual and interpretive history. It should not be lazily merged with Canaanites. Any contemporary example needs the speaker, date, original language, full quotation, intended audience and context. **This edition does not attribute an unverified Canaanite quotation to a named politician or military officer.** Such claims belong in a documented evidence register, not in the space where a dramatic example would be convenient.

The point is not to ban biblical names. It is to recognise when someone attempts to turn them into an exemption from treating contemporary people as human beings.

## 🧨 5. When the Dog Whistle Comes with a Gun

Jewish communities already have reasons to recognise how hostile language operates. *Globalist* can carry an antisemitic conspiracy meaning in particular contexts; *Great Replacement* names a racist conspiracy narrative that often incorporates antisemitic claims; *Zionist* has legitimate political and historical meanings but can also be used as a euphemism for Jews when paired with antisemitic tropes. Those terms are not identical, and **criticism of Zionism is not automatically antisemitism**.

The lesson is not that words have secret permanent definitions. The lesson is that **meaning depends on speaker, audience, historical associations, surrounding claims and consequences**.

Now apply that skill when the people at risk are Palestinian.

A Jewish reader might hear *Judea and Samaria* as familiar sacred geography. A Palestinian farmer might hear it in a speech accompanying a policy that restricts access to their family's land. The word is the same. The informational environment is not.

We should ask:

- Who is speaking, and what authority do they have?
- What does the phrase mean to the intended audience?
- Does the speaker distinguish historical attachment from the rights of present inhabitants?
- Are Palestinians being described as neighbours, civilians and rights-holders, or as a biblical enemy to be removed?
- Is there a documented connection between the rhetoric and orders, conduct or tolerated violence?
- Can the targeted people obtain protection and redress?

A slur or dog whistle uttered by a private commentator is serious. A comparable statement by someone who commands armed personnel, controls permits or influences enforcement raises additional institutional dangers. Power and exposure matter.

And we must be specific about asymmetry. Antisemitic threats and violence are real. We do not need to minimise them. But Palestinians under occupation are exposed to particular forms of direct coercion, movement restriction, settler violence and loss of property or livelihood. A phrase circulating among people who exercise power over them can have immediate material implications.

**Equal human dignity does not mean equal exposure to danger.**

### 🕎 Learning to hear the whistle when it isn't whistling at you

Here is the more useful exercise.

If you have lived with antisemitism, you may know immediately why a phrase feels threatening. The associations have become part of your experience. Explaining them to somebody who has never had to learn them can be exhausting.

But learning to recognise anti-Palestinian racism *from outside that experience* teaches us something else: **how recognition itself is learned**.

You have to listen. You have to acquire the history. You have to ask which combinations of words matter. You have to notice who says them and what happens next. You have to allow for the possibility that a phrase which reassures you is frightening to someone else for reasons you have not yet understood.

And then, when someone asks how they can learn to recognise antisemitism, you possess not only your experience of being its target but also your experience of *learning to recognise another person's danger*. You remember which explanations helped, which assumptions needed changing, and why good intentions were insufficient without context.

**Experiencing prejudice teaches us something about recognising it. Learning to recognise prejudice directed at others teaches us how recognition itself is learned.**

That is a transferable safeguarding skill. It does not erase the different histories of antisemitism, anti-Palestinian racism or Islamophobia. It helps us listen better across them.

And the moral argument comes first: people deserve safety because they are people. The pragmatic argument is available for those who need it. It is not the price of admission to human rights.

## ⚖️ 6. Retribution Is Not an Investigation

Two Israeli soldiers were killed. Their deaths deserve acknowledgment; their families deserve care and an effective, lawful response. The question is whether the response protects life, investigates wrongdoing, and holds the people responsible accountable—or transfers the consequences to civilians who did not commit the attack.

We should not spend six pages lovingly reconstructing the most sympathetic possible justification for somebody else's suffering. We can record an official rationale without endorsing it. We can test conduct against law without converting the node into a debating society about whether the bereaved deserve additional bereaved neighbours.

**Grief is not a debt that can be collected from somebody else.**

### 🕎 Jewish law — justice is not an unlimited licence for vengeance

Leviticus 19:18 prohibits vengeance and bearing a grudge in its stated interpersonal context. Deuteronomy 24:16 articulates individual responsibility for wrongdoing in a legal setting: parents and children are not to be put to death for each other's offences. Leviticus 19:16 prohibits standing idly by the blood of one's neighbour. These are distinct texts with their own interpretive histories, not interchangeable slogans.

Jewish legal traditions also discuss duties of rescue, judicial punishment, defensive force and warfare; there are difficult passages and disagreements. We should not pretend all halakhic authorities offer a single modern theory of armed conflict, nor claim a categorical prohibition on any use of force after an attack.

But it is entirely legitimate to ask why a response presented as righteous protection should fall upon people who were not responsible. The duties to protect life and pursue justice do not turn unrelated civilians into the perpetrators.

**A family's grief does not become less real because we refuse to weaponise it.**

### ⚖️ Israeli law — investigate the offence; examine the enforcement

The occupied West Bank is not simply governed by ordinary Israeli criminal law. A complicated combination of military orders, retained local law, Israeli legislation and judicial oversight affects the territory; Israeli settlers and Palestinian residents experience profoundly different legal structures. It would be inaccurate to say Israeli law has no effect there at all.

But the complexity is not an answer to the problem. It is part of it.

Even assessing official conduct through the authorities' own claimed powers, we must ask: what is the lawful basis for arrest, detention, seizure or demolition? What evidence links the affected individual or property to a legally relevant objective? What review is available? Why are some civilians protected and others displaced? How do investigations and enforcement differ when the victim is Palestinian rather than Israeli?

The assertion that enforcement is discriminatory should be tested against comparable incidents, the legal architecture, the scope of coercive measures and actual outcomes. **Discriminatory enforcement is not cured by writing the paperwork in a different legal vocabulary.**

### 🌍 International humanitarian law — protected civilians are not substitute defendants

[Article 33 of the Fourth Geneva Convention](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/gciv-1949/article-33) prohibits collective penalties against protected persons for offences they have not personally committed, and also prohibits measures of intimidation or terrorism and reprisals against protected persons and their property. Other rules govern military necessity, protection of civilians, property and occupation. Their application to a particular incident depends on the facts and the relevant legal test.

Not every arrest or search after an attack is collective punishment. Nor does describing an operation as a security measure establish that all its consequences are lawful. A broad restriction that burdens people with no demonstrated connection to an offence, especially if imposed to punish or intimidate, raises grave questions requiring investigation.

**The question is not whether Israeli lives matter. They do. The question is why Palestinian lives should become the means by which that importance is demonstrated.**

## 🫀 7. Grief Is Not Transferable

I want to be particularly clear about something.

The people who died will not come back.

Their families will not become less bereaved because somebody else's family has been displaced, terrified, impoverished or killed. The suffering of Palestinian civilians cannot be paid into an account marked *Israeli grief* until the balance reaches zero.

That is not how loss works.

A person who has lost someone may want many things: answers, justice, protection, a future in which another family does not endure the same. We should not presume to narrate the wishes of every bereaved Israeli family. Nor should their grief be requisitioned as political permission for violence against strangers.

**The dead do not return when somebody else is killed.**

**A Palestinian farmer losing their harvest does not make an Israeli family whole again.**

Justice and protection matter. Accountability matters. They do not require pretending that revenge can reverse death.

## 🧱 8. Build Your Bloody Fence

*Pirkei Avot* 1:1 tells us to **make a fence around the Torah**: *asu siyag laTorah* (ועשו סייג לתורה). The classical idea is a safeguard against transgression. What follows is an ethical application, not a binding halakhic ruling.

If a dangerous reading of scripture is being used to make harm seem righteous, the question is not whether the institution can produce an elegant statement of its values after the fact. It is whether somebody can interrupt the mechanism *before* more people are harmed.

### 🕯️ Religious and communal safeguards

Teach the whole passage, not a territorial promise stripped of its surrounding human beings. Distinguish ancient populations from contemporary civilians. Challenge claims that covenantal attachment cancels another person's rights. Refuse to treat Jewish disagreement as disloyalty. Reject antisemitism without requiring silence about Palestinian suffering. Listen to Palestinians describing the risks they face. And be prepared to discover that your own familiar vocabulary carries implications you had not noticed.

No Jewish person is collectively responsible for what an Israeli official says. But people with authority, audiences, platforms or access to decision-makers can choose whether to amplify, challenge or ignore specific dangerous interpretations.

### 🛡️ Military, policing and administrative safeguards

Protect civilians and their property. Use lawful, reviewable powers rather than collective intimidation. Investigate violence against Palestinian farmers. Prevent settlers from attacking harvesters or taking their crops. Ensure safe, workable routes to groves, presses and markets. Provide meaningful remedies for unlawful destruction or obstruction. Publish enough information to make claims of necessity and protection testable.

A promised harvest-access arrangement is not a safeguard if the farmer cannot actually reach the trees.

### ⚖️ Prevention is not a retrospective press release

Article I of the [Genocide Convention](https://www.un.org/en/genocide-prevention/1948-convention) obliges states parties to prevent and punish genocide. In its 2007 *Bosnia and Herzegovina v. Serbia and Montenegro* judgment, the International Court of Justice explained that the duty to prevent arises when a state learns, or should normally have learned, of a serious risk of genocide. This does not make every wrongful act genocide, and it does not turn synagogues into treaty parties. It illustrates a crucial preventive principle: **waiting until the worst harm is legally established may be waiting far too long to stop it**.

People can also be protected from violence, displacement and collective punishment without first resolving whether genocide's specific legal elements are satisfied.

A fence is supposed to stop someone crossing a dangerous line. It is not a tasteful screen erected around the people who have already crossed it.

## ♻️ 9. The Orchard Is a System with Memory

This is where [🫒 Treasure on Trees](../../../../🌓_3_In_The_Moment/📲_Press_Matters/🇵🇸_Palestine_Factchecking/🍉_Current_Events/🫒_treasure_on_trees.md) meets the [Embodied Information Ecology](../../../🪿_Embodied_Information_Ecology/README.md).

An orchard is not a collection of isolated objects. It is a living system sustained by access, tending, seasonal knowledge, labour, harvesting, processing, markets and family continuity.

**Access → cultivation → harvest → income and knowledge → maintenance → future productive capacity.**

Threaten access, and the entire loop becomes unstable. Missed harvesting days may mean lost fruit. Lost income may mean less capacity to maintain the trees. A family that cannot reach its land may later be described as failing to cultivate it—as though the constraint were evidence of indifference.

**Constrained behaviour → incomplete observation → false inference → policy that reinforces the constraint.**

That is a recognisable cybernetic failure: the system mistakes the consequences of its own coercion for independent information about the people it constrains.

The 2026 Hebron operation adds an urgent question about thresholds. If a family is already struggling to reach its grove, how much further restriction can it absorb before the harvest is lost? We cannot answer that from a headline. We need farmer-level evidence and actual access information. But we can recognise the mechanism before every consequence has become irreversible.

**The orchard does not recover its lost harvest merely because somebody announces that the operation has ended.**

## 🚗 10. Have We Actually Stopped the Vehicle?

Imagine an institution announces that a particular operation has finished. Excellent. Now what?

Are displaced families able to return? Are homes and livelihoods restored? Can farmers safely reach their groves? Have threats stopped? Are crimes being investigated? Can people obtain remedies? Have the people exercising power changed how they behave?

A temporary reduction in violence is not the same as a functioning protection system. A condemnation is not an investigation. A military statement about facilitating the harvest is not the same thing as a completed harvest.

The feedback questions are practical:

| Function | Test |
| --- | --- |
| **Observe** | Whose reports of harm are being recorded, and whose are ignored? |
| **Interpret** | Are officials mistaking restricted behaviour for consent, absence, or abandonment? |
| **Decide** | Who can order a closure, stop an attack, or reopen access? |
| **Correct** | Can affected civilians challenge the decision in time to matter? |
| **Learn** | Does a documented failure change future conduct? |
| **Verify** | Did the farmer actually harvest, or did somebody merely announce an arrangement? |

**Slowing the vehicle is not the same thing as restoring its brakes.**

## 🕯️ 11. We Are Fence Keepers — Guardians at the Gate

We have read a parsha about departure, land, hunger, fear, conflict, rescue, covenant, vulnerability and blessing.

We have encountered a contemporary city whose inhabitants are not scenery in somebody else's sacred narrative. We have examined a military operation that has already displaced Palestinians. We have looked at a harvest under extraordinary pressure, and at the ways religious vocabulary can become politically consequential when the people using it possess power over other people's lives.

We have also examined something we can learn together.

If you know how antisemitic language works because you have lived with it, you already know that a word's danger cannot be read from a dictionary alone. If you learn to recognise anti-Palestinian racism when it is not directed at you, you learn how to listen across unfamiliar experience. You may even become better at explaining antisemitism to people who have never experienced it.

We are not obliged to choose one community's safety over another's. We are obliged to recognise that our ability to prevent harm depends on listening, on evidence, on restraint, and on the willingness to correct ourselves.

The fence exists to protect life. The gate exists because people must be able to move, learn, and return. The keeper must remember that they too are human.

A Palestinian family needs to harvest its olives. An Israeli family is grieving someone who cannot come back. Neither family is made whole by another act of cruelty.

The Torah will still be here next week. The arguments about Abraham will still be here. The arguments about land, history and covenant will still be here.

**But the olives are ripening now.**

We are Fence Keepers. Guardians at the Gate.

Not because we have perfect answers. Because we can learn to do better, and help one another stop before somebody gets hurt.

**Because it's already complicated being human.**
