# 🕯️ Violent Parshiyot
**First created:** 2026-10-24 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-24  
*Weekly Torah study, contemporary occupation posture, and the small matter of not turning sacred text into permission for atrocities.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Welcome to **Violent Parshiyot**.

Every week, we read the Torah. Every week, the world continues doing whatever the fuck it is doing. Occasionally these two activities overlap in ways that ought to concern us.

Particularly when someone looks at a biblical territorial promise, glances at a contemporary military operation, and decides the Almighty has apparently signed off on the paperwork.

**No. We are going to read the whole thing.**

This cluster examines the weekly Torah portions (*parshiyot*) alongside the contemporary political, military, social, and legal conditions in which people encounter them. Its immediate concern is the Israeli occupation of Palestinian territory, the violence inflicted upon Palestinians, and the risks of religious texts being recruited to justify that violence. Its wider concern is what happens when any community begins mistaking a sacred narrative for an exemption from restraint.

This is Jewish-authored, anti-Zionist work. It does not speak for all Jews, and it does not require a reader to share the author's politics before entering. Nor does it make Palestinian protection conditional on what is good for Israel. Palestinians are entitled to safety and rights in their own right.

The point is not that the Torah causes an army to do something. The point is that **what a text says, what a reader hears, and what a reader believes themselves authorised to do are not the same thing**. Those gaps can become dangerous. They can also become places where we intervene.

We are here to learn how.

## 📜 The Weekly Reading Is Not a Vacuum

A *parsha* (also *parashah*, פרשה) is a portion of Torah; the plural is *parshiyot* (פרשיות). The annual reading cycle gives Jewish communities a shared textual rhythm. Readings arrive with sermons, family memories, commentaries, arguments, social-media fragments, and whatever is happening in the world that week.

A soldier preparing to enter Hebron, a Palestinian farmer trying to reach an olive grove, a rabbi preparing a sermon, and a diaspora Jew opening a news feed do not inhabit identical informational environments. They may encounter the same passage and attach radically different significance to it.

That is not an argument for pretending every connection is deliberate. It is an argument for looking at **reception as well as intention**.

We ask, in order:

1. What does the text actually say — including the difficult bits?
2. What have interpreters historically made of it, and where do they disagree?
3. What is happening in the world in which it is being read?
4. Is anyone demonstrably invoking this text to justify contemporary conduct?
5. What interpretive risks are plausible even where direct invocation is not documented?
6. What would responsible restraint require of the people who can intervene?

Some weeks the answer to question four will be *we don't know*. Some weeks there will be no meaningful link at all. We do not manufacture providence to fill a content calendar. That would be an unusually stupid way to criticise selective interpretation.

## 🧱 Make a Fence Around the Torah

The instruction in *Pirkei Avot* 1:1 is familiar:

**וַעֲשׂוּ סְיָג לַתּוֹרָה**  
*And make a fence around the Torah.*

A *siyag* is a safeguard against transgression. This cluster takes that traditional image and asks what it might demand when scripture is being interpreted under conditions of militarisation, fear, political extremism, and mass civilian harm.

This is an ethical application, **not** a claim that the Mishnah contains a modern treaty commentary or that the author is issuing a binding halakhic ruling.

But the underlying question is not complicated: if you believe people are in danger of committing serious wrongs, why are you spending all your time defending the reputation of the people approaching the boundary instead of helping them stop?

**A fence around the Torah is supposed to prevent transgression. It is not a privacy screen around the people committing it.**

And fences are not just for *them*. We need them for ourselves. We are capable of getting frightened, narrowing our attention, defending our own, misunderstanding evidence, and hurting people while sincerely believing we are right.

Nobody gets to appoint themselves the infallible keeper of everybody else's conscience. The keeper needs correction too.

## 🕯️ We Are Fence Keepers — Guardians at the Gate

We are Fence Keepers. Guardians at the Gate.

Not because we are especially holy. Not because we have solved Judaism. Certainly not because the author has achieved the personal distinction of never saying anything regrettable.

We are here because **it is already complicated being human**.

People inherit fear and history. People make mistakes. People change their minds. People sometimes need another person to say, *hang on, that isn't what the text says*, or *that is not an acceptable thing to do to another human being*, or *we need to stop before someone gets hurt*.

We can learn from one another without pretending that disagreement is harmless in every circumstance. We can make room for people to reconsider without asking those facing immediate violence to wait politely while everyone else finishes processing their feelings.

The fence marks a threshold. The gate is where judgment happens. The keeper has to listen, notice danger, and remain answerable for their own decisions.

A fence without a gate becomes a prison. A gate without a keeper becomes an archway. A keeper who will not listen becomes another hazard.

**The gate is not there to exclude everyone who arrives imperfectly. It is there to help us avoid crossing lines we cannot uncross — and to leave room for people to come home.**

## ⚖️ The Genocide Convention Is a Prevention Instrument

The 1948 Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide does not ask states merely to condemn genocide after the fact. Article I commits contracting states to **prevent and punish** it. The International Court of Justice has explained that the duty to prevent is engaged when a state knows, or should normally have known, of a serious risk of genocide; it is not necessary to await a final judgment establishing that genocide has occurred.

This matters because arguments over the final legal classification of conduct can become an excuse to neglect immediate protection. Genocide has a specific legal definition and intent requirement. War crimes, crimes against humanity, unlawful displacement, and other violations also have their own elements. Precision matters. **So does acting before the worst possible outcome is complete.**

The Convention binds states under international law; it does not automatically impose identical treaty duties on every synagogue, rabbi, or diaspora organisation. Those actors can nevertheless use their influence to oppose incitement, demand civilian protection, support scrutiny and accountability, and press their governments to fulfil preventive obligations.

There are useful, limited analogies with Jewish legal and ethical traditions: the prohibition on bloodshed; *lo ta'amod al dam re'echa* (do not stand idly by the blood of your neighbour, Leviticus 19:16); duties concerning participation in wrongdoing; and the practice of building safeguards before transgression. These are **not one-to-one equivalences** between halakha and modern international law. The difficult passages, interpretive disagreements, and differences in legal scope remain part of the work.

Raphael Lemkin, the Polish-Jewish jurist who coined *genocide*, helped develop the international framework that followed the Holocaust. It is a peculiar outcome if a convention built to help prevent the destruction of human groups is treated as a hostile intrusion whenever its standards are applied to a state someone loves.

If your overriding priority is Israel's survival as a Jewish homeland, you still have a reason to insist on effective restraints against atrocity. **The brakes are not an attack on the vehicle.**

## 🧠 Fear Is Real; It Is Not a Rules-of-Engagement Exemption

Persecution, displacement, forced migration, war, and genocide have shaped Jewish histories and institutions. Those experiences deserve to be taken seriously. They do not create one uniform Jewish psychology, and they do not imply any special Jewish disposition towards extremism.

The mechanisms we examine are human ones: threat amplification, confirmation bias, in-group preference, conformity pressure, pluralistic ignorance, moral disengagement, dehumanisation, and the authority conferred by political or religious leaders.

A history of genuine persecution can be invoked to persuade people that ordinary restraints no longer apply. The existence of genuine danger does not make every claimed danger accurate; nor does it turn every response into a lawful one.

The historical account matters, too. Political Zionism predates the Holocaust. Its development took place within European nationalism, antisemitism, imperial power, Jewish aspirations, and contested projects of settlement and statehood. Migrants, ideologues, soldiers, refugees, and subsequent generations did not all arrive with identical intentions or experiences. British imperial governance and later international sponsorship belong in the story alongside Zionist political agency and Palestinian dispossession.

Understanding how a system was built is not the same as assigning collective guilt to everyone who lives within it. Nor does complexity absolve people who have power to stop harm.

## 🪖 This Week's Occupation Posture

The weekly reading is paired with a sober account of relevant contemporary events: military operations; settler attacks; Palestinian movement, livelihood, and displacement; political speeches; religious-nationalist rhetoric; electoral incentives; international legal developments; and the condition of protective institutions.

**The occupied Palestinian territory is not a biblical blank space.** Palestinian civilians are not props in a theological exercise. When the question is an olive harvest, start with whether people can safely reach their trees. When the question is an armed operation, start with who is being harmed, displaced, detained, or protected, and on what authority.

We separate:

- **Established facts** from official descriptions and allegations.
- **Documented religious invocations** from plausible interpretive hazards.
- **Theological argument** from the legal standards governing actual conduct.
- **Individual conduct** from organisational policy, command responsibility, and state responsibility.

A coincidentally timed operation may still be interpreted through the weekly parsha. That possibility is worth examining. It is not evidence, by itself, that planners chose the date for religious reasons or that readers have acted on a particular interpretation.

No imaginary codename confirmations. No invented quotations. No retrospective certainty smuggled into a footnote.

## 🫀 Who Gets Hurt When the Restraints Fail?

First and foremost: the people subjected to the violence. Palestinian lives, safety, homes, land, freedom, and access to care matter without any further justification.

There are also questions about what prolonged violence does to institutions and to the people operating within them. Repeated deployments, threat conditioning, moral injury, impunity, political radicalisation, and strained families can create serious safeguarding concerns. These pathways need investigation; they are not automatic outcomes and do not justify stereotyping veterans or people living with trauma.

Domestic violence is one such question. Are rates changing? Are incidents reported? Are protections working? Are available figures comparable over time? We do not invent an answer because the hypothesis feels plausible. **Absence of reliable recording is not proof of safety, and concern is not proof of an increase.**

The same discipline applies to feminist institutions. Palestinian women should not have to be converted into an argument about someone else's future security before their suffering counts. Nor should invitations to dialogue be confused with immediate protection against assault, displacement, deprivation, or abuse.

Listening matters. It does not, on its own, stop a person being beaten in an olive grove.

## 🕎 What Diaspora Solidarity Owes the Living

Antisemitism is real. Jewish fear can be real. Neither requires silence about violence against Palestinians, nor the defence of every act committed by the Israeli state.

Individuals may be unable to speak publicly, may be acting privately, or may face genuine safety constraints. Silence alone does not prove complicity. Institutions with political access, resources, and public authority can be asked harder questions about what they actually do with those capacities.

If an organisation can mobilise rapidly to protect Israel's reputation, can it mobilise to protect Palestinians from foreseeable harm? Does it challenge incitement and unlawful violence by people it otherwise considers part of its community? Does it make room for Jewish dissent? Does it support independent investigation? Does it press for measures that interrupt violence rather than merely discussing it?

The task is not to make frightened people feel guilty for being frightened. It is to help more people cross the threshold from private discomfort to useful action — while refusing to make the safety of those under attack wait for everybody else's emotional readiness.

**Solidarity can mean stopping people we love from doing something terrible.**

## 🚗 Slowing Down Is Not Stopping

A drop in casualties is important. A pause can save lives. An operation ending can matter enormously.

None of those facts, by itself, establishes that the machinery capable of producing further violence has been disabled.

The question is whether incitement has stopped, people are protected, unlawful conduct is investigated, perpetrators are held accountable, and institutions can reliably constrain those with weapons and authority.

This is the cybernetic spine of the cluster: **what is the system sensing, what feedback does it accept, what behaviour does it reinforce, and where can the loop be interrupted?**

If every warning is reclassified as hostility, every criticism as betrayal, and every demand for restraint as an existential threat, the system is losing the capacity to correct itself.

We will not confuse a slower vehicle with one whose brakes work.

## 🔬 The Weekly Method

Each case study can draw on seven recurring stations. They are a method, not a compulsory theatrical production:

| Station | Question |
| --- | --- |
| 📜 **This week's parsha** | What is actually written, and what do relevant interpretations say? |
| 🪖 **Occupation posture** | What is happening now, to whom, and according to which sources? |
| 🧨 **How to get into theological trouble** | Which shortcuts might turn text into apparent permission? |
| 🕎 **Read the whole bloody thing** | What context, contradictions, and obligations did the shortcut leave out? |
| 🧱 **Build your bloody fence** | What religious, ethical, institutional, and legal restraints apply? |
| 🫀 **Who is being harmed?** | Who needs protection now, and what secondary risks merit investigation? |
| 🚗 **Have we stopped the vehicle?** | What concrete evidence would show that the harmful mechanism has been interrupted? |

Each node should state its date, parsha, scriptural range, contemporary time window, and evidence status. It should use accessible translations and indicate when a reading is disputed. Claims about military or political events require dated sources; reports of alleged wrongdoing must be distinguished from findings. Where a connection is interpretive rather than documented, say so.

Not every portion has to map neatly onto the news. If the honest answer is *no meaningful connection established*, that is an acceptable and sometimes valuable result.

## 🏮 Footer

*🕯️ Violent Parshiyot* is a living cluster of the **Polaris Protocol**. It offers a recurring method for reading Jewish sacred texts alongside contemporary conditions, identifying interpretive hazards, and asking what meaningful safeguards against violence require.

It is neither a rabbinical ruling nor a claim to speak for Jewish communities collectively. It is an invitation to read carefully, disagree responsibly, correct ourselves, and act before preventable harm becomes irreversible.
