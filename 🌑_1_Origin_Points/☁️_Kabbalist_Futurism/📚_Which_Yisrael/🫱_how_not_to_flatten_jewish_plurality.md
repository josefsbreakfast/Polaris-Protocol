# 🫱 How Not To Flatten Jewish Plurality  
**First created:** 2026-08-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-18  
*How non-Jewish readers, institutions, allies, and political movements can engage Jewish disagreement without turning one Jewish voice into permission, veto, authenticity test, or substitute for thought.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Jewish people disagree.

Quite a lot.

This should not be surprising.

Jewish political, religious, philosophical, cultural, and communal traditions have developed across different countries, languages, classes, institutions, migration histories, forms of persecution, relationships with power, and understandings of **Yisrael**.

There is no single Jewish relationship with:

- Medinat Yisrael;
- Eretz Yisrael;
- Zionism;
- anti-Zionism;
- diaspora;
- nationalism;
- Jewish sovereignty;
- Palestinian liberation;
- antisemitism;
- Jewish safety;
- religion;
- secular Jewish identity;
- or Jewish political obligation.

That plurality creates a problem for institutions and non-Jewish participants who would quite like a clear answer to:

> **What do Jews think?**

There usually isn't one.

This node is therefore addressed particularly to people **outside the disagreement**.

Its central request is simple:

> **do not solve Jewish plurality by flattening it.**

Do not find one Jew who agrees with you and make them representative.

Do not find one Jewish institution and assume it speaks for everyone.

Do not turn disagreement between Jews into a referendum on who is properly Jewish.

Do not make Jewish testimony meaningless merely because another Jew disagrees.

And do not outsource your own political judgement to whichever Jewish voice makes your existing position easiest to defend.

---

## 📚 Start By Asking Which Yisrael

Part of the plurality exists because Jews are not necessarily referring to the same object when they say:

> **Israel.**

This cluster distinguishes:

- **Medinat Yisrael** — the modern State of Israel;
- **Eretz Yisrael** — the land;
- **Am Yisrael** — the Jewish people;
- **Kol Yisrael** — the Jewish collective;
- **Atzmi Yisrael** — an external Israel, particularly Medinat Yisrael, incorporated into self-concept;
- **Yisrael she-be'atzmi** — an interior Yisrael from which Jewish ethical, intellectual, spiritual, historical, or covenantal orientation may emerge.

A person can have a strong relationship with one and a weak relationship with another.

They can reject one while preserving another.

They can organise them differently.

So if two Jews disagree about Israel, first establish whether they are actually disagreeing about the same Israel.

## 🪞 Do Not Search For The Authentic Jew

One of the worst habits non-Jewish political spaces develop is the **Jewish authenticity contest**.

A Zionist Jew says:

> **Anti-Zionism feels threatening to me.**

Someone replies:

> **Well, this anti-Zionist Jew says it isn't antisemitic.**

An anti-Zionist Jew says:

> **Please stop making Israel part of my identity.**

Someone replies:

> **Most Jews I know support Israel.**

Neither response actually engages the speaker.

It replaces them.

The implicit game becomes:

> **Which Jew counts?**

That is a terrible question.

Jewishness is not awarded according to political usefulness.

A Jew does not become more authentically Jewish because they agree with a communal institution.

A Jew does not become more authentically Jewish because they reject that institution.

A Jew does not cease being Jewish because their relationship with Medinat Yisrael differs from yours.

Jewish political disagreement should not become a competition in which outsiders distribute authenticity certificates.

---

## 🫱 Do Not Use Jews As Permission Slips

Another common move is:

> **A Jewish person agrees with me, therefore this cannot be antisemitic.**

No.

That does not follow.

A Jewish person can:

- miss antisemitism;
- reproduce antisemitism;
- disagree with another Jewish person's interpretation;
- possess incomplete historical knowledge;
- make political mistakes;
- or simply be wrong.

Identity can provide relevant experience.

It does not produce infallibility.

If a Jewish person says:

> **I don't think this statement is antisemitic**

that is useful evidence.

It may reveal context.

It may identify a distinction outsiders were missing.

It may challenge an overbroad institutional interpretation.

But it is not a transferable certificate stating:

> **THIS ENTIRE POLITICAL POSITION IS NOW JEW-APPROVED.**

Please do not make us into that.

---

## ⚠️ Do Not Use Jews As Vetoes Either

The opposite move is equally bad.

A Jewish person says:

> **I experience this as antisemitic.**

An institution responds:

> **Discussion over.**

No.

The experience matters.

It should be heard seriously.

The person may have recognised:

- an antisemitic trope;
- coded language;
- cumulative harassment;
- a historical reference;
- collective attribution;
- an implication invisible to outsiders.

But the fact that a political statement causes identity-level distress does not automatically establish discrimination.

That distinction becomes especially important where **Atzmi Yisrael** is involved.

A person may sincerely experience criticism of Medinat Yisrael as threatening something deeply integrated into Jewish self-concept.

That psychological reality deserves understanding.

It cannot by itself make a state immune from criticism.

## 🧿 Experience Is Evidence

A useful rule is:

> **experience is evidence, not sovereignty over the category.**

When someone from a minority group says:

> **this harmed me**

listen.

Do not immediately explain their own prejudice to them.

But then investigate.

Ask:

- What was said?
- Who was targeted?
- What history does the language carry?
- Is there a recognisable antisemitic trope?
- Is the speaker collectivising Jews?
- Is the political object actually Medinat Yisrael?
- Is the criticism directed at Zionism?
- Is the listener hearing something through Atzmi Yisrael that another Jewish listener does not share?
- Is there a pattern?
- Is there context that changes the meaning?

Good anti-discrimination work requires both **testimony and analysis**.

Removing either one makes the process worse.

---

## ⚖️ Do Not Outsource Your Judgement To Identity

There is a temptation, especially among people trying very hard not to be racist, to think:

> **I should simply defer to the minority person in the room.**

That instinct comes from somewhere understandable.

Majority groups have often ignored minority testimony completely.

But total political deference creates a different problem.

Which minority person?

What if they disagree?

What if ten Jewish people say ten different things?

What if a Palestinian person's political claim conflicts with a Jewish person's political interpretation?

What if two Jews disagree about whether a statement is antisemitic?

At some point, **you still have to think**.

Listen to relevant lived experience.

Learn the history.

Recognise power.

Understand antisemitic structures.

Understand Palestinian material conditions.

And then make an ethical and political judgement.

Respecting minority voices does not mean abandoning your own capacity for analysis.

## 🕸️ Do Not Treat Institutions As The Jews

Jewish institutions matter.

They can have expertise.

They can represent large memberships.

They can preserve communal knowledge.

They can identify patterns of antisemitism outsiders routinely miss.

They can also have:

- political commitments;
- governance structures;
- donors;
- constituencies;
- ideological histories;
- institutional incentives;
- and internal disagreement.

So:

> **a Jewish organisation says X**

means:

> **this Jewish organisation says X.**

It does not automatically mean:

> **the Jewish community says X.**

Even organisations with broad communal standing should be described with precision.

Who do they represent?

What is their membership?

What political commitments do they declare?

What authority do they actually possess?

Which Jewish traditions or constituencies are absent?

Institutional prominence and universal representativeness are not synonyms.

---

## 🗳️ Majorities Matter — And Still Do Not Erase Minorities

Jewish plurality does not mean:

> **every Jewish position has identical prevalence.**

Polling can matter.

Majority opinion can matter.

If a large majority of a Jewish population holds a particular belief, saying so accurately is useful.

But:

> **most Jews believe X**

does not imply:

> **therefore Jews who believe Y are not genuinely Jewish.**

Nor does it imply:

> **therefore X becomes immune from political criticism.**

A minority position can be:

- historically important;
- intellectually sophisticated;
- morally serious;
- politically consequential;
- or simply correct

without being numerically dominant.

Likewise, minority status does not make a position morally superior.

Numbers tell us prevalence.

They do not finish the argument.

---

## 🥚 Ask Which Jews Are Missing

Any claim about Jewish plurality should immediately raise another question:

> **Which Jewish experiences are actually represented here?**

A conversation dominated by:

- Ashkenazi;
- British;
- American;
- European;
- middle-class;
- highly educated;
- institutionally affiliated

Jewish voices may contain real internal disagreement while still being profoundly narrow.

Mizrahi, Sephardi, Maghrebi, Middle Eastern, North African, Ethiopian, Indian, Central Asian, Latin American, working-class, Haredi, secular, queer, disabled, mixed-background, and other Jewish experiences may reorganise the apparent disagreement.

The point is not to create an identity checklist.

It is to notice when:

> **Jewish plurality**

actually means:

> **several different Ashkenazi people argued on a panel.**

That is plurality.

It is not the whole thing.

## 🗯️ Do Not Assume Intra-Jewish Harshness Means One Side Is Illegitimate

Jews can be spectacularly rude to each other about this.

That is not evidence that one side has accidentally wandered outside Judaism.

Statements such as:

> **You abandoned your people.**

> **You made the state into an idol.**

> **You are endangering Jews.**

> **You are using antisemitism to protect state violence.**

> **You have forgotten Jewish history.**

often sit on top of deep disagreements about:

- Jewish safety;
- political sovereignty;
- diaspora;
- Jewish ethics;
- nationalism;
- collective responsibility;
- Palestinian rights;
- and the meaning of Yisrael.

The severity of the language may reflect the depth of the perceived stakes.

It does not resolve the underlying disagreement.

→ See: [🗯️ What Jews Are Actually Arguing About](./🗯️_what_jews_are_actually_arguing_about.md)

## 📖 Do Not Assume Firmness Means Shallowness

A person with a categorical position may have arrived there casually.

Or they may have spent years getting there.

For some Jews, movement towards anti-Zionism may involve:

- conflict with family;
- conflict with communal institutions;
- engagement with Palestinian testimony;
- historical study;
- confrontation with Israeli state conduct;
- re-evaluation of Jewish education;
- grief;
- fear;
- reconstruction of Jewish identity.

For some Jews, movement towards stronger Zionism may involve:

- rising antisemitism;
- family histories of persecution;
- fear about diaspora security;
- encounters with violence;
- renewed attachment to collective refuge.

You cannot infer the route from the endpoint.

Ask.

→ See: [📖 The Long Way To No](./📖_the_long_way_to_no.md)

## 🌿 Do Not Make Palestinians Referees Of Jewish Identity Either

There is another flattening manoeuvre available:

> **What do Palestinians think Jews should believe about Israel?**

That is not the right question either.

Palestinians should not have to adjudicate the authenticity of Jewish identity.

Palestinian expertise is indispensable for understanding:

- Palestinian history;
- displacement;
- occupation;
- land;
- Israeli state power;
- Palestinian political life;
- material consequences of Zionism;
- Palestinian demands for justice.

It is not their job to decide whether a Jew's relationship with Torah, diaspora, Eretz Yisrael, Am Yisrael, or Jewish collective memory is sufficiently Jewish.

The relevant Palestinian question is much more material:

> **What does this Jewish political project do to Palestinian people?**

That answer matters whether or not the project feels authentically Jewish to the person advocating it.

---

## 🌿 Jewish Identity Cannot Resolve Palestinian Rights

This boundary matters enormously.

Suppose a Jewish person says:

> **Jewish statehood is fundamental to my identity.**

That tells us something important about the person's self-concept.

It does not settle:

- Palestinian rights;
- borders;
- occupation;
- settlement;
- citizenship;
- equality;
- return;
- sovereignty;
- military conduct;
- ownership of land.

Identity explains why an issue matters to somebody.

It does not determine the rights of another population.

Likewise, a Palestinian person's relationship with their homeland cannot by itself settle every question about Jewish collective life.

Political arrangements require analysis of **all the people who have to live under them**.

## 🏠 Do Not Let Metaphor Erase Houses

Non-Jewish participants can become so respectful of Jewish language about:

- return;
- Zion;
- homeland;
- Eretz Yisrael;
- refuge;
- ancestral connection

that they stop asking what happens when those concepts acquire:

- borders;
- soldiers;
- planning law;
- citizenship;
- property regimes;
- settlements;
- demolition orders;
- checkpoints;
- prisons;
- military control.

Respect for Jewish historical and religious relationships with land does not require suspending material analysis.

Likewise, recognising Palestinian dispossession does not require pretending Jewish relationships with the land were fabricated in the nineteenth century.

Both forms of flattening are intellectually lazy.

→ See: [🏠 When A Philosophical Homeland Is Someone Else's Home](./🏠_when_a_philosophical_homeland_is_someone_elses_home.md)

## ⚖️ Do Not Treat Antisemitism As A Team Sport

Political movements are particularly bad at this.

One camp discovers that accusations of antisemitism can be abused.

It gradually becomes sceptical of almost every accusation.

Another camp discovers that antisemitism frequently hides inside discourse about Israel.

It gradually becomes suspicious of almost every strong criticism of Israel.

Both produce blind spots.

The useful standard is not:

> **Which political side said it?**

It is:

> **What happened?**

Antisemitism remains antisemitism when committed by:

- the right;
- the left;
- nationalists;
- anti-nationalists;
- Christians;
- Muslims;
- atheists;
- Israelis;
- Palestinians;
- Jews;
- anyone else.

Likewise, criticism of Israeli state conduct does not become antisemitic merely because it comes from a political movement you dislike.

Evidence first.

Team jersey later.

Preferably never.

## 🪞 Do Not Demand That Jews Perform Israel

Another common flattening occurs when Jewish people are expected to possess a position on Israel merely by appearing in public as Jews.

A Jewish person attends:

- a workplace;
- a university;
- a protest;
- a synagogue event;
- a diversity panel;
- a political meeting.

Someone asks:

> **What do you think about Israel?**

Perhaps they have an answer.

Perhaps they do not.

Perhaps they are exhausted.

Perhaps they came to discuss antisemitism in Britain.

Perhaps Medinat Yisrael is not central to their Jewish identity.

Perhaps it is deeply central and they do not want to litigate that relationship for strangers.

Jewishness is not consent to become a foreign-policy panel.

The expectation itself can reproduce the assumption that Jews possess an intrinsic political relationship with Israel.

---

## 🕯️ Do Not Demand Condemnation As An Entry Fee

Relatedly:

> **Before we listen to you about antisemitism, condemn Israel.**

No.

And:

> **Before we listen to you about Palestine, affirm Israel's right to exist.**

Also no.

People can be asked about relevant political positions when those positions genuinely matter.

But identity-based loyalty tests are corrosive.

A Jew should not have to condemn Medinat Yisrael before being entitled to discuss antisemitism.

A Palestinian should not have to perform reassurance about Zionism before being entitled to discuss Palestinian suffering.

The political argument may eventually require difficult questions.

The person's humanity does not require passing an ideological entrance exam.

---

## 🫱 Do Not Confuse Listening With Agreement

This sounds basic.

It is not always practised.

If a Jewish person explains:

> **This is why Israel feels constitutive of Jewish safety to me**

you can respond:

> **I understand why that relationship formed**

without concluding:

> **therefore every political structure required to sustain it is justified.**

Likewise, if an anti-Zionist Jew explains:

> **This is why I reject Zionism as part of my Jewish ethics**

you can understand that position without declaring:

> **therefore all Jewish attachment to Zionism is morally illegitimate.**

Understanding is not capitulation.

It is simply acquiring a better model of the person you're talking to.

That generally improves political disagreement.

## 🌉 Help People Meet On The Bridge

A useful ally can sometimes do something much less glamorous than choosing a side in an intra-Jewish argument.

They can help increase resolution.

When somebody says:

> **Israel**

ask:

> **Do you mean the state, the land, the people, or something else?**

When somebody says:

> **Zionism**

ask:

> **What do you mean by that here?**

When somebody says:

> **That was antisemitic**

ask:

> **What part landed as antisemitic, and what do you think it was saying about Jews?**

When somebody says:

> **Anti-Zionism is antisemitism**

ask:

> **Which form of anti-Zionism are you describing?**

When somebody says:

> **Zionists are dangerous**

ask:

> **Who exactly are you referring to?**

When somebody says:

> **Jewish safety requires Israel**

ask:

> **Which form of safety, and what political structure do you think is necessary to provide it?**

When somebody says:

> **Palestinian liberation**

ask:

> **What rights and political arrangements are included in that?**

Clarification is not cowardice.

It is often what prevents people from spending hours attacking positions nobody actually expressed.

## 🧿 A Better Way To Handle Conflicting Jewish Testimony

Suppose an institution receives two Jewish accounts.

Jew A:

> **This was antisemitic.**

Jew B:

> **I don't think it was.**

Do not:

1. count Jews;
2. decide which Jew seems more respectable;
3. ask which one represents the larger institution;
4. choose whichever answer creates the least administrative inconvenience.

Instead:

**Preserve both accounts.**

Then ask:

- What exactly occurred?
- What was the target?
- What language was used?
- What surrounding context exists?
- Was there repetition?
- Was there a classical antisemitic trope?
- Was Medinat Yisrael being criticised?
- Were Jews being made responsible for it?
- Was somebody's Jewish identity being challenged?
- Was the dispute actually about Zionism?
- Which definitions were each participant using?
- Are there material power asymmetries relevant to the incident?

Jewish disagreement is data.

Do not delete it because it complicates the form.

## 🕸️ Plurality Does Not Mean Neutrality

Respecting Jewish plurality does not require an institution to say:

> **All Jewish political positions are equally good, so we cannot judge anything.**

Absolutely not.

A Jewish position can be:

- racist;
- authoritarian;
- dishonest;
- discriminatory;
- historically inaccurate;
- violent;
- supremacist;
- antisemitic;
- anti-Palestinian;
- or otherwise politically objectionable.

Jewishness does not purify politics.

The same is true for every identity group.

Plurality means:

> **do not erase disagreement by pretending one position automatically represents the group.**

It does not mean:

> **suspend moral judgement whenever identity enters the room.**

---

## 🪞 Nor Does Plurality Mean Everyone Is Equally Powerful

Another flattening problem:

> **Two Jews disagree, therefore this is symmetrical.**

Not necessarily.

One may represent:

- a major institution;
- substantial donors;
- government access;
- established communal infrastructure;
- legal influence;
- media access.

Another may be:

- marginalised;
- institutionally excluded;
- politically isolated;
- professionally vulnerable.

The existence of two opinions does not mean both have equal capacity to define:

> **the Jewish position**

in public life.

Plurality should therefore be analysed alongside **power**.

Who gets heard?

Who gets invited?

Who gets described as representative?

Whose definitions become institutional policy?

Who gets dismissed as fringe?

Who has resources to reproduce their interpretation?

That matters.

---

## 🥚 And Sometimes “Mainstream Jewish Opinion” Is A Governance Category

The phrase:

> **mainstream Jewish opinion**

deserves examination whenever it appears.

Sometimes it accurately describes a broad distribution.

Sometimes it means:

> **the organisations institutions already know how to contact.**

Those are not necessarily the same thing.

Institutional convenience can become representation.

Representation can become authority.

Authority can become:

> **this is what Jews think.**

Then dissenting Jews appear not merely politically different but somehow outside Jewish legitimacy.

That is precisely the flattening this cluster is trying to avoid.

---

## 🌿 Meanwhile, Palestinians Cannot Wait For Perfect Jewish Consensus

There is another practical reason this matters.

Jews may never agree about:

- Zionism;
- diaspora;
- sovereignty;
- Eretz Yisrael;
- nationalism;
- Jewish statehood;
- what Jewish safety requires.

That disagreement cannot become a mechanism for postponing Palestinian rights.

Palestinian life does not enter suspended animation while Jewish communities complete an internal philosophical reconciliation.

Shared language may help Jews speak more accurately.

It may reduce some unnecessary conflict.

It may help institutions distinguish antisemitism from political disagreement more reliably.

But the political consequences of state power continue while the conversation happens.

That asymmetry must remain visible.

## 🧭 A Short Guide For Non-Jewish Readers

If you remember nothing else, remember this:

1. **Do not ask which Jew is the real Jew.**
2. **Do not use one Jewish person to invalidate another Jewish person's experience.**
3. **Do not use one Jewish person's experience as an automatic political veto.**
4. **Do not assume Jewish institutions represent every Jew.**
5. **Do not assume Jewish plurality means antisemitism cannot be identified.**
6. **Do not assume attachment to Israel means allegiance to Israel.**
7. **Do not assume rejection of Zionism means rejection of Jewishness.**
8. **Do not make Jews responsible for Medinat Yisrael merely because they are Jewish.**
9. **Do not treat Palestinians as objects inside a Jewish identity dispute.**
10. **Ask what people mean before deciding what they believe.**
11. **Learn enough history to recognise old antisemitism wearing new nouns.**
12. **Keep thinking.**

The last one is annoyingly important.

## 🧿 The Point

Jewish plurality is not a problem to be solved.

It is a condition to be represented accurately.

The problem begins when outsiders, institutions, or Jewish organisations themselves turn that plurality into:

- one authoritative voice;
- one authenticity standard;
- one communal veto;
- one permission slip;
- one definition of Israel;
- one definition of Zionism;
- one definition of Jewish safety;
- one acceptable relationship with Medinat Yisrael.

That flattening makes everybody worse at recognising what is actually happening.

It makes antisemitism harder to analyse.

It makes Jewish disagreement harder to understand.

It makes Palestinian political claims easier to subordinate to Jewish identity processing.

And it encourages people to fight over who represents Jews instead of examining the political claim in front of them.

A better starting point is:

> **Jewish testimony matters. Jewish disagreement also matters. Neither removes the need for evidence, context, political judgement, or moral reasoning.**

Listen.

Distinguish.

Do not manufacture an authenticity contest.

And if two Jews are screaming apparently contradictory things about Israel, before choosing your favourite:

> **ask which Yisrael each one means.**

You may discover that the disagreement is clearer than it first appeared.

You may discover that it is considerably deeper.

Either outcome is better than flattening it.
