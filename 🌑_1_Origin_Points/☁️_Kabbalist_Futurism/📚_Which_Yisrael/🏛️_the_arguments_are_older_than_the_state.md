# 🏛️ The Arguments Are Older Than The State  
**First created:** 2026-08-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-18  
*Jewish disagreement about land, exile, sovereignty, peoplehood, return, diaspora, and political power did not begin with Medinat Yisrael — and cannot be understood as though it did.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

There is a strange compression that happens in contemporary discussions about Israel.

Jewish disagreement is often narrated as though there was once a reasonably coherent Jewish position on Israel, followed by the establishment of **Medinat Yisrael** in 1948, followed eventually by contemporary disagreement about Zionism and Israeli state conduct.

That is not a useful history.

The arguments are older than the state.

Much older.

Jewish communities have spent centuries arguing about:

- what **Yisrael** means;
- what makes Jews a people;
- what exile means;
- whether exile is principally catastrophe, condition, punishment, political reality, theological category, or something else;
- what relationship Jews living elsewhere retain with **Eretz Yisrael**;
- what Jerusalem means when most Jews do not live there;
- whether Jewish collective life requires territorial sovereignty;
- whether sovereignty is desirable;
- whether sovereignty is dangerous;
- whether political return should precede redemption;
- whether humans may accelerate redemption;
- whether Jewish political agency should be territorial or diasporic;
- whether Jewish flourishing requires autonomy;
- whether autonomy requires a state;
- whether Jewish particularity and universal political commitments can coexist;
- and what obligations Jews owe the societies in which they actually live.

Modern Zionism did not invent these questions.

It offered a particular family of modern political answers to some of them.

And Jews immediately argued about those answers too.

---

## 🧭 A Warning About The Timeline

This node is necessarily compressed.

It is not a complete history of Jewish political thought.

It is certainly not a complete history of Jewish relationships with Eretz Yisrael.

And it should not be read as a neat intellectual railway line:

**Temple**  
↓  
**exile**  
↓  
**rabbinic Judaism**  
↓  
**Zionism**  
↓  
**Israel**

There is no such tidy line.

Jewish political thought developed across different empires, languages, legal systems, theological traditions, migrations, class structures, geographic communities, and encounters with surrounding peoples.

Ideas disappeared and returned.

The same text acquired different meanings.

Political possibilities changed.

Jewish communities developed sophisticated forms of collective governance without possessing sovereign states.

Messianic expectation did not mean the same thing as political nationalism.

Longing for Jerusalem did not automatically mean support for a particular programme of territorial sovereignty.

And Jewish residence in Eretz Yisrael did not begin with modern Zionist settlement.

The point of this node is therefore narrower:

> **do not mistake the establishment of Medinat Yisrael for the beginning of the Jewish argument about what Yisrael is.**

---

## 🕯️ After Sovereignty

The destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE and the suppression of subsequent Jewish revolts did not produce an instantaneous, universal Jewish political philosophy called **diaspora**.

But over the long period that followed, Jewish collective life increasingly developed without a Jewish sovereign territorial state.

That matters.

Because Jewish civilisation did not simply stop doing politics.

Jewish communities governed communal institutions.

They developed systems of law.

They collected and distributed communal resources.

They maintained schools, courts, charities, religious institutions, burial societies, systems of representation, and mechanisms of communal discipline.

They negotiated with rulers.

They negotiated with other communities.

They argued internally about authority.

They developed institutions capable of maintaining collective life across extraordinary geographic dispersion.

Modern political theory has often treated **sovereignty** as the obvious horizon of political existence.

Jewish history makes that assumption considerably less obvious.

A people can possess law, institutions, collective memory, political argument, systems of authority, transregional relationships, communal obligations, and mechanisms of continuity without possessing a sovereign territorial state.

That does not prove that statelessness is desirable.

It establishes something more basic:

> **Jewish political existence and Jewish state sovereignty are historically not the same thing.**

Scholars of Jewish political thought have therefore had to grapple seriously with political traditions formed under conditions of statelessness rather than treating those centuries as merely a waiting room before sovereignty returned. ## 🌿 Eretz Yisrael Did Not Disappear

None of this means Eretz Yisrael became irrelevant.

It remained present in Jewish prayer, text, law, memory, pilgrimage, messianic imagination, study, settlement, burial practices, communal philanthropy, and religious thought.

Jewish communities continued to exist in the land itself.

Other Jews travelled there.

Others supported communities there materially.

Others understood return principally through a messianic framework.

Others interpreted Jewish life elsewhere through an ongoing relationship with the land without treating immediate territorial sovereignty as a practical political programme.

These are not contradictions once we stop assuming that:

> **relationship with land = modern nationalism**

Jewish attachment to Eretz Yisrael is older than Zionism.

Modern Zionism therefore did not need to invent Jewish attachment to the land.

What it did was something historically more specific:

**it translated inherited relationships with land, peoplehood, return and collective memory into modern political projects operating within an age of nationalism, empire, territorial sovereignty, mass migration and nation-state formation.**

That distinction is crucial.

## 🌉 Jerusalem Was Already More Than A City

Jerusalem demonstrates the problem particularly well.

Jerusalem was and is an actual place.

But Jerusalem also accumulated enormous textual, liturgical, theological, messianic, poetic, mystical, historical and emotional meanings.

A Jew could orient towards Jerusalem without possessing Jerusalem.

A Jew could mourn Jerusalem without proposing a municipal government.

A Jew could imagine return without possessing a nineteenth-century theory of the nation-state.

A Jew could understand Jerusalem as central to Jewish existence while living an entire Jewish life somewhere else.

That does not make the geographical Jerusalem unreal.

It means **geographical Jerusalem and philosophical Jerusalem have never been perfectly reducible to one another**.

That distinction becomes politically consequential once symbolic return and territorial sovereignty become joined.

## 🏛️ Modernity Changes The Question

European modernity dramatically altered the political conditions under which many Jews were asking these questions.

Emancipation raised one possibility:

> **What if Jews could become equal citizens of the states in which they already lived?**

Nationalism raised another:

> **If nations are supposed to possess political expression, what kind of nation are Jews?**

Antisemitism raised another:

> **What happens if emancipation does not actually make Jews safe?**

Secularisation raised another:

> **What remains of Jewish collective identity when Jewishness is no longer organised principally through religious observance?**

Socialism raised another:

> **Is the Jewish problem principally national, or is Jewish oppression inseparable from class society and broader structures of exploitation?**

Modern colonialism and empire shaped the political environment in which territorial projects could be imagined and pursued.

And the development of the modern nation-state increasingly encouraged everyone to answer an extraordinarily consequential question:

> **Where does this people belong politically?**

There was no single Jewish answer.

---

## 🕸️ One Answer: We Belong Here

One of the most important alternatives to Zionism developed within Jewish socialist politics.

The **Bund** eventually articulated the principle commonly associated with **doikayt** — *hereness*.

The basic political orientation was radically different from a politics organised around territorial relocation.

Jewish workers were already somewhere.

They had homes.

Languages.

Neighbourhoods.

Political struggles.

Employers.

Schools.

Communities.

Oppressors.

Comrades.

The task was not necessarily to discover somewhere else for Jews to become political.

The task was to fight **where Jews already were**.

The Bund combined socialism with demands for Jewish civic equality and, increasingly, national-cultural autonomy. Yiddish became central to its understanding of Jewish mass culture. During the interwar period, *doikayt* became particularly associated with its insistence that Jewish futures should be built in the places Jews actually inhabited.  [oai_citation:1‡YIVO Encyclopedia](https://encyclopedia.yivo.org/article.aspx/bund?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This was not simply:

> **we don't like Zionism.**

It contained a different answer to the political question.

> **Jewish collective life does not require abandoning diaspora in order to become politically meaningful.**

That argument remains extremely important.

---

## 📖 Another Answer: We Are A Religious Community

Some currents of Reform Judaism produced a different response.

Nineteenth-century Reform thinkers could reject the proposition that Jews constituted a political nation requiring territorial restoration.

The 1885 Pittsburgh Platform in the United States famously described Jews not as a nation but as a religious community and rejected restoration to Palestine as the organising political future of Judaism.

That position itself changed.

By the twentieth century — particularly under the pressure of escalating European antisemitism, Nazism and the destruction of European Jewish life — Reform Judaism's relationship with Zionism shifted substantially.  [oai_citation:2‡My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/reform-judaism/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That historical change is important precisely because it demonstrates that Jewish relationships with Zionism are not timeless psychological properties.

Communities reinterpret themselves when the world changes.

What appears to one generation to be incompatible with Jewish belonging can appear to another generation indispensable to Jewish survival.

---

## ✡️ Another Answer: You Cannot Manufacture Redemption

Parts of Orthodox Judaism opposed political Zionism for very different reasons.

Their objection was not necessarily:

> **Jews are merely a religious community and should assimilate politically into liberal nation-states.**

Quite the opposite.

For some Orthodox opponents, the problem was that Jewish return, sovereignty and redemption belonged within a theological structure that a secular nationalist movement could not simply appropriate and accelerate.

If restoration was ultimately bound to divine redemption, then human political actors declaring:

> **we will establish sovereignty ourselves**

could represent not fulfilment but theological transgression.

Other Orthodox Jews reached different conclusions.

Religious Zionist movements developed arguments through which settlement, return and eventually sovereignty could acquire religious significance.

And Orthodox political thought itself developed in multiple directions. Scholarship on figures such as Isaac Breuer demonstrates just how inadequate a simple equation of **religious politics = religious Zionism** can be: modern Orthodox political thought could embrace forms of sovereignty while remaining explicitly anti-Zionist.  [oai_citation:3‡Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/harvard-theological-review/article/how-jewish-orthodoxy-became-a-state-isaac-breuer-and-the-invention-of-the-statist-theocracy/8A49010B61AAFB7CA99074EF47D2D7FF?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Again:

the categories do not line up neatly.

---

## 🌱 Another Answer: Zionism

Modern Zionism emerged within this crowded field.

Its origins are themselves debated, although the **Ḥibat Tsiyon / Lovers of Zion** movements of the 1880s form an important part of the immediate genealogy of organised modern Zionist politics. Following the pogroms beginning in 1881, groups supporting Jewish settlement in Palestine proliferated, and Ḥibat Tsiyon was formally organised at the 1884 Katowice conference.  [oai_citation:4‡YIVO Encyclopedia](https://encyclopedia.yivo.org/article.aspx/parties_and_ideologies?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Theodor Herzl subsequently gave political Zionism a particularly influential programme.

But even here, we immediately encounter the problem explored elsewhere in this cluster:

**there was never only one Zionism.**

Zionists disagreed about:

- whether the immediate objective was refuge or sovereignty;
- whether Palestine was indispensable;
- whether Jewish cultural regeneration mattered more than immediate statehood;
- what language Jewish national life should use;
- what relationship Zionism should have with religion;
- what economic system should be built;
- whether class conflict could be subordinated to national struggle;
- what relationship the movement should have with imperial powers;
- what relationship Jewish settlement should have with the existing Arab population;
- what territorial form Jewish political life should eventually take.

The famous dispute over the British East Africa proposal alone demonstrates that even early political Zionists could disagree profoundly about whether the essential object was **a Jewish refuge somewhere** or specifically **return to Eretz Yisrael**.  [oai_citation:5‡Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Zionism?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Zionism therefore did not end the older argument about Yisrael.

It became one of its most consequential modern arenas.

## 🧿 Even Sovereignty Does Not Mean One Thing

There is another trap here.

It is easy to retrospectively sort Jewish political history into:

**Zionists**  
= wanted Jewish sovereignty

versus:

**anti-Zionists**  
= did not want Jewish political existence.

That is nonsense.

There have been Jewish political traditions organised around:

- territorial sovereignty;
- cultural autonomy;
- communal autonomy;
- socialist internationalism;
- diasporic nationalism;
- religious authority;
- liberal citizenship;
- binationalism;
- non-state collective institutions;
- messianic restoration;
- and combinations that fit badly into contemporary categories.

Martin Buber, for example, could be a Zionist while articulating political ideas profoundly suspicious of conventional sovereignty. His political theology drew upon an interpretation of divine sovereignty that resisted transferring absolute sovereignty to human political institutions.  [oai_citation:6‡Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/buber/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The Bund could be fiercely committed to Jewish collective existence while rejecting Zionism and territorial nationalism.

Orthodox anti-Zionists could reject secular Jewish sovereignty while retaining an enormously strong conception of Jewish peoplehood.

Classical Reform Jews could reject Jewish nationhood in favour of religious community.

These positions disagree radically.

But none can be adequately translated as:

> **cares about Jews / does not care about Jews.**

They are competing theories of **what Jewish collective existence is**.

## 🧠 This Is Why Contemporary Arguments Feel So Deep

This history helps explain something psychologically important.

When contemporary Jews argue about Israel, they are often not merely arguing about:

> **the foreign policy of a state established in 1948.**

They may also be encountering inherited answers to much older questions.

Where does Jewish safety come from?

What is diaspora?

Is diaspora a home or a condition to escape?

What does return mean?

What is a Jewish people without political sovereignty?

What happens when sovereignty itself becomes dangerous?

Can Jewish collective life flourish as a minority?

What does Eretz Yisrael require of Jews?

What does Jewish history require after catastrophe?

Can Jewish political power be subjected to the same moral suspicion Jewish tradition developed under conditions of powerlessness?

Who gets to define Jewish collective interests?

Those questions sit underneath contemporary vocabulary.

This is one reason two Jews can encounter the same sentence about Israel and understand its implications very differently.

## 🥚 And This History Is Often Told Too Ashkenormatively

There is an obvious problem with the account above.

A substantial portion of the familiar modern story runs:

**European emancipation**  
↓  
**European nationalism**  
↓  
**European antisemitism**  
↓  
**European Jewish political movements**  
↓  
**Zionism / Bundism / Reform / Orthodoxy**

That history matters enormously.

It is also not the history of all Jews.

Jewish communities across North Africa, the Middle East, the Ottoman world, Persia, Central Asia, Ethiopia, India and elsewhere encountered modernity, empire, colonialism, nationalism, Zionism and Israeli state formation from different political locations.

The categories produced within European Jewish argument cannot simply be treated as the universal Jewish baseline and everybody else added afterwards.

This matters especially because Zionism itself emerged substantially within European Jewish political conditions while its eventual consequences reorganised Jewish life far beyond Europe.

So this node knowingly begins with an incomplete map.

That incompleteness needs correcting rather than disguising.

## 🌿 And Palestinians Were Already There

There is another historical correction without which this discussion becomes morally and analytically distorted.

The fact that Jews had been arguing for centuries about land, return, sovereignty and Yisrael did not make Palestine an empty philosophical surface upon which the argument could eventually be resolved.

Palestinians existed outside the Jewish argument.

They had homes, towns, villages, farms, religious institutions, political relationships, family histories, property, languages, economies and their own developing forms of collective identity.

Once a Jewish argument about return became a territorial political project, another population became materially implicated in an argument it had not created.

That changes the analysis.

The history of Jewish longing cannot by itself answer the political consequences of implementing that longing through settlement and sovereignty.

The history of Jewish persecution cannot by itself determine Palestinian political rights.

And the depth of Jewish disagreement about Yisrael cannot make Palestinian displacement an internal Jewish philosophical problem.

It is a Palestinian material reality.

## 🪞 Do Not Project 2026 Backwards

One of the most useful disciplines for this cluster is therefore:

> **do not make historical Jews answer contemporary survey questions.**

“Zionist” and “anti-Zionist” are not timeless Jewish personality types.

A medieval Jewish thinker did not secretly possess a position on the 1949 armistice lines.

Liturgical longing for Jerusalem is not automatically a nineteenth-century nationalist programme.

Opposition to secular Zionism does not automatically imply contemporary support for a Palestinian state.

Jewish diasporic flourishing does not automatically constitute Bundism.

Religious attachment to Eretz Yisrael does not automatically constitute support for Medinat Yisrael.

And modern Zionism cannot simply claim every earlier Jewish expression of longing for Zion as retrospective endorsement of its particular political programme.

The reverse error is equally bad.

The fact that modern political Zionism belongs to modernity does not mean Jewish attachment to Eretz Yisrael was invented by nineteenth-century nationalists.

Both shortcuts flatten history.

## 🔮 Yisrael Survived Without Medinat

There is a fact sitting underneath this entire cluster that is so obvious it can become strangely difficult to see.

For most of the last two thousand years:

**Yisrael existed without Medinat Yisrael.**

Not without suffering.

Not without persecution.

Not without longing.

Not without catastrophic vulnerability.

And certainly not without relationships with Eretz Yisrael.

But Jewish civilisation continued producing:

law;

philosophy;

poetry;

mysticism;

science;

politics;

languages;

ritual;

argument;

communities;

families;

institutions;

revolutions;

music;

literature;

theology;

jokes;

and Jews.

This does not prove that statehood was unnecessary.

It does not prove that diaspora was safe.

It does not prove that Zionism was wrong.

It establishes something more conceptually important:

> **Medinat Yisrael cannot retrospectively become the condition that made Yisrael possible.**

Yisrael was already there.

That is part of why **Yisrael she-be'atzmi** is worth exploring.

There are forms of Jewish continuity, obligation, interiority and collective identity whose genealogy does not begin with the modern state.

## 🧭 The Point

This node does not ask the reader to conclude:

> **therefore Zionism is wrong.**

Nor:

> **therefore Zionism is right.**

Its claim is simpler.

When contemporary Jews disagree about Israel, we should stop imagining that one side possesses the obvious historical Jewish position while everyone else has recently departed from it.

There has never been only one Jewish answer to:

> **What is Yisrael?**

There has never been only one Jewish answer to:

> **What does exile mean?**

There has never been only one Jewish answer to:

> **What should Jewish political life look like?**

And modern Jewish politics did not suddenly become plural when Jews started disagreeing about the conduct of Medinat Yisrael.

The plurality was already there.

Zionism inherited it.

Anti-Zionism inherited it.

Medinat Yisrael inherited it.

We inherited it.

So when contemporary Jewish arguments seem to contain radically different assumptions about land, state, peoplehood, sovereignty, diaspora, safety, religion, history and political obligation, sometimes the explanation is extremely straightforward:

> **we are still having the argument.**

The state did not settle it.
