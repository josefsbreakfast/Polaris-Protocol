# 🪬 One Can Never Speak For All  
**First created:** 2026-08-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-18  
*Rabbinic argument, preserved disagreement, and why no Jew, institution, movement, or state gets uncomplicated ownership of the sentence “Judaism says.”*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

There is a sentence that appears constantly in political arguments involving Jews:

> **Jews believe...**

Or:

> **Judaism teaches...**

Or:

> **The Jewish position is...**

Sometimes the sentence that follows is perfectly defensible.

Jewish traditions contain teachings.

Halakhah reaches decisions.

Communities establish norms.

Religious movements have positions.

Scholars can describe majority and minority traditions.

Rabbis can make arguments from Jewish law and text.

None of this requires pretending Jewish thought is infinitely indeterminate.

But there is another claim hiding inside the grammar:

> **I can speak in the singular for Yisrael.**

That deserves considerably more suspicion.

Rabbinic literature did not preserve a tradition in which every serious disagreement was edited away once somebody won.

It frequently did the opposite.

It preserved:

- argument;
- minority opinion;
- rejected opinion;
- competing schools;
- named disagreement;
- reasoning;
- reinterpretation;
- argument about the rules for argument.

Sometimes halakhah is decided.

The disagreement may remain on the page anyway.

That is not an accidental editorial failure.

It is part of the architecture.

---

## 📚 This Is Not “Anything Goes”

Before going further, an important correction.

The rabbinic tradition does **not** teach:

> **every opinion is equally correct.**

Halakhic systems require decisions.

Majorities can prevail.

Arguments can fail.

Claims can be rejected.

There are boundaries.

There are interpretive rules.

There are positions a tradition does not accept.

So:

> **one can never speak for all**

does not mean:

> **nobody can ever say what Judaism teaches about anything.**

It means something narrower and more demanding:

> **the existence of judgement does not require the erasure of plurality.**

A decision and a record of disagreement can coexist.

That distinction matters enormously for this cluster.

---

## 🕯️ The Losing Opinion Is Still In The Book

One of the most striking features of rabbinic literature is that we frequently know who **lost**.

We know what Beit Shammai argued even where halakhah follows Beit Hillel.

We know minority opinions.

We know rejected arguments.

We know that sages disagreed.

The tradition could have given us:

> **ANSWER: HILLEL. NEXT QUESTION.**

It did not.

It preserved the argument.

*Mishnah Eduyot* even stops to ask why opinions that do not determine the final ruling are being recorded.

One answer concerns intellectual humility: earlier authorities themselves did not simply remain fixed in their own positions.

The fact that this question appears at all is important.

The tradition is self-conscious about preserving dissent.

---

## 🪞 A Tradition That Remembers Who Disagreed

That produces an unusual archive.

A later reader does not encounter only:

> **what the institution eventually decided.**

They can also encounter:

> **what another serious Jewish thinker thought instead.**

That matters because an archive of disagreement prevents institutional victory from becoming identical with:

> **the only Jewish thought that ever existed.**

A position may lose juridically.

It does not necessarily disappear intellectually.

That is a very different architecture from one in which the minority voice is deleted after the vote.

---

## 🧿 *Elu V'Elu* Does Not Mean Everyone Wins

The famous passage in **Eruvin 13b** describes the long disagreement between Beit Hillel and Beit Shammai.

The heavenly declaration says, in effect:

> **These and those are words of the living God.**

And then:

> **the halakhah follows Beit Hillel.**

Both parts matter.

If we preserve only:

> **these and those**

we can turn rabbinic plurality into mush.

If we preserve only:

> **the halakhah follows Hillel**

we lose what makes the passage remarkable.

The text manages to hold:

**multiple serious interpretations**  
and  
**the necessity of practical decision**

at the same time.

That is not relativism.

It is structured plurality.

---

## 🌉 Beit Hillel Had To Teach The Other Side

The same passage gives a reason Beit Hillel merited having halakhah established according to their view.

They were described as patient and forbearing.

And, crucially, they studied and taught:

> **their own position and the position of Beit Shammai.**

Indeed, the passage emphasises their willingness to present Beit Shammai's position with respect.

That is an extraordinary argument about authority.

The winning school is not praised because:

> **they successfully destroyed the opposition.**

Part of their merit lies in their ability to **represent the opposition**.

That has obvious relevance here.

If you cannot explain what another Jewish position actually says without converting it into a caricature, your certainty that you speak for Judaism should probably decline somewhat.

---

## 🪬 Intellectual Authority Requires Representational Discipline

This gives us a useful rule:

> **Before claiming that another Jewish position is outside the tradition, demonstrate that you can describe the position accurately enough that its holder would recognise it.**

That does not mean agreeing.

Beit Hillel and Beit Shammai did not stop disagreeing because everybody became nicer.

The disagreement remained.

But intellectual seriousness required preserving the thing disagreed with.

That is very different from:

> **I know what those Jews believe because I have named their political category.**

---

## 🕯️ *Machloket L'Shem Shamayim*

**Pirkei Avot 5:17** distinguishes between dispute:

> **for the sake of Heaven**

and dispute that is not.

Its paradigmatic example of the former is the disagreement between Hillel and Shammai.

Again, the striking thing is that:

> **disagreement itself is not treated as evidence of communal failure.**

There are bad arguments.

There are destructive arguments.

There are disputes driven by ego, domination, faction, or self-interest.

But the solution is not:

> **therefore Jews should stop disagreeing.**

The tradition instead distinguishes **what kind of disagreement is taking place**.

That is a much more useful question.

---

## 🗯️ The Problem Is Not That We Argue

This cluster is not trying to cure Jews of disagreement.

We would need a different project and substantially more storage.

The problem is not:

> **Zionist Jews and anti-Zionist Jews disagree.**

Or:

> **religious and secular Jews disagree.**

Or:

> **diasporists and nationalists disagree.**

Or:

> **different Jews understand antisemitism differently.**

The problem begins when disagreement becomes:

> **You cannot possibly be speaking Jewishly because you disagree with me.**

Or:

> **Our institution represents Jews, therefore your Jewish disagreement is politically irrelevant.**

Or:

> **There is one Jewish interpretation, and conveniently it is ours.**

That is where plurality becomes a governance problem.

---

## 📚 The Mishnah Does Something Modern Institutions Often Don't

A contemporary institution may receive:

**majority opinion**  
and  
**minority opinion**

and publish:

> **stakeholders agreed...**

The Mishnah has rather more appetite for leaving the scar tissue visible.

It can preserve:

> **Rabbi X says this.**

> **The sages say that.**

> **Rabbi Y disagrees.**

The reader sees the disagreement.

That is valuable information.

The archive does not become cleaner by deleting the losing voice.

It becomes poorer.

---

## 🕸️ Jewish Disagreement Is Data

This gives us a principle that belongs across Polaris:

> **disagreement is data.**

If Jews disagree sharply about:

- Zionism;
- Medinat Yisrael;
- diaspora;
- antisemitism;
- Jewish safety;
- land;
- sovereignty;
- Palestinian liberation;

the task is not necessarily to find the answer that allows us to discard the disagreement.

The disagreement may itself reveal:

- different histories;
- different meanings of Yisrael;
- different threat models;
- different ethical priorities;
- different social locations;
- different relationships with power.

Flattening the disagreement destroys information.

---

## 🧠 Different Minds Are Not A Bug

There is a wonderfully relevant passage in **Berakhot 58a** concerning a multitude of Israel.

A blessing is recited acknowledging the One who knows secrets because people's:

> **minds differ from one another**

just as their faces differ.

There is something almost comically useful about this for the current discussion.

Apparently:

> **Jews do not all think the same thing**

was already sufficiently unsurprising to become liturgical material.

Difference of mind is not presented as a mysterious corruption of collective existence.

It is part of collective existence.

---

## 🪞 Am Yisrael Is Not A Hive Mind

That matters for the distinction between:

**Am Yisrael**

and:

**one Jewish political opinion.**

A people can remain a people while disagreeing.

A community does not require neurological uniformity.

Collective belonging does not require:

> **everyone reaches the same interpretation of Medinat Yisrael.**

Nor:

> **everyone defines Zionism identically.**

Nor:

> **everyone experiences criticism of Israel identically.**

The collective contains different minds.

That is not evidence that the collective has failed.

---

## ⚖️ Decision Is Still Possible

None of this means collective governance becomes impossible.

Rabbinic law develops mechanisms for deciding.

Courts decide.

Majorities matter.

Communities adopt practice.

Later authorities weigh earlier authorities.

The existence of multiple arguments does not prevent action.

That is important because plurality can otherwise become another excuse for paralysis:

> **Jews disagree, therefore nobody can ever make a judgement.**

No.

The tradition gives us another model:

**hear disagreement**  
↓  
**preserve disagreement**  
↓  
**reason**  
↓  
**decide where decision is required**  
↓  
**do not pretend the losing position never existed**

That is much more sophisticated.

---

## 🏛️ Majority Does Not Become Totality

This gives us another crucial distinction.

Suppose:

> **most Jews believe X.**

That may be empirically important.

Suppose:

> **the halakhic majority rules X.**

That may be normatively decisive inside a particular legal framework.

Suppose:

> **a major Jewish institution adopts X.**

That matters institutionally.

None of those sentences means:

> **Every Jew believes X.**

Nor:

> **No serious Jewish argument for Y exists.**

Nor:

> **Y has ceased to be part of Jewish intellectual history.**

A majority is a majority.

It is not totality.

---

## 🪬 Even Heaven Does Not Simply End The Argument

The famous **Oven of Akhnai** narrative in *Bava Metzia 59b* pushes the problem of interpretive authority even further.

Rabbi Eliezer produces increasingly extraordinary signs supporting his position.

Eventually a heavenly voice supports him.

Rabbi Yehoshua answers:

> **It is not in heaven.**

The story is complicated and should not be flattened into a modern slogan about democracy.

But one thing is striking.

Rabbinic legal interpretation is represented as a process entrusted to human interpreters operating through the received system of argument and decision.

Even extraordinary authority does not simply abolish the interpretive community.

That gives rabbinic disagreement enormous seriousness.

Interpretation is work.

Authority is structured.

And humans remain responsible for what they decide.

---

## 🧠 “God Says” Is Therefore A Dangerous Shortcut

This does not mean Jewish tradition never makes strong claims about divine command.

Obviously it does.

But the Oven of Akhnai makes it difficult to treat:

> **God agrees with me**

as a convenient way to terminate rabbinic argument.

If anything, the story warns that:

**certainty of revelation**

and:

**authority to settle a communal legal dispute**

are not always identical questions.

That should make everybody slightly more cautious about political claims of the form:

> **Judaism simply requires my preferred constitutional arrangement.**

Perhaps.

Show your working.

---

## 🔮 Yisrael She-be'atzmi Is Therefore Not One Voice Either

This matters especially for the cluster's proposed concept of:

**Yisrael she-be'atzmi.**

If the Yisrael within becomes:

> **the authentic Jewish conscience**

we have simply created another monopoly.

No.

Different Jews may cultivate different interior Yisraels.

They may draw upon:

- different texts;
- different traditions;
- different communities;
- different histories;
- different teachers;
- different languages;
- different encounters with the world.

The point of Yisrael she-be'atzmi is not:

> **look inside and discover the correct Jewish political answer.**

It is:

> **there are forms of Jewish orientation that do not require Medinat Yisrael to monopolise Yisrael.**

Those forms will still argue with each other.

Of course they will.

## 🧠 Atzmi Yisrael Can Make Plurality Feel Threatening

The opposite architecture can produce a particular difficulty.

If Medinat Yisrael has become incorporated into **Atzmi Yisrael**, another Jew's rejection of the state may not feel like:

> **a Jewish person has reached a different political conclusion.**

It may feel like:

> **a Jew is rejecting something constitutive of us.**

That makes internal plurality more difficult to tolerate.

The disagreement becomes existential.

Now dissent can be translated into:

- betrayal;
- abandonment;
- self-hatred;
- communal disloyalty;
- danger.

Understanding that process does not determine who is politically correct.

It helps explain why one Jewish voice may become motivated to exclude another from:

> **the Jewish position.**

→ See: [🧠 When Medina Becomes Self](./🧠_when_medina_becomes_self.md)

---

## 📖 Minority Does Not Mean Un-Jewish

This matters particularly for Jews occupying minority political positions.

An anti-Zionist Jewish position does not become:

> **not Jewish**

merely because most Jews in a particular population may reject it.

A Zionist position does not become:

> **not Jewish**

because an anti-Zionist Jewish intellectual tradition exists.

A Bundist does not erase a religious Zionist.

A religious Zionist does not erase an Orthodox anti-Zionist.

A secular diasporist does not erase Jewish attachment to Eretz Yisrael.

The point is not:

> **every position is equally representative.**

The point is:

> **representativeness and Jewish legitimacy are different questions.**

---

## 🥚 And “Rabbinic Judaism” Is Not One Cultural Voice Either

There is an Ashkenormative danger here too.

When English-speaking Jews say:

> **the rabbis**

the mental picture can become strangely European.

Rabbinic Judaism developed across:

- Babylonia;
- the Land of Israel;
- North Africa;
- Iberia;
- the Middle East;
- Europe;
- and later Jewish worlds across enormous geographic range.

Jewish interpretive traditions were carried through:

- Hebrew;
- Aramaic;
- Judeo-Arabic;
- Ladino;
- Yiddish;
- Persian and other Jewish languages.

The tradition of argument is itself geographically plural.

We should not celebrate rabbinic plurality and then populate the entire argument with Eastern European men in black coats.

→ See: [🥚 Ashkenormativity And Allied Problems](./🥚_ashkenormativity_and_allied_problems.md)

## 🖤 One Can Never Speak For All Means All

That includes Black Jews.

Mizrahi Jews.

Sephardi Jews.

Ashkenazi Jews.

Mixed-background Jews.

Religious Jews.

Secular Jews.

Converts.

Jews born into Jewish families.

Jews whose family histories cross the categories contemporary politics wants kept separate.

Nobody becomes:

> **more available to be spoken over**

because their Jewish experience complicates the neat version of the communal narrative.

If the claim is:

> **one can never speak for all**

then the Jews most easily omitted from the imagined **all** are exactly the people the framework has to keep looking for.

---

## 🕸️ Institutions Need To Learn The Difference Between Representation And Totality

A Jewish organisation can legitimately say:

> **We represent our members.**

It can say:

> **Our organisation's position is X.**

It can say:

> **Polling suggests X is the majority view in this population.**

Those are intelligible claims.

The problem comes when:

> **we represent many Jews**

quietly becomes:

> **we represent Jewishness.**

Then:

**institutional access**  
becomes  
**communal authority**

becomes  
**epistemic authority**

becomes  
**the Jewish position.**

That is precisely where outsiders need enough Jewish literacy to ask:

> **Who is not in this room?**

---

## ⚖️ Rabbinic Plurality Does Not Remove Expertise

There is another overcorrection to avoid.

If someone has spent thirty years studying:

- Talmud;
- halakhah;
- Jewish philosophy;
- history;
- Hebrew;
- Aramaic;

their expertise matters.

Plurality does not mean:

> **my vibes are equivalent to your scholarship.**

Likewise, a rabbi may possess forms of authority within a community that another person does not.

The correct distinction is:

> **expertise can establish expertise without establishing total ownership of Jewishness.**

Authority can be real.

Its scope still has to be named.

---

## 🧿 “Judaism Says” Requires A Citation

This would be a useful cultural improvement.

Whenever somebody announces:

> **Judaism says...**

a small imaginary librarian should appear and ask:

> **Where?**

Which text?

Which period?

Which tradition?

Which authority?

Which interpretation?

Is this:

- Torah?
- Mishnah?
- Babylonian Talmud?
- Jerusalem Talmud?
- Midrash?
- medieval philosophy?
- kabbalah?
- responsa?
- modern denominational interpretation?
- contemporary political theology?
- communal custom?

And:

> **does anybody disagree?**

The sentence may still survive.

It will usually become better.

---

## 🗯️ “But All Jews Know...”

No.

Almost certainly not.

There are things widely known in particular communities.

There are strong cultural norms.

There are enormously common understandings.

But:

> **all Jews know**

is doing heroic amounts of work for a population distributed across continents, languages, political systems, religious traditions, and thousands of years.

Use:

> **many Jews understand...**

or:

> **in this tradition...**

or:

> **a common interpretation is...**

or:

> **this institution teaches...**

Precision is available.

We should use it.

---

## 🌿 Palestinians Should Not Be Forced To Solve Jewish Interpretive Plurality

There is another boundary.

The existence of Jewish disagreement does not mean Palestinians have to wait until Jews achieve theological consensus before Palestinian political rights can be recognised.

Likewise, Palestinians do not have to determine:

> **which Jewish interpretation is authentically Jewish.**

That is not their job.

Jewish argument belongs to Jews.

Palestinian rights do not depend upon the argument reaching completion.

→ See: [🌿 Palestinians Are Not A Footnote To Jewish Self-Concept](./🌿_palestinians_are_not_a_footnote_to_jewish_self_concept.md)

## 🏠 Nor Can Rabbinic Argument Turn Material Questions Into Pure Interpretation

A Jewish text can support multiple readings of:

- land;
- return;
- sovereignty;
- obligation.

That does not convert:

- houses;
- citizenship;
- borders;
- military control;
- property;
- displacement

into purely rabbinic questions.

The moment interpretation becomes a territorial political programme, people outside the interpretive tradition are materially affected.

Their rights do not depend upon which rabbi wins.

→ See: [🏠 When A Philosophical Homeland Is Someone Else's Home](./🏠_when_a_philosophical_homeland_is_someone_elses_home.md)

---

## 🌉 Jerusalem Demonstrates The Limit

Jews have argued about Jerusalem for centuries.

Jewish traditions can preserve multiple understandings of:

- return;
- holiness;
- sovereignty;
- messianism.

Actual Jerusalem still contains actual residents.

The rabbinic argument can shape what Jerusalem means to Jews.

It cannot, by itself, determine the political rights of everyone who lives in the city.

→ See: [🌉 Philosophical Jerusalem And Actual Jerusalem](./🌉_philosophical_jerusalem_and_actual_jerusalem.md)

## 🫱 A Note To Non-Jewish Readers

Jewish plurality does not mean:

> **find whichever rabbi agrees with you.**

Please do not turn two thousand years of rabbinic disagreement into a quotation-mining service.

A Jewish source can illuminate a Jewish argument.

It does not automatically prove your preferred contemporary political conclusion.

Likewise:

> **Rabbi X said this**

does not mean:

> **Judaism says this.**

Ask about:

- context;
- tradition;
- reception;
- disagreement;
- authority;
- interpretation.

The fact that Jews preserve arguments does not give outsiders permission to select one historical Jew as the authentic spokesperson for everybody else.

→ See: [🫱 How Not To Flatten Jewish Plurality](./🫱_how_not_to_flatten_jewish_plurality.md)

## 🪬 The Duty To Teach The Other Position

Perhaps the most useful rule this node can borrow from the Hillel/Shammai tradition is this:

> **If you want authority inside an argument, learn to represent the argument you oppose.**

For this cluster, that means:

A Zionist Jew should be capable of explaining why another Jew rejects Zionism without immediately translating the position into hatred of Jews.

An anti-Zionist Jew should be capable of explaining why another Jew experiences Israel as protection without immediately translating the attachment into bloodlust or stupidity.

A religious Jew should be capable of distinguishing Jewish anti-Zionisms with religious genealogies from secular anti-nationalism.

A secular Jew should not assume religious language is merely camouflage for politics.

Nobody has to agree afterwards.

That is the point.

## 🧠 Disagreement Can Reveal The Shape Of The Question

Sometimes persistent disagreement tells us that the original question is poorly specified.

Ask:

> **Do Jews support Israel?**

Which Israel?

Ask:

> **Is Zionism Jewish?**

Which Zionism?

What does *Jewish* mean in the sentence?

Historical origin?

Majority identification?

Theological legitimacy?

Political obligation?

Ask:

> **Does Judaism require Jewish sovereignty?**

According to which Jewish political theology?

Which conception of sovereignty?

At what historical moment?

Disagreement can be evidence that the noun needs unpacking.

That is precisely what **Which Yisrael?** is trying to do.

## 🕯️ One Can Never Speak For All

The title of this node is deliberately categorical.

Obviously people can sometimes speak **about** all Jews.

One can say:

> **all Jews are human beings.**

One can make carefully bounded demographic claims.

The point is not grammatical.

It is political and epistemic.

No individual Jew gets uncomplicated authority to announce:

> **I speak as the Jewish people.**

No political faction does.

No synagogue does.

No rabbi does.

No advocacy organisation does.

No government does.

And **Medinat Yisrael does not acquire that authority merely by being a Jewish state**.

It can speak for:

> **Medinat Yisrael.**

Its government can speak through the authorities granted to it by its political system.

Israeli citizens can debate whether that government represents them.

But:

**state of Israel**

is not synonymous with:

**Am Yisrael.**

That distinction is non-negotiable if we are serious about both Jewish plurality and antisemitism.

---

## 🇮🇱 Medinat Yisrael Cannot Speak For Every Jew

This matters especially because outsiders frequently collapse:

**Israel**  
→ **Israeli government**  
→ **Israeli Jews**  
→ **Jews**

and then reverse the chain.

The state does something.

A diaspora Jew is expected to answer.

A minister speaks.

The statement becomes:

> **the Jews think...**

A Jewish organisation supports the state.

The organisation becomes:

> **the Jewish community.**

No.

Medinat Yisrael is enormously important to many Jews.

It is home to a very large Jewish population.

It has profound significance across Jewish life.

It remains:

> **Medinat Yisrael.**

It does not become the mouth of every Jew on Earth.

---

## 🔮 Nor Can The Anti-Zionist Jew Speak For Everyone

The reciprocal warning matters.

A Jewish anti-Zionist cannot say:

> **As a Jew, I can tell you Zionism has nothing to do with Judaism.**

Not without considerable qualification.

They can say:

> **Zionism is not constitutive of my Jewishness.**

They can produce Jewish intellectual, religious, socialist, diasporist, or other traditions supporting their argument.

They can criticise Zionism from inside Jewish life.

They cannot delete the millions of Jews for whom Zionism or Medinat Yisrael genuinely occupies a significant Jewish meaning.

Plurality has to work in both directions.

Otherwise:

> **one can never speak for all**

has simply become:

> **my minority position gets to speak for all instead.**

---

## ⚖️ Nor Can Majority Opinion Speak For The Minority's Identity

Likewise:

> **Most Jews identify with Israel**

cannot become:

> **therefore Jews who do not are deficient Jews.**

A demographic distribution does not possess theological authority.

Nor does it determine self-concept.

We can describe majorities honestly without turning them into authenticity rules.

That is one of the central disciplines of this cluster.

---

## 🌱 The Rabbinic Future Is Not Consensus

There is a tendency to imagine a healthy community as one that eventually reaches:

> **agreement.**

Rabbinic literature offers another possibility.

A healthy intellectual tradition may be one capable of:

- disagreeing;
- deciding when necessary;
- preserving dissent;
- transmitting losing arguments;
- revisiting questions;
- refusing to confuse authority with totality.

That is a much more robust model for Jewish futurity.

The goal is not:

> **one Jewish voice.**

The goal is:

> **an argument capable of remaining a people.**

---

## 🧿 The Point

Jewish tradition does not provide a cheap argument that:

> **everybody is right.**

It provides something more interesting.

A tradition can:

**argue fiercely**  
and  
**make decisions**  
and  
**preserve the disagreement.**

It can remember the minority.

It can praise the winning school partly for teaching the losing school's position.

It can distinguish principled disagreement from destructive faction.

It can recognise a multitude of Israel whose minds differ from one another.

That should make us extremely cautious whenever somebody claims:

> **The Jews think...**

or:

> **Judaism says...**

or:

> **Israel represents the Jewish people, therefore...**

or:

> **Real Jews believe...**

Sometimes there is a strong majority.

Say so.

Sometimes halakhah is clear within a particular tradition.

Say so.

Sometimes an institution represents a large constituency.

Say so.

But say **that**.

Do not inflate:

> **many**

into:

> **all.**

Do not inflate:

> **authority**

into:

> **totality.**

Do not inflate:

> **decision**

into:

> **the disappearance of disagreement.**

And if somebody insists that their political position is unquestionably:

> **the Jewish position**

there is a very old Jewish response available:

> **Which rabbi says that?**

Followed, almost inevitably, by:

> **And who disagrees with them?**

Welcome back to the argument.
