# 🥚 Ashkenormativity And Allied Problems  
**First created:** 2026-08-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-18  
*Why Jewish political analysis breaks when Ashkenazi experience is treated as the default version of Jewish history, identity, Zionism, diaspora, or antisemitism.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

It is very easy to write about Jewish political identity and accidentally write about **Ashkenazi political identity**.

The histories are so prominent in English-language Jewish discourse that they can quietly become the baseline:

- European emancipation;
- European antisemitism;
- the Haskalah;
- Yiddish political culture;
- the Bund;
- European socialism;
- European Zionism;
- Eastern European Orthodoxy;
- German Jewish philosophy;
- the Shoah;
- postwar European and American Jewish communal life.

All of that matters enormously.

It is also not the whole Jewish world.

If a discussion of:

- Zionism;
- anti-Zionism;
- diaspora;
- Eretz Yisrael;
- Medinat Yisrael;
- Jewish safety;
- Jewish political identity;
- antisemitism;
- colonialism;
- migration;
- religion;
- language;
- Jewish racialisation

quietly assumes an Ashkenazi historical pathway, then whole sections of the Jewish population disappear.

That is not a small methodological problem.

It changes the argument.

---

## 🥚 The Egg Problem

Ashkenormativity is not simply:

> **there are too many Ashkenazi examples.**

It is what happens when Ashkenazi experience becomes the **unmarked category**.

Ashkenazi history becomes:

> **Jewish history.**

Ashkenazi food becomes:

> **Jewish food.**

Ashkenazi religious practice becomes:

> **Jewish practice.**

Ashkenazi experiences of Europe become:

> **the Jewish relationship with majority society.**

Ashkenazi pathways into Zionism become:

> **why Jews became Zionists.**

Ashkenazi pathways out of Zionism become:

> **why Jews became anti-Zionists.**

And a frequently white or white-passing Ashkenazi Jewish subject quietly becomes:

> **what a Jew looks like.**

Everyone else then appears as:

> **variation.**

That is the problem.

The egg was Ashkenazi before anybody noticed there was an egg.

- migration;
- antisemitism;
- Arab nationalism;
- Israeli state formation

were therefore not simply delayed copies of an Eastern European story.

## 🖤 Black Jews Exist

This should not need saying.

It does.

**Jewishness is not synonymous with whiteness.**

Black Jews exist.

They exist across multiple communities, histories, geographies, denominations, political traditions, and routes into Jewish life.

That includes, among others:

- Ethiopian Jews;
- Black American Jews;
- African Jewish communities;
- Afro-Sephardi and Afro-Mizrahi Jews;
- Black converts;
- mixed-background Jews;
- Jews whose racialisation differs depending upon country and context.

These are not edge cases outside an otherwise white Jewish population.

They are Jews.

A framework that discusses:

- Jewish identity;
- antisemitism;
- Zionism;
- diaspora;
- belonging;
- racialisation;
- Jewish safety;
- communal representation

while implicitly imagining a white Jewish subject will produce bad analysis.

This matters especially in discussions that alternate carelessly between:

> **Jew**

and:

> **white Jew**

without noticing the substitution.

They are not interchangeable categories.

---

## 🧠 Jewishness And Racialisation Are Not The Same Thing

Jewish identity does not map neatly onto modern racial categories.

Different Jewish populations are racialised differently in different places.

A Jew may be read as:

- white;
- Black;
- Arab;
- Middle Eastern;
- North African;
- mixed;
- racially ambiguous;
- or differently across different environments.

Those categories themselves are historically and geographically unstable.

Antisemitism also does not require Jews to be understood as non-white in every social context.

A person can experience forms of white privilege in one domain while encountering antisemitic racialisation in another.

Likewise, a Black Jew can encounter:

- anti-Black racism;
- antisemitism;
- both simultaneously;
- racism within Jewish institutions;
- exclusion from assumptions about what Jews look like;
- demands to explain or prove Jewishness;
- political assumptions based upon race;
- and the repeated experience of being treated as unexpected.

Those intersections cannot be reconstructed from an Ashkenazi-white baseline.

---

## 🪞 “You Don't Look Jewish” Is Part Of The Problem

Ashkenormativity often carries an implicit visual template.

The imagined Jew becomes:

- white or white-passing;
- European;
- Ashkenazi;
- culturally legible through familiar European Jewish markers.

Then Black Jews encounter:

> **Are you Jewish?**

or:

> **How are you Jewish?**

or:

> **Did you convert?**

before anybody asks the same questions of the white Jew standing next to them.

That is not merely awkwardness.

It reveals the hidden category:

> **Jew = white Ashkenazi unless specified otherwise.**

A cluster about Jewish plurality cannot reproduce that category.

And merely adding:

> **Ethiopian Jews**

to a list somewhere does not solve the problem.

The issue is the **default model of the Jew** being carried through the analysis.

---

## 🕸️ Black Jewish Experience Also Changes The Political Analysis

Black Jewish experience complicates simple discussions of:

- whiteness;
- diaspora;
- anti-Black racism;
- antisemitism;
- Zionism;
- policing;
- nationalism;
- solidarity;
- Jewish institutional power.

A Black Jew may encounter Jewish institutions simultaneously as:

> **community**

and:

> **a place where anti-Black racism can occur.**

They may encounter wider society simultaneously through:

> **anti-Black racialisation**

and:

> **antisemitism.**

They may enter political movements where one part of their experience is recognised while another is flattened.

They may also encounter political models in which:

> **Jew**

has already been coded as:

> **white**

and therefore find their own existence inconvenient to the framework being offered.

Those experiences are not optional detail.

They affect how Jewish safety, solidarity, representation, racism, antisemitism, and political coalition actually work.

---

## 🧿 Do Not Turn Black Jews Into Diversity Evidence

There is another trap.

Once somebody remembers Black Jews exist, they can become:

> **proof that Jews are diverse.**

That is not enough.

Black Jews are not illustrative material for somebody else's argument about Jewish inclusivity.

They are participants in Jewish life whose own histories, politics, communal relationships, disagreements, traditions, and interpretations need to be represented on their own terms.

The correct move is not:

> **add one Black Jewish example.**

It is:

> **stop building the model as though Black Jews were absent until the diversity paragraph.**

That distinction applies across this cluster.

Black Jewish experience belongs inside analysis of:

- Jewish plurality;
- Jewish institutions;
- diaspora;
- Zionism;
- anti-Zionism;
- Israel;
- antisemitism;
- Jewish safety;
- racial politics;
- coalition;
- belonging.

Not merely inside the node about diversity.

---

## 🕸️ “Diaspora” Does Not Mean One Historical Condition

Even the word:

> **diaspora**

can become Ashkenormative.

If diaspora is imagined principally through:

**Eastern Europe**  
→ **pogroms**  
→ **emancipation failure**  
→ **Shoah**  
→ **refuge / Israel**

then Jewish life elsewhere gets forced into the same narrative arc.

But Jewish communities in:

- Baghdad;
- Fez;
- Sana'a;
- Isfahan;
- Alexandria;
- Salonika;
- Mumbai;
- Addis Ababa

did not inhabit identical political worlds.

Their relationships with:

- neighbours;
- rulers;
- religion;
- language;
- trade;
- land;
- empire;
- colonial intervention;
- migration

differed.

This does not mean those histories were peaceful.

It means they were **different histories**.

---

## 🧭 Zionism Arrived Differently

Zionism also did not arrive everywhere through the same route.

For some European Jews, modern Zionism emerged within arguments about:

- nationalism;
- emancipation;
- antisemitism;
- socialism;
- Jewish cultural survival.

For Jews elsewhere, Zionism could arrive through:

- schools;
- emissaries;
- Hebrew revival;
- religious networks;
- colonial institutions;
- migration channels;
- changing regional politics;
- the establishment of Medinat Yisrael itself.

Some communities encountered Zionism before major rupture.

Others encountered it during or after profound political destabilisation.

Others were incorporated into Israeli society under conditions shaped by:

- class;
- race;
- language;
- institutional hierarchy;
- development policy;
- settlement patterns;
- state-building.

There is no single Jewish route into Zionism.

---

## 🏛️ Medinat Yisrael Also Reordered Jewish Difference

The establishment of Medinat Yisrael did not simply gather:

> **Jews**

into one neutral national container.

The state inherited and produced internal hierarchies.

Ashkenazi elites played disproportionately powerful roles in many early Israeli institutions.

Mizrahi and Sephardi Jews arriving from the Middle East and North Africa frequently encountered:

- social marginalisation;
- economic inequality;
- geographic peripheralisation;
- cultural devaluation;
- pressure around language and identity;
- political paternalism.

Black Jewish experience also requires specific attention rather than automatic incorporation into a generic:

> **non-Ashkenazi**

category.

The histories and experiences of Ethiopian Jews, for example, cannot simply be treated as another variation on Mizrahi migration.

Questions of:

- race;
- recognition;
- religious legitimacy;
- migration;
- policing;
- institutional discrimination;
- integration;
- class

have their own histories.

Those histories matter.

A discussion of:

> **Jewish sovereignty**

that treats Jews as one internally undifferentiated population misses how sovereignty and power were distributed within Jewish society too.

## 🥚 Ashkenormativity Can Hide Inside Anti-Zionism As Well

This is important.

Ashkenormativity is not only a Zionist problem.

Anti-Zionist Jewish politics can reproduce it too.

A familiar anti-Zionist genealogy may run:

**European nationalism**  
↓  
**Herzl**  
↓  
**Bundism**  
↓  
**European socialism**  
↓  
**Palestinian dispossession**  
↓  
**diasporism**

That is still overwhelmingly an Ashkenazi intellectual map.

If Mizrahi, Sephardi, Maghrebi, Black, Arab-Jewish, Persian, Yemenite, Ethiopian, Indian and other histories appear only at the end as:

> **additional complexity**

then the framework remains Ashkenormative.

Different politics.

Same baseline.

---

## 🪞 The “Arab Jew” Question Complicates Everything

Some Jewish histories resist the binary:

> **Jew**

versus

> **Arab**

itself.

There have been Jews who lived within Arabic-speaking societies, participated in Arab culture, wrote and spoke Arabic, and understood themselves through categories that do not map neatly onto contemporary political separations.

The term **Arab Jew** is itself contested.

Some Jews embrace it.

Some reject it.

Some prefer Mizrahi, national, regional, religious, linguistic, or other forms of identification.

That disagreement should be preserved.

The important point is:

> **Jewish and Arab histories have not always existed as sealed civilisational boxes.**

Any framework that requires them to have been permanently separate will distort the past.

---

## 🌿 This Matters For Palestine Too

Ashkenormativity can also affect how Jewish people discuss Palestinians.

If the entire conflict is imagined as:

> **European Jews arrive in an Arab land**

then Jewish histories from the Middle East and North Africa can disappear.

But the opposite move is also dangerous.

The existence of long-standing Jewish communities across the Middle East cannot be used to erase:

- Palestinian dispossession;
- settler-colonial structures;
- occupation;
- unequal political power.

Multiple histories can coexist.

Jewish histories in Arab and Muslim-majority societies matter.

Palestinian history matters.

Neither cancels the other.

The concept of **Atzmi Yisrael** also needs to be tested beyond an Ashkenazi frame.

For one family, Medinat Yisrael may be incorporated through:

**Shoah**  
↓  
**refuge**  
↓  
**1967**  
↓  
**Jewish military agency**

For another:

**departure from Iraq / Morocco / Yemen / Egypt**  
↓  
**arrival in Israel**  
↓  
**state absorption**  
↓  
**new forms of Jewish collective identity**

For another:

**Ethiopia**  
↓  
**long-standing Jewish identity and religious tradition**  
↓  
**migration to Israel**  
↓  
**encounter with Israeli institutions, racialisation, and contested belonging**

For another, the state may be associated with both:

**refuge**

and:

**racial or class marginalisation inside Israel.**

For another, Medinat Yisrael may be primarily:

**family.**

For another:

**religious fulfilment.**

For another:

**political alienation.**

Atzmi Yisrael cannot be theorised adequately from one Jewish developmental pathway.

## 🔮 Yisrael She-be'atzmi Must Also Be Plural

The same warning applies to **Yisrael she-be'atzmi**.

If the interior Yisrael is built only from:

- Maimonides;
- European Jewish philosophy;
- Hasidism;
- Bundism;
- Ashkenazi liturgy;
- Eastern European memory;

then we have produced another Ashkenazi universal.

Jewish interiority has also been shaped by:

- Sephardi philosophy;
- Andalusi intellectual traditions;
- Mizrahi religious traditions;
- North African rabbinic cultures;
- Iraqi Jewish scholarship;
- Persian Jewish traditions;
- Yemenite traditions;
- Ethiopian Jewish traditions;
- Indian Jewish communities;
- Black Jewish intellectual and religious traditions;
- different mystical and liturgical traditions.

Yisrael she-be'atzmi should become more plural as the cluster develops.

Not less.

## 📚 Maimonides Is Actually Useful Here

Maimonides is an especially good reminder of the problem.

He is frequently incorporated into a modern Jewish intellectual canon as though:

> **major Jewish thinker**

quietly means:

> **European Jewish thinker.**

It does not.

Maimonides was born in Córdoba, lived across the Mediterranean and North Africa, and eventually became one of the most important Jewish intellectual figures in Cairo.

His world was deeply entangled with:

- Arabic language;
- Islamic philosophy;
- Mediterranean intellectual networks;
- Sephardi history.

Even one of the most canonical Jewish philosophers already breaks the imagined equation:

**Jewish thought = European Jewish thought.**

The canon itself is trying to tell us something.

---

## 🕸️ Language Matters

Ashkenormativity is also linguistic.

English-speaking Jewish discourse often treats:

- Hebrew;
- Yiddish;

as the principal historical Jewish language pair.

But Jewish intellectual and communal history also includes:

- Judeo-Arabic;
- Ladino;
- Judeo-Persian;
- Judeo-Tat;
- Judeo-Berber varieties;
- Jewish Malayalam;
- Ge'ez and related Ethiopian Jewish liturgical traditions;
- and other Jewish linguistic worlds.

Language carries categories.

When those languages disappear from analysis, the concepts carried through them can disappear too.

Then English tries to do all the work.

This entire cluster exists partly because English is already struggling.

---

## 🥚 Who Gets To Be “Traditional”?

Another Ashkenormative distortion appears around religion.

A particular Eastern European model of:

> **Orthodox**

can quietly become:

> **traditional Judaism.**

Then Jewish traditions developed elsewhere are measured against it.

Likewise:

> **secular Jew**

can be imagined through European Jewish secularisation.

But boundaries between:

- religious;
- communal;
- cultural;
- linguistic;
- familial;
- political

Jewish identity do not fall in the same places everywhere.

A framework that begins with European categories can misread other Jewish lives before analysis even starts.

---

## 🗳️ Political Representation Reproduces The Problem

Ashkenormativity is not only intellectual.

It can become institutional.

Which Jewish organisations receive:

- government meetings;
- media invitations;
- consultations;
- antisemitism-policy roles;
- university advisory positions;
- funding;
- public legitimacy?

Which Jewish voices are treated as:

> **community leaders?**

Which Jewish communities have enough institutional infrastructure to be visible to the state?

And within those institutions:

- who holds leadership?
- who is visible?
- whose Jewishness is immediately recognised?
- whose experiences of racism are understood?
- whose experiences require explanation before they are believed?

Representation can reproduce historical power inside Jewish communities.

Then:

**visible Jewish institution**

becomes:

**Jewish community**

and:

**Jewish community**

becomes:

**Jews.**

That is exactly the kind of flattening this cluster is trying to resist.

## 🧿 “Most Jews Think...” Needs A Location

Whenever someone says:

> **Most Jews think X**

ask:

> **Which Jews?**

British Jews?

American Jews?

Israeli Jews?

French Jews?

Haredi Jews?

Secular Jews?

Black Jews?

Mizrahi Jews?

Ashkenazi Jews?

Sephardi Jews?

People affiliated with communal institutions?

People answering a particular poll?

Which generation?

Which question?

Which wording?

Majority opinion can be real and important.

But demographic claims require geography, sampling, time, and definition.

Otherwise:

> **most Jews**

quietly means:

> **the Jews most visible to me.**

---

## 🌉 The Bridge Has More Than Two Sides

The metaphor of:

> **meeting on the bridge**

can also become too simple.

There is no single:

**Zionist Jewish side**

and:

**anti-Zionist Jewish side.**

There are multiple Jewish positions approaching from different histories.

A Mizrahi Jew whose family left Iraq may understand:

- Arab nationalism;
- Zionism;
- Israel;
- diaspora;
- Jewish vulnerability

through a different historical archive from an Ashkenazi Jew whose grandparents fled Poland.

A Black Jewish reader may bring another set of experiences around:

- race;
- Jewish belonging;
- antisemitism;
- anti-Blackness;
- communal recognition;
- policing;
- solidarity;
- political coalition.

Neither archive is the universal Jewish memory.

Shared language should make those differences more visible.

Not force them into one genealogy.

---

## 🌿 Palestinian Experience Must Not Become The Corrective Footnote Either

There is one more trap.

We can write:

> **Ashkenormative Jewish history is incomplete**

and then tack on:

> **also Palestinians**

at the end.

No.

Palestinian experience is not merely one of several missing diversity perspectives.

Palestinians are a distinct people whose land, political rights, homes, displacement, and relationship with Israeli state power are central to the material history being discussed.

The corrective to Ashkenormativity cannot become:

> **add more Jewish diversity and then remember Palestinians too.**

These are different analytical obligations.

→ See: [🌿 Palestinians Are Not A Footnote To Jewish Self-Concept](./🌿_palestinians_are_not_a_footnote_to_jewish_self_concept.md)

## 🧭 What This Cluster Needs To Do Better

This cluster should therefore continually ask:

> **Which Jewish history is doing the explanatory work here?**

If the answer is repeatedly:

- Germany;
- Poland;
- Russia;
- Eastern Europe;
- Britain;
- the United States;

then something is missing.

Future revisions should deliberately incorporate:

- Sephardi scholarship;
- Mizrahi scholarship;
- Maghrebi Jewish histories;
- Middle Eastern Jewish histories;
- Black Jewish scholarship and histories;
- Ethiopian Jewish histories;
- Indian Jewish histories;
- Jewish histories under Ottoman rule;
- Jewish histories under European colonial regimes;
- Arab-Jewish intellectual traditions;
- non-European approaches to Zionism and anti-Zionism;
- internal Israeli histories of race and class.

Not as:

> **special topics.**

As Jewish history.

## 🪞 A Useful Self-Audit

For every node in this cluster, ask:

1. **Whose Jewish history is being treated as normal?**
2. **Which languages are carrying the concepts?**
3. **Which communities are absent?**
4. **Are non-Ashkenazi Jews appearing as actors or only as examples?**
5. **Are Black Jews present in the analytical model, or only remembered when race is explicitly being discussed?**
6. **Are we implicitly using white Jewish experience as the baseline for antisemitism or Jewish safety?**
7. **Are we assuming European nationalism is the only route into Jewish modernity?**
8. **Are we treating the Shoah as the sole organising catastrophe of Jewish political identity?**
9. **Are histories of Jewish migration from Arab and Muslim-majority countries being represented accurately and independently?**
10. **Are internal Israeli racial and class hierarchies visible?**
11. **Are Palestinian histories being treated as material history rather than Jewish context?**
12. **Would this argument still make sense if the reader's grandparents came from Baghdad rather than Warsaw?**

If the answer to number twelve is:

> **not really**

the node probably needs work.

## 🧿 This Is Not A Competition In Suffering

Decentring Ashkenormativity does not require:

> **Ashkenazi history mattered less.**

The Shoah does not become less catastrophic because other Jewish communities have different histories.

European antisemitism does not become less important.

Eastern European Jewish political thought does not become less intellectually rich.

Likewise, recognising Black Jewish experience does not require constructing another hierarchy of authenticity or suffering.

The task is not to replace:

**Ashkenazi universalism**

with:

**another universalism.**

It is to stop requiring one Jewish historical route, racialised experience, or cultural template to stand in for everyone.

Plurality should add dimensions.

Not build a new hierarchy.

---

## 🕸️ Nor Should Mizrahi, Sephardi Or Black Jewish History Be Instrumentalised

Another warning.

Mizrahi and Sephardi Jews are frequently mobilised in political arguments as evidence.

For example:

> **Jews were expelled from Arab countries, therefore Zionism is justified.**

Or:

> **Mizrahi Jews prove Israel is not settler colonial.**

Or:

> **Arab Jews prove Jews and Arabs lived together perfectly before Zionism.**

Black Jews can be instrumentalised in similar ways:

> **Black Jews exist, therefore Jews cannot participate in whiteness.**

Or:

> **Black Jews exist, therefore Israel cannot reproduce racism.**

Or:

> **Black Jews exist, therefore this Jewish institution is racially inclusive.**

None of those conclusions follows merely from the existence of the people being invoked.

People are not argumentative props.

Jewish migration and displacement from Arab and Muslim-majority countries involved different histories in different places.

Black Jewish histories likewise contain different communities, migrations, racial formations, religious traditions, political positions, and experiences of Jewish institutions.

They should be investigated as histories.

Not transformed into political tokens.

---

## 🧠 Different Trauma Does Not Produce One Politics

Likewise:

> **this community experienced persecution**

does not logically yield one political conclusion.

Trauma can contribute to:

- nationalism;
- anti-nationalism;
- religious revival;
- secularisation;
- withdrawal;
- solidarity;
- suspicion;
- state-building;
- diasporism.

There is no neurological rule:

**persecution → Zionism.**

Nor:

**persecution → anti-Zionism.**

And there is no rule that Jews subjected to different forms of racialisation will interpret Jewish safety, state power, policing, nationalism, or solidarity identically.

History shapes political possibility.

It does not mechanically determine ideology.

## 🌱 The Payoff

Decentring Ashkenormativity does more than make the cluster:

> **more inclusive.**

It improves the analysis.

Suddenly:

**diaspora**

is not one thing.

**Arab/Jew**

is not a clean binary.

**Jew/Black**

is not a contradiction.

**Jew/white**

is not a universal equivalence.

**Jewish modernity**

has multiple routes.

**Zionism**

arrives differently in different communities.

**Medinat Yisrael**

occupies different locations in different family histories.

**antisemitism**

intersects with different systems of racialisation.

**Jewish safety**

does not necessarily mean the same thing to every Jew.

**Yisrael**

carries different linguistic and philosophical inheritances.

The map becomes more complicated.

It also becomes more accurate.

---

## 🧭 The Point

It is wild to leave out whole sections of the Jewish population.

Not merely rude.

Not merely politically unfortunate.

**Analytically wild.**

You cannot plausibly build a theory of:

> **Jewish relationships with Yisrael**

while quietly using:

> **European Ashkenazi Jewish relationships with Yisrael**

as the underlying dataset.

And you cannot claim to have corrected that problem merely by mentioning Mizrahi, Sephardi, Ethiopian, Black, or other Jews once and then returning to an implicitly white Ashkenazi Jewish subject for the rest of the analysis.

The result will fail precisely where this cluster is trying to become useful.

It will claim plurality while reproducing a hidden default.

So this node acts partly as warning and partly as instruction:

> **Every time the cluster thinks it has explained “the Jewish experience,” check whether it has actually explained one very historically powerful Jewish experience.**

And:

> **Every time the cluster pictures “a Jew,” check who appeared in the picture without being named.**

Ashkenazi history belongs here.

It matters enormously.

It simply does not get to be everyone.

Black Jews exist.

Mizrahi Jews exist.

Sephardi Jews exist.

Ethiopian Jews exist.

Jews whose histories do not fit our neat categories exist.

If the model cannot hold them without treating them as exceptions, **the model is the thing that needs fixing.**

*🥚 Ashkenormativity And Allied Problems* is a living node of the **Polaris Protocol**.
