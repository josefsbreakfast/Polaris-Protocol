# 🔖 Why Zionism Has Ten Definitions  
**First created:** 2026-08-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-18  
*Why one word came to describe multiple Jewish political projects, historical experiences, relationships with Yisrael, and contemporary claims — and why asking whether someone is a Zionist often tells us considerably less than we think.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Ask ten Jewish people what **Zionism** means and there is a reasonable chance you will receive considerably more than one definition.

This can look ridiculous from the outside.

Sometimes it is ridiculous from the inside too.

One person says:

> **Zionism means believing Israel has a right to exist.**

Another:

> **Zionism means Jewish self-determination.**

Another:

> **Zionism means believing Jews should have somewhere safe to go.**

Another:

> **Zionism means Jewish return to Eretz Yisrael.**

Another:

> **Zionism is the national liberation movement of the Jewish people.**

Another:

> **Zionism was a nineteenth-century nationalist movement.**

Another:

> **Zionism means support for Medinat Yisrael.**

Another:

> **Zionism means the political project through which a Jewish state was established in Palestine.**

Another:

> **Zionism means the maintenance of Jewish sovereignty in a Jewish-majority state.**

Another:

> **Zionism means the settler-colonial political project responsible for Palestinian dispossession.**

Then everybody starts arguing about which definition is the *real* definition.

This node begins somewhere else.

> **What if the instability of the word is itself historically meaningful?**

There have been multiple Zionisms.

Those Zionisms emerged from multiple Jewish answers to older questions about **Yisrael**.

They changed over time.

The establishment of Medinat Yisrael changed what the political project was capable of meaning.

Different Jewish communities inherited different parts of that history.

And contemporary political arguments routinely compress all of those layers back into one binary question:

> **Are you a Zionist?**

No wonder this is going badly.

---

## 🕯️ First: Zionism Is A Modern Political Movement

We need one anchor before allowing the word to multiply indefinitely.

Modern political Zionism belongs to **modernity**.

It developed particularly among European Jews in the nineteenth century amid nationalism, emancipation, antisemitism, imperial politics, secularisation, Jewish migration, changing forms of Jewish collective identity, and arguments about whether Jews constituted a religion, nation, people, culture, or some combination of them.

Earlier Jewish attachment to **Eretz Yisrael**, Jerusalem, Zion, return, or redemption is not automatically Zionism.

Jewish prayer directed towards Jerusalem is not nineteenth-century nationalism.

Messianic expectation is not automatically a programme for a nation-state.

Jewish communities living in Palestine before modern Zionism were not therefore secretly participating in a political movement that had not yet developed.

And medieval Jewish thinkers cannot simply be assigned contemporary positions on Zionism.

That distinction matters because modern Zionism drew enormous symbolic power from much older Jewish traditions.

But:

> **Zion is older than Zionism.**

> **Eretz Yisrael is older than Zionism.**

> **Jewish longing for Jerusalem is older than Zionism.**

> **Jewish presence in the land is older than Zionism.**

Modern Zionism did not invent those things.

It **politicised, reorganised, secularised, nationalised, interpreted, and mobilised parts of an older Jewish inheritance within the conditions of modern nationalism**.

That is a more interesting claim than either:

> *Zionism is simply two thousand years of Jewish longing.*

or:

> *Zionism invented Jewish attachment to Palestine in the nineteenth century.*

Neither is adequate.

---

## 🏛️ The Argument Was Already In Progress

Zionism did not arrive in a Jewish world waiting patiently for someone to explain what Yisrael meant.

Jewish communities had already spent centuries arguing about:

- exile;
- return;
- redemption;
- Eretz Yisrael;
- Jerusalem;
- Jewish collective obligation;
- political authority;
- relations with surrounding societies;
- communal autonomy;
- Jewish law;
- diaspora;
- peoplehood;
- sovereignty;
- and what forms Jewish continuity could take without a Jewish state.

Modernity added new pressures.

Could Jews become equal citizens of European states?

Were Jews principally a religious community?

Were Jews a nation?

Could Jewish culture survive assimilation?

Could socialism solve antisemitism?

Was antisemitism a temporary political pathology or a durable feature of Jewish minority existence?

Was diaspora something to defend, reform, transcend, or escape?

Did a modern people require territorial sovereignty?

Different Jews answered differently.

Zionism was one family of answers.

## 🔖 Then There Were Several Zionisms

Even once we narrow the term to the modern Zionist movement, the singular immediately becomes difficult.

There were competing Zionist schools, organisations, intellectual traditions, class interests, strategies and imagined futures.

They did not merely disagree about tactics.

Sometimes they disagreed about **what Zionism was for**.

---

## 🗳️ Political Zionism

The political Zionism associated particularly with **Theodor Herzl** placed enormous emphasis upon the political problem of Jewish insecurity and the need for an internationally recognised political solution.

Herzl's *Der Judenstaat* — *The Jewish State* — belongs to the political world of late nineteenth-century European nationalism and antisemitism.

The problem was not merely cultural.

It was political.

Jews constituted a collective problem within European political systems that emancipation had not solved.

Political Zionism therefore sought political power, diplomacy, territory and international recognition.

But even that did not yet answer every later question about:

- borders;
- political institutions;
- religion;
- language;
- economic organisation;
- relations with the Arab population;
- or what Jewish culture inside the political project should become.

“Jewish state” was already an enormous proposition.

Its contents still had to be fought over.

---

## 📚 Cultural Zionism

**Ahad Ha'am** famously criticised forms of Zionism focused too narrowly upon immediate political state-building.

His cultural Zionism placed greater emphasis upon the creation of a Jewish spiritual and cultural centre capable of renewing Jewish civilisation.

This was still Zionism.

But compare the governing questions.

One formulation asks:

> **How do Jews acquire political security?**

Another asks:

> **How does Jewish civilisation regenerate itself?**

Those are related projects.

They are not identical.

And that distinction survives inside contemporary Jewish uses of the word.

Someone describing Hebrew revival, Jewish cultural renewal, attachment to Eretz Yisrael and a Jewish cultural centre as **Zionism** may be invoking a considerably different object from somebody using Zionism to mean support for the contemporary Israeli state.

---

## 🌹 Socialist And Labour Zionisms

Socialist Zionists attempted another synthesis.

Jewish national revival would occur alongside forms of socialist transformation.

Labour, agriculture, collective organisation, workers' institutions and the creation of a new Jewish society became central to important currents of Zionist thought and practice.

But this immediately generated tensions with other socialist Jewish movements.

Bundists could ask:

> **Why should Jewish workers leave the places where they already live rather than fight alongside other workers there?**

Zionist socialists could answer that Jewish workers required a national framework in which Jewish collective life could be transformed.

Both could speak the language of workers.

Both could oppose antisemitism.

Both could understand Jewish collective existence as politically meaningful.

They nevertheless produced radically different answers to:

> **Where should Jewish emancipation happen?**

That disagreement matters enormously for the genealogy of contemporary Jewish left politics.

---

## 🏴 Revisionist Zionism

Revisionist Zionism, associated especially with **Ze'ev Jabotinsky**, developed a different political tradition emphasising Jewish national sovereignty, territorial claims, military capacity and a more explicitly nationalist politics.

Its relationship with Labour Zionism was deeply adversarial.

These were not minor disagreements among people who otherwise meant exactly the same thing by Zionism.

They disagreed over strategy, economics, political authority, military force, territory and the character of the future Jewish polity.

Later Israeli political traditions inherited substantial portions of those conflicts.

So even historically:

> **I am a Zionist**

did not answer:

> **What kind?**

---

## ✡️ Religious Zionisms

Then religion complicates everything further.

Some Orthodox Jews rejected Zionism precisely because a secular nationalist movement appeared to appropriate theological concepts of exile, return and redemption.

Other religious thinkers developed ways of interpreting Zionist settlement and political return through theological frameworks.

Religious Zionism itself subsequently developed multiple traditions and political expressions.

So even:

> **I support Jewish return because of Torah**

and:

> **I oppose Zionist political return because of Torah**

can both emerge from deeply Jewish religious reasoning.

Again:

the disagreement is not adequately described as:

**religious Jew versus secular Jew.**

The objects being interpreted are themselves contested.

---

## 🗺️ Even The Territory Was Not Initially As Obvious As Retrospect Makes It Look

Early Zionist debates also reveal something contemporary definitions can obscure.

If Zionism simply and timelessly meant:

> **Jewish sovereignty specifically in Eretz Yisrael**

then proposals for Jewish settlement elsewhere should have been conceptually impossible.

They were not.

The controversy surrounding the British East Africa proposal — commonly remembered as the **Uganda Scheme** — exposed a real fault line.

Was the immediate priority:

> **a refuge for endangered Jews?**

Or:

> **Jewish national restoration specifically in Eretz Yisrael?**

The fact that Zionists could fight bitterly about that question tells us something important.

Even inside the movement, **safety, sovereignty, refuge, territory and inherited relationship with Eretz Yisrael were related but distinguishable objects**.

They did not automatically point to the same political conclusion.

---

## 🇮🇱 Then The State Exists

1948 changes the semantic problem enormously.

Before Medinat Yisrael exists, Zionism is principally a movement attempting to bring about some future political condition.

After Medinat Yisrael exists, the movement has partly succeeded.

Now what does **Zionism** mean?

Does it mean:

> **the historical movement that produced the state?**

Does it mean:

> **support for the state's continued existence?**

Does it mean:

> **support for Jewish sovereignty?**

Does it mean:

> **support for Israel as a specifically Jewish state?**

Does it mean:

> **support for aliyah?**

Does it mean:

> **attachment to Israel?**

Does it mean:

> **support for the policies of an Israeli government?**

Most Zionists would reject that last equivalence.

But political discourse routinely slides between these categories anyway.

The existence of the state therefore produces an unusual semantic afterlife:

> **What does a successful nationalist movement mean after it has produced a state?**

The answer has never been entirely stable.

---

## 📚 Which Yisrael?

This is where the parent vocabulary becomes useful.

Suppose ten people say:

> **Zionism matters to me.**

What is the object?

For one:

**Eretz Yisrael**  
→ ancestral relationship with land.

For another:

**Am Yisrael**  
→ collective Jewish survival.

For another:

**Medinat Yisrael**  
→ continued existence of the contemporary state.

For another:

**Jewish sovereignty**  
→ the principle that Jews should possess political power somewhere.

For another:

**refuge**  
→ somewhere Jews can flee when other societies become unsafe.

For another:

**Hebrew culture**  
→ revival of language and Jewish cultural production.

For another:

**family**  
→ relatives, memories, migration and personal attachment.

For another:

**religion**  
→ theological understandings of land and return.

For another:

**post-Shoah survival**  
→ a political guarantee against catastrophic Jewish vulnerability.

For another:

**normalisation**  
→ Jews becoming a nation like other nations.

All ten may call the object **Zionism**.

That does not mean their political positions are equivalent.

It means the label alone has poor analytical resolution.

---

## 🧠 Atzmi Yisrael Complicates It Further

There is another layer.

For some people, Medinat Yisrael is not merely a political institution they support.

It has become incorporated into Jewish self-concept.

The parent node calls this **Atzmi Yisrael**.

In that structure:

**Medinat Yisrael**  
↓  
**collective survival / safety / morality / Jewish agency**  
↓  
**incorporated into self-concept**

Now asking:

> **Are you a Zionist?**

can cease to feel like a question about political theory.

It can feel like:

> **Do you believe Jews deserve safety?**

or:

> **Do you accept my people?**

or:

> **Do you accept something about me?**

Another Jewish person may hear exactly the same word and understand:

> **Do you support a political ideology I believe produced Palestinian dispossession?**

Those people are going to have an absolutely terrible conversation unless they first establish what object the word is pointing towards.

## 🪞 This Is Why Jews Can Hear Different Sentences

Consider:

> **I oppose Zionism.**

One Jewish listener may hear:

> **I oppose Jewish collective safety.**

Another:

> **I oppose Jewish self-determination.**

Another:

> **I oppose the political structure of a Jewish-majority ethnonational state.**

Another:

> **I oppose the historical movement through which Palestinians were dispossessed.**

Another:

> **I oppose Israeli government policy.**

That final use may be conceptually sloppy, but people absolutely use political language sloppily.

Likewise:

> **I am a Zionist.**

might mean:

> **I support every action of Israel.**

But much more commonly it does not.

It might mean:

> **I believe Israel should continue to exist.**

Or:

> **I believe Jews require collective self-determination.**

Or:

> **Israel is important to my Jewish identity.**

Or:

> **I believe Jews need somewhere safe to flee.**

Or:

> **I feel attached to Eretz Yisrael.**

Or:

> **I believe in a Jewish national home while opposing the occupation and supporting Palestinian statehood.**

Those propositions have different political implications.

Collapsing them because the speakers used the same noun guarantees confusion.

→ See: [🪞 Why Jews Can Read The Same Sentence Differently](./🪞_why_jews_can_read_the_same_sentence_differently.md)

## ⚖️ This Is One Reason IHRA Arguments Become So Difficult

Definitions of antisemitism enter this already unstable semantic environment.

If **Zionism** means:

> **Jewish collective existence**

then anti-Zionism can sound inherently anti-Jewish.

If Zionism means:

> **support for a particular nationalist political project**

then opposition to Zionism can look like ordinary political disagreement.

If Zionism means:

> **support for Medinat Yisrael continuing to exist**

then anti-Zionism can be heard as a proposal to eliminate an existing state.

If Zionism means:

> **the political system through which Jewish sovereignty has been maintained at Palestinian expense**

then anti-Zionism can be understood as opposition to that political structure rather than hostility towards Jews.

And none of those semantic choices, by themselves, tells us whether a particular statement is antisemitic.

Context still matters.

Language matters.

Intent can matter.

Effect can matter.

Historical tropes matter.

Treatment of Jews as Jews matters.

Political content matters.

This is one reason disputes around the IHRA Working Definition and the Jerusalem Declaration cannot be understood solely by placing their sentences next to each other.

They enter communities whose underlying definitions of **Israel**, **Zionism**, **Jewish collective rights**, and **antisemitism** are already contested.

→ See: [⚖️ IHRA, JDA And Which Yisrael](./⚖️_ihra_jda_and_which_yisrael.md)

## 🗯️ Sometimes The Definition Changes During The Argument

There is also a less charitable problem.

People sometimes move between definitions because doing so is politically useful.

A person may defend Zionism using the narrow proposition:

> **Jews deserve self-determination.**

Then defend a particular state practice using the broader historical legitimacy of Zionism.

Then, when challenged about Palestinian dispossession, return to:

> **Why do you oppose Jewish self-determination?**

Those are not necessarily the same claim.

Likewise, an opponent can begin by defining Zionism as:

> **the concrete political history of Palestinian dispossession**

and then slide towards treating every Jewish cultural, historical or religious attachment to Eretz Yisrael as inherently colonial.

That is also a category error.

Semantic instability can be sincere.

It can also be exploited.

So the question is not merely:

> **What is your definition of Zionism?**

It is:

> **Are you using the same definition throughout the argument?**

That is a much better diagnostic.

---

## 📖 Why Anti-Zionist Jews Can Become Extremely Precise

There is another downstream consequence.

A Jewish person who rejects Zionism may discover that almost every softer formulation is absorbed back into somebody else's definition.

They say:

> **I recognise Jewish historical relationships with Eretz Yisrael but reject Jewish ethnonational sovereignty.**

Someone responds:

> **That's basically Zionism.**

They say:

> **I believe Israeli Jews have collective rights and must be safe, but I don't believe those rights justify a specifically Jewish state maintained through Palestinian inequality.**

Someone responds:

> **That's still a form of Zionism.**

They say:

> **I don't identify as Zionist.**

Someone responds:

> **Fine, you're a non-Zionist who supports Israel.**

Eventually:

> **No.**

becomes necessary simply to stop the person's position being translated back into a category they have explicitly rejected.

Strong language can therefore sometimes be the endpoint of extensive differentiation rather than evidence of its absence.

## 🌿 But Palestinian Experience Cannot Become Definition Eleven

There is a danger in everything this node has done so far.

We can become so fascinated by why Jews mean ten different things by **Zionism** that Palestinian experience becomes merely another semantic complication.

It isn't.

For Palestinians, Zionism is not only an argument Jews have about Jewish self-definition.

It is also a historical political movement whose institutions, settlement practices, military conflicts, state formation and subsequent territorial politics have had direct material consequences for Palestinian life.

Whatever Jewish people mean internally when they say **Zionism**, Palestinians have encountered political projects operating under that name through:

- migration and settlement;
- land;
- state formation;
- war;
- displacement;
- borders;
- citizenship;
- military power;
- occupation;
- settlements;
- movement restrictions;
- and competing claims to political sovereignty.

A Jewish person may sincerely mean:

> **Jewish safety.**

A Palestinian person may sincerely hear:

> **the political project under which my family lost its home.**

Those are not competing dictionary entries.

They are different historical relationships to the same political tradition.

The Jewish interior meaning does not erase the Palestinian material one.

And Palestinian material experience cannot be reduced to a misunderstanding of what Jews *really mean* by Zionism.

## 🏠 A Philosophical Homeland Becomes Material

This is where the distinction between **Eretz Yisrael** and **Medinat Yisrael** becomes especially important.

Eretz Yisrael can operate within Jewish life as:

- sacred geography;
- ancestral geography;
- textual geography;
- historical memory;
- liturgical orientation;
- theological promise;
- cultural inheritance;
- longing;
- imagined return.

But Zionism turned questions about Jewish relationship with land into questions of modern political power.

Once that happens, another set of questions becomes unavoidable:

Who lives there?

Who owns land?

Who controls migration?

Who controls borders?

Who has citizenship?

Who possesses military force?

Whose political institutions govern?

Who can return?

Who is displaced?

Who is permitted to remain?

A philosophical relationship with homeland and a territorial programme for statehood cannot be treated as though they impose identical moral questions.

→ See: [🏠 When A Philosophical Homeland Is Someone Else's Home](./🏠_when_a_philosophical_homeland_is_someone_elses_home.md)

## 🥚 And We Should Be Suspicious Of The European Baseline

Even this history of multiple Zionisms can reproduce another distortion.

The familiar genealogy is overwhelmingly European:

Herzl.

Ahad Ha'am.

Bundism.

European socialism.

European nationalism.

European antisemitism.

European emancipation.

European Orthodoxy.

European Jewish secularisation.

Those histories matter enormously because modern political Zionism developed substantially within that environment.

But once Medinat Yisrael exists, Jews from the Middle East, North Africa and elsewhere become incorporated into a state whose political categories were not simply products of their own prior histories.

Mizrahi, Sephardi, Maghrebi, Yemenite, Persian, Iraqi, Kurdish, Ethiopian, Indian and other Jewish experiences complicate any neat binary between:

**European Jewish Zionist**

and:

**European Jewish anti-Zionist**.

Their histories with Arab and Muslim societies, colonial power, antisemitism, migration, expulsion, dispossession, Israeli absorption, class, race and state-building require their own treatment.

If our explanation of “ten definitions of Zionism” contains only ten Ashkenazi definitions, we have not solved the problem.

We have demonstrated it.

## 🧭 So What Should We Do With The Word?

We probably cannot abolish semantic plurality.

Nor should we pretend that one contemporary dictionary definition can resolve more than a century of political and philosophical conflict.

But we can become more disciplined.

When somebody says:

> **Zionism**

ask:

> **What do you mean by it here?**

Is the claim about:

- the historical Zionist movement?
- Jewish national self-determination?
- Jewish sovereignty?
- Medinat Yisrael?
- Israel's continued existence?
- a Jewish-majority state?
- Jewish refuge?
- aliyah?
- attachment to Eretz Yisrael?
- cultural Zionism?
- religious return?
- Israeli state ideology?
- settlement?
- Palestinian dispossession?
- the political structure established in 1948?
- the political structure operating today?

Then ask another question:

> **Is that definition remaining stable throughout the conversation?**

And another:

> **Which Yisrael is doing the work inside it?**

Those three questions will not solve the political disagreement.

They will at least tell us what the disagreement is.

---

## 🧿 The Point

The fact that Zionism has multiple definitions does **not** mean:

> **words mean whatever anybody wants them to mean.**

There is a history.

There are texts.

There are organisations.

There are political movements.

There are institutions.

There are states.

There are material consequences.

Definitions can therefore be historically stronger or weaker, broader or narrower, more or less useful for a particular analytical purpose.

But there is no serious route through this history that allows us to pretend **Zionism has always meant one simple proposition**.

The movement was internally plural.

The Jewish questions it attempted to answer were older than it.

The establishment of Medinat Yisrael changed the meaning of the project.

Different Jewish communities inherited different relationships with the state.

Palestinians encountered Zionism through a radically different material history.

And contemporary speakers continue to use one word for several of those things at once.

So when ten Jews produce ten definitions of Zionism, the answer is not necessarily:

> **nine of you are using the word wrong.**

Sometimes the better question is:

> **How did this word become the container for so many different things?**

And then:

> **Which one are you asking me to defend?**
