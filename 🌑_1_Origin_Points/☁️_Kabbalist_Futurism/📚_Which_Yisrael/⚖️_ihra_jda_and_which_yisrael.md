# ⚖️ IHRA, JDA And Which Yisrael?  
**First created:** 2026-08-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-18  
*Why Jews can read the same antisemitism frameworks differently — and why no definition can settle an upstream disagreement about what Yisrael is.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

There is an odd thing that happens when Jewish people argue about the IHRA Working Definition of Antisemitism.

One person reads it and thinks:

> **Obviously. This is describing things I recognise as antisemitism.**

Another Jewish person reads the same text and thinks:

> **Hang on. Why has criticism of a state become so entangled with the definition of hostility towards me as a Jew?**

Another thinks both of those things simultaneously.

Then somebody produces the Jerusalem Declaration on Antisemitism — the **JDA** — and another argument begins.

Did it solve the problem?

Did it weaken protections against antisemitism?

Did it protect legitimate criticism of Israel?

Did it distinguish Jews from Medinat Yisrael more successfully?

Did it simply move the boundary?

This node argues that at least part of the difficulty sits **upstream of both definitions**.

Jewish people do not all organise the relationships between:

- Jewish self;
- Am Yisrael;
- Eretz Yisrael;
- Medinat Yisrael;
- Zionism;
- Jewish collective safety;
- Jewish self-determination;
- and antisemitism

in the same way.

So a framework mentioning “Israel” enters different Jewish psychological and philosophical architectures.

That does not mean antisemitism becomes whatever an individual Jewish person feels it is.

It means that **the disagreement itself needs explaining**.

---

## ⚖️ First: What IHRA Actually Is

The **International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance Working Definition of Antisemitism** was adopted by IHRA in 2016.

IHRA describes it as a **non-legally binding working definition**.

Its core definition is deliberately short:

> **“Antisemitism is a certain perception of Jews, which may be expressed as hatred toward Jews.”**

The definition is accompanied by **eleven illustrative examples**.

Several concern familiar antisemitic conduct independent of Israel:

- calling for or justifying harm to Jews;
- conspiracy myths about Jewish power;
- collective accusations against Jews;
- Holocaust denial;
- accusations of dual loyalty.

Seven of the eleven examples concern Israel.

These include circumstances involving:

- denying Jewish self-determination;
- applying double standards to Israel;
- using classic antisemitic symbols or tropes to characterise Israel or Israelis;
- comparing contemporary Israeli policy with Nazi policy;
- holding Jews collectively responsible for Israeli state conduct.

IHRA also explicitly states that:

> **criticism of Israel similar to that levelled against any other country cannot be regarded as antisemitic.**

This matters.

A serious critique of IHRA cannot simply pretend that sentence is absent.

But neither does that sentence automatically resolve the disputes created by the examples around it.

---

## 🧿 The Problem Is Not Simply “IHRA Says Criticising Israel Is Antisemitic”

It doesn't.

That is too crude.

The harder problem is that the document establishes a space in which **Israel-related political speech sometimes becomes evidence relevant to identifying antisemitism**.

That is necessary in some cases.

Classic antisemitic narratives are absolutely capable of being translated into apparently political speech about Israel.

“The Jews secretly control governments” does not cease being antisemitic merely because someone substitutes the word **Zionists** while obviously continuing to mean Jews.

Holding a random British Jew responsible for Israeli military conduct is not ordinary foreign-policy criticism.

Accusing Jews of covert allegiance to Israel because they are Jewish reproduces an old antisemitic structure.

Using blood-libel imagery against Israelis does not magically become historically innocent because the target has changed.

Those problems are real.

The difficulty begins when we ask:

> **Where exactly is the boundary between identifying antisemitism travelling through discourse about Israel and treating Israel itself as an extension of Jewish identity?**

That is considerably harder.

---

## 📚 Which Yisrael Enters The Definition?

The parent node distinguishes several objects:

- **Medinat Yisrael** — the modern state;
- **Eretz Yisrael** — the land;
- **Am Yisrael** — the Jewish people;
- **Kol Yisrael** — the Jewish collective;
- **Atzmi Yisrael** — an external Israel, particularly Medinat Yisrael, incorporated into self-concept;
- **Yisrael she-be'atzmi** — an interior Jewish Yisrael from which ethical, spiritual, intellectual or covenantal orientation may emerge.

Those distinctions become extremely useful here.

Because when a definition says:

> **Israel**

different readers may not encounter the same object.

For one reader:

> **Israel = Medinat Yisrael, a state.**

For another:

> **Israel = Jewish collective survival.**

For another:

> **Israel = Jewish self-determination.**

For another:

> **Israel = somewhere Jews can flee when other countries become unsafe.**

For another:

> **Israel = Eretz Yisrael and an inherited relationship with land.**

For another:

> **Israel = part of my Jewish self.**

And for another:

> **Israel = a state whose actions should not be attributed to me merely because I am Jewish.**

Those readers can encounter exactly the same antisemitism example and experience its boundaries differently.

---

## 🧠 Enter Atzmi Yisrael

This is where **Atzmi Yisrael** becomes analytically useful.

For some Jewish people, Medinat Yisrael may become incorporated deeply into self-concept.

The relationship can look something like:

**Jewish self**  
↓  
**Jewish collective survival**  
↓  
**Jewish sovereignty**  
↓  
**Medinat Yisrael**  
↓  
**safety / continuity / moral legitimacy**

The state is no longer represented only as:

> **a sovereign political institution outside me.**

It also participates in:

> **who I understand myself and my people to be.**

Now introduce an institutional framework that correctly recognises that **some attacks on Israel can function as attacks upon Jews**.

A potential reinforcement loop becomes possible:

**Israel feels constitutive of self**  
↓  
**criticism of Israel feels personally / collectively threatening**  
↓  
**institutional antisemitism framework recognises some Israel-directed hostility as antisemitic**  
↓  
**experience of threat receives external validation**  
↓  
**distinction between threat to Medinat Yisrael and threat to Jewish self may become harder to maintain**

This does **not** mean:

> **IHRA causes Jews to identify with Israel.**

The historical and psychological relationship is considerably older and more complicated than a document adopted in 2016.

Nor does it mean:

> **any Jew who supports IHRA has Atzmi Yisrael.**

That would be absurd.

The narrower question is:

> **What happens when a framework containing Israel-related examples encounters a Jewish self-concept in which Israel is already highly self-referential?**

That is a legitimate psychological question.

---

## 🪞 Another Jew Can Read The Same Text Backwards

Now consider a Jewish person whose self-concept maintains a strong distinction:

**Jewish self**  
≠  
**Medinat Yisrael**

They may understand themselves principally through:

- Am Yisrael;
- diaspora;
- religious practice;
- family;
- Torah;
- Yiddishkeit;
- Sephardi or Mizrahi traditions;
- Bundism;
- universalist ethics;
- local community;
- Yisrael she-be'atzmi;
- or any number of other Jewish architectures.

That person encounters an Israel-focused example in an antisemitism framework and may experience the problem in almost the opposite direction.

Instead of:

> **thank you for recognising an attack upon something Jewish**

they may hear:

> **why is a foreign state's political legitimacy being placed so close to my protected status as a Jew?**

They may actually experience that proximity as dangerous.

Because one of the oldest antisemitic moves is precisely:

**Jew**  
→ **foreign allegiance**

or:

**Jew**  
→ **collective political agency somewhere else**

or:

**Jew**  
→ **responsibility for actions attributed to other Jews**

For this reader, protecting Jews therefore requires a **stronger boundary** between Jews and Medinat Yisrael.

That person is not necessarily less worried about antisemitism.

They may be worried about a different antisemitic failure mode.

---

## 🧿 Two Jewish Safety Strategies

We can therefore end up with two positions that both experience themselves as protecting Jews.

### Strategy One

> **Recognise attacks upon Israel when they are functioning as coded attacks upon Jews.**

The fear is:

**political language becomes a laundering mechanism for antisemitism.**

This fear is justified.

### Strategy Two

> **Maintain a bright distinction between Jews and Medinat Yisrael so Jews are not made politically responsible for a foreign state.**

The fear is:

**political identification with Israel becomes a mechanism for dual-loyalty accusations and collective attribution.**

This fear is also justified.

The problem is that these strategies can pull in opposite directions when badly implemented.

One worries that separating Israel too sharply from Jews will make contemporary antisemitism harder to recognise.

The other worries that connecting Israel too tightly to Jews will reproduce an antisemitic category error.

That is a much more useful description of the disagreement than:

> **one side cares about antisemitism and the other doesn't.**

---

## 🌉 Enter The Jerusalem Declaration

The **Jerusalem Declaration on Antisemitism** was launched in 2021.

Its authors explicitly described it as a response to IHRA.

They argued that the IHRA framework had become unclear in important respects, particularly around Israel and Zionism, and that this created confusion about the boundary between antisemitism and legitimate political speech.

The JDA therefore attempted two things simultaneously:

1. **strengthen the identification of antisemitism;**
2. **protect space for political argument about Israel, Palestine and Zionism.**

Its core definition is considerably more direct:

> **“Antisemitism is discrimination, prejudice, hostility or violence against Jews as Jews (or Jewish institutions as Jewish).”**

It then provides fifteen guidelines.

Five are general.

Ten concern Israel and Palestine.

That second fact is worth noticing.

The JDA criticises IHRA for placing disproportionate weight upon Israel while itself devoting two-thirds of its guidelines to Israel/Palestine.

That is not necessarily contradictory.

It reflects the problem the document was specifically attempting to solve.

The Israel question had become the fault line.

---

## 🧭 What JDA Clarifies

The JDA does something analytically useful that IHRA does less explicitly.

It provides examples not only of things that **may be antisemitic**, but also of things that, on their face, **are not**.

Among other things, JDA says that the following are not in themselves antisemitic:

- supporting Palestinian political, national, civil and human rights;
- criticising or opposing Zionism as a form of nationalism;
- proposing different constitutional arrangements for Jews and Palestinians;
- evidence-based criticism of Israel, including its institutions and founding principles;
- identifying systematic racial discrimination;
- comparisons with historical cases including settler colonialism or apartheid;
- boycott, divestment and sanctions directed at Israel;
- political speech merely because it is excessive, contentious, disproportionate or perceived as applying a double standard.

That is a substantial clarification.

It creates a larger explicit space between:

**criticism I regard as wrong or offensive**

and:

**antisemitism.**

Those categories desperately need separating.

---

## 🕸️ JDA Also Protects Jews From Conflation

Importantly for this cluster, the JDA identifies as antisemitic:

- holding Jews collectively responsible for Israel's conduct;
- treating Jews as agents of Israel merely because they are Jewish;
- requiring Jews to condemn Israel or Zionism simply because they are Jewish;
- assuming non-Israeli Jews are necessarily more loyal to Israel than to their own countries.

That is doing something very important conceptually.

It is protecting the boundary:

> **Jew ≠ agent of Medinat Yisrael**

which this cluster also needs.

It therefore helps address precisely the danger that can emerge when **Am Yisrael**, Jewish identity and Medinat Yisrael are collapsed together.

---

## ⚖️ But JDA Does Not Say “Anything Goes”

There is another flattening worth avoiding.

JDA does **not** say:

> **anti-Zionism can never be antisemitic.**

Its own FAQ explicitly rejects that interpretation.

The distinction it makes is:

> **anti-Zionism and antisemitism are categorically different concepts.**

That means opposition to nationalism or Zionism is not automatically hostility towards Jews.

But anti-Zionist discourse can still become antisemitic.

For example:

**criticism of Israeli state influence**

can become:

> **Jews secretly control governments.**

**criticism of Zionist organisations**

can become:

> **the Jews operate a hidden global network.**

**criticism of Israeli conduct**

can become:

> **Jews are collectively evil.**

**opposition to Medinat Yisrael**

can become:

> **attack random Jews because Israel did something.**

The political object does not immunise the language around it from antisemitism.

This distinction is absolutely necessary.

---

## 🔎 Context Still Exists

JDA repeatedly insists upon context.

That is inconvenient.

It is also correct.

There is no linguistic machine where we insert a sentence and receive:

> **ANTISEMITISM: 87.4%**

The same phrase may function differently depending upon:

- who is being addressed;
- who is being blamed;
- whether Jews are being collectivised;
- whether an old antisemitic trope is being activated;
- whether the speaker is talking about a state or about Jews;
- the surrounding discourse;
- patterns of behaviour over time;
- historical context;
- political context;
- and sometimes the speaker's own position within the conflict.

JDA itself gives the useful example that hostility towards Israel might arise from antisemitic animus, from response to a human-rights violation, or from Palestinian experience of Israeli state conduct.

Those are obviously not psychologically or politically interchangeable.

---

## 🌿 Palestinians Are Not An Edge Case

This is one place where JDA makes an important intervention.

A definition of antisemitism cannot treat Palestinian political speech as though the speaker encounters Israel in the same way as a Jewish person whose principal association with Israel is refuge, safety or collective self-determination.

For a Palestinian, **Medinat Yisrael may principally be encountered as state power**.

That can mean:

- military force;
- displacement;
- occupation;
- settlement;
- borders;
- permits;
- prisons;
- property;
- citizenship;
- family separation;
- restrictions on movement;
- or direct personal and family history.

That doesn't create a licence for antisemitism.

Palestinians can say antisemitic things.

Jews can say antisemitic things.

Anybody can.

But Palestinian hostility towards a state exercising power over Palestinian life cannot simply be interpreted through the internal Jewish meaning of that state.

Otherwise:

> **how Israel feels to Jews**

becomes the framework through which:

> **what Israel does to Palestinians**

is permitted to be described.

That is backwards.

---

## 🧠 Has JDA Solved The Which Yisrael Problem?

No.

And it cannot.

That is not necessarily a criticism of JDA.

An antisemitism definition is not capable of settling several thousand years of Jewish disagreement about Yisrael.

JDA can establish:

> **criticising Zionism is not inherently antisemitic.**

It cannot establish:

> **what Zionism should mean to Jews.**

It can establish:

> **Jews must not be treated as agents of Israel simply because they are Jewish.**

It cannot establish:

> **whether an individual Jew should experience Israel as central to Jewish selfhood.**

It can establish:

> **different constitutional arrangements for Israelis and Palestinians can be advocated without that advocacy being inherently antisemitic.**

It cannot establish:

> **which political arrangement Jews or Palestinians ought ultimately to support.**

It can clarify the traffic rules.

It cannot choose everybody's destination.

---

## 🌉 A Better Traffic System, Not The End Of The Argument

For the purposes of this cluster, that may be the most useful description of JDA:

> **a better traffic system across the disagreement, not a resolution of the disagreement.**

It provides clearer space for saying:

**Jew**  
≠  
**Israel**

**anti-Zionism**  
≠ automatically  
**antisemitism**

**criticism of a state**  
≠ automatically  
**hostility towards a people**

while simultaneously preserving:

**antisemitic tropes do not become innocent because someone says “Israel” instead of “Jews.”**

That is useful.

But the deeper problem remains.

Two Jews can still read:

> **denying Jewish self-determination**

and disagree about what political object the phrase requires.

One may hear:

> **denying Am Yisrael collective political existence.**

Another may hear:

> **requiring Medinat Yisrael to remain a Jewish ethnonational state.**

Another may believe Jewish self-determination could exist through binational or non-state institutions.

Another may reject nationalism as the appropriate political framework altogether.

The definition cannot settle that dispute because the dispute concerns **what Jewish collective political existence is**.

That argument is older than either document.

---

## 🔖 Zionism Makes This Worse Because Zionism Also Has Ten Definitions

This cluster has already established another problem.

When IHRA or JDA discusses:

> **Zionism**

we still have to ask:

> **which Zionism?**

If somebody understands Zionism as:

> **Jews deserve somewhere safe**

then opposition to Zionism may sound like:

> **Jews do not deserve safety.**

If somebody understands Zionism as:

> **Jewish national self-determination**

anti-Zionism may sound like denial of collective Jewish rights.

If somebody understands Zionism as:

> **a historical nationalist movement that established a Jewish state through processes that dispossessed Palestinians**

anti-Zionism may mean:

> **opposition to that political structure and its continuing consequences.**

If somebody understands Zionism as:

> **support for Israel's continued existence**

then anti-Zionism can sound like a programme for state destruction.

One word.

Completely different propositions.

→ See: [🔖 Why Zionism Has Ten Definitions](./🔖_why_zionism_has_ten_definitions.md)

## 🪞 This Is Why Jewish Testimony Can Conflict

There is a deeply uncomfortable question that emerges in institutional settings.

Suppose one Jewish person says:

> **That statement was antisemitic.**

Another Jewish person says:

> **I do not experience it as antisemitic.**

What now?

The answer cannot be:

> **find the authentic Jew.**

Jewish identity does not make every Jewish person infallible about antisemitism.

Nor does disagreement between Jews make antisemitism impossible to identify.

A Jewish person can miss antisemitism.

A Jewish person can over-identify antisemitism.

A Jewish person can reproduce antisemitism.

A Jewish person can understand a political statement through a self-concept another Jewish person does not share.

This is why institutional assessment needs:

- context;
- evidence;
- history;
- knowledge of antisemitic tropes;
- attention to who is being targeted;
- attention to actual political content;
- and enough Jewish literacy to understand that **Jewish disagreement is not a malfunction**.

→ See: [🪞 Why Jews Can Read The Same Sentence Differently](./🪞_why_jews_can_read_the_same_sentence_differently.md)

---

## 🫱 What Non-Jewish Allies Should Not Do

There are two extremely tempting responses to this disagreement.

Both are bad.

### ❌ Find A Jewish Permission Slip

A non-Jewish person agrees with a political position.

They find a Jewish person who agrees.

Then:

> **Well, a Jewish person says it isn't antisemitic.**

No.

Jewish plurality does not turn individual Jews into transferable immunity certificates.

### ❌ Find A Jewish Veto

Alternatively:

> **A Jewish person experienced this as antisemitic, therefore political discussion ends.**

Also no.

Experience matters.

It should be heard.

But identifying discrimination cannot depend entirely upon whether one member of a heterogeneous group personally experiences a proposition as offensive or threatening.

Otherwise disagreement inside minority communities becomes impossible to handle.

The more useful task is:

> **understand what the Jewish speakers disagree about and investigate the actual claim.**

→ See: [🫱 How Not To Flatten Jewish Plurality](./🫱_how_not_to_flatten_jewish_plurality.md)

---

## 🧿 Experience Is Evidence, Not Sovereignty Over The Category

This distinction is important enough to state plainly.

When somebody says:

> **I experienced that as antisemitic**

that is meaningful evidence.

It may reveal:

- a trope another reader missed;
- historical resonance;
- a pattern of harassment;
- an encoded accusation;
- a context invisible to an outsider;
- or the cumulative effect of repeated hostility.

Listen.

Investigate.

But:

> **I experienced this as an attack upon something inside my self-concept**

does not automatically establish:

> **the external political object is protected from criticism because it is inside my self-concept.**

That would be an impossible rule.

People incorporate:

- countries;
- political movements;
- religions;
- institutions;
- professions;
- families;
- ideologies;
- leaders;
- histories

into self-concept all the time.

Psychological significance cannot by itself produce political or legal immunity.

This is exactly why the distinction between **Atzmi Yisrael** and **Medinat Yisrael** matters.

---

## 🔮 Yisrael She-be'atzmi Offers Another Possibility

There is also a different route through Jewish identity.

If **Yisrael she-be'atzmi** refers to an interior Jewish inheritance — covenant, argument, ethical obligation, Torah, memory, justice, responsibility, wrestling, peoplehood — then criticism of Medinat Yisrael need not threaten the Jewish self.

The direction can run the other way:

**Yisrael she-be'atzmi**  
↓  
**Jewish ethical commitments**  
↓  
**standards of judgement**  
↓  
**Medinat Yisrael**

Here:

> **Israel has done something wrong**

does not necessarily mean:

> **something constitutive of me has become wrong.**

It can mean:

> **something constitutive of me requires me to say that Israel has done wrong.**

That is one reason Jewish political disagreement can become so intense.

Two people may both believe they are acting from Jewish integrity while their relationships between **self and state** run in opposite directions.

→ See: [🔮 Yisrael She-be'atzmi](./🔮_yisrael_shebeatzmi.md)

## ⚠️ The Affirmation Problem

This brings us to one of the central propositions of the cluster.

Institutional frameworks must be careful not to transform:

> **some attacks upon Israel function as attacks upon Jews**

into:

> **therefore Israel is inherently part of protected Jewish identity.**

Those propositions are not the same.

The first identifies a mode through which antisemitism can travel.

The second makes a political object constitutive of a protected group.

That risks creating a circular structure:

**Israel feels like part of me**  
↓  
**criticism of Israel feels like an attack upon me**  
↓  
**institution recognises attacks upon Jewish identity as antisemitism**  
↓  
**my experience of threat validates the classification**  
↓  
**Israel becomes harder to distinguish from Jewish identity**  
↓  
**future criticism becomes still more threatening**

That is not a healthy way to identify prejudice.

A framework should help distinguish categories.

It should not make differentiation harder.

---

## 🕸️ But Differentiation Cannot Become Naivety

The opposite mistake is equally dangerous.

We cannot simply announce:

> **Israel is a state, Jews are Jews, problem solved.**

Antisemitism does not respect neat conceptual boundaries.

It adapts.

It codes itself.

It borrows politically legitimate subjects.

It attaches old fantasies to new nouns.

“Zionist” can sometimes actually mean **Zionist**.

Sometimes it means **Israeli**.

Sometimes it means **supporter of Israel**.

And sometimes the speaker is very obviously using it as a substitute for **Jew**.

Context matters.

The purpose of differentiation is therefore not to create a loophole through which old antisemitism can travel wearing a keffiyeh, an Israeli flag, a MAGA hat, a suit, a cassock, or anything else.

The purpose is to identify **the object actually being targeted**.

---

## 🌿 Palestinian Rights Cannot Depend Upon Jewish Psychological Comfort

There is another boundary this cluster needs.

Political rights do not disappear because their assertion causes identity distress elsewhere.

A Palestinian claim concerning:

- equality;
- return;
- statehood;
- land;
- occupation;
- displacement;
- settlement;
- military violence;
- citizenship;
- political representation

has to be evaluated as a political, historical, moral and legal claim.

It cannot become antisemitic merely because accepting it would force some Jewish people to reorganise Atzmi Yisrael.

That would make Palestinian political possibility dependent upon the stability of somebody else's self-concept.

No minority-rights framework can sensibly operate that way.

At the same time, Palestinians do not acquire an exemption from antisemitism because they have experienced injustice.

Both propositions can coexist:

> **Palestinian political claims must be allowed to confront Israeli state power.**

and:

> **Palestinian suffering does not make antisemitism harmless or legitimate.**

We should be capable of holding two thoughts at once.

---

## 🧭 So Which Framework Should We Use?

This node is not going to issue a universal instruction.

IHRA has strengths.

It identifies forms of antisemitism that genuinely travel through discourse about Israel.

Its emphasis on classical tropes, collective responsibility, dual loyalty and coded hostility addresses real dangers.

JDA has strengths.

It more explicitly separates Jews from Medinat Yisrael, distinguishes anti-Zionism from antisemitism, protects space for Palestinian political claims and state criticism, and makes clear that offensive or unreasonable political speech is not automatically antisemitic.

JDA was written in substantial part because its authors believed IHRA had created ambiguity in precisely this area.

That historical relationship should not be disguised.

For this cluster, however, neither document should be treated as a substitute for thought.

The useful questions remain:

> **Who is being targeted?**

> **What political object is actually being criticised?**

> **Are Jews being collectivised?**

> **Is Medinat Yisrael being treated as representative of Jews?**

> **Are Jews being treated as agents of Medinat Yisrael?**

> **Is a classical antisemitic trope doing the analytical work?**

> **Is a legitimate political criticism being reclassified because Israel occupies a particular place within somebody's self-concept?**

> **Which Zionism?**

> **Which Yisrael?**

---

## 🧠 Has Either Framework Solved The Psychological Problem?

No.

Because neither can.

A definition can help identify prejudice.

It cannot decide the architecture of Jewish selfhood.

It cannot decide whether:

**Medinat Yisrael belongs inside Jewish identity.**

It cannot decide whether:

**Eretz Yisrael entails sovereignty.**

It cannot decide whether:

**Jewish self-determination entails a nation-state.**

It cannot decide whether:

**diaspora is exile or home.**

It cannot decide whether:

**Zionism is liberation or nationalism or refuge or colonisation or several of those things simultaneously depending upon whose history is being described.**

Those are philosophical, historical, political and theological arguments.

Some are thousands of years old.

A modern antisemitism definition cannot solve them by administrative fiat.

---

## 🌉 What The Frameworks Can Do

What they *can* do is give us better guardrails.

They can help us say:

> **Do not hold Jews responsible for Israel.**

> **Do not disguise classical antisemitism as political criticism.**

> **Do not assume Jewish allegiance to Israel.**

> **Do not require Jews to perform condemnation of a state because they are Jewish.**

> **Do not treat Palestinian rights as inherently antisemitic.**

> **Do not treat criticism of Zionism as automatically hostility towards Jews.**

> **Do not treat hostility towards Jews as legitimate merely because the speaker calls it anti-Zionism.**

That is already a lot.

The remaining work belongs to us.

---

## 🧿 The Point

IHRA and JDA are often treated as rival answers to:

> **What counts as antisemitism around Israel?**

They are.

But underneath that disagreement sits another question:

> **Why is Israel sitting inside an antisemitism definition at all, and what relationship between Jews and Israel does each reader assume when they encounter it there?**

That question does not have one Jewish answer.

For some Jews, protecting Israel from particular forms of delegitimisation feels inseparable from protecting Jewish collective existence.

For others, protecting Jews requires insisting that Medinat Yisrael is a state and **not an extension of Jewish personhood**.

For still others, both concerns are real and must somehow be held together.

The JDA clarifies some of the boundary problems created by IHRA.

It does not resolve the deeper Jewish disagreement.

It cannot.

Because that disagreement is not finally about two antisemitism definitions.

It is about:

**self;**

**people;**

**land;**

**state;**

**safety;**

**sovereignty;**

**diaspora;**

**Palestine;**

**and which meanings we permit one word — Yisrael — to carry.**

So when two Jews read the same sentence and one says:

> **This attacks something fundamental about me.**

while another says:

> **That state is not me.**

before deciding which Jew is wrong, we should probably ask:

> **Which Yisrael did each of you just hear?**

That will not settle the argument.

It may finally tell us what argument we are having.
