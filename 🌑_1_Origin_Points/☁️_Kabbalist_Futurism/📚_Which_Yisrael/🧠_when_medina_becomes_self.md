# 🧠 When Medina Becomes Self  
**First created:** 2026-08-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-18  
*How Medinat Yisrael can become incorporated into Jewish self-concept — and why criticism of a state can then be experienced as a threat to safety, morality, belonging, or the self.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

For some Jews, Medinat Yisrael is important.

For some Jews, Medinat Yisrael is very important.

And for some Jews, something more psychologically complicated can happen:

**Medinat Yisrael becomes incorporated into the architecture through which the person understands themselves as Jewish.**

This cluster calls that structure:

**Atzmi Yisrael — עצמי ישראל.**

This is a **working Polaris term**.

It is not being presented as an established category in Jewish studies, developmental psychology, psychoanalysis, rabbinic literature, or the Hebrew psychological literature.

It names something we need language to discuss.

The distinction is between:

> **I care deeply about Israel.**

and:

> **the Israel I carry psychologically has become part of who I understand myself to be.**

Those are not the same thing.

Nor is Atzmi Yisrael inherently pathological.

Human beings incorporate external objects into self-concept all the time:

- countries;
- families;
- communities;
- religions;
- professions;
- political movements;
- institutions;
- historical narratives;
- places;
- languages;
- teams;
- schools;
- movements for liberation.

The interesting question is what happens **when the external object behaves in ways the internal object cannot easily accommodate**.

For Medinat Yisrael, that question has become particularly consequential.

---

## 🧭 A Working Definition

For the purposes of this cluster:

> **Atzmi Yisrael describes an external representation of Israel — particularly Medinat Yisrael — incorporated into psychological self-concept strongly enough that changes in the perceived condition, legitimacy, safety, or morality of Israel can affect the person's own sense of safety, belonging, continuity, or moral integrity.**

The movement is:

**Medinat Yisrael / external Israel**  
↓  
**representation of Israel**  
↓  
**incorporation into self-concept**  
↓  
**Atzmi Yisrael**

This does not require Israeli citizenship.

It does not require dual citizenship.

It does not require formal allegiance to the Israeli government.

It does not require conscious political reasoning of the form:

> **Israel is part of me.**

Self-concept formation does not need to announce itself.

Something can become foundational precisely because it has been present for so long that it no longer feels like a proposition requiring examination.

It simply feels like:

> **how things are.**

---

## 🧠 This Is About Development, Not Political Validity

There is an important distinction at the beginning.

To say:

> **something has become part of someone's identity or self-concept**

does **not** mean:

> **therefore it is morally valid.**

It does not establish that the thing is:

- factually correct;
- ethically good;
- politically legitimate;
- legally privileged;
- immutable;
- entitled to protection from criticism;
- or something other people must incorporate into their own identities.

Human beings can build morally admirable things into self-concept.

Human beings can also build:

- nationalism;
- racism;
- class status;
- institutions;
- ideologies;
- abusive families;
- professions;
- myths;
- false beliefs

into self-concept.

Psychology tells us something about **how the belief is held**.

It does not decide **whether the belief should be held**.

This node is explanatory.

It is not an exoneration mechanism.

---

## 🧩 How Medina Gets In

There is no single developmental pathway.

For some Jews, the relationship may begin very early.

Medinat Yisrael can appear inside childhood Jewish life through:

- family;
- school;
- synagogue;
- youth movements;
- Hebrew education;
- Jewish summer camps;
- fundraising;
- communal commemorations;
- Holocaust education;
- prayer;
- Israeli relatives;
- travel;
- stories of persecution;
- stories of refuge;
- celebrations;
- war;
- antisemitism;
- communal fear;
- communal pride.

Sometimes the political state appears alongside:

**Jewish peoplehood.**

Sometimes alongside:

**Jewish survival.**

Sometimes:

**never again.**

Sometimes:

**Jewish strength.**

Sometimes:

**home.**

Sometimes:

**the place we can go if this goes wrong.**

Sometimes:

**the thing that proves Jews cannot simply be rounded up again.**

Those associations can become deeply emotionally coherent before a child possesses anything resembling a sophisticated theory of nationalism.

That is normal cognitive development.

Children receive worlds before they analyse them.

---

## 🕯️ The Shoah Changes The Emotional Architecture

No account of this process can ignore the Shoah.

For many postwar Jewish families, the question:

> **What happens when the state does not protect Jews?**

was not theoretical.

Neither was:

> **What happens when other countries refuse Jews refuge?**

Jewish families carried direct and inherited knowledge of:

- persecution;
- statelessness;
- borders closing;
- failed asylum;
- dispossession;
- deportation;
- mass murder;
- relatives who disappeared;
- relatives who survived because they escaped;
- relatives who did not escape.

The modern State of Israel could therefore become attached to something psychologically enormous:

> **This time there is somewhere.**

And:

> **This time Jews can defend Jews.**

Those propositions contain genuine historical force.

They also create the conditions under which a state can become more than a state inside self-concept.

---

## 🛡️ “Never Again” Can Acquire Institutions

There is an important transition here.

**Never again** can be an ethical command.

It can mean:

> **No human population should ever be subjected to this again.**

But it can also become a security proposition:

> **Jews must never again be dependent entirely upon somebody else's willingness to protect us.**

That proposition points towards:

- borders;
- army;
- intelligence;
- refuge;
- sovereignty;
- weapons;
- state capacity.

Now Medinat Yisrael is no longer simply:

> **a country containing lots of Jews.**

It can become:

> **the institutional answer to Jewish helplessness.**

That gives the state extraordinary psychological weight.

Attacking the adequacy of the answer can then feel dangerously close to reopening the original question:

> **What protects us if this disappears?**

---

## 🏛️ 1948 Is Not The End Of The Story

The creation of Medinat Yisrael in 1948 obviously matters enormously.

But if we want to understand the psychological development of Atzmi Yisrael in many diaspora communities, **1967 deserves particular attention**.

Because 1967 can operate as something more than another Israeli war.

It can become a dramatic demonstration of a story about **Jewish agency**.

---

## ⚡ 1967: From Vulnerability To Power

In the weeks preceding the June 1967 war, there was considerable fear about Israel's survival.

Then the war lasted six days.

Israel defeated Egyptian, Jordanian, and Syrian forces and dramatically expanded the territory under its military control.

For many Jews, particularly in diaspora communities still living very close historically to the Shoah, the psychological meaning could be extraordinary.

The story could be experienced as:

**Jews are threatened**  
↓  
**Jews are not helpless**  
↓  
**Jews defend themselves**  
↓  
**Jews survive**  
↓  
**Jews win**

That is an astonishingly powerful reversal.

Within a single generation, Jewish collective imagery could move from:

**Auschwitz**

towards:

**an army capable of preventing another Auschwitz.**

That does not tell us how the war should be evaluated politically.

It tells us why the event could acquire enormous psychological significance.

---

## 🛡️ 1967 And The Protective Israel

For some diaspora Jews, 1967 could reinforce an image of Medinat Yisrael as:

- capable;
- brave;
- militarily competent;
- protective;
- sovereign;
- difficult to destroy;
- able to act without waiting for permission;
- proof that Jews no longer had to accept helplessness.

That image can travel across generations.

A child born decades after 1967 may inherit not the war itself but the **meaning assigned to it**.

The emotional lesson becomes:

> **Israel is what happens when Jews can finally protect themselves.**

And therefore:

> **Israel protects us.**

That second sentence is psychologically important because for a Jew living in London, New York, Paris, Toronto, Buenos Aires, Melbourne, or elsewhere, the protection may be partly **symbolic or anticipatory rather than immediate**.

Medinat Yisrael is not ordinarily the institution dispatching police when somebody attacks a synagogue in Britain.

It does not generally prosecute an antisemitic employer in America.

It does not operate the local hate-crime system.

It does not physically stand between most diaspora Jews and everyday antisemitism.

But it can still **feel protective**.

---

## 🪞 Symbolic Protection Is Still Psychologically Real

Calling that protection symbolic does not mean:

> **fake.**

Symbols regulate human beings.

Institutions regulate human beings.

Believing:

> **there is somewhere I could go**

can alter felt safety even if the person never goes there.

Believing:

> **there is an army that would fight for Jews**

can alter a person's sense of Jewish vulnerability even if that army is thousands of miles away.

Believing:

> **we are no longer helpless**

can transform the emotional inheritance of persecution.

So the protective function can be psychologically genuine while being materially indirect.

That distinction matters.

---

## 🧯 Defence Can Become Part Of Identity

Now the structure becomes:

**historical Jewish vulnerability**  
↓  
**need for protection**  
↓  
**Jewish sovereign power**  
↓  
**Medinat Yisrael**  
↓  
**proof that Jews can defend Jews**  
↓  
**felt Jewish safety**

Once repeated sufficiently, this can become:

**Medinat Yisrael = Jewish protection**

and then:

**threat to Medinat Yisrael = threat to Jewish protection**

and potentially:

**criticism perceived as delegitimising Medinat Yisrael = threat to Jewish safety**

This is one route through which political criticism can acquire an unexpectedly existential emotional register.

The listener may not be defending:

> **that particular minister**

or:

> **that particular military operation**

in the first instance.

They may be defending:

> **the existence of an architecture they associate with not being helpless.**

---

## 🪞 But The Protective Story Can Become Too Smooth

There is a problem.

Once Medinat Yisrael becomes psychologically encoded as:

> **the thing that protects Jews**

information that complicates the protective role becomes harder to process.

For example:

- Israeli state actions can increase anger directed towards diaspora Jews;
- political actors can wrongly attribute Israeli conduct to Jewish communities abroad;
- close identification between Jews and Israel can strengthen dual-loyalty assumptions;
- diaspora Jews may experience antisemitism precisely because outsiders treat them as representatives of Israel.

The relationship between:

**Israel**

and:

**diaspora Jewish safety**

is therefore not a single-direction machine.

Something can feel protective while also producing or interacting with other forms of risk.

Those propositions can coexist.

---

## 🧠 Atzmi Yisrael Is Not Merely “Support For Israel”

This distinction is essential.

Someone can strongly support Israel without Atzmi Yisrael becoming heavily developed.

They might simply believe:

> **Israel is strategically useful.**

Or:

> **Israel has a right to exist.**

Or:

> **I support Israeli policy.**

Those are political positions.

Atzmi Yisrael concerns something different:

> **what happens when the represented Israel participates in maintaining the coherence of the self.**

Now:

**Israel is safe**  
can help mean  
**we are safe.**

**Israel is strong**  
can help mean  
**we are not helpless.**

**Israel is legitimate**  
can help mean  
**our collective existence is legitimate.**

And, crucially:

**Israel is good**  
can become entangled with  
**we are good.**

That final relationship is where things can become especially difficult.

---

## ✨ The Shining-Star Israel

For some people, Medinat Yisrael is not merely understood as necessary.

It is idealised.

It may be represented as:

- exceptionally democratic;
- exceptionally moral;
- exceptionally restrained;
- technologically brilliant;
- socially progressive;
- courageous;
- ingenious;
- humane despite extraordinary threats;
- a miraculous national restoration;
- a refuge created after catastrophe;
- proof of what Jewish people can build.

Some of those descriptions may correspond to real achievements.

Some may be partial.

Some may become mythology.

The psychological problem emerges when:

> **Israel is good**

becomes necessary for:

> **my relationship with Israel to remain morally coherent**

and therefore:

> **my relationship with Jewishness to remain morally coherent.**

Now contrary evidence has somewhere dangerous to land.

---

## 💥 When Medina Fails The Internal Israel

Consider:

> **Medinat Yisrael has committed a grave wrong.**

For someone whose relationship with the state remains externally differentiated, this can be processed as:

> **A state I care about has done something terrible.**

Painful.

But cognitively possible.

For someone whose Atzmi Yisrael contains:

**Israel = Jewish survival + Jewish agency + Jewish morality + Jewish safety + part of me**

the proposition may expand:

**Medinat Yisrael has done something terrible**  
↓  
**the protective Jewish project has done something terrible**  
↓  
**the moral object inside my Jewish self is threatened**  
↓  
**my understanding of Jewish goodness / safety / history is threatened**  
↓  
**self-integrity is threatened**

That is no longer merely a foreign-policy update.

It can require psychological reconstruction.

---

## 🧠 The Expensive Update

The brain now has several possibilities.

It can say:

> **The state is not the self.**

It can differentiate government from country.

It can differentiate country from people.

It can differentiate Medinat Yisrael from Am Yisrael.

It can differentiate Medinat Yisrael from Eretz Yisrael.

It can revise Zionism.

It can retain Zionism while rejecting state conduct.

It can abandon Zionism.

It can grieve.

It can reconstruct Jewish identity around other objects.

It can deepen Yisrael she-be'atzmi.

Or it can make the incoming evidence go away.

Not necessarily literally.

Psychologically.

---

## 🛡️ Identity-Protective Cognition

When accepting information threatens an important self-concept, human beings can become exceptionally creative interpreters.

The response may include:

- discounting sources;
- narrowing the acceptable definition of wrongdoing;
- focusing on the motives of critics;
- demanding impossible certainty;
- changing terminology;
- exceptionalising the state;
- treating criticism as hostile;
- expanding the category of antisemitism;
- interpreting contrary Jewish voices as betrayal;
- repeatedly returning to existential danger.

None of these behaviours proves that the underlying political conclusion is false.

People can distrust bad sources.

Critics can be antisemitic.

Israel can face genuine security threats.

Terminology can genuinely matter.

The analytical question is:

> **How much of the reasoning is evaluating the external proposition, and how much is protecting the internal structure that accepting it would destabilise?**

Those processes can occur simultaneously.

---

## 🪞 “But They're Trying To Kill Us”

This sentence needs particular care.

Sometimes:

> **they are trying to kill us**

describes a genuine threat.

Jewish history contains enough examples that dismissing Jewish threat perception as inherently neurotic would be grotesque.

But the pronouns matter.

Who is:

> **they?**

Who is:

> **us?**

If an attack upon Israelis becomes immediately represented as:

> **an attack upon all Jews**

then Medinat Yisrael and Am Yisrael have begun to overlap psychologically.

Sometimes that overlap correctly recognises antisemitic motivation.

Sometimes it does not.

The distinction requires evidence.

Threat does not remove the need for differentiation.

It makes differentiation harder.

---

## ⚡ 1967 Also Creates Another Israel

There is another reason 1967 is such an important hinge.

The same war that could be experienced by Jews as a dramatic triumph over vulnerability also transformed Palestinian life and the territorial structure of Israeli power.

Israel took control of:

- the West Bank;
- East Jerusalem;
- Gaza;
- the Sinai Peninsula;
- the Golan Heights.

Sinai was later returned to Egypt.

But the war inaugurated the Israeli occupation of the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and Gaza, with consequences that became central to subsequent Israeli and Palestinian politics.

So 1967 produces two sharply different historical memories.

One can be:

> **We were threatened and survived.**

Another:

> **Our land came under Israeli military occupation.**

Both refer to the same six days.

This is a particularly clear example of why Jewish psychological meaning cannot substitute for Palestinian material history.

---

## 🌿 The Protective Miracle And The Occupied Palestinian

The difficult sentence is:

> **The event that can function as proof of Jewish safety can simultaneously function as the beginning of a new structure of Palestinian domination.**

That is not an argument that Jewish fear in 1967 was fake.

It was not.

It is not an argument that Jewish relief afterwards was fake.

It was not.

It is an argument that **one population's experience of protection can coexist with another population's experience of the power through which that protection is secured.**

That is exactly the kind of collision this cluster needs to hold.

If Atzmi Yisrael preserves only:

> **1967 = Jewish survival**

while Palestinian history preserves:

> **1967 = occupation**

then political conversations beginning from those memories are going to diverge immediately.

The work is not to tell either population that memory is irrelevant.

The work is to reconnect memory with the whole event.

---

## 🌉 Jerusalem Intensifies Everything

1967 also transforms the relationship with Jerusalem.

The capture of East Jerusalem and Israeli control of the Old City produced an extraordinarily powerful symbolic moment for many Jews.

A place carried for centuries through:

- liturgy;
- memory;
- longing;
- ritual;
- text;
- mourning;
- pilgrimage;
- theological imagination

was suddenly represented as:

> **in Jewish hands.**

That phrase alone tells us how quickly:

**spiritual geography**

can become:

**political sovereignty.**

And how easily:

**political sovereignty**

can inherit the emotional force of:

**spiritual return.**

For Palestinian residents of East Jerusalem, however, the same event concerns:

- military conquest;
- annexation;
- residency;
- land;
- planning;
- housing;
- state power;
- political rights.

One Jerusalem.

Different historical objects.

## 🧬 Generations Can Inherit The Association Without The Event

A person does not need to remember 1967 for 1967 to shape their self-concept.

That is how cultural transmission works.

One generation experiences:

**fear → war → victory → relief → pride**

The next receives:

**Israel saved itself.**

The next:

**Israel is what keeps Jews safe.**

The next:

**Without Israel, Jews are unsafe.**

By the time the proposition reaches somebody born decades later, the historical sequence may have become an identity axiom.

That does not mean every Jewish family transmitted 1967 this way.

Many did not.

Different Jewish communities remember the war differently.

Israeli politics changed.

Religious interpretations changed.

Palestinian histories remained present.

Some Jews later rejected the triumphant narrative entirely.

The point is simply:

> **historical events can become psychological inheritance without being remembered autobiographically.**

---

## 🕸️ Communal Reinforcement Matters

Self-concept does not develop alone.

If a person's:

- parents;
- grandparents;
- rabbi;
- school;
- youth group;
- synagogue;
- Jewish newspaper;
- communal organisations;
- friends;
- political institutions

all reinforce:

> **Israel protects Jews**

the proposition acquires social proof.

It becomes part of what:

> **people like us know.**

At that point, questioning the proposition can carry not only cognitive cost but social cost.

The person risks:

- disagreement;
- family conflict;
- communal rejection;
- loss of belonging;
- accusations of betrayal;
- isolation;
- uncertainty about Jewish identity.

Atzmi Yisrael can therefore be held by both **internal cognition and external community**.

---

## 📖 This Helps Explain The Long Way To No

For Jews raised inside that architecture, rejecting Zionism can require considerably more than changing a political opinion.

It may require disentangling:

**Jewishness**  
from  
**Medinat Yisrael**

and:

**Jewish safety**  
from  
**state sovereignty**

and:

**Jewish survival**  
from  
**a particular territorial political structure**

and:

**Jewish morality**  
from  
**the moral reputation of a state.**

That can be emotionally brutal.

Which helps explain why some people who eventually arrive at:

> **No. I reject this.**

arrive there very firmly.

They may have already spent years passing through the uncertain middle.

## 🔮 Yisrael She-be'atzmi Offers A Different Place To Stand

Atzmi Yisrael is not the only way Yisrael can exist inside Jewish selfhood.

This cluster distinguishes it from:

**Yisrael she-be'atzmi — ישראל שבעצמי.**

Where Atzmi Yisrael describes:

> **external Israel → incorporated into self**

Yisrael she-be'atzmi describes an interior Jewish inheritance from which the external world can be encountered and judged.

That might include:

- covenant;
- Torah;
- study;
- argument;
- memory;
- responsibility;
- justice;
- peoplehood;
- wrestling;
- obligation towards the stranger;
- relationship with ancestors and descendants.

Now the structure becomes:

**Yisrael she-be'atzmi**  
↓  
**Jewish ethical / intellectual / spiritual commitments**  
↓  
**standards of judgement**  
↓  
**Medinat Yisrael**

This architecture does not require the state to remain morally innocent.

The state can fail.

And something Jewish inside the self can be precisely what requires the person to say so.

## 🪞 Differentiation Does Not Require Emotional Detachment

There is a danger of making the alternative sound emotionally bloodless.

The goal is not:

> **healthy Jews should feel nothing about Israel.**

That would be nonsense.

Someone can:

- love Israel;
- have family there;
- speak Hebrew;
- grieve Israeli deaths;
- fear Israeli destruction;
- feel attached to Eretz Yisrael;
- value Jewish sovereignty;
- retain Zionist commitments

while still differentiating:

**self**  
from  
**state.**

The psychologically important capacity is:

> **something I love can do something wrong without the accusation destroying my ability to know who I am.**

That is differentiation.

It applies to countries.

Families.

Religions.

Movements.

Institutions.

People.

And selves.

---

## 🧿 The Diaspora Paradox

This returns us to the peculiarity of protection.

A diaspora Jew can experience Medinat Yisrael as protective while being protected day-to-day by:

- British law;
- American law;
- French law;
- Canadian law;
- local police;
- workplace protections;
- neighbours;
- civil society;
- anti-racist institutions;
- constitutional rights;
- local Jewish communal infrastructure.

The Israeli state may nevertheless occupy the deeper imagined role:

> **the final guarantor.**

The place available:

> **if everything else fails.**

That means the protective relationship is partly **counterfactual**.

Its psychological force comes precisely from:

> **I hope I never need it.**

That can make the relationship difficult to evaluate empirically.

A system designed for the moment all other protections fail gains significance from an event that, by definition, most diaspora Jews hope never occurs.

---

## 🛡️ The Lifeboat Problem

One metaphor is:

> **Israel as lifeboat.**

You may spend your entire life on the ship.

You may never enter the lifeboat.

You may dislike the lifeboat.

You may criticise its captain.

But knowing it exists can still make you feel safer.

The problem comes when questions about:

- how the lifeboat was built;
- who was displaced to build it;
- who is allowed into it;
- who controls it;
- what the crew does;
- whether it is actually increasing danger around the ship

become interpreted as:

> **you are trying to sink the lifeboat.**

Now the metaphor has begun governing the politics.

The fear can be real.

The metaphor can still be inadequate.

---

## 🌿 Palestinians Cannot Become The Price Of Somebody Else's Felt Safety

This is the moral boundary.

Suppose Medinat Yisrael genuinely makes a diaspora Jew feel safer.

That fact matters psychologically.

It does not answer:

> **what political arrangements may be imposed upon Palestinians to preserve that feeling?**

The emotional necessity of the internal object cannot determine the rights of another population.

Otherwise the structure becomes:

**my historical vulnerability**  
↓  
**creates my need for safety**  
↓  
**my safety requires this political arrangement**  
↓  
**therefore your rights become conditional upon maintaining my psychological security**

That cannot be the endpoint.

Trauma explains needs.

It does not automatically allocate land, sovereignty, citizenship, rights, or coercive power.

## 🏠 A State Is Still A State

This cluster keeps returning to a deliberately boring sentence:

> **Medinat Yisrael is a state.**

That means it can:

- make laws;
- police;
- imprison;
- tax;
- wage war;
- occupy territory;
- negotiate treaties;
- discriminate;
- redistribute resources;
- protect rights;
- violate rights;
- elect governments;
- remove governments;
- make good policy;
- make catastrophic policy.

No amount of psychological meaning removes those functions.

The state may be sacred to somebody.

It remains a state.

The state may represent survival.

It remains a state.

The state may occupy a place inside self-concept.

It remains a state.

And states must remain available for political, moral, historical, and legal evaluation.

## 🧠 A Useful Diagnostic

When criticism of Medinat Yisrael feels overwhelmingly personal, ask:

> **What exactly feels threatened?**

Is it:

- family?
- Israeli civilians?
- Eretz Yisrael?
- Jewish refuge?
- sovereignty?
- Jewish military power?
- the possibility of escape?
- Jewish collective dignity?
- memories of persecution?
- the Shoah?
- the belief that Jews are no longer helpless?
- the moral image of Israel?
- one's own previous political commitments?
- belonging within a Jewish community?
- one's understanding of being Jewish?

The answer may contain several things.

Naming them does not make the threat disappear.

It improves resolution.

---

## 🌉 Meeting On The Bridge

The useful conversation is not:

> **Your attachment to Israel is irrational.**

Nor:

> **If you criticise Israel, you don't understand Jewish safety.**

Try:

> **When you say Israel makes you feel safe, what kind of safety do you mean?**

And:

> **Which part of that safety do you believe requires the present political structure of Medinat Yisrael?**

And:

> **Which criticism of the state feels like criticism of Jews to you, and why?**

And:

> **What happens to your Jewish identity if the state you understand as protective behaves in ways you believe are morally wrong?**

And:

> **Can the thing that symbolises Jewish protection also exercise power that makes another population unsafe?**

Those are difficult questions.

That is the point.

---

## 🧿 The Point

Atzmi Yisrael helps name a phenomenon that otherwise produces confusing political arguments.

Medinat Yisrael can become more than:

> **a state I support.**

It can become:

> **the thing that means Jews survive.**

> **the thing that means Jews can fight back.**

> **the thing that means there is somewhere to go.**

> **the thing that means never again is enforceable.**

> **the thing that proves we are not helpless.**

> **the thing that proves something about us.**

1967 matters because it could make that protective story extraordinarily vivid.

A Jewish population living within living memory of catastrophic powerlessness saw a Jewish state demonstrate overwhelming military agency.

But the same event also expanded Israeli territorial control over Palestinians and inaugurated structures whose political consequences continue to define the conflict.

So the protective Israel and the powerful Israel are not two unrelated objects.

They are sometimes the **same state viewed from opposite relationships to its power**.

That is where the psychological problem becomes politically consequential.

The task is not to mock the need for protection.

Jewish history makes that need very easy to understand.

The task is to make the objects distinguishable enough that:

> **Jewish safety does not require Medinat Yisrael to remain morally innocent.**

And:

> **Medinat Yisrael can be criticised without the Jewish self collapsing with it.**

And, crucially:

> **Palestinian life does not become the price paid to keep somebody else's internal Israel intact.**

That is the bridge.
