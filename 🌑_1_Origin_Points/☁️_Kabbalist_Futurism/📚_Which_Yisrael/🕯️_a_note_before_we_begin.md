# 🕯️ A Note Before We Begin  
**First created:** 2026-08-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-18  
*An invitation to speak more precisely across Jewish disagreement — without allowing Jewish interiority to obscure Palestinian life.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

This cluster is written primarily to a Jewish audience.

And, fundamentally, it is being written because:

**guys, we need to start getting on the same fucking page.**

Not the same political page.

Not the same theological page.

Not the same position on Zionism, Israel, diaspora, statehood, one state, two states, no states, or what Jewish collective life should look like fifty years from now.

We are Jews.

That seems unlikely.

What we need is enough **shared language** to understand what the fuck the other person is actually saying.

Because at the moment, we very often do not.

And the consequences of that failure are no longer confined to an interesting philosophical disagreement.

They matter for Palestinian life.

They matter for Jewish communal life.

They matter for our ability to recognise and respond to antisemitism.

They matter for our ability to distinguish antisemitism from legitimate criticism of a state.

They matter for whether Jews with profoundly different relationships to Israel can remain in conversation with one another at all.

The current situation is not working.

We need to start meeting on the bridge.

---

## 🌉 We Need To Meet On The Bridge

Some Jewish people experience an attack on Israel as an attack upon something profoundly constitutive of Jewish identity, collective safety, historical survival, or belonging.

Some Jewish people do not experience **Medinat Yisrael** as constitutive of their Jewish identity at all.

Some understand **Eretz Yisrael** as deeply important while rejecting the political structure or conduct of Medinat Yisrael.

Some understand **Am Yisrael** as central while rejecting Zionism.

Some understand Zionism principally as Jewish safety, refuge, sovereignty, or self-determination.

Some understand it principally as a political movement whose implementation produced and continues to structure Palestinian dispossession.

Some understand it as several of those things simultaneously.

Some carry an interior, theological, ethical, historical, cultural, linguistic, familial, or philosophical **Yisrael** that cannot adequately be translated into any of those political categories.

And then we attempt to discuss all of this using approximately six English words.

It is not going brilliantly.

We say:

> **Israel.**

And assume everybody knows which Israel.

We say:

> **Zionism.**

And discover three hours later that we have been arguing about different definitions.

We say:

> **That was antisemitic.**

And another Jewish person says:

> **I did not experience that as antisemitic at all.**

Then, too often, instead of asking why, we begin arguing about which Jew possesses the authentic Jewish response.

This is getting us nowhere.

---

## 📚 The Argument Did Not Begin In 2023

There is a temptation in contemporary political discussion to behave as though Jewish disagreement about Israel suddenly appeared after October 2023.

It did not.

It did not begin in 1948 either.

Jewish arguments about **Yisrael**, land, peoplehood, exile, return, sovereignty, Jerusalem, covenant, political authority, messianism, diaspora, collective obligation, assimilation, nationalism, and what it means to remain a people without a state are considerably older than the modern State of Israel.

Modern Zionism entered an argument already in progress.

Zionists then disagreed with other Jews.

Zionists disagreed with Zionists.

Anti-Zionist Jews disagreed with other anti-Zionist Jews.

Religious Jews disagreed about the theological meaning of political return.

Secular Jews disagreed about nationalism.

Diasporists disagreed with people who understood diaspora principally as a condition to be escaped.

Socialists disagreed with nationalists and with other socialists.

And subsequent Jewish generations inherited different combinations of all of it.

So when contemporary Jews appear incapable of producing one stable definition of **Zionism**, that is not necessarily evidence that somebody is being evasive.

Part of the problem is upstream.

We do not all mean the same thing by **Yisrael** either.

---

## 🧠 The Disagreement Is Real

The purpose of this cluster is not to flatten those differences.

Some cannot and should not be reconciled.

There are political disagreements here with material consequences.

There are moral disagreements.

There are theological disagreements.

There are disagreements about history.

There are disagreements about nationalism, sovereignty, safety, diaspora, colonialism, indigeneity, racism, antisemitism and Palestinian liberation.

Shared vocabulary will not magically resolve them.

But it might allow us to identify **where the disagreement actually is**.

That matters.

Because if one person says:

> **Israel is part of me.**

and another says:

> **Israel is a foreign state.**

it is possible that neither has yet explained what they mean by *Israel*.

If one person says:

> **Anti-Zionism attacks something fundamental about Jewish identity.**

and another says:

> **My Jewish identity requires me to be anti-Zionist.**

we have learned something important.

We have not discovered that one of them must secretly not be Jewish enough.

We have discovered that **Jewish people are organising the relationships between self, Yisrael, peoplehood, land, statehood, history, safety and political obligation differently**.

We need language capable of describing that.

---

## 🧭 Which Yisrael?

The parent node to this cluster proposes a working vocabulary.

It distinguishes, among other things:

- **Medinat Yisrael — מדינת ישראל** — the modern State of Israel;
- **Eretz Yisrael — ארץ ישראל** — the Land of Israel;
- **Am Yisrael — עם ישראל** — the People of Israel;
- **Kol Yisrael — כל ישראל** — the Jewish collective / language of collective relationship;
- **Atzmi Yisrael — עצמי ישראל** — an external Israel, particularly Medinat Yisrael, incorporated into psychological self-concept;
- **Yisrael she-be'atzmi — ישראל שבעצמי** — an interior Yisrael from which the self may derive ethical, intellectual, spiritual, covenantal, historical, or interpretive orientation.

The first four terms have histories much larger and more contested than those brief glosses.

The final two are **working Polaris coinages**.

They are not being presented as established categories in Jewish philosophy, rabbinic literature, psychology, psychoanalysis, kabbalah, or Jewish studies.

We are offering terms because we need terms with which to think.

They may prove useful.

They may need refinement.

Someone may produce better language.

Good.

Use the better language.

The objective is not to win naming rights.

The objective is to become capable of telling each other what we actually mean.

---

## ✡️ This Is An Intra-Jewish Intervention

This cluster is therefore, in substantial part, an attempt to say:

> **Can we please explain ourselves to each other better?**

Not because language will eliminate conflict.

Because the current failure of translation is making already profound conflicts considerably worse.

That matters for Jews who are frightened about antisemitism.

It matters for Jews who believe accusations of antisemitism are sometimes being used to suppress legitimate political speech.

It matters for Jews who experience Israel as fundamental to collective Jewish safety.

It matters for Jews who experience the political conflation of Jewishness with Israel as itself dangerous to Jewish safety.

It matters for Jews who remain Zionists but believe Israeli state conduct has become morally intolerable.

It matters for Jews who have rejected Zionism after long periods of intellectual, political, ethical, or religious struggle.

It matters for Jews who have never understood Medinat Yisrael as central to Jewish selfhood in the first place.

Those people need to be capable of speaking to each other.

Not necessarily agreeing.

**Speaking.**

If we cannot distinguish the things we mean by *Yisrael*, we cannot reliably distinguish an attack upon Jews from an attack upon a state, an attack upon Zionism, an attack upon nationalism, an attack upon Israeli policy, an attack upon Jewish peoplehood, or an argument taking place inside Jewish political and philosophical life.

And if we care about antisemitism, that distinction matters enormously.

---

## 🪞 Why Strong Positions Can Have Long Histories

This cluster will also resist a common mistake:

**assuming that the strength of somebody's conclusion tells us how little thought preceded it.**

Sometimes it does.

Sometimes people adopt slogans because slogans are easy.

But sometimes a categorical position is the endpoint of a very long process.

A Jewish person who eventually rejects Zionism may have travelled through:

**inheritance**  
↓  
**attachment**  
↓  
**discomfort**  
↓  
**investigation**  
↓  
**historical confrontation**  
↓  
**Palestinian testimony**  
↓  
**communal conflict**  
↓  
**moral reconstruction**  
↓  
**a settled no**

The same principle applies in other directions.

A person's attachment to Zionism may contain family histories of persecution, expulsion, refuge, vulnerability, migration, antisemitism, the Shoah, or fear about what happens when Jews lack political protection.

Understanding those histories does not require agreeing with every political conclusion derived from them.

Likewise, understanding why somebody eventually rejects a political framework does not require pretending that their conclusion emerged without cost.

Sometimes what looks like absolutism from the outside is **certainty reconstructed after a period of profound uncertainty**.

That distinction deserves to remain visible.

---

## 🗣️ Sometimes Precision Requires Saying No

There is another reason some Jewish political positions become unusually emphatic.

Qualified positions are politically useful.

A person may say:

> **I recognise a profound Jewish historical relationship with the land, but I reject ethnonational supremacy and Palestinian dispossession.**

Someone else may extract:

> **So you support Israel's right to exist.**

The speaker may say:

> **I don't identify as Zionist.**

Someone may translate:

> **Non-Zionist, but broadly supportive.**

The speaker may say:

> **I reject Medinat Yisrael as the proper political expression of Jewish peoplehood.**

Eventually the qualifications become harder to appropriate.

Sometimes:

> **No. I said no.**

is not an absence of nuance.

It is boundary maintenance after nuance has repeatedly been translated into somebody else's political category.

That, too, is worth understanding before deciding that Jews who speak strongly must therefore have thought shallowly.

---

## 🌿 Palestinians Are Not A Footnote To Jewish Self-Concept

There is, however, an asymmetry that this cluster must never disappear.

Much of what follows concerns Jewish interiority.

What does *Yisrael* mean?

How does Jewish identity develop?

Why do Jews understand Zionism differently?

How can Medinat Yisrael become incorporated into self-concept?

What relationships with Yisrael exist independently of the modern state?

Why can Jewish people encounter the same political statement and experience it radically differently?

Those are legitimate questions.

But Palestinians are not supporting characters in a Jewish philosophical argument.

They are not a thought experiment through which Jews discover ourselves.

They are not the difficult final chapter of an argument between Zionist and anti-Zionist Jews.

They are people.

And while Jews conduct extraordinarily complicated arguments about what Israel means to us, **Palestinians bear material consequences from political projects built around particular answers to that question**.

Questions about land, sovereignty, statehood, settlement, borders, security and Jewish collective self-determination are not merely philosophical.

They concern Palestinian homes.

Palestinian families.

Palestinian land.

Palestinian movement.

Palestinian political rights.

Palestinian safety.

Palestinian displacement.

Palestinian imprisonment.

Palestinian injury.

Palestinian life.

There is therefore something inherently uncomfortable about spending thousands of words asking:

> **What does Israel mean to us?**

while another necessary question remains:

> **What has the attempt to make particular meanings of Israel materially real meant for Palestinians?**

That discomfort should remain visible.

---

## 🏠 When Philosophy Acquires Property Deeds

This is particularly important because Jewish relationships with land can be real without answering every political question about land.

A place can be:

sacred;

ancestral;

historically important;

linguistically embedded;

remembered in prayer;

present in scripture;

central to collective imagination;

carried through exile;

and profoundly meaningful across generations.

None of those facts automatically answers:

> **Who currently lives in this house?**

Once philosophical homeland becomes territorial political programme, abstraction encounters material reality.

Who controls the land?

Who owns the house?

Who has citizenship?

Who can return?

Who cannot return?

Who controls movement?

Who controls borders?

Who possesses coercive power?

Who is displaced?

Who is protected by the state?

Who is protected from the state?

At that point, the philosophical meaning of homeland cannot be analysed separately from the mechanisms through which homeland is made materially real.

This is where this cluster must encounter Palestinian history and arguments about settler colonialism directly rather than treating either as vocabulary problems at the edge of a Jewish discussion.

---

## 🌿 Jewish Interiority Cannot Erase Palestinian Exterior Reality

So yes:

this cluster necessarily centres Jewish experience in one important sense.

It is trying to address an **intra-Jewish language problem**.

Some of the philosophical distinctions involved are difficult to express in English.

Some draw upon Hebrew terminology, Jewish textual traditions, histories of exile and diaspora, theological concepts, collective memory, and forms of Jewish self-understanding that require explanation before they can be discussed properly.

A non-Jewish reader should therefore expect substantial Jewish interiority here.

But Jewish interiority cannot become an excuse for Palestinian disappearance.

Again and again, this cluster must return to:

> **And what does this mean for Palestinians?**

Because Palestinians should not have to bear the cost while Jews take another generation to decide what we mean by Israel.

---

## 🕸️ This Account Will Not Be Jewish Enough Yet Either

There is another limitation that needs stating explicitly.

A discussion of Zionism and Jewish political identity conducted principally through European and Ashkenazi Jewish intellectual history can reproduce **Ashkenormativity** even while attempting to complicate Jewish identity.

That is not good enough either.

Mizrahi, Sephardi, Maghrebi, Middle Eastern, North African, Ethiopian, Indian, Central Asian, and other Jewish histories cannot simply be appended as variations upon an implicitly Ashkenazi baseline.

Their relationships with land, Arab societies, Muslim societies, European colonial power, migration, Zionism, Israeli state formation, language, religion, diaspora and Jewish peoplehood have their own histories.

Those histories may destabilise categories developed elsewhere in this cluster.

Good.

**The categories should be capable of being destabilised.**

A framework that can survive only by excluding Jewish experiences that complicate it is not a very useful framework for explaining Jews.

## 🕸️ A Note To Non-Jewish Readers

You are welcome in this conversation.

But some of what follows is deliberately an argument **among Jews**.

That distinction matters.

Jewish disagreement does not require non-Jewish readers to locate **the Jew who agrees with them** and use that person as authentication.

Please do not do that.

Do not select whichever Jewish position most closely resembles your existing politics and promote it as the authentic Jewish position.

Do not assume the most institutionally powerful Jewish organisation represents every Jew.

Do not assume the most anti-Zionist Jew represents every Jew either.

Do not turn Jewish disagreement into an authenticity contest.

Do not mistake Jewish plurality for evidence that antisemitism cannot be identified.

Do not mistake the existence of antisemitism for evidence that every Jewish political interpretation of Israel must therefore be accepted.

Do not treat attachment to Israel as evidence of foreign allegiance.

Do not treat rejection of Zionism as evidence of deficient Jewishness.

And do not outsource political judgement to identity.

Sometimes Jewish people genuinely disagree.

Sometimes the most useful thing an ally can do is understand **what the disagreement actually is** without immediately attempting to resolve it on our behalf.

## 🧿 This Will Be Imperfect

This cluster is being written because it needs to be written.

That does not mean it is finished.

It does not mean this is the perfect vocabulary.

It does not mean this is the definitive account of Jewish relationships with Yisrael.

It does not speak for every Jew.

It does not yet centre Palestinian scholarship, testimony, history and experience as fully as the subject requires.

It does not yet decentre Ashkenormative accounts of Jewish history, identity, Zionism, anti-Zionism, diaspora and relationship with land as thoroughly as it should.

It will miss thinkers.

It will miss traditions.

It will contain terminology that needs refinement.

It may contain arguments that need revision.

That is not a reason to disguise the limitations.

And it is not a reason not to begin.

**Atzmi Yisrael** and **Yisrael she-be'atzmi** are working terms.

If better language emerges, use the better language.

If the architecture fails somewhere, document where it fails.

If Palestinian scholarship demonstrates that our Jewish framing has obscured something material, **the framing should change**.

If Sephardi, Mizrahi, Maghrebi, Ethiopian, Indian, Central Asian or other Jewish histories destabilise assumptions built from overwhelmingly Ashkenazi material, **the assumptions should be destabilised**.

If another Jewish thinker points out that an argument represented here as new has actually been happening for eight hundred years:

excellent.

Add the footnote.

This is a living cluster.

---

## 🧭 Complexity Is Not A Reason To Say Nothing

There is an obvious alternative to publishing an imperfect account of a difficult subject:

keep waiting.

Wait until every historical argument has been resolved.

Wait until every Jewish experience is adequately represented.

Wait until Palestinian experience has been incorporated perfectly.

Wait until the terminology cannot possibly be misunderstood.

Wait until every relevant thinker has been read.

Wait until every Jew agrees what Zionism means.

At that point, nobody writes anything.

So this cluster adopts a different rule:

> **Say what can presently be said carefully. Mark what remains incomplete. Preserve disagreement. Correct errors. Add missing voices. Revise the architecture when the evidence requires it.**

Publication is not a declaration of completion.

It is a commitment to continued revision.

---

## 🕯️ Why Publish It Now?

Because complexity cannot become another mechanism for postponement.

The present situation is not working.

Not for Palestinian life.

Not for Jewish communal life.

Not for serious attempts to confront antisemitism.

Not for people trying to distinguish antisemitism from legitimate criticism of Israel.

Not for Jews trying to explain why something another Jew experiences as existentially threatening does not land that way for them at all.

Not for Jews whose concern about antisemitism is dismissed because other people have encountered bad-faith accusations of antisemitism.

Not for Jews whose criticism of Israel is dismissed because another Jewish person's self-concept places Israel somewhere fundamentally different.

Not for any serious attempt to work together.

We do not have to reach the same conclusion.

But if we actually want to have the conversation, we need enough shared language to explain our starting positions.

We need to be able to say:

> **This is what I mean when I say Israel.**

> **This is what I mean when I say Zionism.**

> **This is what I experience as an attack upon Jewishness.**

> **This is why I do not experience that statement in the same way you do.**

> **This is the thing I am trying to protect.**

> **This is the thing I believe is being harmed.**

And then we need to listen closely enough to discover whether we are actually arguing about the same thing.

Because somewhere underneath an enormous amount of contemporary Jewish conflict sits an unexpectedly basic question:

> **When you say Yisrael, which Yisrael do you mean?**

We should probably start there.
