**And because I need somewhere to “hear me out” re Fagin and Shylock.**

Chloe thinks these are two really, really fucking good characters. The fact that they come to us through antisemitic literary and theatrical inheritances does not mean we must flatten them into examples in a prejudice-awareness presentation. The writing, the performance histories, the actors who found something genuinely human in them, and the arguments they provoke all deserve closer attention.

This is not a claim that antisemitism explains every British prejudice. Anti-Irish discrimination, colonial racism, class contempt and other forms of exclusion have histories of their own. But the mechanisms can intersect, leak into one another, and help us ask better questions across those histories **without talking over them**.

The point is not to throw away the canon. The point is to read it with our eyes open, and enjoy the good bits with considerably more understanding.

## 🎭 1. Fagin Is Not Just the Caricature

Fagin in Dickens's *Oliver Twist* is shaped by an explicitly antisemitic literary inheritance. The stage and screen Fagin carries further layers: performance traditions, physical and vocal choices, adaptations, and the audience's own expectations. Those layers are not interchangeable. In particular, Lionel Bart's *Oliver!* creates opportunities for interiority that should be examined on their own terms, without pretending that the musical has somehow erased the original problem.

The question is not merely **“Is Fagin an antisemitic stereotype?”** We need to ask:

- What was written, and in which historical conditions?
- Which conventions did later performers inherit, repeat, resist, soften, or turn inside out?
- When is an actor showing us a recognisable *type*, and when are they allowing a person to think?
- What does the audience laugh at? What else might it notice while laughing?
- Which interpretations have become British cultural common sense, and who gets to revise them?

An actor who knows their shit can hold the stereotype in view while revealing a person with tastes, fears, ingenuity, loneliness, moral contradictions and a perfectly ordinary fondness for shiny things.

**“My collection of trinkets do be cool tho.”**

The man is allowed to like his trinkets. He does not need to be a saint to deserve a full interior life.

## 🎶 2. *Reviewing the Situation*: Where the Actor Has to Earn It

The revelation songs are often where a character becomes most visible. In *Reviewing the Situation*, Fagin argues with himself in public. A weaker performance can deliver a sequence of comic reversals; a remarkable one makes each imagined alternative genuinely occur to him, seduce him briefly, and then acquire a cost.

This is why the number feels so transformative. The actor must understand what Fagin wants, what he fears, what he knows about the world, and how quickly a hopeful proposition can collapse under the weight of experience.

The song begins with a plea for moral complexity: a man has a heart, even if he has not been a saint. That is not a declaration of innocence. It is a refusal of the idea that a person's entire moral identity can be exhausted by the darkest account of him.

The character then examines possible futures, each with its own attractions and liabilities:

| Imagined alternative | What attracts him | What it might cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Marriage | Companionship, care, domestic stability | Independence, peace, control of money and time |
| High society | Recognition, comfort, display, access to prestige | Deference, unfamiliar codes, exclusion, exposure to authority |
| Respectable acquaintances | Contacts who can open doors | The painful discovery that his own people do not count as suitable contacts |
| Honest employment | Legitimacy, earnings, possible security | A boss, surveillance, fixed hours, dependence on someone else's approval |
| Remaining as he is | Familiarity, community, autonomy, possessions, competence | Danger, prosecution, precarity, loneliness, ageing without protection |

**He is not choosing between an obviously bad package and an obviously good package. He is comparing two deeply imperfect benefits packages.** Some costs are immediate and certain; some promised gains are speculative. An opportunity to advance is not the same as a guaranteed improvement.

And the actor has to let us watch the calculation change his face.

## 🪞 3. The Refrain as a Feedback Loop

The repeated decision to think again is funny. It is also the shape of the trap.

1. Fagin imagines a new life.
2. He identifies a genuine benefit.
3. He tests that future against what he knows about institutions, people and survival.
4. The anticipated costs become visible.
5. He returns to the existing arrangement, which is dangerous but legible.
6. The underlying conditions remain largely unchanged.

**Environment → learned survival model → imagined alternative → anticipated cost → inhibited change → continued environment.**

This can be read cybernetically, but we should not reduce it to a claim that Fagin is irrationally holding himself back. His assessment may be quite sensible given the alternatives available. A feedback loop can be self-reinforcing **because the external constraints are real**.

His imagination is not poor. He can imagine an astonishing range of futures. His problem is that he cannot identify a reliably survivable route into them.

**He can imagine becoming a different person before he can imagine how that person would survive.**

## 🕸️ 4. The Price of Legitimacy Is Belonging

One of the song's sharpest turns comes when Fagin considers finding respectable connections. His companions are the people he already knows; the people he knows are not the people who can certify his entry into respectable society.

This is the double bind:

- His existing relationships supply familiarity, social knowledge, practical support and a place in the world.
- Those same relationships may be treated as disqualifying evidence by the institutions whose approval he needs.
- He may have to abandon solidarity and social capital **before** receiving any replacement.
- The new world may still refuse him.

**The community that helped him survive can become the very thing he is expected to leave behind to be considered legitimate.**

There is a powerful resonance here with histories of Jewish emancipation, assimilation and conditional acceptance. But the song does **not** establish that Fagin's criminal associates are a Jewish community, and that distinction matters. The reading is a comparison of mechanisms, not a claim that the fictional gang stands in for Jewish communal life.

It is also a portal into other histories of respectability and exclusion: class mobility, migration, anti-Irish prejudice, racialised professional gatekeeping, and the costs of leaving a community behind. Those histories need their own evidence and specificity.

## 🏛️ 5. Bosses, Magistrates, and the People You're Supposed to Bow To

Watch what good actors do when Fagin imagines the respectable world. The sighs. The irritation. The flicker of distaste at having to defer to a boss, a magistrate, or some man whose social position grants him authority regardless of whether Fagin considers him worthy of respect.

**Institutional authority and personal merit are not the same thing.**

Fagin is resourceful. He organises people. He understands incentives and social relationships. In his own precarious sphere he exercises a form of autonomy and authority. Respectable employment could require him to surrender that autonomy to somebody whose principal qualification is that the system recognises him.

The imagined greeting to the magistrate is particularly good theatre. A figure associated with the power to judge or punish him becomes, in fantasy, somebody he might greet as a social equal. Then the whole proposition becomes absurd, alarming, or both.

And Fagin still rather fancies the rewards of the hierarchy: the rooms, the carriages, the status, the shiny things. He can dislike the rituals of deference while enjoying the prospect of the privileges.

**For a few minutes, we stop simply looking at Fagin through respectable Britain's eyes and start looking at respectable Britain through Fagin's.**

## 💎 6. Shiny Things, Poverty, and Consumer Capitalism

There is a deeply human beat underneath all this structural analysis:

> “Idk man. I kinda like the shiny things.”

That is not a minor joke. It is a refusal to make marginalised people into morally perfect economic subjects who must never enjoy anything frivolous.

Fagin has preferences. He likes possessions. Some things delight him for no grand reason at all. His pleasures may also bind him to ways of living that carry risks. Both can be true.

The antisemitic trope of the acquisitive Jew makes this especially revealing. British respectable society has often condemned Jewish attachment to money or objects while treating property, wealth and accumulation among the socially powerful as ambition, achievement or good breeding. The question is not whether every instance is equivalent; it is **whose desire is made respectable, and whose is made suspect**.

And then the absurd modern thought experiment:

**Fagin versus Temu. Nightmares.**

“Fagin, why are there forty-seven parcels?”

“Little things.”

“Why have you bought a mechanical frog that plays the violin?”

“**LOOK AT HIM.**”

He would be an early adopter. He would understand referrals, incentives, discount stacking, and the pleasure of getting a little something for almost nothing. The Artful Dodger would be managing the affiliate links by lunchtime.

The joke has an analytical point: modern retail systems can deliberately optimise for attention, novelty, repeated acquisition and the pleasure of a bargain. The individual magpie impulse is no longer just an individual quirk; it can be cultivated at industrial scale.

## 🕯️ 7. The Morality Arrives Before the Route Out

One of the song's most moving contradictions is that Fagin can insist he will remain what he is while also expressing discomfort with harm done on his behalf and the danger of the life he leads.

His declared identity and his developing moral objections do not line up neatly.

That is more interesting than a simple redemption arc. He does not have to transform into a respectable model citizen by the end of the number. The audience can instead witness an unresolved human being, trying to decide which sacrifices are bearable and which future is possible.

The actor's job is not to make every reversal into cowardice. Sometimes it is apprehension. Sometimes disappointment. Sometimes delight. Sometimes a perfectly justified dislike of bosses. Sometimes grief at the thought of losing everyone he knows.

**The responsibility for choosing is placed on the individual; the conditions of the choice are not of his making.**

And society may blame him whichever way it goes.

## ⚖️ 8. Shylock Is Not a Footnote to Fagin

Shylock needs his own close reading, not an automatic transfer of conclusions from *Oliver!* to *The Merchant of Venice*. The works are separated by genre, period, authorship, and dramatic machinery. What they share is their place in a British cultural inheritance in which Jewish figures are repeatedly asked to carry anxieties about money, law, belonging and the legitimacy of their claims.

Working questions for the Shylock node:

- How does Shylock speak about injury, reciprocity, law and the conditions under which he is recognised as human?
- What does the Venetian legal order promise him, and what happens when he attempts to claim its protections?
- How do religious power, mercantile power, gender, performance and legal interpretation collide in the courtroom?
- How have different actors handled Shylock's anger, intelligence, dignity, cruelty, vulnerability and refusal to be easily reconciled?
- How does Portia's performance of legal authority alter the power relation onstage?
- What happens when the audience is invited to celebrate a reversal of power that also carries devastating consequences for Shylock?
- What can a Jewish reader find in the character beyond either apologising for him or treating him solely as a specimen of antisemitism?

And yes: **the Portia–Shylock encounter is charged with extraordinary theatrical power dynamics.** Authority, disguise, performance, coercion, humiliation, reversal, and the intensely staged business of who may command whom. A deliberately provocative reading may find that *kinky as shit*. That is an interpretive provocation about the scene's dynamics, **not** a claim that the play establishes a consensual erotic relationship between them.

We are allowed to have fun with the interpretive possibilities while remaining exact about what the text does.

## 🎟️ 9. What the Performance Adds

A character exists at several levels at once:

- **Written:** the language, actions, plot, silences and constraints of the original work.
- **Adapted:** what a composer, librettist, screenwriter or director adds or changes.
- **Performed:** voice, timing, gesture, breath, humour, casting, physicality, stillness and the actor's chosen interior logic.
- **Taught:** what classrooms, criticism, programmes and popular explanations tell audiences to notice.
- **Remembered:** the residue that becomes a social trope even among people who have never read the original.

A performer can do more than reproduce the received caricature. They can make an audience feel the cost of a choice, the pleasure of an object, the indignity of enforced deference, or the moment a character catches himself wanting something he cannot quite have.

We should give those actors, musicians, writers and directors their due. Their labour is part of the history of the character, not an ornamental afterthought.

**The laughter does not interrupt the humanity. Sometimes it is how the humanity gets through.**

## 🌍 10. Why This Matters Beyond the Stage

These readings are not intended to prove that antisemitism is the foundation of all British racism. That would erase too much, including the distinct histories of anti-Irish discrimination, empire, anti-Black racism, anti-Muslim prejudice and class oppression.

But the study of one mechanism can help illuminate another: conditional belonging; the price of respectability; suspicion attached to outsiders' wealth; the differential legitimacy of identical behaviours; pressure to renounce a community; and the assumption that the excluded person must solve the exclusion by making a better personal choice.

The comparisons are **portals, not equivalences**. Each new conversation needs its own evidence.

Nor should education about anti-Jewish hatred require every account of Jewish identity to revolve around Zionism or the State of Israel. British Jewish history, diasporic culture, literature, language, class, religious diversity and secular Jewish life provide abundant ways to teach Jewish humanity and complexity on their own terms. Contemporary politics can be discussed where relevant without becoming the compulsory centre of every Jewish story.

**Read more closely, not enjoy less.**

## 🧰 11. Nodes and Questions to Develop

These are research and drafting prompts, **not** claims already demonstrated:

- **Fagin on stage:** compare performances of *Reviewing the Situation*, especially changes in timing, vocal colour, self-amusement, fear and contempt for authority. Record cast, production and date before making claims about a particular actor.
- **Dickens versus Bart:** trace precisely what *Oliver!* inherits, revises, softens or amplifies from *Oliver Twist*.
- **Shylock and Portia:** close-read the trial scene alongside contrasting productions; distinguish the written legal logic from the staging of power.
- **Respectability as an exchange:** map who has to surrender which relationships to become institutionally legible.
- **Theatrical Jewishness:** separate recognisable Jewish traditions of argument and self-questioning from lazy assertions about what Jewish people are inherently like.
- **British schoolroom transmission:** examine what audiences were taught to feel about these characters, not merely what they were assigned to read.
- **Consumer capitalism's magpie:** the difference between individual desire, stereotype, and systems designed to monetise attention.
- **Cross-discrimination portals:** anti-Irish, class and colonial comparisons with careful attention to where the analogies break.
- **Jewish futures beyond state-centred framing:** ways of teaching and imagining Jewish cultural life without reducing it to a modern nation-state.

Keep the evidence tiers distinct: **textual observation; performance observation; historical context; interpretive argument; joke**. A good joke can reveal a mechanism without becoming evidence by itself.

## 🎭 Postscript: Hear Me Meowt

Look. All of this matters. British cultural inheritance matters. Antisemitism matters. How we teach, perform, remember and reinterpret these characters matters.

But perhaps I would also really, really wants you to listen to me “hear me out” about Shylock and Fagin.  

Because they are two really fucking good characters.

And some actors have done extraordinary work making them vivid, difficult, funny, frightening, intelligent, tender, maddening and painfully human. We do not always give those performers, or the artists who adapted these works, the tip of the hat they deserve.

We do not need to pretend the ugly inheritance is absent. We also do not need to surrender the brilliant art made in and around it.

And, while we're here, the Portia and Shylock dialogic?

I am shocked, quite frankly, that the GOP haven’t banned this for children.  

We are going to have to talk about it. Because apparently British literary education expected us to sit through all that and then calmly discuss symbolism.

Absolutely not. We have questions.
