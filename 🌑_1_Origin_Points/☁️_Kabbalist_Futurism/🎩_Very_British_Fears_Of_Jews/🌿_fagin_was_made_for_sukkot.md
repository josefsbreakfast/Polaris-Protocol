# 🌿 Fagin Was Made for Sukkot
**First created:** 2026-10-08 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-08  
*An extravagantly decorated temporary dwelling, eighteen chandeliers, a mechanical frog, and one Jewish gentleman who would like Britain to stop inspecting the premises.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation — HE WAS BORN TO DECORATE A SUKKAH

I have recently been considering Fagin, from Lionel Bart's *Oliver!*, as a Jewish man negotiating the terms of British respectability. The resulting conclusion was that Britain has offered him a contract under which he assumes all the obligations and Britain retains all the discretion.

Fagin, reasonably enough, is reviewing the situation.

But there is another matter. A matter of considerable cultural and decorative importance.

**Fagin's Sukkot would be absolutely fucking banging.**

The man is a collector. The man has networks. The man appreciates a shiny thing. The man has spent years organising people, objects and living arrangements under circumstances not exactly blessed with planning permission.

**HE WAS BORN TO DECORATE A SUKKAH.**

And beneath the silliness is a question worth keeping: what happens when a man whose relationship to respectable British society is conditional gets to build a temporary Jewish space where he is not applying for admission? Where, instead, he is the host?

This is an affectionate literary imagining, not a claim that Dickens or Bart wrote a Sukkot episode, nor an attempt to make Fagin an uncomplicated representative of Jewish communal life. His exploitation of children remains exploitation. His decorative instincts, however, are beyond dispute in this entirely imaginary scenario.

---

## 🪙 1. The Sukkah of Questionable Provenance

The man has been collecting decorations for sixty years and has finally found a religiously sanctioned occasion to display **EVERY SINGLE ONE OF THEM**.

> 🎩🕎: I've acquired some lovely decorations.
>
> 🧒: Where from?
>
> 🎩🕎: Don't trouble yourself with the details, my dear.

There are garlands. There are fruits. There are lanterns. There are fabrics of indeterminate origin, embroidered cushions, polished objects, a small decorative bird, and a frankly ambitious number of reflective surfaces.

He is absolutely the sort of man who would begin with *a few modest touches* and finish with a structure that can be located from the Thames by its festive glow.

> 🎩🕎: Behold my sukkah!
>
> 🧒: It's got seventeen chandeliers.
>
> 🎩🕎: Eighteen, darling. **Chai.**
>
> 🧒: Is that a grandfather clock?
>
> 🎩🕎: Festive.
>
> 🧒: Why is there a mechanical frog playing the violin?
>
> 🎩🕎: **WHY WOULDN'T THERE BE?**

Eighteen, of course, evokes *chai*, the Hebrew word for life, conventionally associated with the number eighteen through gematria. It does not establish a religious obligation to hang eighteen chandeliers in a sukkah. I am sure this clarification will be a tremendous disappointment to Fagin.

The mechanical frog has no theological justification whatsoever. It is simply excellent.

If this man had access to contemporary online shopping, the parcels would reach the ceiling. The Artful Dodger would have an affiliate programme. Somebody would have to intervene before the delivery of a second ornamental fountain.

**LOOK AT HIM.**

---

## 🌿 2. Unfortunately, the Roof Has Rules

Sukkot is a Jewish festival associated with the harvest and with the temporary shelters of the wilderness journey. A *sukkah* is a temporary structure in which people traditionally eat, gather, and, depending on practice and circumstances, sleep during the festival. The roof covering, *s'chach*, is central to its religious character.

It is not merely a shed with festive branding.

There are actual rules governing the construction of a kosher sukkah, including requirements concerning its walls and roofing. The roofing is traditionally made from suitable detached plant material. There are differing details and interpretations within Jewish law and practice; this is not a construction manual. One often hears that you should be able to see the stars through the roof, but that is not a universal literal test of validity.

Which is a pity, because Fagin has already drawn up plans for a chandelier gallery.

> 🎩🕎: Eighteen chandeliers.
>
> 🧒: But the roof has to let us see the sky.
>
> 🎩🕎: Then we shall arrange them tastefully *beneath* it.
>
> 🧒: And the grandfather clock?
>
> 🎩🕎: Essential.
>
> 🧒: To Sukkot?
>
> 🎩🕎: To the atmosphere, darling.

To be clear, this is a joke about Fagin's enthusiasm, not practical guidance on what is safe or permissible to suspend from a sukkah. Eighteen heavy chandeliers and an improvised roof are an excellent way to make the local authorities' objections suddenly rather more persuasive.

**The rabbi has been invited to inspect the roof. Fagin is trying to distract him with the frog.**

---

## 🏠 3. A Temporary Home Is Still a Home

Now for the part that makes the joke more than an inventory of stolen candlesticks.

Fagin's musical self-examination revolves around security: marriage, work, old age, money, status, possessions, the people he knows, and the risk of abandoning the only world in which he understands how to survive.

Sukkot makes temporary shelter visible rather than hiding it. A sukkah is not supposed to impersonate a permanent mansion. Its impermanence is part of the point.

That does not mean precarious housing is romantic. It certainly does not mean people should be grateful for poverty because a religious festival has found meaning in temporary shelter. Ritual vulnerability and involuntary insecurity are not equivalent experiences.

But there is a fruitful distinction here between **a dwelling's permanence** and **the experience of belonging inside it**.

A home is not only the building. It is also the people who recognise you, the food shared at the table, the practices that make the space intelligible, and the ability to say, without asking an institution to certify your suitability: *come in*.

Fagin's imagined respectable future asks him to become somebody acceptable to other people's rooms. His imagined Sukkot gives him a room—well, an extremely enthusiastic temporary structure—into which he can invite other people.

**The host and the applicant are not the same social position.**

Nor is hospitality a cure for structural exclusion. A beautiful festival does not supply secure employment, housing, protection from antisemitism, or restitution to the children Fagin has exploited. But it can show a different relationship between dignity and admission: belonging enacted through community, rather than deferred until an external gatekeeper approves the paperwork.

---

## 🍋 4. The Guest List Is Extensive and the Etrog Is Under Supervision

Sukkot is also associated with hospitality, including the tradition of *ushpizin*, welcoming symbolic ancestral guests, and the very ordinary, very important practice of having actual people round.

This is where Fagin would become insufferable in the most entertaining possible way.

The entire neighbourhood has been invited. Food everywhere. Somebody is playing music. Children are running about. Fagin is proudly demonstrating a mechanical frog to a person who has already seen it four times.

> 🎩🕎: A guest! Come in, come in. Have something to eat.
>
> 🧒: He said that to the last six people.
>
> 🎩🕎: And was I wrong?

There are the four species—*lulav*, *etrog*, myrtle and willow—used in Sukkot ritual. Fagin would absolutely admire the etrog as an object, although admiring an expensive citrus fruit is not the same as understanding its ritual use.

> 🧒: You can't put the etrog in the trinket cabinet.
>
> 🎩🕎: I was merely considering its presentation.
>
> 🧒: You were pricing a velvet cushion.
>
> 🎩🕎: **A man may appreciate craftsmanship.**

A note on the children: it is tempting to turn Fagin into a cuddly neighbourhood grandfather because the musical allows him charm. We must not use a festival of hospitality to launder his treatment of children in the original story. The imaginary Sukkot works best if we keep that discomfort visible. His talent for gathering people is real; whether he uses it to care for them or exploit them is a moral question, not a decorative one.

**Community is not automatically benign just because it is community.**

---

## 🇬🇧 5. Britain Reviews the Building Regulations

Naturally, respectable Britain has arrived.

Not with a dish. Not with a bottle of anything. With observations.

> 🇬🇧: Your dwelling appears insufficiently permanent.
>
> 🎩🕎: **THAT'S THE POINT, DARLING.**

Britain has concerns about the roof, the ornaments, the guests, the unconventional use of space, and the gentleman's failure to exhibit an appropriate level of gratitude for being permitted to exist within the national landscape.

> 🇬🇧: Is this quite structurally appropriate?
>
> 🎩🕎: **YOU'RE WELCOME TO REVIEW THE SITUATION FROM OUTSIDE.**

Which is not to say that genuine safety rules are a form of antisemitism. They are not. Nobody gets to suspend a dangerous chandelier over somebody's head in the name of diasporic cultural expression.

The satire concerns something else: the habit of treating Jewish difference itself as a suspicious departure from the respectable norm, even when the practice has its own coherent meanings and traditions.

> 🇬🇧: We expect you to behave respectably.
>
> 🎩🕎: Naturally. And what do I receive in return?
>
> 🇬🇧: The opportunity to be considered respectable.
>
> 🎩🕎: Considered?
>
> 🇬🇧: Subject to certain conditions.
>
> 🎩🕎: Ah. So you've offered me a contract in which I assume all the obligations and you retain all the discretion.
>
> 🇬🇧: That's rather an uncharitable interpretation.
>
> 🎩🕎: **I'M REVIEWING THE SITUATION.**

The difference now is that Fagin has a table, guests, decorations, and a festival to get on with. Britain may continue conducting its assessment. It need not be the principal event of the evening.

## 🕎 6. Not an Assimilation Exam, Not a Redemption Arc

There is a danger in giving an excluded fictional character a lovely communal festival and announcing that the problem has been solved.

It has not.

Fagin remains Fagin. The historical caricature of the Jewish criminal does not vanish because we have imagined him hanging lanterns. Dickens's Fagin and Bart's Fagin remain different literary constructions, and neither should be mistaken for a representative portrait of actual Jewish men.

Nor is there one correct way to celebrate Sukkot. Jewish observance varies by tradition, community, household, resources, climate, disability, housing circumstances and personal practice. Not everybody can build or use a sukkah. A person's Jewish belonging is not measured by the quality of their roof, the size of their guest list or their capacity to buy an absurd number of chandeliers.

That matters particularly for a character whose whole song is about being told what sort of person he would have to become before he might be accepted.

**The sukkah should not become another examination.**

What makes the thought experiment affectionate is not that Fagin has suddenly passed a test of Jewish authenticity. It is that we can imagine him encountering a cultural setting in which his love of objects, his social ingenuity, his humour and his delight in hosting might be put to better uses.

That is not absolution. It is the possibility of redirecting capacities that already exist.

And, crucially, the people around him still get to say no when his ideas are terrible.

> 🧒: We are not hanging the grandfather clock from the roof.
>
> 🎩🕎: I see that you have all become very difficult.
>
> 🧒: It's a *clock*.
>
> 🎩🕎: A *beautiful* clock.

## 👑 7. The Gentleman Will Be Keeping His Trinkets

The companion argument in *Fagin Reviews the Situation* is that a humane social order must offer more than conditional acceptance in exchange for surrendering everything familiar. This Sukkot fantasy approaches the same question from the other direction.

What does belonging look like when it is made through shared practice rather than granted by a suspicious institution?

It might look like a temporary structure with a sensible roof and entirely unreasonable decorations. It might look like a table full of people, an elderly Jewish man delighted by his possessions, and somebody gently informing him that the mechanical frog cannot lead the blessings.

It might look like a place where impermanence is acknowledged without treating the people inside as disposable.

And it might look like a British official standing outside with a clipboard, trying to work out why nobody has asked for permission to enjoy themselves.

> 🇬🇧: We do have further questions about the premises.
>
> 🎩🕎: Of course you do.
>
> 🇬🇧: When will this structure be taken down?
>
> 🎩🕎: After the festival.
>
> 🇬🇧: So you admit it isn't permanent?
>
> 🎩🕎: **My dear, you are very close to understanding Sukkot.**
>
> 🧒: Can we eat now?
>
> 🎩🕎: At last, somebody with a sensible question.

**Fagin's Sukkot. Five stars. No notes.**

Except, possibly, a note from the rabbi about the chandeliers.

🌿🪙 **The gentleman will be keeping his trinkets.**
