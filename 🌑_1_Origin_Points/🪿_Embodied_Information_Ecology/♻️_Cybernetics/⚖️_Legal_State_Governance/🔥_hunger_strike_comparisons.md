# 🔥 Hunger Strike Comparisons
**First created:** 2025-12-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-07  
*What hunger strikes reveal about embodied counter-sovereignty, spectacular coercion, democratic legitimacy, historical memory, and the security cost of making equal treatment under law difficult to see.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

This node compares hunger strikes across different political contexts — including suffragette imprisonment, internment and the H-Blocks in Northern Ireland, the 1981 Irish republican hunger strike, and contemporary Palestine Action-associated remand prisoners — to identify **repeating governance responses without pretending the cases are identical**.

The comparison is structural, not moral.

It does not require every prisoner to have the same politics, every government to have the same intention, every emergency power to have the same legal basis, or every historical period to be flattened into one enormous British administrative soup.

It asks a narrower question:

> **What happens when a person held by the state has very few channels left through which to refuse the state's account of what is happening to them, and turns their own metabolism into the remaining communications system?**

The state then has choices. It can respond to the signal. It can negotiate. It can suppress it. It can reinterpret it. It can transfer responsibility back onto the prisoner. It can wait.

And sometimes it waits until the body itself becomes the spectacle.

That is where this stops being only a prison-governance problem.

It becomes a problem of **democratic legitimacy, historical memory, public authentication, deterrence, and national resilience**.

---

## ✨ Key Features

- Hunger strikes as last-resort embodied political speech.
- The body as a communications channel when ordinary channels are constrained.
- Starvation reframed as voluntary choice so institutional responsibility can be displaced.
- Delay presented as administrative neutrality.
- Mockery and delegitimation as precursors to tolerable harm.
- Internment and exceptional procedure as part of the receiving history, not a claim that every later case is identical.
- Bobby Sands and the 1981 hunger strike as living political memory, particularly in Ireland and Irish diasporic communities.
- Palestinian and Palestine-solidarity communities receiving contemporary detention through their own histories of imprisonment, occupation and political resistance.
- The distinction between **state intent** and **communicative effect**.
- The spectacle of coercion as deterrence: showing everybody else how expensive resistance can become.
- The point at which deterrent spectacle reverses and the suffering body instead displays the inflexibility of the state.
- Wealth, status and legal capital as part of the public comparison set.
- Procedural legitimacy as security infrastructure.
- The internet as a machine for making inconvenient historical comparisons available in real time.
- The small but constitutionally vital proposal for **🔥 Arrest the Prince Day**.

## 🪻 Suffragettes: The Body Refuses The Category

Hunger striking by imprisoned suffragettes was not simply refusal of food.

It contested the category the state had imposed upon them.

The state could say **criminal**.

The prisoner could say **political prisoner**.

The prison could regulate movement, clothing, correspondence, association, sleep, work and food.

The prisoner's remaining refusal was bodily.

Force-feeding made the conflict brutally explicit: if refusing food was an act of bodily autonomy, the state could attempt to defeat even that refusal by taking control of the body through medicalised coercion.

The informational conflict therefore exceeded the prison cell.

The question became:

> **What is the public being asked to see when the state responds to political resistance by putting instruments into a woman's body to keep her governable?**

Mockery mattered because ridicule lowers the perceived moral cost of harm.

Once a dissenter is represented as ridiculous, hysterical, manipulative, fanatical or attention-seeking, the audience is being prepared to interpret subsequent suffering as self-authored.

The mechanism is old:

```text
political demand
    ↓
delegitimise speaker
    ↓
reframe resistance as pathology / manipulation
    ↓
reframe bodily harm as personal choice
    ↓
institution becomes observer rather than participant
```

The institution has not necessarily ceased acting.

It has changed the story about **who is acting**.

## ☘️ Before The H-Blocks: Internment Is Part Of The Story

The 1981 hunger strike cannot be understood properly if the story begins with:

> IRA prisoners were convicted, then some of them refused food.

The receiving history is larger.

Internment without trial was introduced in Northern Ireland in August 1971. On 9 August, 342 men were arrested. Long Kesh became an internment camp. Contemporary official records themselves distinguish people interned or detained under exceptional powers from people arrested and charged through ordinary criminal process.

That distinction matters.

People were not experiencing one clean criminal-justice pipeline.

They were living inside a security environment containing:

- detention and internment without ordinary trial;
- intelligence-led arrest;
- emergency powers;
- ordinary criminal charges running alongside exceptional detention;
- later non-jury Diplock courts;
- changing prison classifications;
- and an extended political conflict over whether republican prisoners should be treated as ordinary criminals or as prisoners arising from political conflict.

None of that means every person detained was innocent.

None of it means the security threat was imaginary.

It means that **the history entering the prison was already a history of exceptional state power**.

This is important when later governments expect communities to evaluate new exceptional measures as though historical memory were an irrelevant emotional contaminant.

It isn't.

Historical memory is information.

## 🧱 Long Kesh → Maze → H-Blocks

The useful sequence is:

```text
internment without trial
        ↓
Long Kesh
        ↓
emergency criminal-justice architecture
        ↓
ending of Special Category Status
        ↓
H-Blocks
        ↓
blanket protest
        ↓
no-wash / dirty protest
        ↓
hunger strike
```

This is not one uninterrupted legal mechanism.

It is an **information sequence** through which people learned what British state power could look like under security pressure.

The ending of Special Category Status sharpened the conflict over classification.

The state position was, in effect:

> **You are criminals.**

The prisoners' counterclaim was:

> **Our imprisonment arises from political conflict. Calling us ordinary criminals does not make the political context disappear.**

The blanket protest, dirty protest and hunger strike progressively moved the dispute onto the body.

When institutional classification would not move, the prisoner made the cost of maintaining that classification visible.

## 🔥 Hunger Strike As Counter-Sovereignty

A prisoner is unusually constrained.

The institution controls:

- the room;
- the doors;
- movement;
- possessions;
- schedules;
- contact;
- surveillance;
- access to other people;
- and much of the prisoner's immediate physical environment.

A hunger strike identifies the residual boundary:

> **You can imprison this body. You cannot make this body cooperate with its own maintenance without confronting what that means.**

That is why hunger strikes are such extreme political signals.

They are expensive to the sender.

There is very little plausible way to interpret prolonged starvation as cheap speech.

The striker progressively converts health, bodily function and ultimately survival into signal cost.

In cybernetic terms:

```text
ordinary channels constrained
        ↓
high-cost embodied signal
        ↓
institution receives escalating evidence of commitment
        ↓
institution must choose:
respond / negotiate / suppress / reinterpret / delay
```

The hunger striker therefore creates a problem of **counter-sovereignty**.

Not because the prisoner becomes sovereign over the prison.

Because the prisoner demonstrates that state control still has a boundary, and makes the institution decide publicly what it is willing to permit at that boundary.

## 🕯️ Bobby Sands And The 66-Day Problem

Bobby Sands died on 5 May 1981 after 66 days on hunger strike.

During the strike, he was elected MP for Fermanagh and South Tyrone.

That fact radically complicated the informational picture.

The British state could maintain its criminal classification.

But an electorate had simultaneously turned the dying prisoner into an elected representative.

The body in the cell was therefore carrying competing political meanings at once.

Sands became a particularly powerful figure in Irish republican memory, but the wider importance is not dependent upon adopting republican politics.

His death demonstrates a control problem:

> **The sovereign does not monopolise the meaning of the spectacle it permits.**

The intended message may be:

> We will not yield to coercion.

The received message can become:

> You allowed an elected representative to starve to death in state custody rather than move.

Those are not the same message.

And once the second interpretation becomes culturally durable, future governments inherit it whether they enjoy the inheritance or not.

## 🧮 46–73 Days Is Not An Abstract Number

The ten men who died in the 1981 hunger strike died after periods ranging from 46 to 73 days without food.

That range matters when evaluating later hunger strikes because it supplies a historically legible danger zone.

In January 2026, Palestine Action-associated remand prisoner Heba Muraisi reached **73 days** without food before ending her strike.

Kamran Ahmed ended his strike at **65 days**.

The comparison therefore does not need exaggeration.

```text
1981:
46 days ─ earliest death among the ten hunger strikers
66 days ─ Bobby Sands dies
73 days ─ Kieran Doherty dies

2026:
65 days ─ Kamran Ahmed ends hunger strike
73 days ─ Heba Muraisi ends hunger strike
```

Muraisi reached the same duration as the longest-surviving of the ten men who died in 1981.

That does not make the two political conflicts identical.

It means **a British government again had a prisoner in its custody refusing food for a duration already embedded in British and Irish political memory as lethal**.

That is information.

## ⛓️ Palestine Action-Associated Remand Prisoners

The contemporary case is especially difficult for public legitimacy because the relevant prisoners were not serving sentences.

A Ministry of Justice parliamentary answer recorded that, as of 12 December 2025, 29 Palestine Action protesters were in prison, seven were on hunger strike and awaiting trial, and **none of the 29 was serving a prison sentence**.

An Early Day Motion eventually attracted 71 signatures and expressed concern that prisoners associated with Palestine Action felt they had no other recourse regarding their prison conditions than hunger strike.

Oxford City Council later recorded that eight remand prisoners detained in connection with alleged Palestine Action activity had participated in hunger strikes, that some expected to spend more than a year on remand before trial, and that their demands included bail, fair-trial rights, communications rights and de-proscription.

Again:

> **None of this, by itself, proves that the government lacked serious security information.**

The public does not possess all classified intelligence.

It is possible in principle for a government to know things that cannot safely be made public.

That concession does not solve the democratic problem.

It identifies it.

## 🛰️ Assume The Government Has A Good Reason

Take the strongest version of the state's case.

Assume:

- there was relevant intelligence unavailable to the public;
- officials genuinely believed the security risk was serious;
- some decisions that looked disproportionate from outside had information behind them that the public could not see;
- and ministers were not secretly sitting around Whitehall plotting how best to recreate every historically disastrous British detention controversy at once.

Fine.

Now what?

A democracy still operates through **observable legitimacy**.

The citizen receives:

```text
state action
    +
public explanation
    +
visible procedure
    +
historical memory
    +
treatment of comparable powerful actors
    +
media environment
    +
personal / community experience
    =
received meaning
```

The government controls only some of those inputs.

It cannot simply insert:

```text
CLASSIFIED SECURITY ASSESSMENT
```

and expect the output to be:

```text
PUBLIC TRUST = TRUE
```

Trust is not an API call.

## 🍉 Intent Is An Evidential Question; Communicative Effect Is Not

This distinction is essential.

It would require evidence to claim that ministers deliberately intended the treatment of Palestine Action-associated prisoners as a message of hostility towards Palestinian communities or people resisting the destruction of Gaza.

That intention should not be invented.

But the **communicative effect** is independently observable and analysable.

A Palestinian person does not receive British detention policy in a historical vacuum.

An Irish person does not receive a 65- or 73-day hunger strike in a historical vacuum.

A woman looking at institutional responses to sexual violence does not receive elite procedural protection in a historical vacuum.

A working-class person does not receive visible differences in access to lawyers, money, reputation management and institutional attention in a class vacuum.

The state does not possess exclusive authorship over the meaning of its actions.

That is not unfairness.

That is communication.

## 🌐 Unfortunately For The State, We Have The World Wide Web

1971 Britain did not have the web.

1981 Britain did not have the web.

2026 Britain does.

A citizen can now place, within the same afternoon:

- parliamentary answers;
- court judgments;
- police statements;
- prison reporting;
- government press releases;
- Irish historical archives;
- family statements;
- human-rights material;
- American court and government records;
- old news footage;
- campaign material;
- and current reporting

into the same information environment.

Some sources will be excellent.

Some will be partisan.

Some will be incomplete.

Some will be absolute bollocks.

But the state no longer controls the comparison set.

This changes democratic governance.

> **“We have information you cannot see” can be a legitimate security position. It is not a complete legitimacy strategy.**

If the public can see apparently unequal access to procedure while being asked to trust invisible reasons for exceptional coercion elsewhere, the state acquires an authentication problem.

## ⚖️ Legal Capital Is Still Capital

Equal formal rights do not create equal practical capacity to use them.

Money can purchase:

- specialist lawyers;
- repeated applications;
- rapid advice;
- expert evidence;
- litigation stamina;
- reputation management;
- communications advice;
- time;
- and the ability to keep contesting state power without immediately losing housing, employment or basic financial security.

That does not make the resulting legal action improper.

Quite the opposite.

The problem is not:

> **Powerful people have too many procedural rights.**

The problem is:

> **Why does coercive state power appear most negotiable to the people with the greatest resources for resisting it?**

That is the comparison the public makes.

Not because every case is legally comparable.

Because every case enters the same legitimacy environment.

## 👑 Unfortunately, Andrew Is Standing Very Close To The Diagram

As of October 2026, Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor is using the courts to challenge the legality of search warrants used by Thames Valley Police in searches connected to an ongoing investigation. He was arrested on his 66th birthday on suspicion of misconduct in public office and released under investigation. A High Court judge has permitted his challenge to proceed.

Good.

Procedural rights are supposed to survive public dislike.

Judicial review of coercive police power is a feature of the rule of law, not an embarrassing loophole that should disappear when the person invoking it is unpopular.

Unfortunately for the British state's visual communications department, Andrew is also an almost catastrophically inconvenient demonstration of what **robust access to procedure** looks like.

The public can therefore see:

```text
extremely powerful / connected man
        ↓
police action
        ↓
lawyers
        ↓
court challenge
        ↓
High Court scrutiny of police power
```

while elsewhere seeing:

```text
politically controversial remand prisoner
        ↓
prolonged detention before trial
        ↓
hunger strike
        ↓
serious physical deterioration
        ↓
argument about whether the state should move
```

The conclusion **“therefore these cases are legally equivalent”** would be nonsense.

The conclusion **“people will compare what access to state restraint looks like in each case”** is unavoidable.

Andrew does not have to be guilty of anything else for this function.

He merely has to stand there being procedurally visible.

Mate.

You are extremely close to the problem.

## 🎭 The Prince: Spectacular Coercion And Public Will

There is a point here that is very *The Prince*.

Coercion does not communicate only with the body receiving it.

It communicates with everyone watching.

The sovereign cannot directly coerce an entire population all the time.

Visible punishment therefore carries information about the cost of resistance.

A hunger strike can produce the following attempted message:

```text
you may protest
    ↓
you may be arrested
    ↓
you may be detained
    ↓
you may refuse cooperation
    ↓
you may damage your own body
    ↓
the state still will not move
```

The political information is:

> **Look how expensive resistance can become before power has to yield anything.**

That can suppress political participation without requiring mass detention.

People can watch.

The result need not be enthusiastic obedience.

It may be:

- fear;
- exhaustion;
- political withdrawal;
- cynicism;
- atomisation;
- learned futility;
- or the belief that meaningful challenge is simply too expensive.

This is coercion as spectacle.

But spectacular coercion contains its own failure mode.

## 🔁 When The Spectacle Reverses Direction

Machiavellian power is not simply **maximum cruelty forever**.

Cruelty that produces durable hatred can destabilise the ruler.

The deterrent spectacle therefore has a threshold.

```text
VISIBLE COST OF RESISTANCE
        ↓
fear / deterrence
        ↓
reduced challenge to power
```

But:

```text
VISIBLE COST BECOMES EXCESSIVE OR UNJUST
        ↓
identification with target
        ↓
state conduct becomes the spectacle
        ↓
anger / solidarity / historical memory
        ↓
legitimacy loss
        ↓
increased resistance
```

Hunger strikes are unusually dangerous to state control because the same deteriorating body can carry both messages.

At first:

> **This is what resistance costs.**

Later:

> **This is what the state is willing to watch happen rather than move.**

The body has not changed.

The audience has.

That is a cybernetic reversal.

## 🧠 Historical Memory Is Part Of The Receiving System

This is why:

> **2026 is not 1981.**

is true but insufficient.

Nobody needs to claim:

> **Palestine Action = IRA.**
>
> **2026 = 1981.**
>
> **remand = internment.**
>
> **current government = Thatcher government.**

The stronger proposition is:

> **People whose communities remember 1971 and 1981 can recognise components of the pattern.**

Historical memory modifies the receiver.

The same signal therefore means different things to different observers.

A Whitehall department may see:

> contemporary security administration.

An Irish observer may additionally see:

> exceptional procedure → political classification → prisoner resistance → bodily deterioration → government refusal to move.

A Palestinian observer may additionally see:

> political detention → resistance → state control of movement and communication → bodily risk → demands that the prisoner prove legitimacy through suffering.

Neither observer needs permission from the issuing institution to possess that context.

**History is part of the receiving system.**

## 🛡️ Procedural Legitimacy Is Security Infrastructure

This is where the issue becomes relevant beyond criminal justice.

A state can possess:

- accurate intelligence;
- capable police;
- lawful warrants;
- legitimate secrecy;
- serious security threats;
- and competent armed forces

while degrading the public trust required for future security warnings to work.

Imagine:

> Government: We have intelligence that cannot presently be disclosed.
>
> Public: Oh. This again.

That is an authentication failure.

It does not prove the intelligence is wrong.

That is precisely the problem.

A government can be **correct about the threat and unable to persuade enough people that the warning is authentic**.

Institutional trust is therefore not a decorative democratic feeling.

It is part of the communications infrastructure through which a population decides whether to:

- believe warnings;
- comply with emergency measures;
- share information;
- accept temporary restrictions;
- distinguish genuine security threats from political convenience;
- and cooperate with institutions during crisis.

NATO's 2026 resilience framework explicitly uses whole-of-government and whole-of-society approaches and treats resilience as part of credible deterrence and defence.

That does not mean NATO should adjudicate British criminal cases.

It means an alliance interested in societal resilience should care when an Ally creates conditions in which **legitimate security signals become harder for its own population to authenticate**.

---

## 📡 Make The Adversary Lie

There is a useful information-security principle hiding here.

A resilient democracy should try to make hostile propaganda work hard.

Ideally, an adversary has to:

- fabricate;
- forge;
- invent;
- materially distort;
- or conceal decisive context

to produce a damaging narrative.

You do not want to hand them:

- authentic parliamentary answers;
- real hospitalisations;
- real prolonged remand;
- real historical hunger-strike durations;
- real court challenges by exceptionally powerful people;
- real arguments about exceptional powers;
- and a public archive searchable from anywhere

and leave their principal task as:

> **editing.**

The resulting propaganda may still be misleading.

But it is much harder to rebut when every component image is real.

Procedural legitimacy therefore has defensive value.

**Make the adversary lie.**

Do not make their job merely arranging your own receipts.

## 🔥 The Governance Script

Across these cases, a repeating script can appear:

1. A political demand is rejected or administratively contained.
2. The dissenter escalates to an embodied form of refusal.
3. The state reframes starvation as voluntary choice.
4. Institutional responsibility is displaced onto the striker.
5. Delay becomes the default.
6. Deterioration becomes normalised through repetition.
7. The public is invited to interpret concern as capitulation to manipulation.
8. If death occurs, the vocabulary changes.
9. The person becomes tragic.
10. Institutions insist the outcome was regrettable but unavoidable.

This is the horror at the centre of retrospective mourning.

> **The dead person becomes easier to sympathise with once sympathy can no longer save them.**

That is not a neutral memorial cycle.

It can function as a governance technology.

---

## 🪦 Retrospective Compassion Is Cheap

A state can be exceptionally generous to the dead.

The dead no longer require bail.

They do not require policy change.

They cannot continue the hunger strike.

They cannot walk out of prison.

They cannot embarrass a minister tomorrow.

They can be:

- regretted;
- historicised;
- commemorated;
- described as a tragedy;
- turned into a lesson;
- and safely placed in the past tense.

The political question is what institutions do **before** the person becomes memorial material.

This is why comparing real-time dismissal with retrospective mourning matters.

Compassion after irreversible harm is not the same political act as preventing the harm while the dissenter still has agency.

## 👀 The Public Are Fucking Baffled

This is not an analytically trivial condition.

Public bafflement is information.

When institutions repeatedly need highly technical explanations for why:

- this exceptional power is unlike the old exceptional power;
- this prolonged remand is legally different from that historical detention;
- this person's access to procedural challenge is correct;
- that person's bodily deterioration does not require political engagement;
- this security claim cannot be evidenced publicly;
- this historical comparison is apparently inappropriate;
- and none of the visible asymmetries mean what they appear to mean

the problem may not simply be that the public requires better civics education.

The system may be emitting an incoherent picture.

That matters because democratic legitimacy is partly the ability of ordinary people to look at public institutions and perceive some stable relationship between:

```text
rule
person
power
procedure
outcome
```

If wealth, status or political acceptability appear to change that relationship, distrust is predictable.

## 🧿 What The Joke Is Actually Doing

The satire is not:

> **Ha ha, punish Andrew because we dislike him.**

That would reproduce the problem.

The joke is:

> **If equality before law has become difficult for the public to see, apparently Britain must turn visible procedural equality into a fucking folk custom.**

Andrew's rights remain rights.

The hunger strikers' rights remain rights.

A defendant does not become less entitled to due process because the public hates him.

A political protester does not become less entitled to due process because the government believes the politics create a security problem.

The point is consistency.

The spectacle required by democracy is not spectacular punishment.

It is **visible constraint on power**.

## 🔭 The Wider Proposition

The hunger strike is an unusually concentrated test of democratic systems because it forces a state to confront several kinds of power simultaneously:

- coercive power;
- classificatory power;
- medical power;
- narrative power;
- temporal power;
- legal power;
- and the prisoner's residual power over their own body.

The state may have legitimate reasons for refusing a demand.

The striker may be wrong.

The striker may be right.

The public may misunderstand important facts.

Classified intelligence may materially alter the government's assessment.

None of those possibilities abolish the feedback loop.

A democracy must govern not only through correct internal reasoning but through **legible external conduct**.

When the public watches powerful people deploy law effectively while less powerful political defendants deteriorate in custody, historical comparison becomes easier.

When those comparisons involve a 73-day hunger strike in Britain, Bobby Sands is not an obscure footnote.

When Irish councils and communities recognise the pattern, that is not evidence that 2026 has magically become 1981.

It is evidence that **1981 remains inside the receiving system**.

When Palestinian communities read the treatment through their own histories, the state cannot delete that interpretation by insisting it intended something else.

And when NATO talks about whole-of-society resilience, democratic states should understand the security implication:

> **Procedural legitimacy is part of the authentication infrastructure of national security.**

A population that cannot tell whether exceptional power is being used fairly becomes harder to mobilise around exceptional power when it is genuinely necessary.

That is not softness.

That is systems engineering.

It is the **public comparison environment**.

That environment exists whether institutions approve of it or not.
