# 🐢 Behavioural Proxies All the Way Down  
**First created:** 2025-12-20 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-24  
*How behavioural signals become substitutes for identity, intent and risk, and why the infrastructure built to improve governance can also make coercive power easier to exercise.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Governance systems rarely have direct access to the thing they claim to measure.

They cannot look inside a person and determine, with certainty:

- Whether they intend harm.
- Whether they are trustworthy.
- Whether they hold a particular belief.
- Whether they present a genuine threat.
- Whether they will behave in a particular way tomorrow.
- Whether their anger reflects danger, distress or a perfectly reasonable objection.
- Whether their political associations indicate solidarity, friendship, professional interest or operational coordination.

Instead, institutions rely on signals.

They examine behaviour, language, association, records, location, professional impressions, historical information and patterns of activity.

Those signals become proxies.

A proxy is not the underlying thing.

It is a measurable or observable stand-in used to estimate something that cannot be observed directly.

Sometimes proxies help institutions identify real danger.

Sometimes they reproduce assumptions, flatten context or turn ordinary human variation into suspicion.

And sometimes a system constructed to make governance more responsive also reduces the friction required to govern coercively.

That is the central problem:

> **The same infrastructure that makes protective intervention more capable can also make high-harm governance easier to scale, coordinate and conceal.**

The issue is not whether a system uses information.

The issue is whether it can distinguish an observable signal from the story it has decided the signal tells.

If the answer to every question is another proxy, eventually someone has to ask what the whole stack is actually standing on.

It cannot be turtles all the way down.

## ✨ Key Features

- Defines behavioural proxies and distinguishes observation from interpretation.
- Explains how institutions infer identity, intent or risk from measurable signals.
- Examines the difference between legitimate risk assessment and coercive classification.
- Identifies the governance catch-22 created by increasingly capable surveillance and information infrastructure.
- Explains how apparently neutral criteria can reproduce discriminatory assumptions.
- Integrates risk-vector inversion, calibration drift and classification failure into the broader proxy framework.
- Examines how solidarity, disability, trauma and political dissent can disrupt identity-based assumptions.
- Distinguishes documented institutional practice from hypotheses about technical systems or intent.
- Treats independent review, evidential quality and protective friction as essential safeguards.
- Preserves the distinction between a person who may be at risk and a person who supposedly presents one.

## 🧭 What Is a Behavioural Proxy?

A behavioural proxy is an observable feature used to estimate something less directly observable.

The feature might include:

- Frequency of communication.
- Tone of speech.
- Emotional intensity.
- Online activity.
- Search behaviour.
- Political expression.
- Professional associations.
- Repeated contact with an institution.
- Attendance at a public event.
- Membership of a community.
- Movement through particular locations.
- Previous reports or referrals.
- Patterns of interaction with other people.
- Use of particular words or phrases.

The inferred characteristic might be:

- Intent.
- Credibility.
- Vulnerability.
- Aggression.
- Radicalisation.
- Institutional loyalty.
- Professional reliability.
- Risk of offending.
- Risk of self-harm.
- Likelihood of non-compliance.
- Suspected association with a harmful actor.
- Potential disruption.

The difference matters.

An observation might be:

> “This person sent six emails requesting clarification.”

The proxy-based inference might be:

> “Repeated communication indicates fixation.”

The first statement may be directly verifiable.

The second depends on context, assumptions and competing explanations.

If six earlier requests were ignored, persistence may indicate an unanswered question rather than a behavioural disorder.

The proxy is not the person.

The signal is not the conclusion.

The number is not the meaning.

## 🔬 Observation, Proxy and Conclusion

A defensible assessment should distinguish three separate stages.

| Stage | Example | Relevant Question |
|---|---|---|
| Observation | A person attended three public meetings. | Is the observation accurate? |
| Proxy | Repeated attendance is treated as an indicator of political involvement. | Is that indicator relevant and appropriately validated? |
| Conclusion | The person is classified as presenting a security concern. | Does the available evidence justify that conclusion? |

The gap between these stages is where institutional error can enter.

A person may attend a public meeting because they are:

- A journalist.
- A student.
- A researcher.
- A legal observer.
- A resident.
- A community organiser.
- A concerned relative.
- A political participant.
- Someone seeking information.
- Someone accompanying a friend.

Attendance alone does not settle intent.

The same principle applies to:

- Emotional expression.
- Religious identity.
- Social association.
- Professional disagreement.
- Academic research.
- Disability-related communication.
- Repeated requests for support.
- Public criticism of an institution.

The more serious the potential consequences, the less acceptable it becomes to treat the proxy as though it were the underlying fact.

## 🐢 Why It Is Proxies All the Way Down

Risk systems often contain several layers of substitution.

An institution may begin with:

> “This person is high risk.”

But that classification depends on:

> “The person displayed concerning behaviour.”

Which depends on:

> “The person communicated persistently.”

Which depends on:

> “The person contacted several organisations.”

Which depends on:

> “The person was trying to establish who had responsibility for an unresolved decision.”

At each stage, an interpretation has replaced the context beneath it.

By the time the final classification reaches another institution, the original situation may have disappeared.

The receiving organisation sees:

> “High risk.”

It may not see:

> “A person attempted to obtain an explanation after several institutions gave contradictory answers.”

The classification appears authoritative because the earlier interpretive steps are hidden.

Each turtle is standing on another turtle.

Eventually, the actual event is so far below the stack that nobody remembers to check whether it was ever dangerous.

## 🧠 Why Institutions Use Proxies

Institutions use proxies because they cannot investigate every circumstance in unlimited detail.

Practical constraints include:

- Time.
- Staffing.
- Resources.
- Legal obligations.
- Incomplete information.
- Large volumes of referrals.
- Urgent safety concerns.
- The need to prioritise cases.
- Coordination across several organisations.
- Difficulty observing future behaviour directly.

Proxies can support legitimate decisions where they are:

- Relevant to the risk being assessed.
- Grounded in reliable evidence.
- Interpreted in context.
- Appropriate for the population involved.
- Reviewed by someone with suitable expertise.
- Open to correction.
- Used proportionately.
- Combined with alternative explanations.

A credible threat, documented pattern of violence or repeated boundary violation may properly inform risk assessment.

The existence of a proxy is not evidence that the assessment is unlawful or discriminatory.

The problem arises when a weak, ambiguous or context-dependent signal is treated as sufficient proof.

Efficient triage is useful.

Replacing judgement with an unexamined shortcut is not.

## 🕸️ From Observation to Infrastructure

Behavioural proxies become more consequential when they are embedded in connected systems.

Relevant infrastructure may include:

- Case-management software.
- Professional records.
- Referral pathways.
- Shared databases.
- Automated alerts.
- Risk-scoring tools.
- Searchable incident histories.
- Identity verification systems.
- Communications records.
- Institutional watchlists.
- Platform moderation systems.
- Procurement frameworks.
- Information-sharing agreements.
- Human discussions operating alongside technical systems.

Not every organisation uses every mechanism.

The existence or use of a particular system must be established on evidence.

The structural point is that once a classification enters an information environment, it may influence decisions beyond the original context.

A local concern can become:

- A repeated note.
- A shared flag.
- A professional warning.
- An administrative category.
- A restriction.
- A referral.
- A reason to decline support.
- A basis for additional scrutiny.

The more interoperable the infrastructure becomes, the easier it is for an interpretation to travel.

That can improve protection when the interpretation is accurate.

It can amplify harm when the interpretation is wrong.

## ⚖️ The Governance Catch-22

Better governance often requires institutions to improve:

- Information quality.
- Coordination.
- Case visibility.
- Early intervention.
- Pattern recognition.
- Accountability.
- Cross-organisational communication.
- Identification of serious harm.
- Detection of repeated misconduct.

These improvements can be valuable.

But the same capabilities also make it easier to:

- Track behaviour across settings.
- Associate people through shared records.
- Infer beliefs from communication patterns.
- Extend a local classification into another institution.
- Apply restrictions before an individual understands the concern.
- Centralise sensitive information.
- Reduce the practical role of human judgement.
- Make intrusive decisions appear procedurally neutral.
- Reuse information for purposes different from those originally described.
- Expand institutional power without equivalent expansion of oversight.

This creates the catch-22:

> **Infrastructure intended to improve governance can also reduce the operational friction required for high-harm governance.**

The system becomes more capable.

Whether that capability protects or harms depends on its purpose, controls, evidence and accountability.

A sharper instrument can support better surgery.

It can also make a bad incision considerably more efficient.

---

## 🛡️ Friction Is Sometimes a Safeguard

Institutional reform often treats friction as a defect.

Delays are reduced.

Authorisations are simplified.

Records become easier to access.

Information moves more quickly.

Decision-making becomes automated.

These changes may improve legitimate responses.

But some friction exists for a reason.

Protective friction can include:

- Independent authorisation.
- Purpose limitations.
- Restricted access.
- Human review.
- Requirements to record reasons.
- Opportunities to challenge an assessment.
- Time limits on retention.
- Separate legal thresholds for different interventions.
- Professional scrutiny.
- Independent oversight.
- Verification before onward disclosure.
- Requirements to distinguish allegations from established facts.

Removing these protections may make a system faster.

It may also make questionable decisions easier to reproduce.

A governance system should distinguish:

> **Friction that obstructs necessary protection.**

From:

> **Friction that prevents powerful institutions from acting without adequate justification.**

Not every obstacle needs streamlining.

Some are the brakes.

## 🧩 Proxy Laundering

A rule does not have to mention a protected identity explicitly to reproduce an identity-based pattern.

A system can classify people through apparently neutral features such as:

- Communication style.
- Political association.
- Religious practice.
- Place of residence.
- Travel history.
- Language.
- Social networks.
- Emotional presentation.
- Institutional distrust.
- Previous contact with public services.
- Attendance at particular events.
- Perceived cultural unfamiliarity.

These features may correlate with:

- Race.
- Religion.
- Disability.
- Class.
- National origin.
- Political belief.
- Trauma exposure.
- Community membership.
- Migration history.

The institution may say:

> “We do not assess identity. We assess behaviour.”

That statement does not resolve the problem if the selected behaviour functions as an indirect substitute for identity.

A behavioural category can appear neutral while reproducing assumptions built into:

- Historical data.
- Professional culture.
- Earlier policing patterns.
- Unequal reporting.
- Selective enforcement.
- Media narratives.
- Unrepresentative training populations.
- Existing institutional records.

The proper question is:

> **What does this supposedly neutral feature actually select for in practice?**

If the answer is “people already subjected to disproportionate scrutiny,” neutrality may be doing rather more cosmetic work than analytical work.

## 🧬 Classification Is Not Identity

Classification systems work by drawing boundaries.

Human beings have an inconvenient habit of crossing them.

A person can be:

- Jewish and critical of Israeli government policy.
- Muslim and opposed to political violence.
- Disabled and professionally competent.
- Traumatised and factually accurate.
- Angry and entitled to a clear answer.
- Highly educated and dependent on support.
- Politically engaged and committed to non-violence.
- Critical of an institution and concerned about its proper functioning.
- Closely associated with several communities without belonging exclusively to any of them.

A model that assumes fixed alignment between identity and behaviour will struggle when people refuse to behave like its categories.

That is not a defect in the people.

It is a defect in the assumptions.

An administrative dropdown menu does not become a theory of human existence simply because someone has attached a risk score to it.

## 🔄 Risk-Vector Inversion

A risk-vector inversion occurs when the assumptions used to distinguish “safe” from “concerning” begin producing unexpected or internally contradictory classifications.

For example:

- A system associates a particular community with one political position.
- A member of that community expresses a different position.
- The mismatch is interpreted as suspicious rather than prompting reassessment of the original assumption.

The same problem can arise where:

- A survivor remains articulate after serious harm.
- A clinician challenges an established institutional account.
- A public servant criticises a process they are expected to defend.
- A disabled person communicates in a way that does not match professional expectations.
- A person belongs to several communities whose assumed interests are treated as incompatible.
- Cross-group solidarity contradicts inherited security categories.

The institution may treat the inconsistency as evidence that the person is difficult to classify.

A better interpretation may be that the framework was too narrow from the beginning.

Risk-vector inversion does not prove that a particular model exists or that an institution deliberately targets a specific group.

It identifies a potential failure mode:

> **When risk categories depend on simplified assumptions about identity or allegiance, real human complexity can be misread as threat.**

## 🌍 When Solidarity Breaks the Model

Identity-linked risk assumptions often depend on predictable boundaries.

A system may expect:

- One community to support one political position.
- Another community to oppose it.
- Institutional insiders to defend existing decisions.
- Survivors to remain passive.
- Professionals to stay within recognised hierarchies.
- Religious identity to predict political allegiance.
- Social association to indicate shared intent.

Cross-group solidarity disrupts those expectations.

Examples include:

- Jewish and Muslim communities organising together.
- Clinicians supporting patients who challenge institutional decisions.
- Disabled activists collaborating across political or professional boundaries.
- Survivors sharing evidence with independent researchers.
- Public officials questioning assumptions within their own organisations.
- Communities refusing to treat inherited stereotypes as reliable descriptions of one another.

Where a classification framework depends heavily on assumed group alignment, these relationships may appear anomalous.

But the anomaly may belong to the model.

Not the people.

Solidarity does not become suspicious merely because it makes a simplistic classifier harder to operate.

## 📉 Calibration Drift

A proxy is only as useful as the relationship between the signal and the outcome it is supposed to estimate.

That relationship can change.

A model may be poorly calibrated because:

- It was developed for a different population.
- The underlying social environment has changed.
- Political language has shifted.
- The original data was incomplete.
- Historical reporting reflected discriminatory enforcement.
- New forms of communication have altered behavioural norms.
- The organisation applies the tool outside its intended purpose.
- Relevant contextual information is missing.
- The model has not been independently reassessed.

For example:

- Repeated online contact may mean something different in a remote-working environment.
- A phrase associated with one political context may have a different meaning elsewhere.
- A trauma response may resemble a behavioural indicator originally developed for another purpose.
- Social connections may reflect public professional collaboration rather than coordinated action.

A model can appear consistent while becoming increasingly detached from reality.

Consistency is not accuracy.

It is entirely possible to be wrong with excellent internal discipline.

## 🧪 The Life-Sciences Version

For readers more comfortable with a laboratory bench than a software architecture diagram:

A behavioural proxy is similar to a biomarker.

A biomarker may help identify a condition.

It is not the condition itself.

Interpretation depends on:

- Sample quality.
- Reference population.
- Clinical context.
- Sensitivity.
- Specificity.
- Confounding factors.
- Baseline prevalence.
- Confirmatory testing.
- Whether the assay has been validated for the relevant use.

If the sample is contaminated, the reference range is inappropriate and the result is interpreted without context, the output may still look impressively precise.

It is not therefore reliable.

Risk software deserves at least as much scrutiny as a questionable laboratory result.

You would not diagnose a patient from one poorly validated marker and then circulate the diagnosis to six departments without checking it.

A classification system should not receive a free pass merely because the assay is wearing a lanyard.

## 📊 False Positives and Unequal Consequences

A false positive occurs when someone is flagged as presenting a concern they do not actually present.

A false negative occurs when a genuine concern is missed.

Both matter.

A system designed to catch every possible threat may generate many false positives.

A system designed to avoid inconvenience may miss serious harm.

The consequences of error are not evenly distributed.

A false-positive classification may result in:

- Increased scrutiny.
- Loss of employment.
- Interrupted education.
- Restricted travel.
- Reduced access to services.
- Reputational harm.
- Unnecessary safeguarding intervention.
- Inaccurate information sharing.
- Difficulty obtaining a fair review.
- Prolonged distress.

A false negative may result in:

- Missed violence.
- Failure to protect a vulnerable person.
- Continued access by a harmful actor.
- Delayed intervention.
- Repeated institutional neglect.

Responsible governance does not deny either possibility.

It asks whether the chosen balance is justified and whether the people affected can challenge an error.

A system cannot describe itself as protective while refusing to examine the harm caused by its own mistakes.

---

## 📈 Why Base Rates Matter

A rare event can remain unlikely even when a particular signal appears concerning.

If a system screens a large population for an uncommon threat, a modest false-positive rate can generate substantial numbers of incorrect classifications.

That matters when:

- The underlying behaviour is common.
- The actual threat is rare.
- The signal has several innocent explanations.
- Large numbers of people are assessed.
- A flag produces serious consequences.
- The model is applied beyond its validated setting.

The presence of an indicator is not the same as the presence of a condition.

A person may share one feature with a risk category without belonging to it.

The more common the feature and the rarer the outcome, the more careful the interpretation must be.

Otherwise, the system begins discovering threats at the same rate it discovers people.

## 🧾 When Historical Bias Becomes Future Risk

Risk systems may draw on existing records.

Those records may reflect earlier institutional decisions.

If particular communities have historically been scrutinised more heavily, they may appear more frequently in the available data.

A later model can mistake that pattern for evidence that the communities are inherently higher risk.

The sequence becomes:

1. A population receives disproportionate scrutiny.
2. More observations and incidents are recorded.
3. The resulting dataset contains more information about that population.
4. A classifier treats the increased visibility as evidence of increased risk.
5. Additional scrutiny follows.
6. The dataset becomes even more skewed.

The system appears to confirm itself.

What it may actually be measuring is the history of its own attention.

An institution that repeatedly searches the same drawer will eventually conclude that the drawer contains most of the things it has found.

That does not establish that the rest of the house is empty.

## 🧬 Gossip as Unofficial Training Data

Not all classification begins in a database.

Informal information can influence interpretation before a formal risk decision is made.

Examples include:

- “There is a history with this person.”
- “They can be quite difficult.”
- “You should be careful around that case.”
- “Someone mentioned concerns.”
- “I think another department has already dealt with them.”
- “They are known to make complaints.”

These statements may be:

- Accurate.
- Partially accurate.
- Outdated.
- Misleading.
- Based on hearsay.
- Detached from their original context.
- Influenced by professional discomfort.

Once repeated, they can affect how later behaviour is interpreted.

A person enters the room already carrying a reputation they may not know exists.

The informal narrative becomes a kind of unofficial training data.

## 🧠 Fast Thinking and Premature Classification

Behavioural proxies become more influential when institutions operate under pressure.

A staff member may rely on:

- Familiar categories.
- Previous experiences.
- Professional shorthand.
- Templates.
- Automated recommendations.
- The views of a senior colleague.
- An existing risk label.
- The apparent emotional tone of a communication.

These shortcuts can support rapid triage.

They can also substitute plausibility for proof.

A person who sounds angry may be classified before anyone asks why.

A person who has contacted several services may be treated as problematic before anyone checks whether the services gave contradictory advice.

A person with a complex history may be described as high risk before anyone examines the accuracy of the history.

Speed is not inherently the problem.

Unreviewed speed is.

## 🌀 When the Proxy Becomes Self-Confirming

A classification can produce the very behaviour later used to justify it.

For example:

1. A person is labelled difficult.
2. Staff become guarded.
3. Explanations become vague.
4. The person experiences uncertainty.
5. They ask more questions.
6. Their persistence is treated as confirmation that they are difficult.
7. The original classification becomes harder to challenge.

A similar process can occur where:

- A person is described as unstable.
- Support is reduced.
- Their distress increases.
- The increased distress is treated as evidence supporting the original description.

The problem is not simply that the initial classification was wrong.

It is that the institutional response creates a feedback loop that makes the classification appear increasingly convincing.

A system should ask:

> **Would this behaviour still appear concerning if the institution had responded clearly, accurately and proportionately?**

If not, the institution may be observing its own contribution to the outcome.

## 🏛️ Authoritarian Potential Without Authoritarian Certainty

A society does not become authoritarian merely because it uses risk assessments, digital records or information-sharing systems.

Public institutions need the ability to identify genuine harm.

However, infrastructure can create authoritarian potential when it allows power to be exercised with:

- Limited transparency.
- Weak legal safeguards.
- Broad discretion.
- Extensive data collection.
- Indefinite retention.
- Minimal independent review.
- Opaque classification criteria.
- Inaccessible appeal routes.
- Automated onward disclosure.
- Punitive consequences based on uncertain inference.

The distinction is important.

The existence of capability is not proof of misuse.

But the absence of present misuse is not a substitute for safeguards.

A system should be assessed not only by the intentions of its current operators, but also by what the architecture makes possible under future pressure.

Tools built for one government, one institution or one emergency can remain available to the next.

Infrastructure has a longer memory than ministerial reassurance.

## 🧭 What Happens When Risk Is Defined Backwards?

A person may be:

- At risk.
- A source of risk.
- A witness to risk.
- Someone reporting risk.
- Someone incorrectly associated with risk.
- Someone affected by an institution’s own response to risk.

These positions are not interchangeable.

A survivor reporting a harmful actor may be at risk.

That does not make the survivor the source of the threat.

A whistleblower may create reputational exposure for an institution.

That does not mean the whistleblower presents a danger to the public.

A disabled person may require additional support.

That does not make disability evidence of unreliability.

A journalist may publish embarrassing information.

That does not establish a security threat.

The question is:

> **Risk to whom, risk from whom, and risk of what?**

If those questions disappear, institutional discomfort can quietly replace actual harm as the thing being managed.

## ⚖️ Legal Disentanglement and Classification Persistence

An inaccurate proxy-based classification can become difficult to correct once it has travelled across several systems.

Questions may arise about:

- Who created the original record.
- Which organisation controls it.
- Whether the classification has been copied.
- What information can be disclosed.
- Which body has authority to amend or remove it.
- Whether another institution continues relying on it.
- What happens if the original source is corrected.
- Whether decisions made downstream also require review.

The more connected the architecture, the more important these questions become.

A system cannot fairly rely on the convenience of shared information while treating correction as somebody else’s administrative problem.

## 🔮 The Promise of Predictive Governance

Proxy systems are often justified by an attractive promise:

> If enough information can be collected, connected and interpreted, institutions will anticipate harm before it occurs.

The promise includes:

- Earlier intervention.
- Better coordination.
- More efficient resource allocation.
- Reduced uncertainty.
- Improved public safety.
- Identification of overlooked patterns.
- Faster access to support.

These goals can be legitimate.

But predictive capability depends on:

- The quality of the underlying data.
- The validity of the chosen proxies.
- The purpose of the model.
- The assumptions built into the classification.
- The availability of human review.
- The ability to identify and correct error.
- The distribution of consequences across affected groups.

A prediction is not a prophecy.

An automated score is not a moral judgement.

A system does not know the future simply because it has found a way to present uncertainty as a number.

## 🧮 When the Machine Answers the Wrong Question

A risk model may produce a technically coherent output while solving the wrong problem.

It might be asked to identify:

- Institutional disruption.

When the real concern should be:

- Harm to a vulnerable person.

It might measure:

- Frequency of complaint.

When the relevant issue is:

- Whether the complaint has been answered.

It might predict:

- Reputational exposure.

When the proper objective is:

- Accuracy, safety and lawful decision-making.

The output may be precise.

Its relevance may still be questionable.

A machine can return an answer with considerable confidence while nobody has agreed whether the question was worth asking.

## 🛠️ Protective Design Requirements

A responsible proxy-based system should include:

### Clear Purpose

Identify what risk is being assessed and whose interests the system is intended to protect.

### Evidence Quality

Distinguish verified facts, allegations, inference and professional opinion.

### Proxy Validation

Establish whether the selected signal genuinely relates to the outcome being assessed.

### Contextual Interpretation

Consider alternative explanations, disability, trauma, cultural background and the circumstances in which the behaviour occurred.

### Appropriate Reference Populations

Ensure that models and thresholds are relevant to the people and setting in which they are used.

### Error Awareness

Assess false positives, false negatives, calibration problems and unequal impacts.

### Human Review

Provide meaningful professional scrutiny rather than treating human oversight as a ceremonial signature beneath a predetermined output.

### Access and Correction

Enable people to challenge materially inaccurate information and understand consequential decisions where lawful and appropriate.

### Purpose Limitation

Prevent information collected for one legitimate reason from being repurposed without proper authority.

### Independent Oversight

Ensure that the organisation responsible for a classification is not the only body capable of reviewing it.

### Time Limits

Review classifications regularly and avoid indefinite retention or continuing restrictions without current justification.

### Protective Friction

Maintain the procedural barriers necessary to prevent powerful systems from acting too easily on weak assumptions.

The aim is not to make institutions blind.

It is to stop them confusing a blurred image with perfect sight.

## 📡 Diagnostic Questions

When assessing a behavioural proxy, ask:

1. What behaviour or signal was observed?
2. Is the observation accurate?
3. What characteristic is the behaviour being used to infer?
4. What evidence supports the connection?
5. What other explanations exist?
6. Was the proxy validated for this context?
7. Does it correlate with protected identity, disability or social disadvantage?
8. Is the person being assessed as at risk or as a source of risk?
9. What information has been omitted?
10. Has the classification been shared with other organisations?
11. Can the person challenge a material error?
12. What would disprove the concern?
13. Are the consequences proportionate?
14. Who is responsible for the decision?
15. What independent oversight exists?
16. What safeguards prevent the system from being repurposed coercively?

The final question should always be:

> **If the proxy were wrong, how would anybody know?**

If the answer is “they would not,” the problem is no longer only classification.

It is governance.

## 🧀 When Proxy Errors Align

A single weak proxy may cause limited harm.

Several interacting failures can create a much larger problem.

For example:

- A person’s distress is recorded without its context.
- An informal warning amplifies the concern.
- Another organisation interprets the record as verified.
- An automated or procedural flag increases scrutiny.
- Access to support becomes restricted.
- The person asks repeated questions.
- The questions are treated as confirmation of the original concern.
- No institution accepts responsibility for reviewing the complete sequence.

The final outcome may look like a coherent risk assessment.

It may actually be a stack of untested substitutions.

One turtle standing on another.

Each looking very official.
