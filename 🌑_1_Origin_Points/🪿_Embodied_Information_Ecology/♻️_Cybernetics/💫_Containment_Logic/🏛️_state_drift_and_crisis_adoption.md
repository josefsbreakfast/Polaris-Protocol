# 🏛️ State Drift & Crisis Adoption  
**First created:** 2025-12-26 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-12  
*Why governments say "yes".*  

---

## Summary

This node explains how governments adopt high-impact analytics platforms **without making an explicit political decision to do so**.

Rather than ideology or coercion, adoption is driven by:
- crisis conditions
- institutional exhaustion
- delivery pressure
- inherited systems
- and slow-moving law

The result is **state drift** — where governance changes direction without formal consent.

---

## The Myth of the Single Decision

Public debate often assumes that technologies like Palantir enter government through:
- a clear political choice
- a single controversial contract
- or a decisive moment of alignment

In reality, adoption is:
- incremental
- fragmented
- distributed across institutions
- and justified differently at each step

No one “decides the future.”
The future accumulates.

---

## Crisis as the Point of Entry

Crises lower resistance.

Examples include:
- pandemics
- war and geopolitical instability
- cyber insecurity
- migration pressure
- system backlogs and institutional failure

In crisis conditions:
- speed is prioritised over deliberation
- safeguards are deferred
- emergency procurement becomes normal
- “temporary” measures persist

Platforms introduced during crises benefit from:
- urgency
- fear of disruption
- and moral pressure to “do something”

This is how exceptional systems become routine.

---

## Austerity and Institutional Exhaustion

Long-term underfunding leaves public institutions:
- understaffed
- technologically fragmented
- risk-averse
- and operationally brittle

In this context, external platforms appear as:
- relief
- coherence
- competence
- and capacity substitution

The platform is not just a tool — it becomes:
> “The thing that holds the system together.”

Once that happens, withdrawal feels irresponsible.

---

## Delivery Politics

Modern governance increasingly rewards:
- visible action
- measurable outputs
- short-term stabilisation
- narrative control

“Delivery” becomes a political virtue.

Platforms that promise:
- clarity
- dashboards
- prioritisation
- and rapid insight

align perfectly with this incentive structure.

Long-term harms, diffuse impacts, and rights concerns are:
- harder to measure
- slower to surface
- easier to discount

---

## Inherited Systems and Path Dependence

New governments rarely start from zero.

They inherit:
- contracts
- platforms
- data architectures
- training regimes
- institutional habits

Even when uneasy, decision-makers are told:
- “this is already embedded”
- “removal would be disruptive”
- “we’ll add safeguards later”

Thus, continuity is framed as responsibility.

Change is framed as risk.

---

## Fragmented Responsibility

No single actor holds the whole picture.

- Procurement teams focus on compliance.
- Civil servants focus on functionality.
- Ministers focus on outcomes.
- Lawyers focus on defensibility.

Each part sees only its slice.

This fragmentation allows:
- ethical concerns to be deferred
- responsibility to diffuse
- and accountability to evaporate

Everyone acts reasonably.
The system still drifts.

---

## Why Centre-Ground Governments Are Especially Vulnerable

Governments positioning themselves as:
- pragmatic
- non-ideological
- competence-driven

are particularly susceptible to infrastructure capture.

Platforms are framed as:
- neutral
- technical
- inevitable

Opposition becomes:
- “impractical”
- “ideological”
- “anti-delivery”

This dynamic normalises adoption without debate.

---

## Drift Without Consent

State drift occurs when:
- decisions accumulate
- justification shifts over time
- and reversal becomes politically costly

No vote is taken.
No manifesto promise is broken.
No law is changed — at first.

Yet the character of governance alters.  

---

## One-line takeaway

> Governments rarely choose infrastructure futures outright — they inherit them, normalise them, and eventually defend them as unavoidable.
