# ⚖️ Legal Disentanglement and Risk Perception  
**First created:** 2025-11-12 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-24  
*How linked records, overlapping responsibilities and perceived legal exposure can make institutional classifications easier to preserve than to examine, correct or safely unwind.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Institutional data rarely remains where it began.

A report becomes a case note.

The case note informs a risk assessment.

The risk assessment is summarised in a referral.

The referral influences another organisation.

That organisation creates its own record, interpretation and decision.

By the time someone challenges the original information, several institutions may hold different versions of it for different purposes.

At that point, the problem is often described as data “ownership.”

That language is understandable but legally incomplete.

Within data-protection governance, the more useful questions are:

- Who determined why the personal data would be processed?
- Who determined how it would be processed?
- Were those decisions made alone or jointly?
- Which organisation merely processed information on another’s instructions?
- What lawful purpose justified each use?
- Which records contain direct facts, professional opinions or derived inferences?
- Who received the information?
- Which later decisions depended upon it?
- What must happen if the source information is corrected?

The Information Commissioner’s Office distinguishes controllers, joint controllers and processors by their actual roles in determining the purposes and means of processing. Two organisations handling the same information are not necessarily joint controllers if they use it independently for different purposes. Conversely, contractual labels do not necessarily settle responsibility where the practical reality shows otherwise.

Legal disentanglement is therefore not simply the deletion of one shared record.

It is the process of reconstructing:

> **Where information originated, how it changed, why it moved, who relied upon it, and which consequences must now be reviewed.**

That work can be administratively difficult.

Difficulty does not make continued error lawful, fair or harmless.

---

## ✨ Key Features

- Replaces vague ideas of data ownership with a clearer analysis of controllership, processing purpose and practical responsibility.
- Distinguishes source records, copied data, summaries, professional opinions, inferred classifications and downstream decisions.
- Explains why correction becomes harder when information has travelled across institutional boundaries.
- Examines how perceived legal and reputational exposure can shift institutions from risk assessment towards defensive risk aversion.
- Identifies the difference between correcting a source record and remedying decisions already influenced by it.
- Explains how distributed responsibility can produce inactivity without removing legal accountability.
- Recognises that different processing regimes and exemptions may apply in different institutional settings.
- Connects data accuracy, transparency, minimisation, impact assessment and human review to containment architecture.
- Treats the impact on the affected person as part of the legal risk rather than an inconvenient externality.
- Provides a practical method for mapping and disentangling a disputed information chain.

## 🧭 Disentanglement Begins With the Right Question

The first question is often framed as:

> “Who owns the data?”

That may conceal several different issues:

- Who created the original record?
- Who currently stores it?
- Who decides why it is used?
- Who has authority to alter it?
- Who has copied it into another system?
- Who has generated a new inference from it?
- Who has acted upon it?
- Who is responsible for explaining the processing?
- Who must correct downstream recipients if the information is inaccurate?

Physical possession does not answer all of those questions.

An institution may hold a copy without controlling the original source.

It may independently determine a new purpose and become responsible for that use.

A contractor may operate a system without deciding the purpose for which the information is processed.

Two institutions may make shared decisions and therefore have joint responsibilities.

The legal analysis follows the practical activity.

A contract can allocate tasks.

It cannot make the underlying reality disappear because the diagram was inconvenient.

## 🗂️ Six Things Commonly Collapsed Into “The Data”

Institutional disputes become harder to resolve when different information types are treated as one undifferentiated record.

### 1. Source Information

The original material may include:

- A statement.
- An incident report.
- A clinical observation.
- A complaint.
- A referral.
- A professional communication.
- A technical log.
- A document supplied by another organisation.

### 2. Recorded Fact

A record may state that an identifiable event occurred:

- A meeting took place.
- An email was sent.
- An arrest occurred.
- A referral was made.
- A decision was communicated.

The accuracy question concerns whether that recorded event is correct and sufficiently complete for the purpose.

### 3. Allegation

A record may preserve what someone alleged without establishing that the allegation was true.

The record can be accurate in stating that an allegation was made while becoming misleading if later readers treat the allegation as an established fact.

### 4. Professional Opinion

A clinician, safeguarding practitioner, police officer, regulator or other professional may record an assessment.

An opinion is not necessarily inaccurate merely because another person disputes it.

But the record should not disguise opinion as direct fact, omit significant context or imply a degree of certainty the evidence does not support.

### 5. Derived Inference

An organisation may infer:

- Vulnerability.
- Credibility.
- Behavioural risk.
- Political alignment.
- Likelihood of non-compliance.
- Association with another person.
- Future risk.

The inference is a new processing act, not merely passive storage of the original information.

### 6. Consequential Decision

The information may then contribute to:

- Restricted access.
- A safeguarding intervention.
- A professional review.
- Refusal of a service.
- Increased monitoring.
- A referral to another body.
- A decision about employment, education, housing or care.

Correcting the first layer does not automatically undo all six.

That is why disentanglement must trace the whole chain.

## 🕸️ The Data Lineage Problem

A simplified information lineage might look like this:

> Original event → source record → summary → shared referral → risk inference → institutional decision

Each transition can introduce:

- Omitted context.
- Different terminology.
- Increased certainty.
- A new processing purpose.
- A change of legal responsibility.
- A behavioural interpretation.
- Additional recipients.
- A new consequence.

The original record might say:

> “The individual sent several emails after receiving conflicting information.”

A summary may say:

> “Persistent contact following advice.”

A referral may say:

> “Escalating pattern of contact.”

A later risk record may say:

> “Known history of concerning escalation.”

Nothing has necessarily been fabricated.

The meaning has still changed.

The legal and governance questions are therefore not confined to whether the first sentence was technically accurate.

They include whether later processing remained:

- Fair.
- Accurate in context.
- Necessary.
- Transparent where required.
- Limited to an appropriate purpose.
- Proportionate to its consequences.
- Open to meaningful correction.

An information chain can preserve every word and still lose the truth through compression.

## ⚖️ Controllers, Joint Controllers and Processors

Under the UK data-protection framework, a controller determines the purposes and means of processing personal data.

A processor handles personal data on behalf of a controller and according to the controller’s instructions.

Joint controllers determine the purposes and means together.

These roles matter because they influence:

- Accountability.
- Transparency.
- Responses to individual rights requests.
- Security obligations.
- Arrangements between participating organisations.
- Responsibility for processors.
- Liability where processing causes damage.

Two organisations using the same information are not automatically joint controllers.

They may be separate controllers if each independently determines its own purpose.

Equally, responsibility cannot always be avoided by claiming that another organisation supplied the information.

A receiving institution that chooses to use information for its own decision may acquire responsibilities for that use.

The relevant questions include:

1. Who decided the purpose?
2. Who decided the essential means?
3. Who could decide whether the processing continued?
4. Who selected the information used?
5. Who determined the consequences?
6. Did the organisations make those decisions together or independently?
7. Was another organisation acting only on instructions?

The answer may differ across stages of the same information chain.

There may be no single controller for “the whole situation.”

That makes accurate mapping more important, not less.

## 🧩 Shared Information Does Not Mean Shared Purpose

The same record may be used differently by different organisations.

For example:

- A healthcare provider may use information to provide treatment.
- A university may use related information to make an educational decision.
- A police force may process information for a law-enforcement purpose.
- A regulator may use it to assess professional or institutional compliance.
- A contractor may operate software on behalf of one of those bodies.
- A complaints organisation may process the record to review a disputed decision.

The legal basis, purpose, necessity and applicable safeguards may differ at every stage.

It is therefore unsafe to assume:

> “Organisation A was allowed to hold this information, so Organisation B was allowed to use it for anything connected with the person.”

Lawful collection for one purpose does not automatically make every later use appropriate.

Nor does an information-sharing relationship eliminate the need to ask:

- Why was this recipient included?
- What did it need to know?
- Was the information accurate enough for the new purpose?
- Was all of the information necessary?
- Was the affected person properly informed, subject to lawful limitations?
- What controls governed onward disclosure?
- What review followed if the information changed?

Data sharing is an activity requiring governance.

It is not a ceremonial passing of the accountability parcel until the music stops.

## 🧬 Accuracy Is More Than Correct Spelling

The UK GDPR accuracy principle requires reasonable steps to ensure that personal data is not incorrect or misleading as to matters of fact. The ICO also states that organisations must carefully consider challenges to accuracy and take reasonable steps to correct or erase information found to be incorrect or misleading.

Accuracy can involve:

- Whether an event occurred.
- Whether a date is correct.
- Whether the right person has been identified.
- Whether an allegation is clearly labelled as an allegation.
- Whether an outcome has been updated.
- Whether a record omits context necessary to prevent it becoming misleading.
- Whether a historical classification remains current.
- Whether a professional opinion is presented with appropriate qualification.
- Whether a summary overstates the source material.

The right to rectification may also permit incomplete personal data to be completed, depending on the purpose of the processing, including through a supplementary statement.

That does not create an unrestricted right to rewrite every professional opinion.

It does mean an institution must properly examine whether the record is factually accurate, complete enough for its use and presented in a way that does not mislead later decision-makers.

A record can be accurate as a quotation and misleading as an institutional conclusion.

## 🔄 Correction Must Travel

When inaccurate or materially misleading information has been shared, correcting the source record may not be enough.

The organisation should consider:

- Who received the original information?
- Whether recipients still retain it.
- Whether it was incorporated into another record.
- Whether it generated a derived risk classification.
- Whether it influenced a consequential decision.
- Whether recipients need to be informed of the correction.
- Whether later decisions require reconsideration.
- Whether the old information remains visible in archives, search results or automated systems.
- Whether a corrected record has been linked clearly to the disputed version.

The ICO’s records-management guidance identifies further processing by third parties as a risk where recipients are not informed promptly that information is inaccurate.

Correction therefore has at least three layers:

1. **Correct the information.**
2. **Correct its circulation.**
3. **Review what the incorrect information caused.**

The third is frequently the difficult one.

An institution may be willing to alter a sentence while resisting any examination of decisions made because of that sentence.

That is clerical correction without substantive disentanglement.

## 🧠 The Difference Between a Disputed Opinion and an Inaccurate Fact

Institutions often defend a contested record by saying:

> “This was the professional’s opinion at the time.”

That may be legally relevant.

It is not necessarily the end of the analysis.

A sound review should distinguish:

- The factual material available to the professional.
- The professional’s recorded observations.
- The interpretation placed upon those observations.
- The degree of certainty expressed.
- Material evidence omitted from the assessment.
- Whether later information changed the position.
- Whether the opinion was used for a purpose beyond its original context.
- Whether downstream readers treated the opinion as settled fact.

An opinion genuinely held at the time may still be:

- Poorly evidenced.
- Outside the author’s expertise.
- Based on inaccurate information.
- Presented too confidently.
- Misleading when separated from its context.
- Outdated.
- Inappropriately reused.

The remedy may not always be erasure.

It may require:

- Qualification.
- Correction of underlying facts.
- Addition of the person’s dispute.
- A supplementary statement.
- Updated professional review.
- Restrictions on further reliance.
- Reconsideration of downstream decisions.

The law does not require institutions to pretend disagreement never occurred.

It does require them to govern consequential personal information responsibly.

## 🧭 Legal Disentanglement

Legal disentanglement means identifying the rights, duties and decision-making authority attached to each part of an information chain.

A practical disentanglement exercise should map:

| Question | What Must Be Identified |
|---|---|
| Source | Where did the information originate? |
| Status | Is it fact, allegation, opinion, inference or decision? |
| Purpose | Why did each organisation process it? |
| Role | Was each participant a controller, joint controller or processor for that activity? |
| Authority | What lawful basis or statutory function supported the processing? |
| Sharing | Which recipients received the information, and why? |
| Transformation | How did summaries, labels or inferences alter its meaning? |
| Consequence | Which decisions relied on it? |
| Accuracy | What is disputed, incomplete, outdated or misleading? |
| Correction | Who can amend each record? |
| Propagation | Who must be told that the source information changed? |
| Remedy | Which downstream decisions require review? |

This work resembles untangling a set of cables after several departments have each helpfully added another adapter.

The fact that the arrangement still carries current does not mean anybody can explain where it is going.

## ⚠️ From Legal Risk Assessment to Legal Risk Aversion

Institutions must consider legal risk.

They may face:

- Data-protection obligations.
- Confidentiality requirements.
- Safeguarding duties.
- Litigation.
- Professional regulation.
- Employment responsibilities.
- Public-law duties.
- Contractual obligations.
- Security concerns.
- Disclosure restrictions.
- Duties owed to more than one person.

A responsible assessment weighs those competing considerations.

Risk aversion is different.

It occurs when the institution primarily asks:

> “Which action creates the least visible exposure for us?”

Rather than:

> “Which lawful and proportionate action best addresses the competing harms?”

Signs may include:

- Repeated referral to legal teams without a substantive decision.
- Continued processing because deletion appears risky.
- Refusal to correct because other organisations already hold copies.
- Indefinite delay while responsibility is debated.
- Treating nondisclosure as the safest default.
- Preserving an earlier classification because reversing it would expose institutional inconsistency.
- Prioritising defensibility over factual accuracy.
- Allowing procedure to continue after its purpose has become unclear.

Legal advice can support lawful decision-making.

It should not become a holding pen where unresolved duties are fed occasionally and kept away from daylight.

## ⚖️ Institutional Risk and Individual Risk

A recurring imbalance arises when institutions compare different categories of harm without treating them equally.

Institutional concerns may include:

- Litigation.
- Regulatory criticism.
- Reputational damage.
- Administrative cost.
- Disclosure of internal disagreement.
- Precedent.
- Political attention.
- The need to revisit earlier decisions.

The person affected may face:

- Loss of healthcare.
- Interrupted education.
- Unemployment.
- Housing insecurity.
- Continuing exposure to harm.
- Damage to professional standing.
- Loss of privacy.
- Restricted access to services.
- Behavioural classification.
- Prolonged uncertainty.
- Inability to correct a record influencing their life.

Both sets of risks can be legally relevant.

But they are not equivalent simply because each can be entered into a risk register.

A fair assessment should identify:

- Who bears each risk.
- How serious it is.
- How likely it is.
- Whether it is reversible.
- Who has the power to reduce it.
- Whether the institution is transferring its risk onto the person.
- What happens if no decision is made.

Institutional caution is not neutral where the consequences of delay continue landing on somebody else.

## 🕸️ Responsibility Diffusion

Linked data environments make responsibility easier to distribute.

An institution may say:

- “We did not create the record.”
- “We cannot alter another organisation’s information.”
- “We relied on professional advice.”
- “The system generated the classification.”
- “The decision was made elsewhere.”
- “We only received a summary.”
- “We are waiting for another process to conclude.”
- “The contractor operates the platform.”
- “Legal responsibility is unclear.”

Some statements may be accurate.

None necessarily answers whether the institution remains responsible for its own use of the information.

A receiving organisation may be unable to change the source record.

It may still need to:

- Record that accuracy is disputed.
- Reassess its own inference.
- Limit further use.
- Seek clarification.
- Review a decision.
- Inform an appropriate recipient.
- Explain its own processing.
- Stop treating uncertain information as established fact.

Responsibility can be distributed.

It does not therefore evaporate.

## 🌀 Ambiguity as Institutional Protection

Legal complexity can generate genuine uncertainty.

Different regimes may apply to:

- General UK GDPR processing.
- Law-enforcement processing.
- Intelligence-services processing.
- Health information.
- Safeguarding information.
- Employment records.
- Regulatory investigations.
- Material protected by legal professional privilege.
- Information concerning other people.

The applicable rights, restrictions and exemptions may differ.

But complexity can also become a form of containment when nobody will explain:

- Which regime applies.
- Which organisation holds responsibility.
- Whether a decision exists.
- What information can be disclosed.
- What route is available for correction.
- When the position will be reviewed.
- What consequences remain active.

A lawful restriction on disclosure does not automatically justify total procedural opacity.

Where full information cannot be provided, institutions should still consider what meaningful explanation can safely and lawfully be given.

## 🐢 Proxy Risk and Legal Consequence

A behavioural proxy may begin as a low-confidence indicator.

Legal and administrative processing can make it increasingly consequential.

For example:

1. A communication pattern is interpreted as concerning.
2. The interpretation enters a professional record.
3. Another organisation receives a summary.
4. The summary informs a formal risk classification.
5. That classification affects access to a service.
6. The resulting distress is recorded as further behavioural evidence.
7. The expanded record is shared again.

The legal issue is not limited to the first observation.

It includes:

- Whether the inference was justified.
- Whether the data was adequate for the purpose.
- Whether the sharing was necessary.
- Whether the person was treated fairly.
- Whether the record remained accurate in context.
- Whether consequential decisions were reviewable.
- Whether correction propagated through the chain.

A proxy does not become a fact by acquiring more recipients.

See [🐢 Behavioural Proxies All the Way Down](./🐢_behavioural_proxies_all_the_way_down.md) for the wider classification problem.

## 🧮 Automated and Assisted Decisions

Risk software may support:

- Triage.
- Prioritisation.
- Fraud detection.
- Safeguarding.
- Allocation of services.
- Moderation.
- Security assessments.
- Professional review.
- Identification of unusual patterns.

The legal analysis depends partly on whether the system:

- Merely supplies information.
- Recommends an outcome.
- Determines the outcome in practice.
- Operates without meaningful human involvement.
- Produces legal or similarly significant effects.
- Processes special category data.
- Combines information from several sources.

Following the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, the UK framework contains revised rules for significant decisions based solely on automated processing, including profiling, together with safeguards. High-risk processing may also require a data protection impact assessment.

A human signature does not necessarily prove that human review was meaningful.

Relevant questions include:

- Could the reviewer understand the output?
- Could they disagree with it?
- Did they examine the underlying information?
- Were alternative explanations available?
- Did the organisation measure errors and unequal effects?
- Could the affected person make representations?
- Could the decision be reconsidered through human intervention?

A person is not meaningfully “in the loop” if their only function is to press approve beneath an output they cannot evaluate.

See [🧮 DeepThought DataGov Problem](./🧮_deepthought_datagov_problem.md) for the problem of computational answers detached from adequately defined governance questions.

## 🧪 Data Protection Impact Assessments

A data protection impact assessment helps organisations identify and minimise risks arising from proposed processing.

The ICO identifies several forms of processing that may require or strongly indicate the need for a DPIA, including combinations involving:

- Innovative technologies.
- Large-scale profiling.
- Data matching.
- Tracking.
- Invisible processing.
- Decisions affecting access to services or opportunities.
- Processing likely to create high risks to individuals’ rights and freedoms.

A useful DPIA should address more than technical security.

It should consider:

- What harm an incorrect classification could cause.
- Whether data from different contexts can safely be combined.
- Whether the proposed proxy measures what it claims to measure.
- Which groups may be disproportionately affected.
- Whether the processing is necessary.
- Whether less intrusive alternatives exist.
- How people can obtain human review.
- How inaccurate data will be corrected.
- How corrections will reach downstream recipients.
- What happens when the system or purpose changes.
- Who remains accountable across the supply chain.

A DPIA completed after the architecture has already hardened is less an impact assessment than an archaeological survey.

The useful moment is before the harm becomes infrastructure.

## 🧬 Correction as a Systems Process

Using the life-sciences analogy, correcting a source record resembles correcting the underlying sequence.

But institutional decisions may already have expressed that information into different forms.

The source information may have become:

- A summary.
- A risk flag.
- A referral.
- A behavioural interpretation.
- A restriction.
- A downstream record.
- A decision made by another organisation.

Correcting the “DNA” does not automatically remove every “protein” already produced.

The governance system must identify:

- Where the information was transcribed.
- How it was edited.
- Where it travelled.
- Which frameworks translated it.
- What institutional responses it produced.
- Which effects remain active.

This is why legal disentanglement is more than database housekeeping.

It is information lineage plus consequential review.

The lab jockey’s question remains useful:

> **If the specimen was wrong, which results, diagnoses and treatment decisions now need to be reconsidered?**

## 🛠️ A Practical Disentanglement Protocol

### Step 1: Define the Disputed Information

Identify the exact statement, classification, omission or inference under challenge.

Avoid attempting to correct “the whole narrative” at once.

### Step 2: Identify the Source

Establish:

- Who created it.
- When it was created.
- What evidence was available.
- Whether it was fact, allegation, opinion or inference.

### Step 3: Map the Copies

Identify where the information was:

- Reproduced.
- Summarised.
- Quoted.
- Imported.
- Linked.
- Shared.
- Incorporated into another record.

### Step 4: Map the Purposes

For each recipient, ask why the information was processed.

The same data may have different purposes and different controllers at different stages.

### Step 5: Map the Transformations

Compare the original wording with later descriptions.

Look for:

- Increased certainty.
- Omitted context.
- Behavioural reframing.
- Changed terminology.
- Broader allegations.
- New risk inferences.

### Step 6: Map the Consequences

Identify decisions that may have relied upon the information.

### Step 7: Determine the Corrective Route

Depending on the context, this may involve:

- Rectification.
- A supplementary statement.
- Restriction of processing.
- Updated professional review.
- A complaint.
- Independent oversight.
- Reconsideration of a consequential decision.
- Notification to previous recipients.

Not every right applies without qualification in every regime.

The applicable route depends on the processing context and any lawful restrictions or exemptions.

### Step 8: Verify Propagation

Establish whether corrections reached:

- Internal teams.
- External recipients.
- Linked systems.
- Decision-makers.
- Contractors.
- Later reviewers.

### Step 9: Review the Outcome

Ask whether changing the source information should alter any continuing decision, restriction or risk classification.

### Step 10: Record Residual Uncertainty

Where a dispute cannot be fully resolved, the remaining uncertainty should be visible rather than silently converted into institutional certainty.

## 🔍 Questions for Reviewers

When reviewing linked information and risk perception, ask:

1. What is the original source?
2. Is the disputed material fact, allegation, opinion or inference?
3. Which organisation determined each processing purpose?
4. Are the participating organisations separate or joint controllers for that activity?
5. Which entities act as processors?
6. What information was shared?
7. Why was each recipient included?
8. How did the description change while travelling?
9. What decisions relied upon it?
10. Is the information accurate and sufficiently complete for those purposes?
11. Has a challenge to accuracy been properly considered?
12. Were recipients informed of a material correction?
13. Did any automated or assisted system generate a further inference?
14. Was human review genuine?
15. What risks arise from correcting the record?
16. What risks arise from leaving it unchanged?
17. Who bears each risk?
18. Does any continuing restriction remain necessary and proportionate?
19. What independent review is available?
20. What would substantive remedy require?

The decisive question is not:

> “Would correction create administrative complications?”

It is:

> **“What continuing harm is produced by refusing to correct, qualify or reassess information that may be wrong?”**

## 🧀 When Legal and Procedural Holes Align

A distributed data problem becomes more serious when:

- One organisation creates an ambiguous record.
- Another treats it as established fact.
- A third derives a behavioural classification.
- A contractor operates the system containing the flag.
- A fourth organisation makes a consequential decision.
- The affected person cannot see the full chain.
- Each participant points towards another body.
- A correction is made at source but not propagated.
- Downstream restrictions remain active.
- No one reassesses the cumulative effect.

Each organisation may possess a partial legal explanation.

The person still experiences one continuous outcome.

See [🧀 When Process Holes Align](./🧀_when_process_holes_align.md) for the broader account of cumulative safeguard failure.

## 🪼 Disentanglement Must Lead Somewhere

The purpose of disentanglement is not to produce a more sophisticated map of an unresolved problem.

It should enable:

- Correction.
- Qualification.
- Restriction of inappropriate use.
- Review of consequential decisions.
- Restoration of necessary support.
- Clear allocation of responsibility.
- Independent scrutiny.
- A safe and intelligible route to resolution.

A system has not remedied an inaccurate classification merely because it can now explain which seven organisations copied it.

Administrative archaeology is useful.

The person still needs the building made safe.
