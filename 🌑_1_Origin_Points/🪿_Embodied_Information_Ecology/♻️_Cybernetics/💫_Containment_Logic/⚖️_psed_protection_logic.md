# ⚖️ PSED Protection Logic

**First created:** 2025-11-18 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-23  
*How the Public Sector Equality Duty should protect affected groups through informed decision-making, and how selective analysis turns an equality safeguard into another institutional blind spot.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The **Public Sector Equality Duty**, commonly shortened to **PSED**, requires public authorities and other bodies exercising public functions to consider how their decisions affect people with relevant protected characteristics.

It is not a requirement to produce one politically preferred answer.

It is not a requirement to make every group equally happy.

It is not a permission slip for deciding that one community’s safety matters and another community’s safety is administratively inconvenient.

The duty exists to ensure equality implications are recognised before decisions are made and remain visible as policies operate in practice.

That includes decisions concerning:

- healthcare;
- safeguarding;
- education;
- policing;
- public-order policy;
- investment and procurement;
- staff conduct;
- communications;
- information sharing;
- complaints;
- access to public services.

The key question is not:

> “Can the institution identify a group it says it was trying to protect?”

It is:

> “Did the institution properly consider the relevant equality implications of what it was proposing to do?”

A public authority cannot meaningfully protect equality by first deciding which affected people count.

## ⚖️ What the Duty Requires

Section 149 of the **Equality Act 2010** requires relevant decision-makers to have due regard to the need to:

1. Eliminate unlawful discrimination, harassment, victimisation and other conduct prohibited by the Act.

2. Advance equality of opportunity between people who share a relevant protected characteristic and those who do not.

3. Foster good relations between people who share a relevant protected characteristic and those who do not.

The duty applies across England, Scotland and Wales.

The general duty is shared across those jurisdictions, although some additional specific duties differ.

It applies to:

- listed public authorities; and
- other organisations when they are exercising public functions.

This distinction matters where services are delivered through:

- private contractors;
- commissioned providers;
- voluntary organisations;
- external technology suppliers;
- outsourced safeguarding arrangements;
- contracted healthcare or custodial services.

A public function does not stop having equality implications because someone moved part of it into a procurement contract.

---

## 🧬 Which Characteristics Are Relevant

The general duty principally concerns:

- age;
- disability;
- gender reassignment;
- pregnancy and maternity;
- race;
- religion or belief;
- sex;
- sexual orientation.

Marriage and civil partnership also fall within the duty in the more limited context of eliminating unlawful discrimination.

The categories require careful application.

For example:

- Jewish identity can engage protections relating to religion and, depending on the circumstances, race.
- Muslim identity can engage protection relating to religion.
- Palestinian, Arab and other identities may engage protection relating to race, nationality or ethnic or national origins, depending on the facts.
- Disability may shape how a person experiences a policy, accesses support or navigates a complaints system.
- A person may experience several relevant forms of disadvantage at the same time.

Political disagreement is not automatically interchangeable with a protected characteristic.

Nor should assumptions about political beliefs be attached to someone simply because of their religion, ethnicity, nationality or family background.

A person does not become an official spokesperson for a government because an administrator has decided their name sounds like it belongs in the same spreadsheet.

---

## 🧭 What “Due Regard” Means

Due regard requires decision-makers to take relevant equality considerations seriously when they are exercising their functions.

This means:

- identifying who may be affected;
- understanding how the decision could affect them;
- considering the available evidence;
- recognising relevant differences between groups;
- examining whether adverse impacts can be reduced;
- making a decision with those considerations in mind;
- reviewing impacts when circumstances change.

The level of analysis should be proportionate to the decision and the seriousness of its potential effects.

The duty does not prescribe one mandatory template.

A document labelled “equality impact assessment” does not automatically establish compliance.

Equally, the absence of that particular document does not automatically prove a failure.

What matters is whether equality implications were genuinely considered when the decision was made.

> Due regard is a way of thinking and deciding.
>
> It is not a decorative form completed after everybody has already agreed what they wanted to do.

---

## ⏳ Timing Matters

Equality considerations should inform a proposal while the decision is still capable of changing.

A public authority should not:

1. Decide what it intends to do.
2. Announce the decision.
3. Encounter criticism.
4. Produce an equality explanation designed to defend the result.

That reverses the purpose of the duty.

The relevant analysis should occur early enough to influence:

- policy design;
- consultation;
- implementation;
- mitigation;
- review;
- withdrawal or amendment where necessary.

The duty is also continuing.

An initial assessment may become inadequate if:

- new evidence emerges;
- affected groups change;
- implementation produces unexpected consequences;
- a contractor adopts a different approach;
- the surrounding risk environment changes;
- complaints reveal impacts that were not previously considered.

An equality assessment is not a magical administrative object that remains valid forever because somebody signed it in a calmer month.

---

## 🧩 Equal Regard Does Not Mean Identical Outcomes

Equality law does not require every group to receive precisely the same treatment regardless of context.

Different groups can experience:

- different forms of disadvantage;
- different levels of exposure;
- different barriers to participation;
- different risks of harassment or exclusion;
- different practical consequences from the same policy.

A policy may therefore require differentiated responses.

Examples include:

- accessible communication for disabled service users;
- consideration of religious observance;
- targeted measures addressing a specific pattern of harassment;
- adjustments for pregnancy or maternity;
- additional safeguards for groups facing a documented risk.

The question is not whether every group receives an identical response.

The question is whether differences are considered honestly, supported by evidence and connected to a legitimate protective purpose.

> Fairness is not achieved by pretending that unequal circumstances become equal when entered into the same form.

---

## 🪞 Protected Groups Are Not Political Monoliths

A recurring equality failure occurs when institutions treat a protected group as though it has one political position.

For example:

- Jewish people do not all hold the same views about Israel or Palestine.
- Muslim people do not all hold the same views about foreign policy.
- Palestinian people are not interchangeable with any political movement.
- Opposition to a government is not automatically opposition to a religious or ethnic group.
- Support for Palestinian rights does not automatically disclose a person’s religion, nationality or beliefs about Jewish people.
- Concern about antisemitism does not automatically establish support for a particular government or political programme.

When institutions collapse identity into presumed political alignment, they can produce several harms simultaneously:

- stereotyping;
- exclusion;
- compelled association;
- mistaken risk classification;
- suppression of internal diversity;
- unequal protection;
- inaccurate consultation;
- distorted public decision-making.

A policy framed as protecting Jewish people may harm Jewish people who do not share the political assumptions embedded within it.

A policy framed as preventing community tension may disproportionately burden Palestinian, Arab or Muslim people while presenting itself as neutral.

A group cannot be meaningfully protected if the institution has replaced its actual members with an imagined collective viewpoint.

---

## 🧠 Distinguishing Harm, Disagreement and Offence

Public authorities should distinguish between:

- unlawful discrimination;
- harassment;
- threats;
- exclusion from services;
- targeted intimidation;
- adverse treatment connected to a protected characteristic;
- disagreement with a political position;
- criticism of a government;
- discomfort caused by contested public issues;
- speculation about how an unidentified group might react.

These categories can overlap.

They are not interchangeable.

A person expressing disagreement is not necessarily creating an unsafe environment.

Equally, genuinely threatening or discriminatory conduct does not become acceptable merely because it is presented as political expression.

The analysis must examine:

- the actual conduct;
- the relevant context;
- who was affected;
- what evidence exists;
- whether a protected characteristic is implicated;
- whether restrictions are justified and proportionate.

> An institution should not translate “someone might object” into “equality law requires us to silence this.”

Good relations are not produced by treating the most assertive complaint as an automatic veto.

---

## 🫀 Advancing Equality of Opportunity

Advancing equality of opportunity involves attention to matters such as:

- disadvantage associated with protected characteristics;
- needs that differ between groups;
- barriers to participation;
- underrepresentation;
- practical obstacles to accessing services or public life.

For disabled people, meeting different needs may require additional steps.

This has consequences for:

- appointment systems;
- accessible communications;
- complaints procedures;
- physical access;
- availability of support;
- digital-only services;
- inflexible reporting requirements;
- safeguarding arrangements;
- decision-making processes that presume identical capacity.

A disabled survivor may be theoretically entitled to use a complaints process while being practically unable to access it.

That gap is not resolved by repeating that the same procedure is available to everybody.

A process can be formally open and functionally closed.

---

## 🕊️ Fostering Good Relations

Fostering good relations includes tackling prejudice and promoting understanding between groups.

It does not mean:

- suppressing every difficult public discussion;
- pretending political differences do not exist;
- assigning a single representative to an entire religious or ethnic community;
- rewarding whichever group is most institutionally connected;
- treating criticism of state conduct as inherently discriminatory;
- making minority staff responsible for managing everybody else’s discomfort.

A defensible approach should ask:

- What prejudice is actually being addressed?
- Which groups may be affected?
- What conduct is creating a problem?
- What evidence supports the proposed response?
- Could the intervention itself reinforce stereotypes?
- Are less restrictive alternatives available?
- Are dissenting voices within the affected communities being heard?

Good relations require understanding.

They cannot be manufactured by administratively flattening everyone into the least inconvenient version of themselves.

---

## 💸 Investment, Procurement and Institutional Association

Questions about investment, procurement and institutional association can engage equality considerations when they affect:

- access to public services;
- confidence in public institutions;
- experiences of exclusion;
- inter-group relations;
- perceptions of discriminatory treatment;
- staff welfare;
- the safety of affected communities.

For example, a public body considering an investment or supplier relationship may receive concerns that an association with alleged harmful conduct abroad has consequences for communities in Britain.

Those arguments may be relevant to an equality assessment.

But several distinctions must be preserved.

The PSED:

- does not automatically require divestment;
- does not automatically prohibit divestment;
- does not decide whether an allegation about overseas conduct is true;
- does not replace procurement rules, financial obligations or other legal duties;
- does not permit a decision-maker to ignore impacts on other affected groups;
- does require relevant equality implications to be considered as part of the decision.

The defensible position is not:

> “Equality law gives us the answer.”

It is:

> “Equality law requires us to understand how the available answers may affect people.”

---

## 🧮 Assessing Both Action and Inaction

An equality assessment can fail if it considers only the consequences of acting.

It may also need to consider the consequences of maintaining the existing position.

For example:

- retaining an institutional relationship may affect one set of communities;
- terminating it may affect another;
- imposing a restriction may reduce one perceived risk while creating another;
- refusing to intervene may allow an existing pattern of exclusion to continue.

A useful comparison asks:

| Option | Potential equality consideration |
|---|---|
| Maintain the existing arrangement. | Does inaction preserve or worsen an existing disadvantage? |
| Change the arrangement. | Would the change create new exclusion, stereotyping or disproportionate impacts? |
| Adopt a partial restriction. | Is the restriction coherent, targeted and justified? |
| Provide additional safeguards. | Can harm be reduced without unnecessary exclusion? |
| Consult affected groups. | Are the people consulted genuinely representative of relevant differences? |
| Delay the decision. | Does delay itself impose unequal or foreseeable burdens? |

The goal is not to generate artificial equivalence.

Some impacts may be more serious, more immediate or better evidenced than others.

The goal is to avoid pretending that only one side of a decision has consequences.

## 🏥 Healthcare, Staff Expression and Patient Safety

Healthcare organisations may face difficult questions about:

- political symbols;
- staff dress;
- professional neutrality;
- patient confidence;
- discrimination;
- harassment;
- community tensions;
- safety within clinical settings.

Those questions should be approached through evidence and context.

A policy restricting a particular symbol may affect:

- staff connected to a specific nationality, ethnicity or community;
- religious or ethnic minorities;
- patients seeking reassurance that their identities will be respected;
- employees expressing humanitarian concern;
- people with differing views within the same protected group.

A legitimate analysis should distinguish:

- a symbol used as targeted intimidation;
- a symbol used to express solidarity;
- a symbol wrongly assumed to communicate hostility;
- a restriction that applies inconsistently;
- an intervention based on evidence;
- an intervention based primarily on anticipated reputational criticism.

The existence of complaints does not automatically determine the correct response.

Nor does a claimed protective purpose remove the need to examine who is burdened by the restriction.

> A public authority should not solve a hypothetical equality problem by creating a visible, measurable one for someone else.

---

## 🐼 Policing, Safeguarding and Risk Classification

PSED considerations also arise when public functions depend on:

- behavioural indicators;
- risk flags;
- policing intelligence;
- safeguarding referrals;
- counter-extremism assessments;
- automated prioritisation;
- information sharing;
- public-order decisions.

Equality risks can develop where:

- cultural or religious expression is misread as suspicious;
- disability-related communication is interpreted as evasive;
- trauma responses are classified as aggression;
- political speech becomes a proxy for ethnicity or religion;
- minority communities experience disproportionate scrutiny;
- unverified allegations circulate between agencies;
- a person’s identity influences whether their account is believed.

These risks may persist even when a rule appears neutral on its face.

A neutral-looking process can still produce unequal consequences if it relies on biased data, selective interpretation or proxy indicators closely associated with protected characteristics.

The relevant question is not simply:

> “Does the policy mention a protected group?”

It is:

> “How does the policy actually behave when applied to different people?”

---

## 📊 Data Collection Is Not Automatically Protection

Public authorities may need sufficient information to understand how decisions affect different groups.

That does not mean unlimited collection is justified.

Equality monitoring should remain attentive to:

- necessity;
- lawful processing;
- data minimisation;
- accuracy;
- transparency;
- security;
- purpose limitation;
- retention;
- access controls.

Information about ethnicity, religion, disability or other sensitive personal circumstances can help identify discrimination.

It can also expose vulnerable people if collected, combined or shared without adequate safeguards.

Particular caution is needed where protected characteristics are:

- inferred from names;
- deduced from location;
- estimated from online behaviour;
- associated with political expression;
- predicted through automated systems;
- reconstructed from multiple datasets.

A system cannot defend excessive profiling by announcing that the profiling has been given an equality-themed folder.

> Data collected to protect people should not become a convenient map of who is easiest to expose.

---

## 🧾 Outsourcing Does Not Remove Responsibility

Public bodies increasingly rely on:

- private suppliers;
- software platforms;
- risk-assessment tools;
- external consultants;
- contracted safeguarding services;
- outsourced complaint handling;
- data-sharing partnerships.

This creates an accountability problem when equality implications are split between organisations.

A public body may assume:

- the supplier checked the model;
- the contractor reviewed the policy;
- the safeguarding team assessed affected groups;
- another agency verified the data;
- the complaints department considered access barriers.

If nobody examines the combined outcome, the equality safeguard becomes another hole in the process.

Contracting out a public function does not allow the commissioning authority to assume equality considerations have disappeared into someone else’s terms and conditions.

Relevant questions include:

- Who assessed the equality impact?
- What evidence was available?
- Did the assessment examine the real-world operation of the service?
- Can the authority inspect the supplier’s methods?
- Can affected people challenge inaccurate or discriminatory outcomes?
- Who remains responsible when different systems interact?

The contract may distribute tasks.

It should not dissolve accountability.

---

## 🕳️ Failure Mode: Selective Equality Analysis

Selective equality analysis occurs when decision-makers recognise the needs of some affected groups while overlooking others.

It can include:

- consulting only organisations aligned with a preferred interpretation;
- assuming one representative speaks for an entire community;
- treating politically prominent concerns as more credible than documented everyday harm;
- overlooking disabled people because the policy was framed primarily around race or religion;
- ignoring minority views within a protected group;
- collapsing nationality, religion and political opinion into one category;
- considering the consequences of intervention but not inaction;
- failing to examine whether a policy produces unequal practical burdens.

The resulting decision may look equality-conscious.

It may even contain the correct vocabulary.

But an incomplete map cannot support a reliable balancing exercise.

> If a group disappears from the assessment, its safety cannot meaningfully appear in the decision.

---

## 🔁 Failure Mode: Equality as Institutional Containment

An equality framework can also become a mechanism for managing institutional discomfort.

This happens when equality language is used to:

- discourage complaints;
- suppress discussion of discrimination;
- defend decisions made without adequate consultation;
- restrict minority expression;
- prioritise institutional reputation over lived experience;
- avoid acknowledging disagreement within protected groups;
- justify secrecy without explaining the underlying risk;
- reclassify affected people as sources of tension.

The shift is subtle.

A duty designed to expose unequal impacts becomes a vocabulary for concealing them.

Instead of asking:

> “Who is being disadvantaged?”

the institution asks:

> “Which account of equality makes our existing position easiest to defend?”

At that point, the safeguard is still present in the paperwork.

Its protective function has gone missing.

---

## 🧰 Diagnostic Questions

A serious equality analysis should ask:

1. **Function:** What public function or decision is being exercised?

2. **Authority:** Which organisation remains responsible for the decision?

3. **Characteristics:** Which relevant protected characteristics may be engaged?

4. **Groups:** Who may be affected directly, indirectly or through downstream implementation?

5. **Internal diversity:** Are groups being treated as politically or socially uniform?

6. **Evidence:** What information supports the assessment?

7. **Uncertainty:** Which assumptions remain untested?

8. **Impact:** What are the actual or foreseeable consequences for different people?

9. **Disability:** Are practical barriers, communication needs or accessibility issues being overlooked?

10. **Alternatives:** Could the same objective be achieved with less harmful or less exclusionary measures?

11. **Data:** Is sensitive information being collected and used proportionately?

12. **Contractors:** Have outsourced or supplier-controlled functions been considered?

13. **Timing:** Did equality analysis occur while the decision was still capable of changing?

14. **Review:** Is there a mechanism for identifying and correcting unexpected impacts?

15. **Protection:** Does the outcome improve substantive safety, or merely improve the institution’s explanation of itself?

These questions do not dictate one answer.

They make it harder to pretend that no question existed.

---

## 🪄 What Good Equality Governance Looks Like

A defensible public decision should demonstrate:

- clear identification of the decision under consideration;
- recognition of relevant protected characteristics;
- awareness of the people likely to be affected;
- evidence proportionate to the seriousness of the issue;
- attention to differences within and between groups;
- recognition of disability-related access needs;
- separation of protected identity from presumed political allegiance;
- consideration of both action and inaction where relevant;
- assessment of realistic alternatives;
- proportionate information handling;
- continuing review;
- accountability for outsourced functions;
- a genuine ability to revise the decision if new evidence emerges.

Good governance does not require pretending that every group faces the same risk.

It requires being honest about who faces which risk, why, and what the institution intends to do about it.

> Equality protection fails when it becomes selective visibility.
>
> The duty exists to make the missing people visible.
