# 🐼 Due Diligence and Operational Capacity in Policing

**First created:** 2025-10-11 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-23  
*Why effective police safeguards depend on accurate information, professional judgment, adequate capacity and independent correction—not simply the existence of policies, databases or oversight bodies.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Police governance depends on more than written rules.

A force may have:

- professional standards;
- information-management policies;
- safeguarding procedures;
- supervisory structures;
- complaints systems;
- equality duties;
- external inspection;
- independent oversight.

Those safeguards matter.

But their practical value depends on whether someone has the time, authority and information needed to use them.

An officer cannot check a record they cannot access.

A supervisor cannot correct a risk assessment they have never seen.

An investigator cannot distinguish a victim from a suspect if the database collapses both into the same operational category.

A regulator cannot identify an error that was never recorded.

An individual cannot challenge a decision if the available process cannot explain what needs to be challenged.

This node uses **due diligence** as an operational description of the care, verification and judgment required for lawful and effective policing.

It does not suggest that every policing failure falls under one standalone legal duty called “due diligence.”

The relevant obligations arise from the particular legal, professional, ethical and operational framework applicable to the decision.

The central point is straightforward:

> A safeguard is only as effective as the conditions that allow someone to operate it.

---

## ✨ Key Features

- Distinguishes formal compliance from operationally effective protection.
- Examines workload, staffing, supervision and decision-making capacity.
- Explains why police records must distinguish facts, allegations, opinions and assessments.
- Addresses the separation of victims, witnesses, suspects and convicted individuals.
- Examines data sharing, regional collaboration and third-party technology.
- Identifies the particular risks associated with trauma, disability and sensitive personal information.
- Explains how inherited classifications can become self-reinforcing.
- Distinguishes internal complaints handling from independent investigation.
- Connects leadership culture and conflicts of interest to front-line decision-making.
- Provides a practical framework for auditing police governance and operational capacity.

## 🧭 What Operational Due Diligence Means

In policing, operational diligence involves asking:

- What is the actual concern?
- What information supports it?
- Where did that information originate?
- Is it fact, allegation, opinion or inference?
- Who is affected?
- What legal power is being exercised?
- Is the response necessary and proportionate?
- What information is being shared?
- Who can access it?
- What might happen if the assessment is wrong?
- Can the person affected obtain a meaningful correction?
- Does the officer or team have the capacity to perform these checks?

This is not an argument that every officer must conduct a miniature public inquiry before taking routine action.

It is an argument that serious decisions should not become detached from basic verification.

The more consequential the action, the more important the underlying diligence.

> A police database can be an investigative tool.
>
> It cannot replace the obligation to ask whether the information inside it makes sense.

## 🏛️ Rules on Paper and Safeguards in Practice

A force may be able to demonstrate that it has:

- a policy for data accuracy;
- a safeguarding lead;
- a complaints procedure;
- an audit programme;
- an ethics framework;
- professional standards officers;
- a data protection officer;
- supervisory review.

That does not establish that each mechanism operates effectively.

A written rule may fail because:

- staff are unaware of it;
- workloads prevent compliance;
- systems make correction difficult;
- access rights are poorly designed;
- responsibility is split across organisations;
- managers discourage challenge;
- relevant records are missing;
- nobody checks whether the policy works in practice.

The difference is between:

> formal capacity;

and:

> operational capacity.

A force formally capable of correcting an error may remain practically incapable of doing so within a meaningful timeframe.

That gap is where rights disappear without anyone needing to announce that they have been removed.

---

## 🕳️ Time Poverty as a Governance Failure

Police work is often organised around immediate demand:

- emergency incidents;
- safeguarding referrals;
- public-order requirements;
- case preparation;
- detention;
- court deadlines;
- missing-person reports;
- digital evidence;
- intelligence processing;
- administrative recording.

When workloads exceed available capacity, tasks are prioritised.

That is unavoidable.

But some of the work most vulnerable to being deprioritised is also the work that prevents serious error.

Examples include:

- checking the provenance of a referral;
- reviewing an inherited risk flag;
- correcting a misleading summary;
- distinguishing witness evidence from officer opinion;
- contacting a vulnerable person in an accessible way;
- checking whether sensitive information was shared appropriately;
- documenting why an intervention is proportionate;
- escalating a conflict between partner organisations.

From the perspective of a pressured team, these may appear secondary.

From the perspective of the affected person, they may determine whether the system recognises them accurately.

> An officer who is never given time to check cannot reasonably be treated as a functioning check.

Workload pressure may explain a failure.

It does not automatically make the resulting harm acceptable.

## 🧠 Supervision Requires More Than a Signature

Supervision is often presented as the corrective layer between an individual decision and institutional harm.

Effective supervision should examine:

- the factual basis of the decision;
- the quality of the available evidence;
- alternative explanations;
- proportionality;
- safeguarding concerns;
- equality impacts;
- whether further inquiry is needed;
- whether the decision remains justified over time.

But supervisory approval can become symbolic where:

- supervisors manage too many cases;
- records are reviewed only through short summaries;
- the same assumptions are repeated at every level;
- the officer’s interpretation is treated as established fact;
- disagreement is discouraged;
- urgent queues displace reflective judgment.

A sign-off does not become independent review merely because someone with a different job title clicked the button.

> Supervision should introduce another perspective.
>
> If it only repeats the first one, the system has added a signature rather than a safeguard.

## 🧬 Facts, Allegations, Opinions and Assessments

Police records can contain several different kinds of information:

- verified facts;
- allegations;
- witness accounts;
- professional observations;
- intelligence;
- risk assessments;
- working hypotheses;
- third-party opinion;
- automated or model-generated outputs.

These categories are not interchangeable.

A person may report an allegation without it being established.

An officer may form an opinion without that opinion becoming a verified fact.

A risk assessment may justify further inquiry without proving that the person presents the risk described.

A database may preserve an interpretation long after the circumstances that generated it have changed.

The ICO’s law-enforcement guidance explains that records should, as far as possible, distinguish personal information based on fact from information based on opinion or assessment.

That distinction matters because decisions travel.

> Once an opinion loses the label identifying it as an opinion, it can return as a fact wearing a police reference number.

Operational diligence requires preserving:

- source;
- date;
- context;
- confidence;
- limitations;
- competing information;
- subsequent corrections.

A record can be administratively complete and still be evidentially misleading.

## 🧍 Victim, Witness, Suspect and Convicted Person

Law-enforcement records may concern people in different roles.

These can include:

- victims;
- witnesses;
- suspects;
- people convicted of offences;
- family members;
- complainants;
- safeguarding subjects;
- professionals;
- other individuals relevant to an investigation.

The ICO explains that, where relevant and as far as possible, police information should distinguish between different categories of people.

This does not mean a person can never appear in more than one capacity across different matters.

It means that relevant roles should not be collapsed into a misleading institutional identity.

Examples of harmful conflation include:

- a victim being treated as suspicious because their trauma history appears repeatedly in police records;
- a witness being classified as risky because they challenged an inaccurate account;
- a complainant becoming associated with the conduct they reported;
- a person’s involvement in a safeguarding process being mistaken for evidence of criminality;
- historical contact with police being presented without the context explaining why it occurred.

> Appearing in a police database does not tell you which side of the incident someone was on.

If the system cannot preserve that distinction, its apparent situational awareness may be built on a category error.

---

## 🩸 Rape, Serious Sexual Offences and Victim Privacy

Investigations involving rape and serious sexual offences can require highly sensitive information.

This may include:

- medical records;
- counselling notes;
- communications;
- family history;
- safeguarding reports;
- information about sexuality;
- evidence of disability;
- previous trauma;
- digital-device material.

Some information may be relevant to a legitimate investigative purpose.

That does not mean all available information should be collected, retained or circulated.

The ICO has published material specifically addressing the processing of victims’ personal data in rape and serious sexual offence investigations.

The governance concern is not theoretical.

A person seeking protection may experience further harm if sensitive information is:

- collected without a properly defined purpose;
- examined more broadly than necessary;
- shared with people who do not need it;
- repurposed for an unrelated assessment;
- interpreted without context;
- retained after the justification has changed;
- disclosed through weak administrative controls.

The more intimate the information, the more important the limits around access and use.

> A survivor does not surrender their entire private life merely because an institution requires evidence about an offence.

Protection cannot depend on turning vulnerability into a reusable administrative resource.

## 🗃️ Law-Enforcement Processing and the Correct Legal Framework

Police information may be processed under different legal regimes depending on the purpose.

Where a competent authority processes personal information for law-enforcement purposes, the relevant framework may be **Part 3 of the Data Protection Act 2018**.

Other processing may fall under the **UK GDPR** and the general processing provisions of the Data Protection Act.

The distinction depends on:

- who is processing the information;
- the purpose of the processing;
- the nature of the receiving organisation;
- whether the information is used for a law-enforcement purpose or another function.

Calling something “police data” does not automatically determine which rules apply.

Neither does moving information into a shared platform resolve the question.

Under the law-enforcement framework, relevant principles include:

- lawfulness and fairness;
- specified and legitimate purposes;
- information that is adequate, relevant and not excessive;
- accuracy;
- appropriate retention;
- security.

Sensitive processing requires additional attention and safeguards.

A force should be able to identify:

- why the information is being processed;
- which regime applies;
- what legal basis supports the activity;
- who can access the information;
- when it must be reviewed;
- how inaccurate information can be corrected.

A large database is not a legal basis.

## 🔐 Access Is Not the Same as Authorisation

A person’s ability to open a record does not establish that they should.

Operational access must be connected to:

- a legitimate role;
- a defined purpose;
- necessity;
- appropriate permissions;
- security requirements;
- auditability.

Access becomes more difficult to govern where:

- records are pooled across teams;
- systems integrate multiple organisations;
- regional units share infrastructure;
- external suppliers manage platforms;
- information is copied between applications;
- account permissions are inherited;
- access logs are incomplete or difficult to review.

The relevant distinction is between:

> technical capability;

and:

> authorised use.

A system that allows thousands of people to retrieve sensitive information may remain formally compliant only if access is genuinely controlled, justified and reviewable.

Otherwise, “need to know” becomes “technically possible.”

Those are not synonyms.

---

## 🛰️ Regional Collaboration and Shared Systems

Police work frequently crosses force boundaries.

Regional collaboration can support:

- organised-crime investigations;
- specialist intelligence;
- cybercrime;
- serious sexual offences;
- safeguarding;
- forensic capability;
- shared technical infrastructure.

These arrangements can improve operational capacity.

They can also complicate accountability.

Questions include:

- Which force originated the information?
- Which organisation controls the shared system?
- Who authorised access?
- Who decides whether the information is accurate?
- Which body responds to a correction request?
- Who investigates misuse?
- Who records onward disclosure?
- Who is responsible when several forces rely on the same error?

A regional arrangement does not erase local accountability.

Nor does local accountability fully explain what happens inside a shared operational environment.

> If five organisations use the same information but none can explain who owns the mistake, collaboration has created a governance seam.

That seam requires explicit management.

---

## 🧰 Third-Party Technology and Supplier Dependence

Police forces increasingly depend on external systems for:

- case management;
- intelligence analysis;
- data integration;
- search;
- digital evidence;
- communications;
- identity matching;
- risk assessment;
- information sharing.

A supplier may provide technical capability without controlling every policing decision.

But technical architecture can still shape:

- what information appears together;
- how a person is categorised;
- which records are visible;
- whether uncertainty remains attached;
- how quickly information spreads;
- how access is logged;
- whether corrections propagate;
- whether data can be exported or repurposed.

Relevant governance questions include:

- What does the supplier process?
- On whose instructions?
- Which organisations can access the platform?
- What happens when a record is corrected?
- Are data sources distinguishable?
- Are automated recommendations explainable?
- Can audit logs be independently inspected?
- What arrangements exist for security incidents?
- Who remains accountable for the policing purpose?

Procurement is not merely a purchasing decision.

It can determine whether the institution still understands the system it depends on.

A shiny platform cannot perform due diligence on behalf of the people who commissioned it.

---

## 🧭 Data Sharing Across Legal and Institutional Boundaries

Police may share information with:

- healthcare providers;
- local authorities;
- safeguarding teams;
- educational institutions;
- government departments;
- regulators;
- other police forces;
- regional units;
- contracted service providers.

Some information sharing is necessary.

A genuine safeguarding risk may require coordinated action.

But necessity has to be assessed.

Questions include:

- What is the purpose of the disclosure?
- Is the information relevant to that purpose?
- Does the recipient need the whole record or a limited extract?
- Is the information accurate and current?
- Does it contain sensitive material?
- Is the recipient operating under the same legal framework?
- What restrictions apply to onward sharing?
- Can the affected person’s role be understood correctly?
- What happens if the information later proves inaccurate?

A disclosure made for one purpose should not quietly become a reusable general-purpose profile.

> Multi-agency working becomes dangerous when each organisation assumes someone else already checked why the information was shared.

## 🔁 The Feedback Illusion

A policing system can appear accurate because it repeatedly encounters its own assumptions.

The pattern may look like this:

1. An initial concern is entered into a record.

2. The concern is shared with another team.

3. The second team treats it as established information.

4. A later interaction is interpreted through that assumption.

5. The resulting interpretation is added to the record.

6. Several agencies now appear to share the same concern.

7. Supervisors or regulators treat repetition as corroboration.

8. The originating assumption remains untested.

The system becomes more confident without becoming better informed.

This is particularly dangerous where the initial information concerns:

- safeguarding;
- political expression;
- mental health;
- disability;
- trauma;
- community identity;
- alleged risk to others.

A person’s distress at being misunderstood may then become further evidence supporting the misunderstanding.

> A system should not be permitted to count the consequences of its own error as independent proof that the error was correct.

## 🧩 When a Risk Flag Outlives Its Purpose

Risk classifications can be necessary.

They may help officers recognise:

- safeguarding concerns;
- threats to life;
- vulnerability;
- violent behaviour;
- access risks;
- investigative priorities.

But a flag should not become permanent simply because it is technically easy to preserve.

A meaningful review should consider:

- why the flag was created;
- whether the underlying information was verified;
- whether circumstances changed;
- whether an investigation concluded;
- whether the assessment remains necessary;
- whether the classification is proportionate;
- whether the person’s role was accurately recorded;
- whether the flag is influencing unrelated decisions.

The conclusion of an investigation does not automatically require every related record to disappear.

Retention and review depend on the applicable legal and operational framework.

But equally:

> A historical record is not a permanent licence to treat someone as though the original concern remains current.

Continued use requires continuing justification.

## 🫀 Trauma, Disability and Interpretive Error

Operational judgments are vulnerable to misunderstanding when officers or systems lack relevant context.

Examples include:

- trauma-related hypervigilance being interpreted as hostility;
- difficulty recalling events sequentially being treated as dishonesty;
- autistic communication being perceived as evasive;
- physical disability being overlooked during detention or transport;
- chronic pain affecting behaviour during an interview;
- repeated requests for clarification being recorded as aggression;
- a survivor’s distrust of institutions being treated as evidence of instability;
- religious or cultural practice being misread as suspicious.

None of these possibilities removes the need for genuine risk assessment.

They do require care in distinguishing:

- observed behaviour;
- interpretation;
- contextual explanation;
- evidence of actual risk.

A professional does not have to know every detail of someone’s history.

But they do need to recognise the limits of what a distressed interaction can establish.

> Misreading vulnerability as threat can turn a protective institution into the source of additional danger.

## ⚖️ Equality Is an Operational Requirement

Equality considerations are not confined to strategy documents.

They arise in:

- stop and search;
- arrest;
- detention;
- safeguarding;
- intelligence assessment;
- public-order policing;
- access to complaints;
- communication;
- disability adjustments;
- decisions about credibility;
- information sharing.

A process can appear neutral while affecting groups differently.

Examples include:

- relying on behavioural cues that disadvantage disabled people;
- associating political expression with religion or ethnicity;
- interpreting minority identity through security stereotypes;
- applying communication requirements that exclude people unable to use standard channels;
- treating a survivor’s distress as less credible than an institution’s polished account.

Operational diligence requires examining how a rule behaves in practice.

> A policy does not become fair simply because everyone is invited to experience the same misunderstanding.

## 📣 Leadership Tone and Organisational Culture

Front-line decisions are shaped by what senior leadership rewards.

If managers consistently prioritise:

- speed;
- case closure;
- positive statistics;
- operational loyalty;
- avoidance of criticism;

staff may learn that careful verification is less valued than producing an administratively useful result.

If leadership treats:

- ethics;
- accurate records;
- accessibility;
- challenge;
- safeguarding;
- disclosure;

as optional extras, those functions will deteriorate under pressure.

Culture is revealed not only in published values but in practical questions:

- Can an officer challenge a senior interpretation?
- Is time provided for record correction?
- Are mistakes acknowledged?
- Are whistleblowers protected?
- Is a vulnerable person’s account taken seriously?
- Are staff punished for raising inconvenient concerns?
- Are equality impacts discussed before decisions harden?

A force does not become ethical because the word “integrity” appears on its website.

Ethical practice requires a working environment in which integrity is survivable.

---

## 🧯 Conflict-of-Interest Architecture

Police work depends on close relationships between:

- forces;
- regional units;
- local authorities;
- government departments;
- prosecutors;
- contractors;
- professional bodies;
- partner agencies.

Those relationships can improve cooperation.

They can also complicate challenge.

A concern may implicate:

- a partner organisation;
- a senior manager;
- a shared supplier;
- an existing investigation;
- a regional collaboration;
- a politically sensitive programme;
- a previous institutional assurance.

The more interdependent the system, the greater the temptation to treat disclosure as a collective threat.

This does not require a coordinated decision to conceal wrongdoing.

It may arise through:

- professional loyalty;
- reputational anxiety;
- concern about relationships;
- uncertainty over authority;
- fear of disrupting an ongoing operation.

The safeguard is not the absence of relationships.

It is the presence of:

- declared interests;
- independent review;
- protected escalation;
- transparent responsibility;
- authority to intervene where a conflict exists.

> Cooperation becomes a problem when nobody can safely say that the cooperative arrangement itself has failed.

## 🪞 Complaints and the Illusion of Independent Intake

The police complaints system in England and Wales includes the Independent Office for Police Conduct.

The IOPC oversees the system, investigates the most serious matters and considers certain reviews.

However, the existence of an independent oversight body does not mean every complaint is independently investigated at the point of submission.

The IOPC explains that complaints submitted through its online form are sent directly to the police force or organisation concerned for initial consideration.

Its form also explains that the information provided, including equality and diversity information entered into the process, is passed to the relevant force.

This arrangement may be consistent with the applicable complaints framework.

It nevertheless raises practical governance questions where the complaint concerns:

- information misuse;
- improper disclosure;
- fear of retaliation;
- disability-related access barriers;
- conduct by senior personnel;
- a conflict involving the force itself;
- uncertainty about who can safely receive sensitive material.

The relevant questions are:

- Does the complainant understand who will receive the information?
- Is a safe and accessible reporting route available?
- Has a conflict of interest been identified?
- Does the matter meet criteria for referral or independent investigation?
- Who reviews a disputed complaint outcome?
- Can sensitive information be protected appropriately?

> An independent watchdog and an independently handled first complaint are not necessarily the same thing.

The distinction should be visible before someone is asked to provide sensitive information.

## 🔍 Inspection, Oversight and What They Cannot See

Police governance includes several external mechanisms.

Depending on the issue, these may involve:

- HM Inspectorate of Constabulary and Fire & Rescue Services;
- the Independent Office for Police Conduct;
- the Information Commissioner’s Office;
- police and crime commissioners;
- courts and tribunals;
- professional or safeguarding bodies;
- parliamentary scrutiny.

Each examines a different aspect of the system.

No single body automatically sees:

- every inaccurate record;
- every cross-agency disclosure;
- every access event;
- every disability-related barrier;
- every complaint redirected internally;
- every commercial dependency;
- every misclassified victim;
- every operational failure caused by workload.

Oversight depends on the information reaching the reviewer.

Where front-line staff lack time to record concerns, regulators may receive an incomplete picture.

Where complaints are classified narrowly, systemic problems may appear as isolated dissatisfaction.

Where several agencies share a platform, no single inspection may examine the combined environment.

> A clean oversight report may reflect an effective system.
>
> It may also reflect an evidence pathway that cannot see its own blind spots.

## 🧀 When Capacity Failures Align

Operational capacity becomes a systemic risk when several weaknesses interact.

For example:

1. A pressured officer records an unverified concern.

2. A supervisor approves the summary without reviewing its source.

3. The information is shared through a regional platform.

4. Another organisation treats the entry as established fact.

5. The affected person reports a mistake.

6. The complaint returns to the force that created the record.

7. A regulator receives only the force’s explanation.

8. The person’s continued attempts to correct the error are interpreted as difficult behaviour.

9. The original concern remains active.

No single stage needs to involve deliberate misconduct.

Each may be understandable within its immediate operational setting.

The combined outcome can still be coercive, discriminatory or seriously harmful.

The question is not simply whether someone behaved badly.

It is whether the system retained any functioning mechanism capable of interrupting the error.

## 🧰 Operational Diligence Audit

### 1. Workload and Staffing

Ask:

- Are caseloads manageable?
- Do staff have protected time for verification?
- Are specialist teams adequately resourced?
- Is supervision reflective or merely administrative?
- Are serious concerns being delayed by routine demand?

A legal safeguard cannot be staffed entirely with optimism.

---

### 2. Information Provenance

Establish:

- where the information originated;
- who recorded it;
- when it was entered;
- whether it was verified;
- whether it represents fact, allegation, opinion or assessment;
- whether uncertainty remains attached.

The record should explain itself without requiring a detective story about the database.

---

### 3. Role Classification

Check whether the information accurately distinguishes:

- victims;
- witnesses;
- suspects;
- convicted individuals;
- complainants;
- safeguarding subjects.

A person’s administrative role should not quietly migrate because the system finds ambiguity convenient.

---

### 4. Access and Logging

Review:

- user permissions;
- access histories;
- exceptional access;
- external suppliers;
- regional sharing;
- onward disclosures;
- whether logs are usable for independent audit.

If an access-control system cannot explain who accessed a record, it is describing security rather than delivering it.

---

### 5. Sensitive Information

Identify whether the material includes:

- health information;
- disability;
- racial or ethnic origin;
- religious or philosophical beliefs;
- political opinions;
- sexual orientation;
- sexual life;
- biometric or genetic information;
- details of sexual violence or abuse.

Check whether the processing is justified under the correct legal framework and supported by appropriate safeguards.

Sensitive data is not less sensitive because an institution already holds it.

---

### 6. Data Sharing

Determine:

- why the information was shared;
- whether the recipient needed it;
- whether the relevant legal regime was identified;
- whether the scope was proportionate;
- whether the information was accurate;
- whether onward sharing was controlled.

The fact that a partner agency asked is not, by itself, an adequate explanation.

---

### 7. Equality and Accessibility

Assess whether:

- disability-related needs were recognised;
- communication routes were accessible;
- cultural or religious assumptions affected interpretation;
- protected characteristics were used as informal risk proxies;
- the person could challenge the decision.

A process cannot meaningfully protect equality if the people most affected cannot enter it.

---

### 8. Complaint Independence

Ask:

- Who receives the complaint first?
- Does that person or organisation have a conflict?
- Can the complainant provide information safely?
- Is external referral required?
- Who decides whether the matter is serious?
- Can the outcome be independently reviewed?

A complaint pathway should not become another channel for exposing the person who raised the concern.

---

### 9. Correction and Downstream Effects

Where information is inaccurate, examine:

- how it can be corrected;
- whether an audit trail is preserved;
- which organisations received the earlier version;
- whether downstream decisions need review;
- whether a risk flag remains active;
- whether the person can obtain an explanation.

A correction that stays inside one database may leave the practical problem untouched.

---

### 10. Leadership and Organisational Learning

Determine whether:

- mistakes are acknowledged;
- staff can challenge decisions;
- recurring concerns are analysed;
- professional standards teams identify systemic issues;
- leadership protects ethical decision-making;
- external findings lead to operational change.

Learning requires more than writing “lessons learned” at the end of the same incident for the fourth time.

---

## 🪄 What Genuine Operational Capacity Requires

A policing system capable of meaningful due diligence needs:

- manageable workloads;
- adequate specialist staffing;
- informed supervision;
- accurate and contextual records;
- clear separation between fact and opinion;
- appropriate distinctions between victims, witnesses and suspects;
- secure and reviewable access;
- purpose-limited information sharing;
- accessible complaint routes;
- independent escalation where conflicts arise;
- protected time for verification;
- trauma-informed and disability-aware practice;
- effective cross-agency coordination;
- transparent supplier accountability;
- practical routes for correction;
- leadership willing to acknowledge error.

This does not imply that individual officers can solve every institutional problem.

Nor does it imply that every failure results from bad faith.

The issue is whether the organisation has built conditions in which lawful, careful and humane policing remains possible.

> The public is not protected by the existence of safeguards alone.
>
> It is protected when someone has the capacity, authority and courage to use them.
