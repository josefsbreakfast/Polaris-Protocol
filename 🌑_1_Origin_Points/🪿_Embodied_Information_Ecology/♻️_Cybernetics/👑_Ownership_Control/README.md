## 🛰️ Orientation

**Ownership & Control** maps what happens after law, policy, governance, and operational systems collide in practice.

The cluster asks a deceptively simple question:

> **Who actually controls the process once rules meet reality?**

Formal responsibility and practical control are not always the same thing.

Authority may be distributed across institutions. Decision-making may depend upon actors who do not formally own the outcome. Escalation routes may exist on paper while failing in practice. A process may continue because everyone controls one part of it while nobody possesses sufficient authority, incentive, or information to take responsibility for the whole.

Here we track:

- custody gaps;
- mandate misfit;
- escalation vacuums;
- bottleneck authority;
- insulation through indispensability;
- responsibility fragmented across actors;
- and outcome ownership where harm emerges through delay, omission, or distributed decision-making rather than a single declaration.

This cluster is structural, not rhetorical.

## 🧭 What This Cluster Tracks

- **Process Custody** — who signs, who stalls, who can stop or redirect a process.
- **Mandate Misfit** — when no institution's primary remit adequately matches the problem requiring action.
- **Bottleneck Power** — how control of a choke point can become operational leverage.
- **Custodial Capture** — when embedded actors become difficult to remove because systems have become dependent upon them.
- **Ownership Diffusion** — when responsibility is fragmented across multiple actors or institutions.
- **Outcome Ownership** — who bears responsibility for consequences when systems appear to “merely delay,” defer, or continue operating.

The emphasis is on **mechanism**: identifying where effective control sits, how decisions propagate through systems, and where formal governance diverges from operational reality.

## 🚦 Routing Guide

Use **Ownership & Control** when the core question is:

> **Who actually controls the process?**

The cluster is particularly useful where formal remit does not adequately describe practical control, or where feedback between decisions, institutional responses, and subsequent conditions affects what happens next.

Where another framework more adequately explains the phenomenon, route or cross-link accordingly.

### During the architecture transition

Do not move a legacy node merely because a newer destination appears conceptually cleaner.

Before final movement:

1. identify its strongest current and prospective relationships;
2. preserve or replace meaningful inbound and outbound links;
3. confirm the destination README can explain why the node belongs there;
4. distinguish **final routing** from useful **cross-linking**;
5. update neighbouring indexes where the move changes navigation;
6. only then retire the legacy route.

The objective is not a cosmetically perfect folder tree.

It is a **survivable knowledge graph**.

Until migration and maintenance are complete, existing locations and links should therefore be read as a mixture of:

- legacy architecture;
- confirmed new routing;
- provisional staging;
- and intentional cross-cluster overlap.
