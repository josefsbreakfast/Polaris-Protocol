# 🍓 Austen Cybernetics 206: The User Has a Good Model of You
**First created:** 2026-09-21 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-21  
*Being accurately understood is not the same thing as being cared for. Sometimes accurate modelling makes you easier to use.*
---
## 🧭 Orientation
In 205, we met a problem familiar to any trust system:
Mr Elliot presents excellent metadata.
He knows how to appear:
- respectable;
- polished;
- socially appropriate;
- attentive;
- materially secure;
- reassuringly legible.
The question was:
> What if somebody understands which signals your system trusts and knows how to produce them?
Now we reverse the direction of observation.
Because Mr Elliot does not merely need other people to form a useful model of **him**.
He can form models of **them**.
And his model of Anne may contain quite a lot of accurate information.
He can recognise that she is:
- intelligent;
- discreet;
- reliable;
- socially competent;
- attentive;
- knowledgeable about her family;
- deeply familiar with Kellynch;
- capable of navigating difficult people;
- unlikely to create unnecessary scandal;
- possessed of considerably better judgement than several other members of the Elliot household.
These are real qualities.
Mr Elliot noticing them is not the problem.
The problem is:
> **What does he want the information for?**
Welcome to the difference between:
**model accuracy**
and:
**objective function.**

## 👁️ 1. Oh No. He Can See Her.
There is an easy version of the Bad Suitor.
He does not understand the heroine.
He likes:
- her face;
- her fortune;
- her family;
- some imaginary version of her.
The audience therefore knows:
> This man doesn't really see her.
Mr Elliot is more interesting if we resist that simplification.
Because he may see quite a lot.
He can notice that Anne is unusually useful within her social environment.
She:
- remembers things;
- understands people;
- behaves discreetly;
- does not constantly manufacture drama;
- exercises judgement;
- knows how the family works;
- understands Kellynch;
- can perform social obligations without becoming Sir Walter.
Mr Elliot:
**Excellent.**
And that is precisely where the problem begins.

## 🧠 2. Accurate Models Are Not Automatically Prosocial
A model can be evaluated along more than one dimension.
For example:
```text
MODEL A:
accurate + benevolent use
MODEL B:
inaccurate + benevolent use
MODEL C:
accurate + exploitative use
MODEL D:
inaccurate + exploitative use

We often collapse:

understands me

into:

cares about me

But these are different properties.

Someone can understand:

* what reassures you;
* what frightens you;
* what you value;
* what you will tolerate;
* what responsibilities you take seriously;
* what you are unlikely to abandon;
* how you respond to pressure;

without treating your welfare as an objective.

Indeed:

the better their model, the more effectively they may be able to influence you.

That is the 206 problem.

⸻

🎯 3. The Objective Function

A model tells an actor something about the environment.

It does not tell us what the actor wants.

Suppose two people accurately observe:

Anne is loyal.
Anne is competent.
Anne is discreet.
Anne knows Kellynch.
Anne takes relationships seriously.

One observer might think:

These are qualities I admire in a person whose flourishing matters to me.

Another might think:

These qualities make Anne extremely useful.

Same information.

Different objective function.

That difference changes everything.

⸻

⚓ 4. Wentworth And Mr Elliot Can Notice The Same Woman

This gives us a useful contrast.

Wentworth notices Anne’s:

* constancy;
* judgement;
* competence;
* loyalty;
* emotional depth.

Mr Elliot can also notice:

* judgement;
* competence;
* loyalty;
* discretion;
* social usefulness.

We therefore cannot distinguish them simply by saying:

Wentworth understands Anne.
Mr Elliot does not.

The more interesting distinction is relational.

Wentworth’s attachment makes Anne’s own interior life matter to him.

He can be:

* angry;
* proud;
* jealous;
* petty;
* spectacularly bad at interpreting Wingman Anne;

but Anne remains a person whose choices and feelings matter independently of what they can do for him.

Mr Elliot’s interest is much more instrumentally structured.

The question becomes:

Is Anne’s value located in Anne, or in what Anne can provide?

🏡 5. Kellynch Again

This is where 🍓 comes home.

Anne loves Kellynch.

It is not merely:

large valuable house in Somersetshire

It is:

* memory;
* place;
* continuity;
* relationship;
* home;
* landscape;
* family history;
* embodied knowledge.

Mr Elliot’s relationship to Kellynch is different.

Kellynch is bound up with:

* inheritance;
* position;
* future status;
* material interest.

And Anne’s relationship with Kellynch can itself become information about Anne.

Mr Elliot can observe:

Anne knows Kellynch.
Anne understands the family.
Anne is responsible.
Anne is discreet.
Anne is competent.

Anne experiences those characteristics as part of a lived relationship with home.

An instrumental observer can translate them into:

useful future Lady Elliot

Same information.

Different ontology.

Anne:

home.

Mr Elliot:

asset plus highly compatible administrator.

Sir Walter:

BARONET HOUSE VERY GOOD.

The Crofts:

Lovely place. Where has the Admiral put the fucking gig?

🧰 6. The Wife-Shaped Governance Appliance

This is where the joke carries something serious.

The crude Mr Elliot model of Anne becomes:

reliable
+
discreet
+
socially competent
+
knows Kellynch
+
understands Elliot family
+
unlikely to humiliate me publicly
+
conventionally appropriate
=
EXCELLENT WIFE

But what does excellent mean here?

Excellent for Anne?

Excellent relationship?

Excellent mutual life?

Or:

excellent component in Mr Elliot’s desired future configuration?

Hence:

Wife-Shaped Governance Appliance.

The phrase is ridiculous.

The underlying distinction is not.

A person can value your characteristics because those characteristics make their system work better.

That does not necessarily mean they value your flourishing.

🕹️ 7. The User Has Read Your Documentation

205 established:

Unfortunately the user has read the system documentation.

206 makes it personal.

Suppose somebody knows:

* you are conscientious;
* you dislike letting people down;
* you respond to reason;
* you honour commitments;
* you avoid unnecessary conflict;
* you care about other people’s welfare.

Those are good characteristics.

They are also potential control surfaces.

For example:

conscientiousness
        ↓
"I am relying on you."
empathy
        ↓
"You wouldn't want to hurt me."
loyalty
        ↓
"After everything we've been through..."
conflict avoidance
        ↓
"Why make a fuss?"

The vulnerability does not arise because the underlying characteristic is bad.

It arises because:

a predictable prosocial response can be incorporated into somebody else’s control strategy.

⸻

🪝 8. Your Strength Can Be An Attack Surface

This is worth making explicit.

People often imagine vulnerability as:

* weakness;
* ignorance;
* incompetence;
* instability.

But strengths can create predictable behaviour too.

For example:

* reliable people can be overloaded;
* compassionate people can be guilted;
* conscientious people can be made responsible for failures they did not create;
* loyal people can remain too long;
* discreet people can be relied upon not to expose mistreatment;
* competent people can be given endless unrecognised labour because everybody knows they will cope.

The lesson is not:

Stop being reliable.

It is:

Understand that good characteristics can become exploitable when another actor models them accurately and has incompatible objectives.

Anne’s reliability is not the flaw.

The objective function of the observer matters.

⸻

🪞 9. Prediction Is Power

A useful model improves prediction.

Prediction can improve coordination.

That can be wonderful.

If I know:

Anne will notice when somebody needs help,

I can coordinate with Anne.

If I know:

Anne takes promises seriously,

I can trust commitments.

But prediction can also improve control.

If I know:

Anne will tolerate X rather than create public conflict,

that knowledge can change what I believe I can get away with.

So:

better model
        ↓
better prediction
        ↓
greater capacity to coordinate
OR
greater capacity to manipulate

The information itself does not choose between them.

The actor’s goals do.

⸻

🔐 10. Privacy Is Not The Only Information-Security Question

A simplistic information-security model asks:

Did somebody obtain information they were not entitled to possess?

Important question.

But 206 gives us another:

What can somebody do with information they are perfectly capable of observing?

Mr Elliot does not need secret surveillance equipment to notice Anne’s character.

Human beings continuously reveal information through:

* behaviour;
* relationships;
* habits;
* responsibilities;
* choices;
* patterns.

Therefore information safety cannot consist solely of:

keep all information secret

The harder problem is governance:

What constraints exist on how knowledge about another person can be used?

That takes us from information to power.

⸻

🧭 11. Care Changes The Control Problem

Suppose I know what you want.

If I care about your autonomy, that knowledge may help me:

* communicate better;
* avoid harming you;
* support your goals;
* coordinate with you;
* recognise when something is wrong.

If I do not treat your autonomy or welfare as important, the same knowledge may help me:

* persuade;
* pressure;
* redirect;
* extract;
* manipulate;
* anticipate resistance.

Thus:

Care is not simply additional information.

It changes what the information is for.

That is why:

high-fidelity model of person

does not imply:

safe relationship with person

🧠 12. Intelligence Without Alignment

For the tech boys, we can make the analogy explicit.

Imagine a system with:

* excellent sensing;
* excellent prediction;
* excellent modelling;
* excellent optimisation.

None of those properties answer:

Optimising for what?

A system can be extraordinarily capable while pursuing an objective that does not include the welfare of the people it models.

Human social intelligence has the same basic problem.

Someone being:

* perceptive;
* charming;
* socially skilled;
* good at reading people;

does not by itself tell us whether those capacities are being used:

with

people

or:

on

people.

That preposition is doing considerable fucking work.

🎩 13. Mr Elliot Does Not Need To Be A Cartoon Villain

Keep this carefully bounded.

The analytical value of Mr Elliot does not depend upon turning him into:

SECRET REGENCY TERMINATOR.

Nor do we need a clinical diagnosis.

The useful literary question is narrower:

What does Austen show us about the relationship between Mr Elliot’s interests and other people’s welfare?

Where those diverge, what appears to govern his behaviour?

That is enough.

We are analysing:

* incentives;
* relationships;
* information;
* instrumental reasoning.

Not diagnosing fictional psychopathology.

The point is stronger when we do not overclaim.

⸻

📚 14. Mrs Smith Changes More Than The Mr Elliot Model

In 205:

Mrs Smith has the backend logs.

In 206, notice what those logs allow Anne to evaluate.

Not merely:

Is Mr Elliot respectable?

But:

How does Mr Elliot behave toward people when their interests and his interests diverge?

That is a far more useful question.

Because instrumental relationships can look perfectly functional while interests align.

The diagnostic moment is often:

MY OBJECTIVE
        =
YOUR OBJECTIVE

versus:

MY OBJECTIVE
        ≠
YOUR OBJECTIVE

What happens then?

That tells us considerably more about the governing relationship.

⸻

⚖️ 15. Alignment Under Divergence

This deserves to become a core concept.

Two actors can cooperate beautifully when their interests coincide.

That alone does not establish:

* loyalty;
* care;
* trustworthiness;
* constraint.

A stronger test is:

What happens when helping you costs me something?

Or:

What happens when your autonomy prevents me obtaining something I want?

Or:

What happens when your welfare and my optimisation target diverge?

This is where relationships reveal their governance.

A safe system needs some mechanism by which:

your interests continue to matter even when they are inconvenient to mine.

That can come from:

* affection;
* ethics;
* law;
* norms;
* duty;
* institutional constraint;
* accountability.

Without such constraints, accurate modelling can become extraordinarily dangerous.

❤️ 16. Wentworth’s Useful Difference

Wentworth is not presented here as Perfect Prosocial Man™.

He has spent several lectures being an absolute bellend.

Good.

That makes the comparison useful.

Wentworth:

* misreads Anne;
* acts from wounded pride;
* behaves ambiguously around Louisa;
* becomes jealous;
* contributes to a spectacular information failure.

But he is capable of recognising:

Anne’s wishes are not merely obstacles between Wentworth and Desired Outcome.

He ultimately has to risk receiving an answer he may not like.

That matters.

Direct declaration creates the possibility:

ANNE SAYS NO

and Wentworth cannot ethically optimise that possibility away.

Love requires exposure to another person’s agency.

That is very different from modelling somebody primarily in order to make them fit your desired configuration.

⸻

💌 17. The Letter Is Also A Surrender Of Control

This gives the famous letter another cybernetic reading.

Wentworth finally communicates.

He supplies Anne with high-fidelity information about his internal state.

But he also relinquishes control over the outcome.

He can say:

Here is what I feel.

He cannot legitimately convert that into:

Therefore you must choose me.

Communication is not control.

This is important.

Healthy information exchange increases another person’s ability to make an informed choice.

Manipulative information exchange attempts to constrain the choice while preserving the appearance of autonomy.

That distinction belongs firmly in 206.

⸻

🧬 18. Prosocial Modelling

We should not leave the reader with:

Modelling other people is creepy.

Humans cannot relate without models.

We continuously learn:

* what people like;
* what upsets them;
* what they need;
* what they mean;
* how they communicate;
* what they can do;
* what they cannot do.

The question is how that knowledge is governed.

A prosocial model might ask:

What does this person want?
How can I coordinate with them?
What boundaries have they expressed?
What information would help them decide?
What am I assuming incorrectly?
How can they correct my model?

An instrumental model tends toward:

What does this person predictably do?
What output do I want?
Which input will produce it?

Both are models.

The relationship to the modelled person is radically different.

⸻

🔄 19. Let The Modelled Person Update The Model

This is a particularly useful design principle.

If I hold a model of you, can you correct it?

Can you say:

No, that’s not what I mean.

And does the model update?

Or does the modeller respond:

I know you better than you know yourself.

A healthy relational information system requires channels for:

* correction;
* disagreement;
* consent;
* revision;
* refusal.

Otherwise the model can become a mechanism of domination.

The person disappears behind the representation of the person.

We have returned to a central cybernetic danger:

Do not mistake the model for the system.

Especially when the system is another fucking human being.

⸻

🕸️ 20. Embodied Information Ecology

This is where 206 plugs directly into the parent framework.

Information about people is:

* embodied;
* relational;
* contextual;
* consequential.

A model of Anne is not floating abstractly in space.

It changes:

* how people approach her;
* what they expect from her;
* what roles they assign her;
* what demands they make;
* what possibilities they imagine for her;
* what they believe they may ask of her.

Information therefore participates in the environment the person must subsequently inhabit.

That is why information ethics cannot stop at:

Is the information true?

We also need:

What does this information enable somebody to do?

⸻

🧪 21. The 206 Problem Set

When somebody possesses a good model of another person:

* How accurate is the model?
* What information does it contain?
* How was that information obtained?
* What does the modeller want?
* Does the modelled person’s welfare appear in that objective?
* Does their autonomy constrain optimisation?
* What happens when their interests diverge?
* Can the modelled person correct the model?
* Can they refuse the outcome?
* Are prosocial characteristics becoming control surfaces?
* Does better prediction increase coordination or extraction?
* Is information being used with somebody or on somebody?
* Does the relationship remain safe when cooperation becomes inconvenient?
* What governance constrains the use of accurate information?

And:

What happens when the user has a very good model of you?
