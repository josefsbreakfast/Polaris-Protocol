# ✉️ No One Writes Letters Like They Used To
**First created:** 2026-10-22 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-22  
*On corporate correspondence, parliamentary pigeons, political influence, and the awkward fact that observation changes the thing being observed.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

An American technology contractor writes to British parliamentarians who have criticised it. Some recipients interpret the letters as pressure. The letters become news. The news raises further questions about the contractor's political relationships. And a British voter, who might reasonably have preferred to ask the governing party about its decisions, finds herself discussing the contractor's stationery instead.

**No one writes letters like they used to.**

This is not primarily a node about whether Palantir is good or bad, nor an attempt to attribute every Labour decision to Peter Mandelson. It is about **observation becoming intervention**: how attempts to manage political information change the information ecology, and how lobbying regulation itself becomes something lobbyists adapt to.

The question is not whether influence can be abolished. It is whether the public can identify, interrogate and contest the influence exerted upon its government.

---

## ✨ Key Features

- A letter is an intervention, not merely a container for propositions.
- Recipients have contexts, audiences and routes for redistribution that senders do not control.
- Political parties, consultants, contractors and departments are distinct actors; their relationships require reconstruction rather than assumption.
- Political donations, legal pressure, expertise, employment relationships and procurement can interact without being identical activities.
- Disclosure changes behaviour. A workable transparency system anticipates displacement rather than merely banning a label.
- The right to petition government is worth defending; unequal capacity to influence it is worth investigating.

## ✉️ I. The Post Has Arrived

Reporting on 22 October 2026 described letters sent on 3 June by Palantir UK chief executive Louis Mosley to Jeremy Corbyn and Rachael Maskell. The letters reportedly rehearsed parliamentary criticisms and offered meetings. Corbyn characterised the correspondence as an attempt to intimidate critics. Palantir's position, as reported, was that it sought to correct inaccurate claims. These are competing accounts of purpose and effect, not interchangeable findings.

Clive Lewis's separate correspondence with former Labour deputy leader Tom Watson, now associated with Palantir, raises an adjacent but distinct question about political networks and the movement of personnel between party leadership and commercial advocacy. Do not merge different letters, authors or recipients into one imaginary document. Obtain the complete texts before performing close rhetorical analysis.

There is nothing inherently improper about a company disputing an MP's account of its business. MPs are not exempt from correction. But the form matters. Individually addressed letters which itemise someone's public criticisms can carry an additional message: *we are paying attention to you*. Whether that is welcome engagement, an attempt at persuasion, or perceived pressure depends partly on the recipient and the relationship.

And, inconveniently for any sender who expected privacy, MPs are capable of showing people their post.

**Source:** [Anadolu Agency: ‘UK lawmakers say letters from Palantir were intended to intimidate critics’](https://aa.com.tr/en/europe/uk-lawmakers-say-letters-from-palantir-were-intended-to-intimidate-critics/4097386).

## 🧿 II. You Have Been Observed. Congratulations.

An actor observes criticism; selects a recipient; chooses a channel; sends a response. That response becomes an observation for the recipient, who brings different prior knowledge, political obligations, personal experience and social relationships to the exchange.

The sender's intended meaning is not identical to the recipient's interpretation. Neither is identical to what a journalist, constituent, parliamentary colleague or campaign organiser subsequently extracts from it.

**Transmission ≠ interpretation ≠ behavioural outcome.**

A corporate letter may aim to correct the record, establish a relationship, discourage further criticism, create a defensible paper trail, or communicate to a wider audience. We cannot infer which objective dominated from its existence alone. We can, however, examine what happened after it entered the receiving system.

### 🪿 The pigeon has friends

An MP with established relationships across grassroots organising and independent journalism is not an isolated mailbox. A message may travel:

**Company → MP → reporter → campaigning networks → public discussion → parliamentary questions → further company response.**

This is a *possible propagation pathway*, not a verified map of every transmission in this case. The point is structural: **the letter becomes fresh evidence about the sender's conduct, regardless of whether the sender wanted that outcome**.

A communication intended to narrow controversy can widen it. Alternatively, a sender may welcome publicity. The outcome alone cannot settle intent.

---

## 🏛️ III. Excuse Me, Who Is Actually Governing?

A British voter has questions for the government. How did a contractor become embedded in public functions? Who selected it? Who advised ministers? What changed between Conservative and Labour administrations? Which contractual, technical and procurement dependencies constrained subsequent decisions?

Those are questions for the state and its political leadership, not only for the supplier.

The MoPAC/Palantir controversy belongs in this reconstruction, but its legal findings and precise contractual history must be stated from the judgments and procurement records, not compressed into a slogan. A decision inherited from an earlier administration is not automatically evidence of improper lobbying; equally, inheritance does not make a later government's decisions unaccountable.

**The democratic principal remains the public.** Ministers and public bodies must explain the decisions for which they are responsible, including why a supplier was retained or a process lawfully departed from open competition.

## 🕸️ IV. One Man Cannot Personally Be the Entire Lobbying Industry

Peter Mandelson, Global Counsel, the Tony Blair Institute, political donors, advisers and commercial suppliers may all be relevant to particular investigations. They are not a single organisation, and association is not proof of a coordinated scheme.

The proper unit of inquiry is a **documented network of representations and decisions**, not a celebrity villain.

For each proposed connection, ask:

1. **Access:** Who met whom, in what capacity, and when?
2. **Representation:** Which client's or institution's interests were being advanced?
3. **Content:** What specific policy, contract or regulatory outcome was sought?
4. **Transmission:** What papers, meetings, donations or personnel relationships carried the argument?
5. **Decision:** What did the responsible institution actually decide, and why?
6. **Attribution:** Is there evidence that the representation affected the outcome, or only that the positions align?
7. **Continuity:** What survived the transition from opposition to government, or between administrations?

Government-published Mandelson appointment material expressly identifies public interest in references to Global Counsel and Palantir, and describes differing redaction rationales. That is a reason to inspect the documentary record carefully—not permission to fill redactions with invented conclusions.

## 🛢️ V. Andy Burnham, Why Did You Sign Off on Jackdaw Like You Did?

A very timely illustration arrived on **21 October 2026**.

The UK government announced that the North Sea Transition Authority had granted production consent for the already-licensed Jackdaw gas field after Energy Secretary **Miatta Fahnbulleh agreed** that consent could be granted. Prime Minister **Andy Burnham publicly welcomed and defended** the decision. Strictly speaking, the published decision-making roles matter: the Prime Minister's political endorsement should not be silently substituted for the Secretary of State's statutory agreement or the regulator's consent.

The government's case included domestic gas supply, energy security, jobs and a proposed Energy Wealth Fund intended to channel North Sea revenues into the energy transition. The government stated that the decision was compatible with climate obligations; opponents dispute the wisdom and climate consequences of further fossil-fuel production. A prior approval had been revisited following litigation and the requirement to consider downstream emissions.

This is precisely where the question about lobbying becomes concrete:

- Which oil and gas interests made representations, directly or through intermediaries?
- What did the public consultation receive, and how were those submissions evaluated?
- Which representations concerned jobs, security of supply, domestic prices, taxation, climate impacts and alternative energy investment?
- What evidence did ministers rely upon, and what alternatives were considered?
- What relationship, **if any**, did party donations, professional lobbying or prior consultancy contacts have to the decision-making process?
- Which records permit a voter to distinguish commercial advocacy from independent departmental analysis?

**The coincidence of a policy decision with an industry's preferred outcome does not establish that the industry caused it.** But it is reasonable to ask how the decision was reached, particularly where the public has outstanding questions about political access and intermediary networks.

A voter can disagree with the outcome and still want the complete reasons. A voter can agree with the outcome and want exactly the same thing.

The timing makes Jackdaw a useful contemporary worked example, not evidence of an unproven Global Counsel–Jackdaw causal chain.

## 💷 VI. The Carrot, the Stick and the Very Expensive Lunch

Lobbying is not one instrument. It is a repertoire: meetings, evidence, employment, policy advice, donations, publicity, legal challenge and sometimes threats of litigation. UKLFI's legal and advocacy interventions and the less visible machinery of energy-sector lobbying offer comparative lines of inquiry, but their mechanisms and legal statuses must be documented separately.

The same organisation can offer valuable expertise while arguing for a commercially favourable outcome. The same political actor can receive both useful information and pressure. A government may genuinely agree with a lobbyist's policy case. Those possibilities must remain open.

The question is not simply whether a particular contact was 'positive' or 'negative'. It is **which costs, benefits, assumptions and options entered the decision-maker's environment**, and whether the public can reconstruct their significance.

## 🗳️ VII. The Right to Petition Is Not a Corporate Invention

Start earlier than the public-affairs consultancy.

Petitioning the Crown and Parliament, organised reform movements, abolitionism, suffrage, trade unionism and disability-rights campaigning all illustrate how people have sought to influence institutions. Commercial lobbying, patronage and chartered-company influence have their own long British histories; the East India Company makes an instructive, if rather alarming, example of the entanglement of commercial and state power.

It would be an extraordinary democratic own goal to respond to corporate lobbying by constructing rules that also make it harder for a disabled constituent, trade union, local campaign or small voluntary organisation to be heard.

**The right to seek influence is broadly shared. The capacity to purchase sustained access is not.**

That asymmetry—not the mere act of petitioning—is the constitutional design problem.

---

## 🪄 VIII. Good News, Chaps. We Have Abolished Lobbying.

Parliament abolishes lobbying.

By Tuesday, everyone is engaged in *strategic stakeholder relations*.

By Wednesday, the lobbyists have become *independent policy advisers*.

By Thursday, somebody has discovered an entirely new form of lunch.

Nobody is lobbying anymore. The stationery has been updated. Democracy is saved.

Except, of course, it isn't.

If regulation targets a job title or narrow channel rather than the **function of attempting to influence public decisions**, some activity will migrate to other channels. This does not mean all research, consultancy, litigation or advocacy is covert lobbying. It means definitions must distinguish legitimate activity from attempts to evade disclosure without pretending that a change of label changes the underlying incentives.

Political donation reform has the same problem: caps, eligibility, corporate funding, overseas connections, cryptocurrency and third-party spending must be considered together. Closing one route may increase the attractiveness of another.

## ♻️ IX. The Observer Has Entered the System

Transparency is not a window positioned outside politics. It is an intervention within politics.

**Activity → observation → disclosure → altered incentives → adaptation → changed activity → renewed observation.**

A meeting register may improve accountability while encouraging some actors to prefer less formal channels. A donation rule may improve traceability while increasing incentives to use intermediaries. A procurement disclosure may improve scrutiny while encouraging contractors to frame their claims differently. These are possible adaptations, not inevitable outcomes.

This is the central cybernetic lesson: **the measurement regime becomes part of the environment to which the measured actors respond**.

An effective system therefore needs feedback of its own: independent review, anomaly detection, anti-avoidance provisions, public accessibility, enforcement and periodic revision. Merely accumulating disclosures is not the same as making influence intelligible.

And a government cannot infer from a tidy register that all consequential influence is registered.

## ⚖️ X. What Could Britain Actually Do Differently?

The following are questions for public deliberation, not a preselected party programme.

| Design area | Options worth examining | Avoidable failure |
|---|---|---|
| Donations | Individual and aggregate caps; verified beneficial ownership; donor eligibility; cryptocurrency traceability | Influence moves to opaque third parties |
| Lobbying | Activity-based registration; client identification; subject and outcome sought; proportionate contact disclosures | Ordinary constituents face corporate-scale compliance |
| Opposition parties | Disclosure of substantial paid policy advocacy and material support before entry into government | Legitimate political association becomes indiscriminately surveilled |
| Revolving door | Cooling-off periods, declarations, independent approval and enforcement | Informal relationships remain unexamined |
| Procurement | Traceable supplier contact, conflict declarations, reasons for exceptions and extensions | Commercial confidentiality swallows the decision record |
| Transparency infrastructure | Searchable, interoperable, timely datasets with stable identifiers | Thousands of technically public fragments cannot be connected |
| Public participation | Citizens' deliberation, civil-society consultation and parliamentary response duties | Incumbent parties define acceptable influence on their own terms |

Regulation must be proportionate to the resources and influence involved. A citizen emailing their MP is not equivalent to a contractor retaining a professional influence operation. But both are entitled to lawful political participation.

---

## 🛡️ XI. Classified, Confidential, or Just Embarrassing?

A criminal investigation, regulatory review, national-security restriction, commercial confidentiality claim and political decision not to comment are different things.

Where an investigation genuinely restricts disclosure, explain what can lawfully be said without compromising it. Where a particular operational detail must remain secret, consider whether the institutional process can still be independently scrutinised. Where no lawful restriction exists, the mere fact that an answer would be awkward is not a public-interest exemption.

There are real limits: publishing sensitive defence specifications or prejudicing legal proceedings can cause harm. **But the existence of a legitimate exception should not be allowed to erase the distinction between protected details and accountable decisions.**

The public should not be asked to accept an implied law-enforcement or national-security explanation where none has been established.

---

## 📮 XII. Return to Sender

We began with letters.

The sender knew what it wanted to say. The recipient knew what the sender represented. The public acquired a new object of scrutiny. And the discussion expanded from a company's reputation to the relationships through which commercial interests interact with political parties and the state.

Then Jackdaw arrived, reminding us why the bigger question matters. A government decision can have substantive arguments in its favour and still require public examination of who sought it, how it was evaluated, and who was accountable for approving it.

The British public does not need to be shielded from knowing that organisations lobby its government. Nor does it need the governing party to decide, without meaningful independent participation, exactly how much of that relationship it is comfortable disclosing.

**We cannot abolish the desire to influence power. We can decide how visible, contestable and accountable that influence must be.**

And perhaps, before sending another carefully composed letter to a parliamentarian, someone might consider what happens when the parliamentarian writes back.

*Or forwards it to a journalist.*
