# ➰ Horror in the Loop
**First created:** 2026-10-02 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-02  
*Pulp horror for the information age: what happens when somebody quietly acquires write-access to another human being's information ecology.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The monster is not AI.

The monster is **write-access to somebody else's information ecology**.

*Horror in the Loop* is a pulp-horror novel concept built around digitally mediated behavioural modification: not a grand state conspiracy, not a secret government programme, and not trafficking with an algorithm pasted over the top.

The horror is smaller, cheaper, stupider, and therefore worse.

A tiny number of apparently ordinary government workers discover that the systems they can access — directly, indirectly, legitimately, illicitly, or by knowing where the seams are — can be composed with commercial platforms, behavioural data, administrative friction, recommendation systems, human intermediaries, and ordinary digital life.

They do not sell people.

They sell **attempts to alter people**.

On a dark-web marketplace, customers can auction for access to a human subject's information environment:

- make her stop going to that café;
- make him contact his ex;
- make them frightened of something they previously ignored;
- get her to attend a particular event;
- make these two people meet;
- make him buy something;
- make her think everybody hates her;
- keep somebody awake;
- get a subject to independently mention a phrase they were never directly shown.

The sellers cannot guarantee the outcome.

That is the product.

> Give me enough money and I will show you what we can make more likely.

The victim remains physically free.

She goes to work. She buys milk. She texts her friends. She changes her mind. She makes choices.

**Her information environment is the room she is being held in.**

## 🎟️ The Marketplace

The marketplace should be disgusting partly because it is so recognisable.

It has:

- listings;
- bids;
- seller reputations;
- customer reviews;
- outcome disputes;
- escalating challenges;
- specialist operators;
- repeat customers;
- favourite subjects;
- performance statistics;
- refunds nobody deserves;
- arguments about whether an outcome actually counts.

A customer is not purchasing a deterministic command.

They are purchasing an **intervention campaign**.

The marketplace therefore treats human behaviour like a mixture of gambling, advertising, penetration testing, and spectator sport.

A difficult request becomes prestigious.

A resistant subject becomes interesting.

An unexpected outcome becomes data.

A successful intervention becomes a reusable recipe.

And eventually the operators are no longer merely fulfilling customer requests.

They are experimenting.

---

## 💰 Humans As Auction Lots

The central obscenity is that the marketplace can discuss a living person almost entirely without discussing their humanity.

A subject might have a profile containing:

- known routines;
- probable vulnerabilities;
- recurring locations;
- social connections;
- inferred fears;
- consumer habits;
- likely sources of reassurance;
- probable sleep patterns;
- susceptibility estimates;
- previous interventions;
- observed reactions;
- outstanding customer challenges.

The operators do not need to think:

> We are torturing this woman.

They can think:

> Subject 41B has unusually high resistance to social-pressure interventions.

And somebody, somewhere, immediately thinks:

> Interesting.

---

## 🧪 The Language Of Not Doing Anything

The perpetrators should not conceptualise themselves as torturers.

That is important.

Their language comes from analytics, advertising, experimentation, bureaucracy, behavioural science, product testing, security work, and administrative systems.

Not **victim**.

**Subject.**

Not **make her frightened**.

**Increase threat salience.**

Not **isolate him**.

**Reduce competing social inputs.**

Not **make her doubt her own perception of reality**.

**Destabilise confidence in environmental inference.**

Not **we fucked with this woman's entire life for six weeks**.

**Successful conversion.**

And when somebody finally says what they are actually doing, the defence is obvious:

> We don't make anybody do anything.

Technically, they do not.

That is the point.

## 🕸️ The Room With No Walls

The victim does not experience a behavioural-modification architecture.

She experiences **a fucking weird month**.

Her recommendations become unusually bleak.

Something important gets administratively delayed.

A person she has not thought about in years suddenly appears somewhere.

An advert keeps touching a strangely specific insecurity.

Somebody online becomes disproportionately interested in her.

Normal sources of reassurance become harder to reach at roughly the same time.

A route changes.

A notification arrives at precisely the wrong moment.

A harmless coincidence becomes frightening because it lands after six other harmless coincidences.

Nothing is individually impossible.

Nothing is individually proof.

Most of it might happen anyway.

Her own explanation is therefore completely reasonable:

> Algorithms are weird. Bureaucracies fuck things up. Coincidences happen. People behave strangely online. I'm exhausted.

The reader should be allowed to live inside that uncertainty with her.

Because the horror does not require every strange thing to have been caused by the operators.

In fact, it is nastier if it was not.

Once enough of the environment has genuinely been manipulated, **ordinary randomness becomes part of the attack for free**.

## 🛒 Meanwhile, At Lunch

Cut away.

Two spectacularly boring government workers are eating Tesco sandwiches.

One checks a marketplace dispute.

> **LOT 41B — CUSTOMER CLAIMS FAILURE**

The customer requested a breakup.

The subject instead got a haircut.

The operator believes the intervention should count as partial behavioural displacement.

The customer wants a refund.

Somebody takes a bite of a prawn sandwich and says:

> “No, that's clearly non-conversion.”

Then they go back to work.

The pulp-horror register matters here.

The perpetrators should not become glamorous hacker gods in black rooms full of green text.

They should have:

- lanyards;
- bad chairs;
- forgotten passwords;
- petty workplace grievances;
- mediocre lunches;
- annual leave;
- someone who keeps stealing teaspoons;
- strong opinions about whether a particular outcome met the terms of the listing.

The machinery is horrifying.

The people operating it can remain painfully, offensively ordinary.

## 🦉 WHY THE FUCK DO I KEEP SEEING OWLS

The novel needs motifs which are frightening to the protagonist and faintly ridiculous to everyone else.

For example:

**Owls.**

She starts seeing owls.

Not actual owls, necessarily.

An owl in an advert.

An owl on somebody's shirt.

A recommended documentary.

An account with an owl avatar.

A children's book left on a seat.

A pub she has never noticed before.

An owl in a dream, because at this point of course she is dreaming about the fucking owls.

Eventually:

> WHY THE FUCK DO I KEEP SEEING OWLS.

The reader does not initially know whether the owls are an intervention, algorithmic coincidence, attentional salience, paranoia produced by other interventions, an operator fucking around, or simply what happens when a person notices an owl and then starts noticing owls.

Possibly the operators do not know either.

That is better.

## 🎰 Behaviour Is Not A Vending Machine

The system must fail.

Repeatedly.

Humans are not programmable.

The operators can manipulate inputs. They cannot cleanly dictate outputs.

This is where the horror gets its pulp-thriller engine and its comedy.

Someone spends £40,000 trying to make a subject afraid of the seaside.

She takes up cold-water swimming.

They try to induce a breakup.

The couple finally have the difficult conversation they have been avoiding for three years and become substantially happier.

They try to make somebody stop attending a café.

He buys the café.

They try to make the protagonist distrust one of her friends.

She finally tells the friend what has been happening.

The friend says:

> “Okay. That's fucking weird.”

Now there are two of them looking.

The operators' logs begin accumulating the same infuriating result:

> **SUBJECT HAS BEHAVED UNEXPECTEDLY.**

> **SUBJECT HAS BEHAVED UNEXPECTEDLY.**

> **SUBJECT HAS BEHAVED UNEXPECTEDLY.**

The technology can alter conditions.

It cannot abolish agency.

## 🕹️ The Resistant Subject Problem

This is where the story turns.

At first, the protagonist is just another listing.

Then she becomes annoying.

Then interesting.

Then valuable.

Somebody on the marketplace starts paying increasingly stupid amounts because she **will not fucking behave correctly**.

Her resistance creates its own market.

Customers start proposing challenges.

Operators begin competing.

Someone wants to be the person who finally produces a clean conversion.

Another customer becomes fascinated by the failures.

Somebody else is betting on her.

At this point the protagonist is simultaneously:

- a victim;
- an experiment;
- entertainment;
- a professional challenge;
- a financial asset;
- a statistical anomaly;
- and, without knowing it, the most famous person in a room she cannot see.

This is where the operators make their fundamental mistake.

They become more interested in producing an observable result than in remaining invisible.

---

## 👁️ The Feedback Loop Bites Back

Behavioural modification requires observation.

If the operators want to know whether an intervention worked, they need feedback.

The more interested they become in the protagonist, the more they watch.

The more they watch, the more infrastructure they expose.

The more interventions they run, the more patterns they create.

The more patterns they create, the more information **she** receives.

The loop therefore runs both ways.

They think they are observing a subject.

The subject is also observing the experiment.

Not from outside it.

From inside.

That asymmetry is enormous, but it is not absolute.

Eventually she begins doing the most dangerous possible thing:

**testing the environment back.**

Not because she knows exactly what is happening.

Because she wants to know whether anything answers.

## ♻️ Horror In The Loop

The title works at several levels.

There is a human in the technical loop.

There is a human trapped inside a behavioural feedback loop.

There are operators observing the effects of their own interventions and adjusting them.

There are customers turning human responses into entertainment.

There are platforms continuously learning from behaviour that has itself been altered by previous platform outputs.

There is a protagonist attempting to infer the existence of a system from the system's reactions to her.

And there is the central information-age horror:

**What happens to responsibility when coercion is decomposed into hundreds or thousands of individually deniable informational actions?**

No single advert imprisoned her.

No single administrative delay imprisoned her.

No recommendation imprisoned her.

No stranger's message imprisoned her.

No search result imprisoned her.

No notification imprisoned her.

No one event proves the existence of the room.

And yet somebody built the room.

## 🧿 The Institutional Horror

Making the sellers government workers does **not** require the government to be running the marketplace.

That distinction makes the story better.

The institution may eventually discover what has happened and be genuinely horrified.

Its failure is different.

It secured systems as though the things requiring protection were:

- records;
- credentials;
- databases;
- classified documents;
- individual permissions;
- identifiable transactions.

But the perpetrators discovered that fragmented access could be composed.

No single system necessarily contained the weapon.

**The relationships between systems did.**

The institution protected data.

What somebody learned to steal was **causality**.

That gives the novel three simultaneous horror stories:

1. **The subject:** discovering that her environment may not be neutral.
2. **The operators:** discovering how powerful mundane infrastructure becomes when assembled adversarially.
3. **The institution:** discovering that its security model was protecting the wrong object.

## 💀 The Pulp Rule

Do not make this too respectable.

This is not required to become a tasteful seminar on algorithmic governance wearing a horror hat.

It should have:

- nasty cliffhangers;
- lurid reveals;
- black humour;
- escalating marketplace depravity;
- stupid customers with too much money;
- operators making catastrophic mistakes;
- genuinely frightening ambiguity;
- moments where the protagonist looks completely unreasonable and is nevertheless noticing something real;
- moments where she is completely wrong;
- bureaucratic absurdity;
- human stubbornness;
- and at least one reveal that makes the reader say **oh, you absolute bastards**.

The serious ideas work better if the book remains willing to be fun.

Proper paperback horror.

The sort of book whose cover should probably be much worse than the book deserves.

---

## 🫀 The Human Rule

The protagonist cannot secretly be a perfect counter-intelligence machine.

Her power is not that she becomes omniscient.

She is still operating with incomplete information.

She gets things wrong.

She becomes frightened.

She sometimes sees intention where there is coincidence.

She sometimes dismisses intention as coincidence.

She cannot simply opt out of the information environment because modern life is inside it.

What protects her, imperfectly, is the exact thing the marketplace keeps underestimating:

**a human being is not merely the terminal output of her inputs.**

She interprets.

She remembers.

She contradicts herself.

She changes strategy.

She asks other people what they saw.

She forms relationships the model did not predict.

She behaves unexpectedly.

Again.

And again.

And again.

---

## 🔥 Governing Horror Proposition

The story begins with a marketplace asking:

> **How much would you pay to change what another human being does?**

It develops into a much nastier question:

> **How would that human being ever prove that somebody tried?**

And underneath both sits the actual monster:

> **Who gets write-access to a human information ecology — and what happens when nobody realises that write-access is a form of power?**
