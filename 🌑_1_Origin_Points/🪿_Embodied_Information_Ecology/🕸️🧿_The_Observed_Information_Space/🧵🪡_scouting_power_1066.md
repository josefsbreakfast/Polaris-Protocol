## 🪡 Anyway: Bayeux

The Bayeux Tapestry, for international readers, is incredibly famous in the UK, as it marks the Norman Conquest, which starts to establish British power in a manner which produces structures and forms of governance recognisable today. It ordinarily lives in Normandy, in France. It is on its holidays here at the British Museum, inside the country for the first time in nearly a millennium, because the conquerors were French. (Hence, we use *colour*, for example.)

It is also, technically, not a tapestry. It is embroidery, but I feel that fight was lost approximately nine hundred years ago and we may allow everyone to continue.

The thing itself is an extraordinary piece of work, but one of the useful complications is that its modern cultural status can obscure what it actually *does*. The Bayeux Tapestry is monumental artwork, but it is also cartoonish: a giant embroidered comic-book strip depicting a war won in order to advertise a new king, which is not especially “high brow” in its raw communicative content but acquires that status through age, survival, proximity to power, historical consequence and graduation of status over time.

This is not unlike Peter Thiel's own journey through power, and the entire branding of everyone close by has spoken to that push towards being considered seriously by traditional power.

Again: we will get there.

## ☄️ The Comet, The Ships, And The Problem Of Looking

There is a famous depiction of what we now know to be Halley's Comet in the Tapestry, but the interesting thing for our purposes is where it appears in the narrative: Harold has been crowned, the star with the streaming tail appears and people look upwards towards it, while underneath them, in the lower border of the embroidery, ghost-like ships begin anticipating an invasion which has not yet properly arrived in the main field of the work. News of Harold's coronation is taken to Normandy, William orders a fleet of ships to be built — although Bishop Odo is shown issuing the instructions — the invaders cross and land unopposed, food is found and cooked, a house is burned, the Normans build their fortification at Hastings, messages move between armies, and William prepares his men for battle.

The comet therefore intersects with power in the *text* of the embroidered work, if you like, in a rather more complicated way than simply being a famous astronomical event somebody happened to sew. Something is observed, and that observation becomes politically meaningful through its placement inside the wider narrative; while the people depicted in the work are looking upwards, the lower border is already allowing the later viewer to look forwards.

This is a neat little problem for **The Observed Information Space**, because the comet itself is, in principle, about as publicly accessible a piece of information as one can imagine — nobody has yet successfully privatised the sky — but observation is not interpretation, and the same visible phenomenon can become omen, narrative device, historical marker or retrospective foreshadowing depending upon who is looking, when they are looking, and what information subsequently becomes available.

Nearly a millennium later, we are now looking at an embroidery depicting people looking at the comet, while Peter Thiel receives exceptional access to look at that embroidery, while museum staff observe Peter Thiel looking at it, while *The Guardian* becomes aware of the observation, while the rest of us look at the resulting article and begin asking what the looking tells us about power.

At some point one does have to concede that the recursion is doing rather a lot of work for a folder called **The Observed Information Space**.

## 💡 A Finite Amount Of Light

The Bayeux Tapestry is also an incredibly precious historical artefact which will degrade if exposed to light for too long, and conservationists have therefore mandated a maximal number of lux-hours for exposure, meaning that the ability to view the thing is not merely socially scarce but materially finite; somebody actually has to turn the lights on, and every period for which those lights remain on consumes some part of the object's total conservation budget for display.

So, firstly: a private viewing, even to a major donor of many decades, would still be quite a flex.

Peter Thiel is not such a donor.

Enough people obviously felt a particular way about this — museums are drama central, and one would hope for loud opinions from those whose job it is to protect and preserve cultural artefacts, quite frankly — that *The Guardian* became aware. I have not yet checked Times/Telegraph, such is my *wtf is this*.

There is also a slightly tedious tendency, whenever this sort of thing becomes public, for wealthy and famous men to rediscover the phrase *private individual*, which I am increasingly tempted to ban from the English language, because there are extremely wealthy people who genuinely achieve privacy and for whom it is really fucking difficult to work out what they are doing, where they went to school, who they spend time with, what they are involved in, or where they were last Tuesday. Money, professional staff, private transport, lawyers and a willingness not to perform oneself publicly are all remarkably effective privacy technologies.

This was not that.

Peter Thiel has had decades in which he could have gone to France and seen the Bayeux Tapestry, and if the objective were simply *I would like to look at the embroidery without anybody making a fuss*, there were routes available; the thing being displayed here, regardless of whether display was the intention, is not simply the ability to see the object but the ability to be **admitted differently**.

Which is also why “a friend offered to let me in” does not necessarily make the story less interesting. It may be a perfectly ordinary and charming human explanation — I too would move at extraordinary speed if a friend offered me private access to Bayeux — but as a description of power, all it does is move the interesting question from the money to the friendship.

Please stop incriminating yourselves.

## 🗝️ The Tapestry Is Already Traded For Access

This becomes still more literal once one remembers that the Tapestry is currently in Britain because access to it has already been traded: Macron announced the loan in return for British objects travelling to French museums, including material from Sutton Hoo, the Lewis chessmen and the Battersea Shield; the UK has accepted very substantial potential financial liability for damage to the object; the British Museum secured multimillion-pound sponsorship for the exhibition; tickets for the first months sold out almost immediately, with tens of thousands of people queueing online; and conservationists, as above, are working with the physical reality that even the amount of light under which the public may see it has to be rationed.

So the conversation of access is not abstract, and nor does access mean only one thing. France can exchange temporary access to Bayeux for temporary access to British cultural objects; the British state can assume financial risk in order to make the exchange possible; a billionaire sponsor can invest money in the institutional capability to display it; the public can exchange money, time and whatever degree of competence one requires to survive a British online ticket queue for an ordinary viewing; conservators determine what the material itself can tolerate; and a sufficiently well-connected individual can apparently be offered an arrangement outside the normal public schedule.

The interesting point is not that any one of these relationships is automatically improper, but that they demonstrate how money, friendship, institutional relationship, state power and cultural access can become convertible into one another, which is a rather different register of wealth from simply possessing something expensive. Once money has become sufficiently established, the flex is no longer necessarily *I can buy the thing*; it can become the ability to access the thing which is not actually for sale, because somewhere within the network there is a person capable of opening the door.

That is a much older register of power than Silicon Valley generally likes to imagine itself inhabiting.

## 🧵 Expensive Fabrics

The Bayeux Tapestry was a monumental piece of artwork. It is also cartoonish, a giant embroidered comic-book strip depicting a war won to advertise a new king. It is not “high brow” in its raw content, but acquires it by age, proximity to power, and graduation of status over time. This is not unlike Peter Thiel's own journey through power, and the entire branding of everyone close by has spoken to that push towards being considered seriously by traditional power.

Which is where *APESHIT* becomes annoyingly useful, because The Carters very kindly gave us *“I get expensive fabrics / I got expensive habits”* while standing inside the Louvre, in a work which is gloriously uninterested in pretending that conspicuous wealth is not one of the registers through which it is operating. The same song gives us *“motorcades when we came through / presidential with the planes too”*, property, clothes, architecture and the ability to command forms of access that most people cannot, which means that, at the purely formal level, it provides an unusually useful vocabulary for understanding why a private Bayeux viewing remains part of the display-of-wealth register even when nobody has attached a number to the experience.

Indeed, at a certain point in the accumulation of wealth, the lack of an available price is precisely what makes something useful as a display of it. One does not need to own the expensive fabric if the institution holding the expensive fabric will turn the lights on specially because one has arrived; the consumption has simply moved from possession of the object towards privileged access to it, and from the ability to purchase what anybody sufficiently rich might purchase towards the ability to activate relationships which are not ordinarily available at the ticket desk.

## 🖼️ The Carters Are Doing Something Fundamentally Different

This comparison has a hard limit, however, because The Carters' takeover of the Louvre for *APESHIT* has a fundamentally different meaning and cultural importance as a statement due to the relationship of both France and the United States with Black bodies as human cargo, the historical viewership of the Black body within slave auctioning, and the wider relationship between European institutions of collection and the histories of empire, extraction, possession and classification which helped determine what enters the museum and who historically entered it as observer rather than object.

It would therefore be an extremely shallow read to collapse the two events into *billionaires get private museum access*. The bodies matter. Black American bodies occupying one of the central institutions of European cultural authority cannot produce the same information as a wealthy white man receiving exceptional access to an object which sits comfortably inside the canon from which his own intellectual and political self-presentation has historically drawn.

The Carters are looked at, certainly, but critically they have organised the looking: they choose the clothes, the positioning, the camera, the choreography, the relationship between their bodies and the canonical works around them, and in doing so take a building full of objects accumulated and arranged through European cultural power and turn that building into the scenery through which their own statement is made. There is an inversion available there because of the historical relationship between Black bodies and the gaze which simply is not available to Thiel.

The comparison remains useful precisely because the difference is informative. The broad technologies — wealth, scarcity, museum access, canonical objects, spectatorship — can be similar while the information produced by the bodies occupying the space is fundamentally different.

This is **Embodied Information Ecology**. Who is standing in the museum changes what standing in the museum means.

## 🪩 Different Maximalisms

There is also an interesting difference in the degree and form of maximalism being performed by each event, because *APESHIT* is maximal in the most obvious possible sense: The Carters fill the Louvre with bodies, couture, choreography, architecture, sculpture, painting, music, wealth and historical reference, making their presence itself the intervention and leaving absolutely nobody confused as to whether the audience is supposed to notice.

Thiel's viewing is maximal in almost the opposite direction. One man, one extraordinary object, a closed gallery, finite light, very few people physically present, and an entire display of wealth and relationship compressed into the fact that the room has been made available at all. The Carters take command of the visual field by filling it; Thiel's version of the flex is produced through the restriction of the field, in which almost nothing needs to happen because the exceptional thing is that everybody else has been removed from the arrangement.

This becomes particularly interesting when we remember how *forward* Thiel could be during the Stanford period, because the comparative restraint cannot simply be explained by saying that this is a man naturally disinclined to make cultural arguments loudly. When he wanted to intervene in an argument about Western culture, he helped build a newspaper to do it. Several decades later, the form through which his relationship to that cultural inheritance becomes legible can be considerably quieter because his position within the wider power structure is no longer the same.

The young man needs a newspaper.

The older man gets the room.

## 🏚️ Look Down

The Halley's Comet sequence becomes more complicated again once one follows the ships rather than stopping at the omen, because after the fleet has crossed and the invaders have landed, the practical requirements of conquest begin to appear: men need food, countryside is used, a house is burned, and underneath the soldiers responsible, rendered at a smaller scale, a woman holds her boy's hand as she appears to appeal for humanity.

This is one of the reasons it would be a mistake to turn Bayeux entirely into a game of elite symbolism between men who would quite like to be taken Very Seriously, because the work itself preserves the relationship between the spectacular movement of power and the bodies upon whom that movement eventually lands. Conquest becomes governance, governance becomes inheritance, and later generations can turn the entire thing into an argument about constitutional development and national identity, but somewhere inside the work itself somebody's house is still burning while her child stands beside her.

This is also, ultimately, why power mapping matters beyond the intellectual pleasure of working out which billionaire knows which board member, because all of our abstractions — infrastructure, governance, strategic alignment, investment, technological dependency, elite networks, national interest — eventually terminate somewhere in the physical world, in consequences experienced by somebody's body. Someone has to supply the food, someone lives on the land, someone becomes subject to the new rule, someone loses the house, and somebody who did not attend any of the meetings is standing underneath the men making history at a smaller scale.

The woman in the margin is not outside the story of power.

She is one of the reasons to read it properly.

## 📖 Comparisons With The Lindisfarne Gospels

There is a particularly awkward comparison here with the Lindisfarne Gospels, which I have ranted about elsewhere in this repo and will presumably continue to do until somebody returns the fucking things, because Britain is currently demonstrating a remarkable degree of institutional sophistication around the proposition that a medieval cultural object can remain intimately connected to the geography whose history gives it meaning, and that temporary movement away from that geography can therefore require diplomacy, exchange, conservation planning, insurance, state involvement and some degree of ceremony.

This apparently becomes a more difficult concept once the object was produced in Northumbria rather than Normandy.

The Lindisfarne Gospels were produced within the Northumbrian cultural world whose name they still carry, and remain one of its most important surviving cultural products, but are permanently housed in London at the British Library — the library version of the British Museum, for the purposes of this particular rant — despite a longstanding campaign for their permanent relocation to the North East, supported at various points by the Bishop of Durham, the Northumbrian Association, Simon Donald and others, and despite multiple possible northern homes having been suggested, including Durham Cathedral, Lindisfarne itself, Newcastle and Sunderland.

The British Library has vigorously opposed permanent relocation, with the result that the North East instead periodically gets to borrow the Gospels back. They were displayed in Durham in 2013, when nearly 100,000 people saw the exhibition and the event expanded into a much wider regional programme of hundreds of performances, exhibitions and related activities; they were exhibited at the Laing in Newcastle in 2022; and, in one of those small institutional details which does an almost unreasonable amount of argumentative work by itself, the clergy of Lindisfarne were presented with a facsimile copy in 1971.

France, meanwhile, has temporarily loaned Bayeux to Britain through an arrangement important enough to involve heads of government, reciprocal loans of major British cultural objects, extraordinary conservation planning, substantial financial liability, private sponsorship and an exhibition sufficiently sought after that more than 80,000 people queued online for the first tranche of tickets.

One does rather begin to notice the asymmetry.

Bayeux entering Britain is an historic international cultural exchange; Lindisfarne returning to the North East is a loan from London.

One may, of course, argue that keeping the Gospels at the British Library “allows everyone to enjoy them”, but this depends upon a rather peculiar definition of *everyone*, in which geographical accessibility appears to be measured from London outwards. If somebody in the North East needs several hours, an expensive intercity train, sufficient physical mobility to make the journey, and enough disposable time and money to turn seeing one of their own region's most important cultural products into an expedition, while somebody already in London can incorporate the same object into an afternoon, the manuscript may be publicly accessible in the strict institutional sense without access to it being meaningfully equal.

“Everyone” is doing quite a lot of work.

The irritation is not simply *give us our old book back*, although, to be clear, give us our old book back. It is that Britain is currently demonstrating through Bayeux that it understands perfectly well that place matters to cultural meaning, that movement away from that place can itself be politically and culturally significant, and that the relationship between an object and the community whose history it carries does not disappear merely because a large national institution is capable of storing it extremely competently.

Apparently we become somewhat more sophisticated about this principle when the relevant place is in France.

## 🚆 What Good Is The Symbol By Itself?

This also intersects with the wider **Number 10 North** conversation, because one of the problems with British decentralisation discourse is that we have a tendency to move the symbol before building the network required to make the movement meaningful, and then become surprised when the symbolic act does not substantially alter the distribution of access or power.

What good is a symbol by itself?

Part of the struggle in the North is the lack of transport interconnection and ease in travel and visiting, which means that the question is not simply whether nationally important cultural products and institutions sit in London, but whether the places to which one proposes moving them are themselves practically accessible across the regions they are supposed to serve. Moving the Lindisfarne Gospels permanently to Newcastle, for example, would answer the cultural-geography argument considerably better than their permanent residence in London, but it would not magically make the object easy to reach from Holy Island, rural Northumberland, Sunderland, Bishop Auckland, Middlesbrough or the many other places for which regional transport can remain awkward, slow or expensive.

This is where the claim that London “allows everyone to enjoy” a national object begins to eat its own tail, because London's accessibility is not an accidental geographical property but the product of generations of infrastructure designed around the fact that London is where important things have been placed. We put institutions in London because everybody can get to London, while investing in transport which allows everybody to get to London because that is where we put the institutions, and eventually the outcome of historical centralisation becomes the justification for continuing to centralise.

The same difficulty applies to **Number 10 North**. A northern centre of government can be meaningful, and an experiment may well teach us useful things; the symbolic geography of political power is real, and having senior government physically operate somewhere other than Westminster is not nothing. But if the decision-making, staffing, capital, transport, institutional weight and surrounding infrastructure remain substantially organised around London, one risks creating a branch office and describing the existence of the branch office as decentralisation.

There is a difference between moving a symbol and changing the network around it.

We should probably start becoming interested in the latter.

## 🏛️ Westminster Was Also A Choice

This is also why the serious version of the **Number 10 North** conversation should probably be allowed to expand into questions Britain tends to treat as considerably more radical than they actually are, including the fairly basic question of why the modern working Parliament must necessarily continue to sit inside the Palace of Westminster.

Parliament did not emerge from the sea fully formed with Westminster attached. Medieval Parliaments met in a number of places, including York — which I apparently disproportionately associate with this history because of my own foibles — alongside Northampton, Nottingham, Winchester and elsewhere, before Westminster gradually became the habitual centre. There was no neat *York → Westminster* relocation in the form my brain would apparently prefer for narrative purposes, but the underlying point survives perfectly well without it: Westminster became the normal physical home of Parliament through historical development rather than because there is some immutable constitutional property of the land beside the Thames which makes representative government function there.

Inherited arrangements can be extremely valuable without being inevitable.

This becomes particularly relevant because the Palace of Westminster is very old, requires an extraordinary amount of TLC, and is already the subject of a vast restoration problem in which Britain is going to have to decide how parliamentarians work while major intervention occurs, what a modern parliamentary workplace actually requires, how much infrastructure needs to be replicated elsewhere, and what precisely we are trying to preserve when we preserve the Palace as both working building and national monument.

There is therefore a serious case to be made for at least discussing a modern working parliamentary build, with Birmingham being one obvious thought experiment, not because Birmingham is magically accessible to everyone or because moving the legislature there would suddenly solve the economic and constitutional geography of the United Kingdom, but because it occupies a meaningfully different position within the transport network and would change the relative ease of attendance for different parts of the country.

There would be an enormous number of sticky bits, obviously. One would have to ask what happens to Whitehall, how much of the civil service would need to move, whether MPs would simply spend their lives shuttling between Birmingham and London, what happens to journalists and lobbying ecosystems, what new pressures appear in housing and local transport, how Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland relate to the new geography, whether Birmingham simply becomes another over-centralised node, and whether communities which are badly connected now remain badly connected after several billion pounds have been spent declaring the problem solved.

These are not arguments against having the conversation.

They are the conversation.

Indeed, if Britain is already going to have to confront the practical question of what Parliament requires as a modern workplace while the Palace undergoes restoration, there is something faintly odd about treating the possibility of learning from that experiment as inherently unserious. The Palace of Westminster would not cease to matter if every administrative function of a twenty-first-century legislature were no longer required to fit inside it; it would remain one of the most recognisable political buildings in the world, an enormously beloved heritage site and tourist destination, and perhaps rather easier to conserve if we stopped asking a nineteenth-century Gothic palace to function simultaneously as legislature, office complex, security environment, broadcast studio, visitor attraction and symbol of the state.

We do not need to decide tomorrow that Parliament is moving to Birmingham.

The important thing is for the UK to start having these conversations seriously, because otherwise the possibility of decentralisation is repeatedly offered as *a bit*: a northern office here, a ministerial visit there, an experiment which remains safely understood as temporary, while the inherited geography of political power is treated as the neutral baseline against which every alternative has to justify itself.

An experiment is fine.

Experiments are supposed to allow us to discover things.

## 🧭 Who Travels To Whom?

This is where Bayeux, Lindisfarne, Parliament and transport begin to collapse into the same question, because the geography of an institution or cultural object does not merely tell us where it is; it helps determine who is expected to travel, who can arrive easily, who needs money and time to participate, whose journey is treated as ordinary, and whose relationship to something described as “national” requires a pilgrimage.

The phrase *national institution* can hide quite a lot of geography, as can *public access*, *for everyone*, and indeed *the capital*, because an object can legally belong to everybody while being practically much easier for some people to encounter than others, and an institution can represent the whole country while remaining physically embedded within a particular social, professional and transport ecosystem which makes access considerably easier for those already adjacent to it.

This is not an argument that every cultural object must remain exactly where it was made, any more than the Parliament discussion requires every part of government to be scattered evenly across the map until nobody can find the Treasury. There are benefits to concentration, specialist conservation, collections sitting in conversation with one another, established expertise, transport hubs and institutional memory, and decentralisation can produce its own forms of exclusion if it simply creates several smaller centres without attending to the infrastructure between them.

But that is precisely why the conversation should be about **systems of access**, rather than assuming that designation as “national” has somehow solved the problem.

Who gets near the thing?

Who has to ask?

Who has to queue?

Who can call a friend?

Who gets the original and who gets the facsimile?

Who gets Parliament and who gets Number 10 North?

Who gets to decide that everybody can already reach London?

Once one begins asking those questions, the private Bayeux viewing stops looking like a tiny celebrity anecdote and begins sitting much more comfortably inside the wider information ecology: not because it proves some grand hidden intention on the part of the visitor, but because arrangements themselves contain information about where power sits and how access travels through it.

## 👀 On Media Surveillance

There is another layer to the timing which is worth separating from the question of whether the private viewing itself was intended as spectacle, because by the time most of us encountered the story, the relevant spectacle was no longer simply Peter Thiel looking at the Bayeux Tapestry. It was **the media looking at Peter Thiel looking at the Bayeux Tapestry**, and choosing to make that act of looking available for everybody else to observe.

This is not an argument that the story should not have been reported. Quite the opposite: there are legitimate questions about conservation capacity, exceptional access to national collections, institutional relationships, donor privilege and what precisely somebody's friendship network can make possible inside a publicly accountable museum. The point is that reporting is itself an allocation of attention, particularly when the underlying event occurred on 18 August but the resulting article was published on 31 August, by which point London was experiencing the main day of the sixtieth-anniversary Notting Hill Carnival. The coincidence therefore belongs less to the original appointment than to the **observed information space in which the appointment subsequently became news**.

This produces a rather extraordinary collection of fabrics.

The most obvious modern image of billionaires taking over a museum remains The Carters' *APESHIT*, in which expensive fabrics, extraordinary wealth, motorcades, Black bodies and canonical European art are deliberately assembled into a maximal spectacle whose relationship to viewership cannot be separated from the histories of France and the United States, the movement and sale of Black people as human cargo, the Black body as something historically displayed and inspected, and the ability of Beyoncé and Jay-Z to seize control of precisely that gaze. As discussed above, this is fundamentally different from a wealthy white man receiving private access to Bayeux; the comparison works because the differences do so much of the analytical work.

Notting Hill Carnival introduces another register again. Here are fabrics, feathers, music, bodies and extraordinary visual production occupying the streets of London rather than the controlled interior of the museum, during the sixtieth anniversary of an institution inseparable from Caribbean migration, Black British culture, racial conflict and the contest over who is permitted to occupy public space and under what description. Unlike the private museum appointment, its spectacular quality is not produced by excluding the public from the room. The crowd is part of the work.

And on the main day of that spectacle, the culture pages offer another object for looking: one Extremely Serious Man™️, privately admitted to look at one extremely old and politically important piece of embroidered fabric.

Again, fine. Report it. I am plainly sufficiently interested that I have now written several hundred lines about the fucking thing.

But **where the gaze settles is information**.

This becomes particularly important in the British context because media attention does not simply record what happened; over time, it helps determine which kinds of behaviour become legible as *power*, which become *culture*, which become *spectacle*, which become *heritage*, which become *security*, and which become background noise despite involving considerably larger numbers of people. A billionaire quietly entering a closed museum can therefore acquire enormous symbolic density precisely because the act is unusual and the individual already sits inside a network understood as powerful, while hundreds of thousands of people occupying streets through music, costume and collective cultural production can become so familiar within the annual media calendar that the extraordinary scale of what they are doing is partially normalised.

There is a seam here which also appears elsewhere in the repo, including around the recent announcement of the inquest into the death of Prof. Jason Arday: the question is not always whether something has been reported, nor whether any particular journalist was wrong to report it, but **what the media apparatus has learned to watch closely enough to recognise as consequential**. Surveillance in this sense need not mean covert state surveillance. A newspaper is also an observing system; editors, reporters, photographers and audiences distribute attention across an environment, and the resulting pattern can tell us something about the observer as well as the observed.

This is particularly useful when several forms of spectacle coexist. Carnival asks what happens when culture occupies the street and makes itself impossible not to see. *APESHIT* asks what happens when people historically subjected to the gaze take command of the museum, the camera and the conditions under which they will be looked at. The Bayeux story asks something quieter: what happens when exceptional access is intended, or at least arranged, for very few eyes, but the fact of that exceptional looking becomes interesting enough that another observing institution turns its gaze upon it.

Peter Thiel did not need to invite the public into the room for the room to become public information.

Which returns us to one of the recurring problems of privacy and power. A powerful person can have very considerable control over access to himself without having complete control over the **meaning generated when other people observe the traces of that access**. One can choose the appointment; one cannot entirely choose whether museum staff find it noteworthy, whether a journalist considers it reportable, what else is happening when the report appears, or which existing pieces of cultural imagery an audience uses to understand what it has seen.

Sometimes that imagery is William the Conqueror.

Sometimes it is *APESHIT*.

Sometimes half of West London is outside wearing feathers.

This does not make those things equivalent. It makes their simultaneous availability to the British imagination interesting.

The useful question, then, is not simply *why were we told that Peter Thiel saw the Bayeux Tapestry?* It is also: **what kind of spectacle does Britain recognise when it sees one, who becomes most intensely visible within that field, and what does the distribution of attention tell us about the people doing the looking?**

## 🎨 The Computer Nerd Wanders Into The Tapestry

There are riffs of street art around Bayeux which show a Norman soldier apparently spray-painting the Tapestry itself, an anachronism which works because contemporary visual language has been inserted directly into an inherited representation of old power; but there is something increasingly useful, for our purposes, in reversing the image and thinking about what happens when the contemporary figure wanders into the old object instead.

The technology industry was, within very recent memory, culturally represented as an industry of nerds sitting in front of computers: new money, young companies, strange founders, rapidly developing infrastructure, people building systems which traditional institutions frequently did not understand until they had already become dependent upon them, and a wider aesthetic of insurgency which relied partly upon not being the banks, governments, defence establishments, universities, old families and cultural institutions which constituted recognisable Old Power.

That is no longer an adequate description of where the successful parts of the industry sit. The companies have become infrastructure, the fortunes have compounded, founders have become political actors, those political actors have acquired networks, and those networks increasingly intersect with governments, militaries, intelligence systems, healthcare infrastructure, universities, inherited wealth, museums and the wider cultural machinery through which established power has historically recognised itself.

And now the computer nerd has wandered into the tapestry.

There is something rather funny about an object which began as an embroidered narrative adjacent to newly established political power eventually becoming so old, protected and institutionally important that governments trade access to it, states insure it, billionaires sponsor it, and another billionaire receives exceptional permission to stand beside it, because the object itself demonstrates the process through which something can graduate from a communicative product of contemporary power into the cultural furniture through which later power demonstrates seriousness.

The technology industry may be somewhere within its own version of that graduation. The people once treated as boys playing with computers are now adjacent to governments, militaries, intelligence agencies, national health systems, old money and cultural institutions; the question is no longer simply whether New Power will disrupt Old Power, because after sufficient time the categories begin to become considerably less clean, with old institutions discovering uses for new infrastructure and new fortunes discovering the legitimacy, continuity and cultural language available through old institutions.

Perhaps this is ultimately more useful than trying to decide whether the Bayeux visit represents New Power conquering Old Power or kneeling before it, because both possibilities assume the categories remain more separate than they may now be.

There will always be a goldrush over new infrastructure, and nothing should be allowed to take from the collective without appropriate scrutiny, but if what the gentlemen in Big Tech are looking for is a nod from Old Power, please can it be given — they have been very clearly requesting it.

The more interesting question is what happens once they receive it.

For now, however:

Very nice embroidery, Peter.

Yes. We have noticed.  

The Hobbits can still see you taking up room in the Shire.

## 🧽 On British Porosity Of Law And Order

There is a wider British problem sitting underneath this which is probably more important than whether Peter Thiel got to look at some embroidery, because the recurring institutional failure is not generally that nobody knows what rules are supposed to exist. Britain is very good at writing rules. We are exceptionally good at producing inquiries after the fact, generating recommendations, creating safeguarding policies, tightening access protocols, adding governance boards, producing new acronyms and then explaining, with great seriousness, that lessons have been learned.

The problem is that the system remains remarkably **porous around status**.

This is one of the things the Savile case should have made culturally unforgettable. The important lesson was never simply *famous man bad*, nor even *institutions failed to stop one particularly manipulative individual*. It was that celebrity, usefulness, fundraising, informal relationships, institutional familiarity and the assumption that somebody senior must surely know what they are doing can combine to make ordinary boundaries strangely permeable. Access which would look exceptional if granted to an unknown person can begin to feel socially ordinary once the individual involved is famous, rich, useful, connected, entertaining, philanthropic, politically important, or simply treated by enough other powerful people as someone who belongs.

This is the bit Britain repeatedly seems not to retain.

We keep asking, after scandals, *how did this happen?* as though the answer will always be found in a secret room containing one villain and one spectacularly corrupt official, when much of the actual machinery is considerably more banal. Someone knew him. Someone vouched for him. Someone thought it would be embarrassing to say no. Someone assumed somebody else had already checked. Someone decided the normal rule was probably a little too rigid for this particular circumstance. Someone senior was aware, which became a substitute for asking whether senior awareness constituted a reason.

The problem is therefore not always absence of law, policy or procedure. It is the ease with which **social permission can bleed through formal boundaries**.

That is what I mean by porosity.

A rule may remain perfectly intact on paper while becoming soft in practice around the right person. The institution can continue to insist that everybody is subject to the same governance regime while simultaneously producing a series of individually defensible exceptions, each one small enough to appear harmless and each one made easier by the fact that the beneficiary already carries enough status to make refusal feel abnormal.

This does not require corruption in the cinematic sense.

Indeed, the more worrying version often requires almost nobody to think they are doing anything wrong.

A friend can arrange something.

A donor can ask.

A board member can suggest.

A minister can call.

A celebrity can be recognised.

A contractor can be treated as unusually important.

A person with enough prestige can become somebody for whom the organisation begins solving the problem of access rather than applying the ordinary question of whether access is necessary.

That distinction matters.

The governance question is not *is Peter Thiel dangerous because he looked at the Bayeux Tapestry?* That would be ridiculous. Nor is it that everybody with a reputational complication should be prevented from entering a museum, which would become both unworkable and rather boring.

The useful question is much smaller:

**why did the exception exist?**

Who authorised it?

What institutional purpose did it serve?

What criterion was being applied?

Would the same reasoning survive if the individual's name were removed from the paper?

And if the answer is simply *well, somebody knew him and it seemed like a nice thing to do*, that may be an entirely satisfactory explanation at the level of friendship while remaining a revealing one at the level of institutional governance.

The timing does not help, because the wider reputational field around Epstein remains unresolved enough that British institutions ought to be unusually alive to the distinction between **allegation against an individual** and **risk created by proximity to an unfinished disclosure environment**. Those are not the same thing, and it would be irresponsible to collapse them; but equally, one does not need to accuse every person appearing anywhere near that network of criminality in order to understand why institutions should be cautious about creating unnecessary forms of exceptional intimacy, privilege or visible endorsement while the factual record remains politically and legally contested.

This is where Britain has a repeated problem of learning the wrong lesson.

After Savile, the lesson should not have been *be suspicious of strange celebrities*. After Epstein, the lesson should not be *never speak to rich men with complicated social networks*. After every safeguarding inquiry, the lesson should not be *invent another category of obviously dangerous person and watch for that exact costume next time*.

The lesson is that **status changes how institutions behave**, and therefore status itself has to be treated as a risk factor in governance.

The more useful, connected, wealthy, famous, politically sensitive or institutionally valuable somebody is, the more conscious an organisation should become of the possibility that ordinary friction is being removed around them without anybody explicitly deciding to remove it.

That is almost the inverse of what often happens.

Instead, status arrives carrying its own solvent.

The queue becomes shorter.

The introduction becomes easier.

The exception feels less exceptional.

The boundary which would be obvious around an ordinary stranger becomes negotiable around somebody who is already socially authenticated by other powerful people.

And then, if something later goes wrong, Britain convenes another inquiry to discover how so many people failed to notice the door had been left open.

This is why the Bayeux viewing is useful precisely because, as far as we know, nothing terrible happened.

It is a low-stakes demonstration of the mechanism.

A prestigious person is offered access which ordinary people do not have, through relationships which appear capable of bypassing the ordinary public route, inside an institution which had already reportedly considered how that access might be perceived.

The light goes on.

The door opens.

Everyone involved can plausibly believe the whole thing is harmless.

And that is exactly the moment at which governance is supposed to be easiest.

Britain does not particularly lack rules.

It lacks friction which remains equally solid when somebody important leans on it.
