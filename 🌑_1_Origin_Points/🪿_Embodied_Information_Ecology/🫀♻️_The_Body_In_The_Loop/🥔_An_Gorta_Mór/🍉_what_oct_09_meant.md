# 🍉 What October 09 Meant
**First created:** 2026-10-06 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-08  
*Sometimes the first information you receive from another person's history is that something hurts.*

---

## 🛰️ 1. Orientation — This Is Not An Equivalence Machine

On 7 October 2023, Hamas and other Palestinian armed groups attacked communities in southern Israel, killing civilians, taking hostages and firing indiscriminate rockets. On 9 October, Israel's defence minister announced a "complete siege" of Gaza: no electricity, food, water or fuel. Humanitarian organisations rapidly warned that civilians could not lawfully be made to bear punishment for acts they did not commit.

That date landed somewhere complicated in me.

I was not receiving it from one clean identity position. I was receiving it through several histories at once: Jewish history; Irish diasporic history; British history; working-class history; histories I knew well; histories I thought I knew well; and histories which, it turned out, I barely knew at all.

And very early, while people were openly arguing for population-level deprivation and talking as though what Hamas had done could transfer moral responsibility onto Palestinian civilians, a thought arrived before I had polished language for it:

> **Oh... so you think we deserved An Gorta Mór?**

That was not a claim that Gaza and An Gorta Mór are the same historical event.

They are not.

The actors are different. The political systems are different. The technologies are different. The legal environments are different. The histories preceding them are different. The intentions, mechanisms, chronology and available forms of evidence require separate examination.

What travelled was not an equivalence between events.

What travelled was a **principle**.

```text
IF civilian deprivation becomes acceptable
because of what some members of a population did,

THEN:

where else am I prepared
to apply that principle?
```

That is the beginning of this node.

Not:

```text
GAZA = AN GORTA MÓR
```

But:

```text
A MORAL PROPOSITION APPEARS
        ↓
RUN THE PROPOSITION
THROUGH OTHER HISTORIES
        ↓
DOES IT STILL LOOK ACCEPTABLE?
```

Historical comparison should increase resolution, not erase difference.

This node is therefore partly about 9 October 2023. It is also about what happened afterwards inside the information system of one person trying to understand why that moment hurt so much, what histories it activated, how many holes appeared in histories I thought I possessed, and what I learned about listening when other people tell me that something hurts them too.

## 🪞 2. Run The Principle Backwards

One useful way to test a moral rule is to remove it from the event in which we first encountered it.

Political events arrive with identities already attached. We know who we fear, who we sympathise with, whose government we trust, whose language feels familiar, which histories we have inherited, which images we have already seen, and which explanations reached us first.

Those things affect interpretation.

So strip the proposition down.

```text
A POPULATION CONTAINS PEOPLE
WHO HAVE COMMITTED A TERRIBLE ACT
        ↓
THE POPULATION IS TREATED
AS A COLLECTIVE MORAL SUBJECT
        ↓
ACCESS TO THE CONDITIONS OF LIFE
MAY BE RESTRICTED
        ↓
CIVILIAN SUFFERING BECOMES
PARTLY ATTRIBUTABLE TO
THE POPULATION ITSELF
        ↓
"THEY BROUGHT THIS ON THEMSELVES"
```

Now move it.

Put different people into the population box.

Put your own people into it.

Put people whose suffering history has already taught you to recognise into it.

Put people you dislike into it.

Put people whose politics you do not understand into it.

Put people whose government has committed atrocities into it.

Put children into it.

If the principle changes depending on whose body appears at the bottom, the principle was never as general as it sounded.

For me, Ireland became one of the tests because Ireland was already inside me as inherited history even where my knowledge of the history was low-resolution.

Jewish history supplied another obvious warning. One of the recurring dangers in antisemitic systems has been the conversion of individual Jews into a collective explanatory object: what one Jew does, what an imagined Jewish group does, what a Jewish institution does, what a state associated with Jews does, becomes available for transfer onto unrelated Jewish people.

The histories are not interchangeable.

The informational failure can nevertheless be examined:

```text
PERSON
  ↓
MEMBER OF GROUP
  ↓
GROUP BECOMES UNIT OF EXPLANATION
  ↓
INDIVIDUAL CONDUCT DISAPPEARS
  ↓
COLLECTIVE RESPONSIBILITY
```

That is dangerous precisely because it is such an efficient compression system.

It saves us the trouble of asking what a particular human being did.

It also saves us the trouble of noticing when they did nothing at all.

> **Compare the proposition before comparing the events.**

That does not tell us in advance whether two historical mechanisms are the same.

It tells us what question to investigate.

## 🫀 3. Sometimes Pain Is The First Information

I have spent a lot of time in Polaris arguing that information is embodied.

This is one of the places where that becomes extremely ordinary.

Sometimes the first information you receive about somebody else's history is not a book, archive, lecture or museum label.

It is that they flinch.

They get angry.

They become unexpectedly quiet.

They tell you that a word you used has a history you did not know about.

They tell you that the political arrangement you regard as normal sits inside a story that does not feel normal from where they are standing.

They tell you that a place matters.

They tell you that something hurts.

The bad information-processing move is:

```text
I DON'T UNDERSTAND
WHY THIS HURTS
        ↓
THEREFORE THE PAIN
IS IRRATIONAL
```

A better move is:

```text
I DON'T UNDERSTAND
WHY THIS HURTS
        ↓
MY MODEL MAY BE
MISSING INFORMATION
```

That does not mean pain is automatically historically accurate.

People can inherit myths. Communities can compress histories. Family stories can change in transmission. Political movements can recruit real pain into inaccurate explanations. Memory can exclude as well as preserve. Trauma does not confer omniscience.

So:

> **Pain is information. Pain is not infallibility.**

The existence of pain does not settle the historical question.

It tells me there may be a historical question I have not yet understood.

That is a very different proposition.

And it is enough to begin with.

## 🧳 4. Diaspora Does Not Give You A Complete Archive

There is an odd assumption that proximity to a history should automatically produce expertise in it.

It does not.

Diaspora can transmit names, foods, jokes, silences, fears, songs, political instincts, family stories, religious practices, grief, pride, fragments of language, relationships to places, relationships to states, relationships to other communities, and extremely strong feelings about events whose actual mechanics were never fully explained.

Sometimes what survives is very high resolution.

Sometimes what survives is a checksum.

```text
THIS HISTORY IS IMPORTANT
TO MY COMMUNITY

≠

I PERSONALLY POSSESS
A HIGH-RESOLUTION MODEL
OF THIS HISTORY
```

I can be Jewish and still discover enormous holes in my knowledge of Jewish and European wartime history.

I can hold Irish diasporic history and still discover that my working model of An Gorta Mór was radically incomplete.

I can be British and discover that large parts of British imperial history barely entered my school education in a coherent form.

None of those things makes the inherited relationship unreal.

It means **inheritance and knowledge are different information channels**.

A community may successfully transmit:

> this mattered;

while incompletely transmitting:

> this is exactly what happened, in this order, through these institutions, with these disagreements, and this is how we know.

That distinction matters enormously for adult learning.

It means that discovering you do not know enough about your own history is not proof that you were fraudulent for caring about it.

It means you have found the edge of your current map.

Now you can go looking.

## 🏚️ 5. The Histories That Do Not Always Make The Survey Course

Not every life leaves the same documentary footprint.

Governments produce minutes.

Departments produce correspondence.

Armies produce orders.

Parliaments produce debates.

Senior politicians produce papers.

Diplomats produce dispatches.

Institutions are extremely good at leaving boxes behind.

Working-class life can leave boxes too, of course. Trade unions, political organisations, friendly societies, newspapers, courts, churches, synagogues, clubs, local government, employers and campaign organisations all generate records.

But a great deal of working-class history also travels through less centralised systems:

- neighbourhood memory;
- family stories;
- oral history;
- union branches;
- community organisations;
- local archives;
- songs;
- photographs;
- banners;
- demonstrations;
- newspapers that did not become the national newspaper of record;
- buildings;
- workplaces;
- street names;
- pubs;
- places where people assembled;
- places where people fought;
- places where somebody eventually put up a plaque because otherwise you could walk straight past and never know.

The problem is therefore not simply:

> **History forgot working-class people.**

That is too blunt.

The better questions are:

```text
WHO GENERATED RECORDS?
WHOSE RECORDS SURVIVED?
WHO COLLECTED THEM?
WHO CATALOGUED THEM?
WHO COULD ACCESS THEM?
WHO WROTE FROM THEM?
WHAT ENTERED EDUCATION?
WHAT ENTERED POPULAR MEMORY?
WHAT REQUIRED SOMEBODY
TO GO LOOKING?
```

A history can exist and still fail to reach most people.

That is an information problem.

It is also a solvable one.

## ✡️ 6. Warsaw — Finding Holes Inside A History I Thought Was Close

Warsaw gave me one of the clearest demonstrations of this.

There are two uprisings which need to remain distinct.

The **Warsaw Ghetto Uprising** began on 19 April 1943 when Jewish fighters and civilians resisted the German attempt to liquidate the ghetto and deport its remaining inhabitants. The resistance lasted nearly a month before German forces crushed it and destroyed the ghetto.

The **Warsaw Uprising** began on 1 August 1944, when the Polish Home Army attempted to liberate Warsaw from German occupation as Soviet forces approached. It lasted 63 days. German forces suppressed it, civilians were deported, and much of Warsaw was destroyed during and after the fighting.

Those are not two names for the same event.

And yet it is very possible to know that there was "an uprising in Warsaw" while possessing only fragments of one, fragments of the other, or accidentally merging them.

That mattered to me because this was not an example of discovering that some distant Other had a history I had never bothered to learn.

This was a hole inside a historical landscape I thought was comparatively close to me.

Even within Jewish communities, familiarity with Holocaust history does not automatically mean high-resolution familiarity with every resistance movement, political organisation, Polish history, Jewish political tradition, local sequence or relationship between events.

The moment you start writing, the holes become visible.

```text
"I KNOW THIS HISTORY"
        ↓
START WRITING IT
        ↓
HANG ON
        ↓
THERE IS A LOT HERE
I DID NOT ACTUALLY KNOW
        ↓
RESEARCH
        ↓
INHERITED MODEL
BECOMES HIGHER RESOLUTION
```

This is humbling in a useful way.

It makes it harder to look at somebody else's incomplete historical knowledge and immediately infer indifference.

Sometimes they do not know because they have never cared enough to look.

Sometimes they do not know because the information never reached them.

Sometimes they know the headline but not the machinery.

Sometimes they have been taught one part of the story so effectively that they never realised another part was missing.

Sometimes they are about to become extremely interested as soon as somebody opens the door.

## 🥔 7. Ireland — I Had The Headline, Not The Machine

An Gorta Mór has been another version of that experience.

I did not begin this cluster knowing nothing.

I also did not begin it knowing nearly enough.

The low-resolution model is familiar:

```text
POTATO BLIGHT
      ↓
FAMINE
      ↓
STARVATION
      ↓
EMIGRATION
```

There are true things inside that sequence.

It is also radically insufficient.

Once the mechanism begins to resolve, the picture becomes something more like:

```text
BLIGHT
+
LAND
+
TENANCY
+
SUBSISTENCE
+
WAGES
+
FOOD ACCESS
+
MARKETS
+
RELIEF
+
TAXATION
+
EVICTION
+
STATE KNOWLEDGE
+
POLITICAL ECONOMY
+
IMPERIAL RELATIONSHIP
        ↓
EMBODIED CATASTROPHE
```

The potato blight hits the potato.

The social system determines what happens to the human being whose potato has died.

That is why this cluster has had to become much larger than a node about a crop disease.

Before there was a famine, somebody had already reorganised the land.

Before somebody died, there were relationships between land, rent, work, food, ownership, relief and political authority.

Before later generations inherited a demographic scar, actual bodies had moved through actual systems.

And that brings me back to 9 October.

The thought **"so you think we deserved An Gorta Mór?"** arrived before I had reconstructed most of this machinery.

Emotion noticed the principle first.

Research then had to ask whether the historical analogy could actually carry what the emotion had put into it.

Sometimes it could.

Sometimes it needed qualification.

Sometimes the comparison revealed a useful question but not the same answer.

That is exactly what I want historical comparison to do.

```text
EMOTIONAL RECOGNITION
        ↓
QUESTION
        ↓
RESEARCH
        ↓
MECHANISM
        ↓
CORRECTION / QUALIFICATION
        ↓
HIGHER-RESOLUTION MODEL
```

Emotion can be information without automatically being the conclusion.

## 🗳️ 8. Republicanism — I Did Not Understand Why You Wanted This

I have talked a lot recently about republicanism because it gave me another, much less catastrophic, example of the same learning problem.

There were political attachments I did not properly understand.

My reaction could have been:

> I don't understand why you want this, therefore wanting it is irrational, sentimental, extreme, outdated or incomprehensible.

Instead I decided I wanted to know why.

So I listened.

I read.

I paid attention to what history people were invoking when they explained their position.

That does not require the endpoint to be:

> I now adopt every political conclusion reached by everybody whose history I understand better.

The more useful endpoint is:

> **I understand considerably more about why another person might reach it.**

That is not trivial.

```text
"I DON'T UNDERSTAND
WHY YOU WANT THIS"
        ↓
DO NOT AUTOMATICALLY CONCLUDE
"THEREFORE IT MAKES NO SENSE"
        ↓
LISTEN
        ↓
LEARN THE HISTORY
        ↓
UNDERSTAND THE PAIN,
MEMORY AND POLITICAL LOGIC
        ↓
"I CAN SEE WHY
YOU MIGHT WANT THIS"
```

Understanding is not compulsory agreement.

Empathy is not ideological conversion.

And this method has to remain available when the history belongs to somebody whose conclusions I find difficult too.

Otherwise it is not a method.

It is just sympathy with my existing preferences wearing academic glasses.

## 🧱 9. Cable Street — People Actually Want To Know

Cable Street gives me a more hopeful piece of information.

On 4 October 1936, Oswald Mosley's British Union of Fascists attempted to march through London's East End. Anti-fascist counterdemonstrators mobilised in opposition. The confrontation remembered as the Battle of Cable Street involved substantial clashes between counterdemonstrators and police; archival records complicate some of the simplified versions that later collective memory has produced.

That complication does not make the memory meaningless.

It makes it interesting.

The National Archives makes almost exactly the distinction Polaris keeps circling: archival evidence can increase the resolution of what happened, while local community activity helps determine how the event continues to be remembered.

And what I have noticed around the renewed building of knowledge about Cable Street is not simply that many people did not know much about it.

It is that **people want to learn**.

That matters.

```text
PEOPLE DON'T KNOW
        ≠
PEOPLE DON'T WANT TO KNOW
```

A missing history is not automatically a rejected history.

Sometimes somebody simply needs to transmit it.

Sometimes the response to:

> You were never taught this bit.

is:

> Wait, what happened?

Good.

Come here.

Look what we found.

That is a much more useful educational posture than treating incomplete knowledge as evidence of moral failure.

It also gives projects like Polaris somewhere legitimate to stand.

Not above the reader.

Beside them, with the archive open.

## 📡 10. Information Exists ≠ Information Was Transmitted

This is the information-ecology problem underneath all of these examples.

An event happening does not guarantee that information about it survives.

Information surviving does not guarantee that it enters an archive.

Entering an archive does not guarantee that somebody writes about it.

Being written about does not guarantee that it enters school education.

Entering school education does not guarantee that somebody remembers it.

Being remembered does not guarantee that the mechanism was understood.

Being important to a community does not guarantee that every member of that community receives it.

```text
HUMAN EXPERIENCE
      ↓
WHO COULD RECORD IT?
      ↓
WHAT SURVIVED?
      ↓
WHAT ENTERED THE ARCHIVE?
      ↓
WHAT BECAME "HISTORY"?
      ↓
WHAT ENTERED EDUCATION?
      ↓
WHAT ENTERED FAMILY /
COMMUNITY MEMORY?
      ↓
WHAT ENTERED POPULAR CULTURE?
      ↓
WHAT REACHED ME?
```

At every arrow, information can be:

- preserved;
- selected;
- compressed;
- translated;
- copied;
- contextualised;
- decontextualised;
- mythologised;
- challenged;
- forgotten;
- rediscovered.

This is why **"the information was available"** is not a sufficient description of an information environment.

Available where?

To whom?

In what form?

Behind what paywall?

In which language?

Catalogued under what term?

Was it taught?

Was it transmitted orally?

Did the community holding it have enough institutional power to make it part of national memory?

Did somebody need to know the correct search term before they could discover that the information existed?

Could a person encounter it accidentally?

Did it require them to already care?

The archive matters.

Transmission matters too.

## 🏫 11. British Empire — "We Were Taught This" Is A Complicated Sentence

I do not remember being taught British imperial history well.

That personal memory should not be inflated into the claim that nobody in Britain is taught empire.

The English national curriculum contains routes through which the British Empire can be taught, and schools and teachers have considerable flexibility. Academies also do not operate under exactly the same curriculum obligations as maintained schools.

At the same time, the existence of curriculum possibilities does not tell us what individual pupils actually receive. Researchers at UCL and Oxford have explicitly been investigating how empire, migration and belonging are taught across English secondary schools because the actual pattern of teaching has been insufficiently mapped. Calls from pupils, teachers and the public for greater attention to colonial history are themselves evidence that formal curricular availability and experienced historical education are not the same thing.

So I want to preserve the autobiographical claim at the correct resolution:

> **I did not receive a sufficiently coherent education in British imperial history to give me the model I later discovered I needed.**

That is enough.

I do not need to manufacture a universal British childhood around it.

And the gap matters because historical education is not only about knowing that an empire existed.

It can give us vocabulary for recognising mechanisms:

- extraction;
- land control;
- racialisation;
- collective punishment;
- emergency powers;
- displacement;
- administrative abstraction;
- dependency;
- resistance;
- counterinsurgency;
- the gap between formal legal status and lived political power;
- the difference between information reaching the state and the state interpreting that information well.

Without that machinery, history can remain a collection of dates and costumes.

With it, history becomes pattern literacy.

## 🧠 12. Critical History Is Pattern Literacy

This is why I think critical examination of our cultural histories in the West is more necessary than ever.

Not because every Western child needs to be issued a sack of inherited guilt.

Not because history should become a tribunal in which living people are assigned the moral status of dead ancestors.

Not because every present event is secretly a replay of empire, fascism or famine.

Because historical knowledge gives us **questions**.

Someone hearing contemporary political language can learn to notice transformations like:

```text
PERSON
→ POPULATION
→ COLLECTIVE RESPONSIBILITY
```

or:

```text
CIVILIAN
→ ENEMY-ADJACENT CATEGORY
→ ACCEPTABLE DEPRIVATION
```

or:

```text
FOOD EXISTS
→ THEREFORE PEOPLE CAN EAT
```

when the missing variable is access.

Or:

```text
STATE RECEIVED INFORMATION
→ THEREFORE STATE UNDERSTOOD
→ THEREFORE STATE RESPONDED
```

when each arrow requires evidence.

Or:

```text
HISTORICAL PATTERN RECOGNISED
→ THEREFORE EVENTS ARE IDENTICAL
```

which is precisely the shortcut we are trying not to take.

The historically literate move is:

> **I recognise a possible mechanism. Now I need to find out whether it is actually operating here.**

Pattern recognition generates a question.

Evidence has to answer it.

## 🫱 13. Please Have Some Fucking Manners

After all that, we arrive at one of the great intellectual technologies of civilisation:

**basic manners.**

You are allowed to be new here.

Just do not behave as though being new makes you the expert.

If somebody tells you that you have stepped on something you did not know was there, you do not necessarily need a 14-part defence of why nobody could reasonably have expected you to know.

Sometimes the correct response is:

> Oh. I didn't know that. I'm sorry. Thank you for telling me.

Then update the model.

```text
I'M NEW TO THIS
→ say so

I DON'T UNDERSTAND
→ ask

SOMEBODY TELLS ME
SOMETHING HURTS
→ don't immediately litigate their pain

I GET SOMETHING WRONG
→ correct it

I OVERSTEP
→ apologise

SOMEBODY KNOWS MORE
→ listen

EVIDENCE CONTRADICTS MY MODEL
→ update the fucking model
```

This is not a demand for permanent deference to anybody who claims an identity connection to a subject.

People can still be wrong about their own community's history.

Experts can be wrong.

Archives can be incomplete.

Witnesses can disagree.

Political interpretations can conflict.

The point is much smaller and much more practical:

> **Do not make somebody else's history harder to discuss because admitting you are new to it feels embarrassing.**

Being corrected is not evidence that you should never have tried to learn.

It is evidence that learning is occurring.

## 🧮 14. Ignorance Is Not Refusal

There is an important moral difference between not knowing and refusing to know.

```text
I DIDN'T KNOW
≠
I DON'T CARE

I GOT IT WRONG
≠
I CANNOT LEARN

I INHERITED AN INCOMPLETE HISTORY
≠
I CHOSE ITS OMISSIONS
```

None of us selected the information environment into which we were born.

We did not choose our school curriculum as children.

We did not choose which family stories survived three generations.

We did not choose which working-class archives were preserved.

We did not choose which histories became blockbuster films and which remained in local record offices.

We did not choose which language our ancestors stopped speaking before we arrived.

But new information changes the situation.

```text
NEW INFORMATION ARRIVES
        ↓
WHAT DO I DO WITH IT?
```

That is where responsibility becomes more interesting.

Do I become curious?

Do I check?

Do I listen?

Do I correct the mistake?

Do I preserve the new information?

Or do I expend more energy protecting the old model than it would have taken to update it?

Historical maturity is not never having been ignorant.

That standard would disqualify the species.

Historical maturity is partly what happens **after ignorance becomes visible**.

At which point we have travelled through diaspora, empire, famine, genocide, resistance, political identity, archives, collective memory and embodied information ecology in order to rediscover Adult Learning 101.

```text
NEW THING
   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓
"OH. SORRY."
   ↓

   ↓

   ↓
```

Be curious.

Listen to people who know more.

Take it on the chin when you overstep.

Correct the factual error.

Carry on.

> **Historical literacy is partly knowing history. Historical maturity is knowing what to do when you discover you didn't.**

Or, in the pocket version:

> **Try not to be a dick while learning.**

## 🪶 16. Polaris As A Place To Transmit

This leaves a question about what Polaris is for.

It cannot hold every history.

It cannot make me omniscient.

It cannot guarantee that every first draft is correct.

It should never pretend that I arrived at every subject already knowing what I was talking about.

In fact, one of the most useful things it can preserve is the opposite.

```text
HERE IS WHAT I THOUGHT
        ↓
HERE IS WHAT HURT
        ↓
HERE IS WHAT I DIDN'T UNDERSTAND
        ↓
HERE IS WHAT SOMEBODY ELSE TOLD ME
        ↓
HERE IS WHAT I WENT LOOKING FOR
        ↓
HERE IS WHAT THE EVIDENCE SUPPORTS
        ↓
HERE IS WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAIN
        ↓
HERE IS WHAT I GOT WRONG
        ↓
HERE IS THE UPDATED MODEL
        ↓
HERE IS WHERE YOU CAN KEEP LOOKING
```

That is not a weakness in the archive.

That is provenance.

It lets another person see the difference between inherited memory, emotional recognition, hypothesis, source evidence, interpretation and correction.

And because people demonstrably do want to learn histories that did not previously reach them, there is a place for transmitting those histories as honestly and accurately as we can.

Not:

```text
YOU SHOULD ALREADY KNOW THIS
        ↓
YOU DON'T
        ↓
FAILURE
```

But:

```text
THIS HISTORY EXISTS
        ↓
ITS TRANSMISSION HAS BEEN UNEVEN
        ↓
SOME OF US DIDN'T KNOW IT EITHER
        ↓
PEOPLE WANT TO KNOW
        ↓
SO LET'S MAKE A PLACE
WHERE THE INFORMATION CAN TRAVEL
```

The tone matters.

Not:

> How dare you not know?

But:

> Oh, you haven't been told this bit? Come here.

There are times when ignorance is wilful.

There are times when historical distortion is politically useful and deliberately maintained.

There are times when somebody has been corrected repeatedly and has chosen the lie anyway.

Those cases deserve their own analysis.

They should not require us to treat every person encountering a history for the first time as though they personally burned the archive.

People learn better when learning remains possible.

## ⚖️ 17. The Law Does Not Treat A Population As One Defendant

The emotional question that began this node is also a legal question, although the two should not be mistaken for the same thing.

International humanitarian law distinguishes civilians from combatants and prohibits punishing protected people for acts they have not personally committed. Article 33 of the Fourth Geneva Convention expressly prohibits collective penalties and measures of intimidation or terrorism against protected persons. Other rules govern starvation of civilians as a method of warfare, siege operations, humanitarian relief, and attacks affecting civilian objects. The rules interact, but they are not synonyms.

That distinction matters when reconstructing 9 October. There is a difference between:

- **A statement:** what a minister publicly announced, in what language, and with what claimed rationale.
- **A decision:** what the government or military authorities actually ordered.
- **Implementation:** which restrictions were applied, where, to whom, and for how long.
- **Consequences:** what happened to access to electricity, water, food, fuel, medicine, sanitation and healthcare.
- **Legal assessment:** which specific obligations applied and whether the documented conduct met the elements of a violation.

The announcement is evidence of a public position. It is not, without further investigation, a complete chronology of everything subsequently done. Nor is the existence of a legal prohibition, on its own, a judicial finding about a particular person or act.

None of those distinctions makes it unreasonable to examine what was being announced. Quite the opposite. The more consequential the announcement, the more necessary it becomes to follow the chain from language to authority to bodily effects.

```text
PUBLIC JUSTIFICATION
       ↓
DECISION-MAKING AUTHORITY
       ↓
ORDERS AND IMPLEMENTATION
       ↓
CIVILIAN ACCESS TO NECESSITIES
       ↓
EMBODIED CONSEQUENCES
       ↓
EVIDENCE, SCRUTINY, ACCOUNTABILITY
```

For this node, the historical record of **7 October 2023** must also remain distinct from **9 October 2023**. The Hamas-led attacks killed civilians and involved hostage-taking. Those acts require recognition in their own right; they do not make every Palestinian civilian a perpetrator. Civilian protection does not depend on whether we find a population's government, armed groups or political movements sympathetic.

The legal research for a publication-ready version should use the original 9 October statement, a reliable translation, dated evidence of subsequent measures, the Fourth Geneva Convention, applicable rules on starvation and relief, and carefully attributed legal findings. Where the source record is incomplete or disputed, say so rather than smoothing it into certainty.

## 🥔 18. The Blight Did Not Put Anybody On Trial

Here is why the Irish question came so quickly, even before the historical research caught up with it.

A pathogen did not examine the character of the household whose potatoes it destroyed. It did not ask whether a cottier supported a particular political movement, whether a child had the correct constitutional loyalties, or whether a family was sufficiently grateful to the state governing them.

And yet the survival of those people depended on arrangements that were profoundly political: who held land, who paid rent, who could purchase alternative food, who received relief, what relief required, and how institutions responded when their assumptions met the evidence of mass suffering.

**The potato blight hits the potato. The social system determines what happens to the human being whose potato has died.**

That is not the same causal chain as a contemporary military siege. In Ireland, reconstructing the catastrophe means following agricultural dependence, tenancy, markets, public works, soup kitchens, Poor Law administration, eviction, disease and emigration. In Gaza, it means independently investigating armed conflict, control over supplies and crossings, military orders, civilian protection, humanitarian access and the conduct of the relevant parties. Different histories. Different governing law. Different evidence.

But the principle that first made me stop was not a claim that the chains were identical. It was the proposed movement from *something some people did* to *what a whole civilian population may be made to endure*.

If someone thinks that movement is acceptable, they ought to be able to explain the principle without quietly changing it when the population becomes familiar to them.

---

## 🕸️ 19. The Two Receivers — A Person And A State

A public announcement does not enter a neutral room. It arrives among people carrying different histories, different knowledge and different reasons to pay attention.

I heard it with Irish history in the room. I also heard it with Jewish history in the room. I did not have to choose which history was allowed to speak before I could ask a question.

```text
PUBLIC STATEMENT
      ↓
MY EXISTING KNOWLEDGE + INHERITED MEMORY
      ↓
"OH... SO YOU THINK WE DESERVED IT?"
      ↓
HISTORICAL AND LEGAL INVESTIGATION
      ↓
MODEL REVISED BY EVIDENCE
```

That is the **individual receiver**. The emotional reaction explains why the question became urgent to me. It does not prove the answer.

The **institutional receiver** is different. States and their agents exercise powers that individuals do not. They receive reports through administrative, military, diplomatic, medical, humanitarian and legal channels. Their duties are not exhausted by having heard the information. What matters is what they knew, when they knew it, what they could do, what they chose, and whether their conduct changed when consequences became visible.

```text
REPORTS OF HUMAN CONSEQUENCES
      ↓
WHO RECEIVED THEM — AND WHEN?
      ↓
WHAT COULD THAT INSTITUTION DO?
      ↓
WHAT DID IT ACTUALLY DO?
      ↓
WHAT HAPPENED TO THE PEOPLE AFFECTED?
      ↓
DID THE FEEDBACK CHANGE THE DECISION?
```

This is why **information collection is not institutional learning**. An archive can grow while a policy remains unchanged. A government can hear a warning and still misunderstand it. It can understand a warning and still decide not to act. Those are different failures and must be investigated separately.

The British state does not need to know everything, agree with everybody or get everything right the first time. Neither does any other state. But institutions exercising power over human lives must be capable of learning from information about what their decisions are doing.

**Reasonably receptive to feedback is healthy.** This is not a revolutionary demand for the abolition of disagreement. It is the minimum requirement for a system that intends to keep governing actual people rather than its own preferred description of them.

## 🍉 20. What October 09 Meant

So what did October 09 mean?

It meant that a present catastrophe collided with several inherited histories at once.

It meant hearing people talk about civilian deprivation and collective responsibility and discovering that one of the first thoughts my brain produced was:

> **Oh... so you think we deserved An Gorta Mór?**

It meant having to ask why that thought appeared.

It meant learning that recognising a mechanism is not the same thing as proving an equivalence.

It meant discovering that I knew less about An Gorta Mór than the strength of my inherited relationship to it had led me to assume.

It meant pulling apart land, tenancy, food access, relief, eviction, markets, political economy and state knowledge until "the potato famine" stopped being a sufficient explanation for human death.

It meant recognising that British imperial history had not reached me in the coherent form I now needed, while being careful not to turn my educational experience into everybody else's.

It meant discovering gaps inside histories much closer to Jewish communal memory too.

It meant Warsaw.

It meant learning to distinguish the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising from the Warsaw Uprising rather than allowing a familiar place-name to compress different histories into one vague memory of resistance.

It meant noticing how many working-class-adjacent histories can sit outside the default survey course while remaining alive in communities, local archives and physical places.

It meant Cable Street, and the rather hopeful discovery that **people actually want to know**.

It meant listening to people explain political attachments I did not understand and deciding that incomprehension was a reason to learn rather than proof that the attachment was irrational.

It meant republicanism.

It meant recognising that another person's pain can be the first piece of information I receive from a history I have not yet learned.

It meant learning that I can acknowledge that pain without pretending pain settles every factual question.

It meant accepting that diaspora gives me relationships to histories, not automatic mastery of them.

It meant accepting that nobody else is automatically master of theirs either.

It meant deciding that the appropriate response to discovering a gap is usually not shame.

It is curiosity.

And perhaps most importantly, it meant deciding that I would rather know.

Not because knowing enough history will make disagreement disappear.

It will not.

Not because everybody who understands one another will reach the same political conclusion.

They will not.

Not because every historical wound can be translated perfectly across cultures.

It cannot.

But because there is a profound difference between disagreeing with an actual human being situated inside their actual history and disagreeing with a low-resolution model you invented for them.

The work is not to know everything before speaking.

The work is to remain teachable.

> **Tell me what happened.**
>
> **I'll try to listen.**
>
> **If I get it wrong, tell me.**
>
> **I'll correct it.**
>
> **And if I find something worth carrying forward, I'll leave it here for the next person.**

That is not a complete political programme.

It is a decent way to be an adult in a world full of other people's histories.

And, honestly, we could do worse.

This node deliberately separates the author's remembered experience and interpretation from externally checkable historical claims.

### 9 October 2023 and collective punishment

- Amnesty International records Israeli Defence Minister Yoav Gallant's 9 October announcement of a complete siege of Gaza cutting electricity, food, water and fuel, and describes collective punishment of civilians as prohibited under international law.  
  https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2023/10/israel-opt-israel-must-lift-illegal-and-inhumane-blockade-on-gaza-as-power-plant-runs-out-of-fuel/
- The International Committee of the Red Cross warned on 13 October 2023 that the complete siege denying food, water and electricity, together with the evacuation instructions then issued, was incompatible with international humanitarian law. It also reiterated that the attacks on Israeli civilians and hostage-taking could not justify limitless destruction in Gaza.  
  https://www.icrc.org/en/document/Israel-and-occupied-territories-evacuation-order-of-gaza-triggers-catastrophic-humanitarian-consequences
- Amnesty International separately documented killings of Israeli civilians, hostage-taking and indiscriminate rocket attacks by Hamas and other Palestinian armed groups as grave violations and war crimes, while also rejecting collective punishment of Gaza's civilian population.  
  https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2023/10/israel-palestinian-armed-groups-must-be-held-accountable-for-deliberate-civilian-killings-abductions-and-indiscriminate-attacks/

- United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, **Warsaw Ghetto Uprising** — the uprising began on 19 April 1943 as German forces attempted to liquidate the ghetto; Jewish fighters and civilians resisted until the uprising was crushed in May.  
  https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/warsaw-ghetto-uprising
- United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, **Warsaw Uprising** — the Polish Home Army began the uprising on 1 August 1944; it lasted 63 days and was suppressed by German forces.  
  https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/the-warsaw-polish-uprising

- The National Archives, **The Battle of Cable Street** — archival overview of the 4 October 1936 confrontation, including the role of police, arrests, and the relationship between archival evidence and later collective/community memory.

  https://www.ucl.ac.uk/ioe/news/2024/mar/how-british-empire-and-its-legacy-being-taught-schools

### Evidence discipline

- The sentence **"Oh... so you think we deserved An Gorta Mór?"** records the author's own reaction. It is not presented as evidence that Gaza and An Gorta Mór are historically equivalent.
- Claims about the author's education, inherited memory, learning process, encounters with republicanism, and observations of what other people appear interested in learning are presented as situated testimony unless separately sourced.
- Historical analogies in this node are used to generate questions about mechanisms. They should not be treated as proof that legal classifications, intentions, political structures or causal chains are identical.
- The detailed evidentiary analysis of An Gorta Mór belongs in the sibling nodes of this cluster rather than being duplicated here.
