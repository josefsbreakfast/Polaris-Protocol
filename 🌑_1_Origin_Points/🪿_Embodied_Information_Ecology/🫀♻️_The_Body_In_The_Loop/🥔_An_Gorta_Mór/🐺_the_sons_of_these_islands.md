# 🐺 The Sons of These Islands
**First created:** 2026-10-12 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-12  
*The wolves we inherited, the institutions that hunted them, and the difference between loving a country and caring for its living world.*

---

*For my sons, the dear wolves of the archipelago.*  
*Our sovereignty. Hunted to death.*

## 🛰️ Orientation

There were wolves here. Not a decorative wolf stitched onto a banner, not a wolf-shaped dog politely attending a regimental function, and not an ancient Celtic wolf goddess invented in a gift shop. Actual wolves. Animals with bodies, families, appetites, territories and the entirely reasonable expectation that they might continue being wolves tomorrow.

Across these islands, people lived alongside wolves for centuries. Sometimes they feared them; sometimes they hunted them; sometimes they made them into stories, names, images and metaphors. In Ireland, the wild wolf eventually disappeared. The words did not. Neither did the stories, nor the fascination with the magnificent dogs bred or remembered for hunting them.

**The wolf on the shield. The wolf absent from the forest.**

That is the problem here. Not that cultural memory is false or useless. Quite the reverse: memory is one of the ways relationships outlive their original circumstances. But a remembered animal and a living animal are not interchangeable. A people can inherit the emblem of a creature while inheriting a landscape in which that creature can no longer survive.

This node follows that fracture through ecology, folklore, land administration, organised persecution, famine-era political economy and contemporary restoration. It does not claim that wolf extermination caused An Gorta Mór, or that wolves represented a universal ancient Celtic doctrine of sovereignty. Its argument about sovereignty is mine: **belonging should entail responsibility towards the living world, not merely a claim to possess its symbols.**

## 🌲 1. Before the Map

A wolf does not need to know which king holds a particular hill. It needs food, space, other wolves and conditions in which its young can survive. Its territory is not a human property title, and its ecological relationships do not depend on the vocabulary of whichever administration happens to be in office.

The wolves of Ireland lived in landscapes that were never frozen in time. Woodland expanded and contracted over the millennia; people cleared land, cultivated crops, grazed animals and altered habitats long before the seventeenth century. It would be absurd to describe every earlier century as an untouched Eden. It would be equally absurd to imagine that the landscape arrived fully formed as somebody's estate.

Ireland's wolves belonged to a changing ecological system. Their prey, movements and chances of survival depended on that system. Later hunting campaigns took place within a landscape already shaped by human decisions about farming, woodland, settlement and property. Those different pressures must be distinguished: a forest cleared for timber is not the same event as a wolf killed for a bounty, even if both alter the animal's prospects.

Nor does an Irish history automatically describe Scotland, Wales or England. Wolves vanished at different times across these places, under different institutions and ecological circumstances. The archipelago is a geographical invitation to compare, not permission to squash several histories into one generic *Celtic* anecdote.

## 🐾 2. The Wolf Is Not the Hound

The Irish Wolfhound is an extraordinary animal. It is also, for the avoidance of administrative doubt, **a dog**.

Large Irish hounds appear in historical traditions and records. Hounds mattered as valuable animals, as hunting companions and as gifts that could travel through aristocratic networks. But the early evidence does not justify drawing an unbroken genetic line from every ancient Irish hunting dog to the modern breed. In the nineteenth century, George Augustus Graham and other breeders undertook a reconstruction of the Irish Wolfhound. That reconstruction is part of the breed's history, not an embarrassment to be concealed behind a fictional pedigree stretching neatly into prehistory.

Here is the peculiarly human arrangement: admire the great dog, celebrate the hunt, remember the wolf in the dog's name — and lose the wild animal.

The wolfhound can survive in a kennel, a portrait, a club register or a ceremonial role. A wild wolf cannot. It requires an ecology. No amount of affectionate discussion of a magnificent hound substitutes for the other creature's continued presence.

This is not an accusation against dogs. Dogs have done absolutely nothing to deserve being appointed the entire historical explanation for human conduct. They were employed in the matter.

## 🪶 3. What We Called Them

Irish preserves several ways of naming wolves. *Mac tíre*, commonly understood as 'son of the land' or 'son of the country', is especially evocative. Historical *fáel* and related forms also survive in names such as *Faolán*. Other Irish and neighbouring languages preserve their own animal terms, names and local associations.

Names carry information. They tell us that an animal was sufficiently present in a society's language to be noticed, described, remembered or used as a comparison. A surname may preserve a wolf-word long after wolves disappear from the landscape where that family lives. A place name may do something similar, although each proposed derivation needs checking against historical forms: not everything that sounds like a wolf was named for one.

This is the first great survival of the wolves: **language outlives the animal**.

But language is not a blank cheque for mythology. A wolf-associated name does not establish that its bearer worshipped wolves, descended from wolves or belonged to an ancient secret society of Wolf People. We have enough interesting evidence without hiring a fictitious priesthood to fill the gaps.

## 🐺 4. The Wolves Who Were People

The medieval Ossory werewolf story is one of the strangest and most revealing things in this history. Gerald of Wales records a priest's encounter with a speaking wolf, whose account of human identity and transformation forces the priest into an extraordinary religious predicament. The dying female wolf and the question of the sacraments make the encounter far more complicated than a straightforward tale of a dangerous beast.

Other medieval Irish material, including traditions associated with Ossory in *Cóir Anmann*, preserves different wolf-human associations. Stories of Saint Ailbe and wolves also belong to the religious and narrative landscape, although their manuscript histories must be kept distinct.

These are **texts and traditions**, not eyewitness zoology. Their value is not diminished by refusing to claim that medieval people literally transformed into wolves. They show that the boundary between human and animal could become a site of moral, religious and social inquiry.

What happens when the being before you looks like one thing, speaks as another, and asks to be treated according to a category your institution was not prepared to accommodate?

A rather substantial classification problem, as it turns out. The wolf has arrived with an ontology and the priest has brought the paperwork.

We should also resist collecting every European werewolf trial, Scottish folktale and continental religious image into one imaginary ancient Celtic wolf cult. The stories are fascinating precisely because their places, periods and meanings differ. A story from Ossory is not automatically a story from Shetland; a Gallic coin is not an Irish ritual object; and a canine-associated deity is not necessarily a wolf deity.

## 🪙 5. The Wolf in the Object

Images can survive where animals do not. Coins, carved stones, manuscripts, heraldic devices and later illustrations preserve creatures — or creatures that viewers identify as wolves — long after the original maker is gone.

The British Museum's so-called Norfolk Wolf stater is an example of an Iron Age object whose animal identification is important to its modern description. Continental Gallic coins and Iberian sculpture provide other examples of wolf or wolf-like imagery. But these are not Irish objects merely because modern commentators place their makers somewhere beneath the sprawling umbrella of 'Celtic'. Their geography is part of their meaning.

The Roman-period dedication to Apollo Cunomaglos is another useful warning. The name has a **hound** association; translating it as 'Wolf Prince' manufactures an animal the inscription has not supplied. The Gundestrup Cauldron, meanwhile, cannot be drafted into evidence for an Irish wolf religion just because its animal imagery is visually irresistible.

We can love a beautiful object and still ask the boring but essential questions: Where was it found? Who made it? How was it dated? Is that actually a wolf? Who first called it one?

Apparently the archaeologists would like the provenance before we establish a national theology. Very unreasonable of them. Also correct.

## 👑 6. The Country as a Woman

Medieval Irish literature offers female figures associated with the land and its naming. Ériu, Banba and Fódla appear in the traditions preserved by *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, whose surviving texts are medieval literary and pseudohistorical compositions, not transcripts of prehistoric political events.

There are wider traditions of female personification and sovereignty to explore. Brigantia and Brigit raise important questions about related names, languages and religious histories. Similarity does not make them identical, and neither figure should be assigned a wolf cult without evidence.

But there is a second distinction I care about more. **A country imagined as a woman is not the same as women possessing political authority within it.** A symbolic woman may be revered while actual women have their rights, labour, sexuality, landholding and voices controlled by other people.

That is not a problem solved by adding more goddesses to the mood board.

The symbolic woman and the living woman are different subjects. The symbolic wolf and the living wolf are different subjects. The resemblance is analytically useful, but neither history should be flattened into the other.

And the wolf as sovereignty? That is **my interpretation here**, not a recovered universal doctrine of ancient Ireland. The wolves belonged to the living landscape before the modern claims of exclusive ownership through which humans later managed it. That is the historical condition from which I am making a contemporary ethical argument.

## 🏰 7. A Matter of Property

The institutions governing Ireland changed repeatedly. Tudor expansion of English Crown authority, Stuart plantation, the wars and confiscations of the seventeenth century, Cromwellian administration, Restoration settlements and later landlord–tenant relations were not one uninterrupted office staffed by the same people wearing the same unfortunate hats.

They did, however, reshape who could claim land, extract resources, impose obligations and determine the permissible uses of a landscape. Confiscation and redistribution helped produce enduring inequalities, even though particular estates changed hands through inheritance, marriage, sale, debt and further political settlement.

The question for the wolves is not whether every landlord personally hated them. It is how wolves came to be treated as an obstacle to particular human uses of land — especially livestock management, hunting interests and property administration — and how institutions gave practical force to that judgement.

A wolf inhabits a territory. A landlord claims an estate. Those are not two versions of the same legal right. They are different systems of relationship with the same material place, and one of them increasingly possessed the authority to organise the destruction of the other.

## 💰 8. The Price of a Wolf

Organised wolf persecution in Ireland is documented in early modern historical research. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century proposals, hunting obligations and bounty arrangements show that the animal could become an object of administrative action. The Cromwellian period is particularly associated with rewards for killing wolves.

The exact amounts, dates and conditions of individual bounty schedules need checking against original records before they become quotations in this node. The reported 1653 Captain Edward Piers arrangement, for example, should not be retold as an unconditional gift of land for killing wolves: the research lead concerns a **lease with alleged hunting conditions**, and the instrument still needs direct examination.

But the institutional mechanism is intelligible without pretending every surviving number has been verified.

First, an authority classifies the animal as undesirable. Then it creates incentives or obligations to destroy it. People act within those incentives. Wolf mortality rises; reproduction and dispersal may no longer compensate for losses; local populations disappear.

A piece of information — *this animal is worth money dead* — becomes a change in behaviour, and then a change in bodies.

This is what makes the story so important to *Embodied Information Ecology*. The administrative classification does not remain safely in an office. It travels into the landscape.

There is something horribly impressive about the bureaucratic capacity involved. The wolf has not filed a complaint, sought an audience or proposed a counter-offer. Nevertheless, the administration has found a way to put a price on its head.

## 🌲 9. Where the Forest Went

Wolf persecution took place amid much longer transformations of Irish land use. Woodland was cleared for different purposes at different times; farming, grazing, fuel demands, timber extraction and estate management changed habitats. None of this can be reduced to a single, tidy graph in which Ireland begins as 80 per cent forest and ends at one per cent on the exact date the final wolf obligingly dies.

Habitat change matters, but its effects need to be demonstrated. Wolves are adaptable animals; they do not require continuous deep forest in every environment. Direct killing, prey availability, human settlement and landscape fragmentation may interact in ways that vary by region and period.

This is a story about the reorganisation of an ecology, not an assertion that one particular tree-felling operation caused the extinction of one particular wolf.

## 🪦 10. The Last Wolf

The year **1786**, associated with a reported killing near Mount Leinster, is conventionally repeated as the date of Ireland's last wolf. It is better understood as a *reported last recorded killing*, whose source chain requires care.

There may never have been a witness who knew they were watching the final wolf die. Extinction does not owe us a scene with a convenient date, a named hunter and a perfectly timed curtain.

The records become thin. The encounters cease. The animal stops appearing in the landscape except through memory, names and stories.

A last record is not necessarily a last life.

I find that distinction worth keeping. The uncertainty does not make the loss smaller. It tells us something about how disappearance can happen: gradually enough that people adjust, until the absence becomes ordinary.

## 🥔 11. An Gorta Mór: Another History of Land and Survival

The wolves were gone before An Gorta Mór. The conventional 1786 wolf account precedes the beginning of the Great Famine in **1845** by nearly six decades. **Wolf extinction did not cause the Famine.** This is not a disguised argument that the same event happened twice to different species.

An Gorta Mór was a human catastrophe with its own history: potato blight, dependence on a vulnerable subsistence crop, unequal access to land, poverty, relief policy, food markets, epidemic disease, eviction, emigration and mass mortality. Those mechanisms deserve their own account, not a passing cameo in a wildlife essay.

The potato blight was caused by *Phytophthora infestans*. But a biological crop failure alone does not explain why households lacked alternatives, why relief operated as it did, or why suffering differed so profoundly between places and people.

Peel's government responded initially with food purchases and relief measures. Russell's government inherited a worsening disaster in 1846 and oversaw changing systems of public works, temporary food relief and Poor Law administration. These policies had distinct intentions, practical constraints and outcomes. The 1847 Poor Law Extension Act, including the provision commonly called the **Gregory clause**, linked relief eligibility for certain occupiers to a quarter-acre land threshold. The exact statutory terms and their administration must be checked before using an extended legal quotation.

Food continued to be produced and traded in Ireland. Exports and imports both matter to the history. So do prices, wages, land access and the capacity to purchase food. A market containing food does not guarantee that a hungry household can obtain it. Nor can every exported commodity simply be counted as food that would otherwise have reached a starving family without analysing timing, distribution and quantities.

Disease killed as well as hunger. Eviction, overcrowding and displacement altered people's exposure to danger. Emigration removed people from the population without making them famine deaths. Counties and districts experienced the catastrophe differently. None of those distinctions should be sacrificed for a dramatic sentence.

Governmental responsibility is a serious historical question, and the relevant actors must be named: Westminster governments, Irish administrators, poor-law authorities and individual landlords exercised different kinds of power. Their decisions should be examined rather than dissolved into a vague claim that 'the British' operated as one unchanged actor across centuries.

The reason this history belongs alongside the wolves is **not equivalence**. It is that both histories compel us to examine the relationship between institutions, land and the conditions of survival. The suffering of the Famine remains human, historically specific and irreducible to metaphor.

## 🫀 12. Who Gets to Survive?

An institution does not need to touch a body directly to alter what happens to it.

In the wolf-bounty system, an animal is assigned a category and a price. That classification changes the incentives facing hunters. The consequences are borne by wolves.

In famine-era relief administration, a household's occupation of land could affect its eligibility for assistance. A classification within a legal and administrative system could therefore alter access to food, shelter or the means of obtaining them. The consequences were borne by people.

These are not morally equivalent histories. They are **different examples of an institutional mechanism**: information is collected or assigned, interpreted through rules, converted into decisions, and experienced materially by living bodies.

The ecological system also has feedback. Remove a predator and other relationships may change. Remove a household's access to land and its options for food, work and shelter change. Neither outcome can be read from the administrative record alone. The living system continues responding after the paperwork is complete.

The central cybernetic point is not that every harm was inevitable or intended by every official. It is that institutions can change survival conditions at scale, and the effects may be distributed unevenly among those with very different capacities to resist them.

A form is not merely a form when it decides whether you eat. A bounty is not merely a number when it encourages somebody to kill.

## 🏛️ 13. The Trouble With Inheritance

The inheritance of a country is not one thing. There is ancestry, which concerns descent. There is property, which concerns legally recognised ownership. There is belonging, which can arise through family, residence, community, language, participation and attachment. And there is responsibility, which concerns what people actually do towards the places and lives with which they are entangled.

These concepts overlap. They are not synonyms.

Ireland's history contains conquest, settlement, migration, emigration and diaspora. It cannot be honestly narrated as one pure ancestral population possessing one uninterrupted and uncontested claim to a static landscape. Nor does acknowledging that complexity mean dismissing the violence of colonisation or the importance of cultural continuity.

When contemporary political actors invoke ancestry or cultural preservation, their actual words matter. We should not assign one position to every nationalist, nor invent an organisation that celebrates wolves while opposing restoration merely because the contradiction would be rhetorically delightful. If a specific organisation makes both claims, we can document it. Until then, the contradiction is a question, not a quotation.

But I can still ask what it means to claim an inheritance whose living systems have been diminished. What precisely are we preserving? A language? A set of family stories? A right to exclude? A landscape? The species and relationships that once made it possible?

The answer need not be the same for everyone. The question is not optional simply because it is inconvenient.

## 🪡 14. Our Very Impressive Cultural Memory

We have retained wolf-words, wolf-stories, wolf-like images, heraldic beasts, literary transformations and the modern Irish Wolfhound. Music and oral traditions may preserve further traces, although the earliest titles and melodies of several proposed examples remain unresolved. *Banríon na Luachra* can plausibly be translated as **Queen of the Rushes**; it has not been established as an ancient wolf-queen title. *Banríon na gCon* means **Queen of the Hounds** as an Irish expression, but a historical title or cult association has not been demonstrated.

Cultural survival is real. It deserves study, not sneering. A name or story may be the only remaining evidence that a relationship once mattered.

But it is possible to preserve an image with exquisite care while neglecting the ecology that image depicts.

The wolf on the coin. The wolf in the family name. The wolf on the shield. The wolfhound in the ceremonial parade.

The wolf in the forest?

Ah. Well. Regarding that.

This is not an accusation that heritage work is useless. It is a reminder that **symbolic preservation and ecological preservation do different jobs**. One cannot quietly be substituted for the other and declared a complete success.

## 🌱 15. Why Are You Not Rewilding?

Here we arrive at the question that makes the historical journey contemporary.

If the living world is part of our inheritance, why is ecological restoration not more central to the way we speak about preserving culture?

This does **not** mean 'release wolves immediately'. Wolf reintroduction would require serious assessment of habitat, prey, dispersal, roads, genetic viability, livestock predation, animal welfare, public safety, law and consent. Ireland is an island: a reintroduced population could not simply rely on routine natural genetic exchange with continental populations. A wolf might also cross the border between the Republic and Northern Ireland, while the relevant laws and public authorities do not cross with it in any equally straightforward fashion.

Sheep farmers would face real questions about risk, labour, protection and compensation. Those costs cannot be wished away by people who enjoy the idea of wolves but do not live with the consequences. Nor should farmers be reduced to stock villains in a romantic restoration story. If coexistence is the goal, the people asked to coexist need meaningful power in the decisions and credible support in practice.

Wolves might affect deer populations, browsing and scavenger communities, but the size of those effects in Ireland is not established. Yellowstone is not a magic ecological recipe that can be photocopied onto another island. A scientifically credible proposal would need Ireland-specific modelling, transparent assumptions and public accountability. The legal framework in the Republic and Northern Ireland would need separate, current examination; historical presence alone is not permission to release an animal.

And here is the crucial part: **rewilding does not begin or end with wolves**.

Native woodland regeneration, deer management, peatland rewetting, river and wetland restoration, habitat connectivity and better protection of existing biodiversity can proceed independently. Those are not consolation prizes. They are material ways to repair ecological relationships.

So when I ask, *If you care about our culture, why are you not rewilding?*, I am not proposing a loyalty test in which every person must endorse the same predator-release programme.

I am asking why the living world so often disappears from the discussion of what a culture is trying to keep.

## 🐺 16. Our Sovereignty

The wolf did not possess sovereignty in the constitutional sense. It did not vote, issue passports or administer a border. I am not attributing a modern political theory to an animal, and I am not claiming that all ancient Irish people worshipped wolves as sovereign beings.

I am using the wolf to ask what sovereignty is *for*.

Human institutions gained the power to organise land, property, hunting, resources and the conditions under which other beings lived. Those powers could be exercised destructively. They can also be exercised differently. The existence of an institution does not settle whether its decisions are wise, just or ecologically survivable.

Ancestry may tell us something about where we came from. Ownership may tell us what the law permits us to control. Neither alone tells us whether we have cared for what is here.

To belong to a place is not necessarily to possess it exclusively. To inherit a landscape is not merely to inherit the right to decide what may be removed from it. And to love a culture is not only to preserve the stories of its lost inhabitants while accepting their absence as the natural order of things.

The wolves were not symbols first. They were alive first.

That is what I want to remember.

**The wolf on the shield. The wolf absent from the forest.**

*For my sons, the dear wolves of the archipelago.*  
*Our sovereignty. Hunted to death.*

The following is a **working source register**, not a claim that every item has been checked against its original manuscript, edition, object record or statute. ### 🔎 Publication safeguards

- **Documented or specialist-supported:** Historical Irish wolves; organised persecution; medieval wolf-related texts; changing land tenure; famine-era crop disease and relief policy; continuing wolf vocabulary; general ecological mechanisms.
- **Qualified:** The reported 1786 last killing; the size of early modern wolf populations; precise bounty amounts; contested animal identifications; particular manuscript dates and translations.
- **Unverified:** A universal ancient Celtic wolf cult; a wolf goddess named *Banríon na Luachra*; historical use of *Banríon na gCon* as an epithet; exact equivalence of named tunes; a specific viable Irish wolf population; any named political organisation simultaneously celebrating wolf symbolism and opposing wolf restoration.
- **Interpretative:** The wolf as sovereignty; ecological care as an obligation of belonging; the comparison of administrative mechanisms across distinct histories.

The source register must be expanded with passage-level citations before numerical or legal precision is added. Uncertainty is not a hole to decorate with an invented fact.
