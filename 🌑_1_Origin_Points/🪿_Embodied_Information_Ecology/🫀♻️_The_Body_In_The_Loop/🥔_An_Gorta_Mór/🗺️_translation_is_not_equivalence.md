# 🗺️ Translation Is Not Equivalence
**First created:** 2026-10-07 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-08  
*How to carry questions, mechanisms and useful models between histories without making the people who lived them interchangeable.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation — The Map Is Not The Territory, And Neither Is The Other Territory

Human beings compare things. This is not a regrettable malfunction to be corrected by a sufficiently stern historian. It is one of the ways we learn. We notice something, recognise part of it, and ask whether something we already know might help us understand what we are seeing.

Sometimes the comparison is illuminating. Sometimes it is wrong. Sometimes it starts well and becomes wrong when a resemblance is promoted into an entire explanation without anyone checking what was actually alike.

The governing distinction here is simple:

> **TRANSLATION asks:** Does something I learned over here help me ask a better question over there?
>
> **EQUIVALENCE claims:** These things are meaningfully the same.
>
> **THE FIRST DOES NOT REQUIRE THE SECOND.**

This is not a ban on equivalence. Two cases may be equivalent *for a specified purpose*, under stated criteria, after the relevant evidence has been examined. A comparison of two relief schemes might establish that both required a particular kind of eligibility test. That does not make the governments, catastrophes, or people identical.

Nor is this a demand for emotional detachment. Historical flattening can erase people. But a blanket prohibition on comparison can prevent us from learning from them. The task is to retain both the capacity to recognise and the obligation to investigate.

An Gorta Mór provides our main worked example. The Choctaw–Irish giving relationship and Indigenous Land Back politics demonstrate two different ways histories can become relevant to one another. Jewish diasporic memory, Cable Street, Palestine and the constitutional history of Ireland offer further tests. None is here to be folded into a single universal story of suffering.

**The people in the second history must be allowed to remain themselves.**

## 1. 🧠 Recognising Something Is The Beginning, Not The Verdict

Nobody approaches an unfamiliar event with an empty mind. We carry knowledge about food, land, law, families, governments, borders, medicine, violence and survival. That knowledge helps us notice things that might otherwise escape us. It also gives us expectations that can mislead us.

Suppose we learn that people can starve in a place where food continues to be produced. Later, another report describes hunger alongside food production. We have learned to ask a useful question: **Who can actually obtain the food?**

We have *not* learned, merely from that resemblance, that the two crises have the same causes, perpetrators, legal setting, remedies or history. The familiar observation supplies a lead. It does not finish the investigation.

Pattern recognition is therefore a hypothesis-generating activity. A resemblance may be evidence worth following. Its explanatory weight depends on what subsequent investigation establishes.

There is a temptation to mistake emotional recognition for causal proof. Recognising hunger in an image may tell us something important about a body. It cannot, by itself, identify every institution or decision responsible for that hunger. Equally, insisting that no two hungry bodies may be compared until every legal distinction has been recited would make learning a remarkably inefficient hobby.

**Recognition can start an inquiry. It cannot exempt the inquiry from evidence.**

## 2. 🗺️ Models Select; They Do Not Reproduce

A road map, a geological map and a political map may all describe the same territory. Each makes different information visible. None is the territory itself; none is useless because it leaves things out.

A historical model does something similar. A food-access model might attend to crops, imports, wages, prices, property, distribution and relief. A constitutional model might attend to representation, sovereignty, legislation and the power to obtain remedies. An embodied-information model asks how those arrangements are experienced in bodies and how evidence of their consequences returns—or fails to return—to decision-makers.

Problems arise when a model built for one question is mistaken for a complete account of everything. A good model makes its selection visible. It also remains correctable.

We should ask: **What does this model illuminate, what does it omit, and what observation would make us change it?** A map that cannot be corrected when the landscape contradicts it is no longer helping anyone navigate.

## 3. 🔍 Five Operations That Must Not Be Confused

| Operation | The work it performs | What it does **not** establish on its own |
|---|---|---|
| **Identification** | Establishes what a person, event, institution or rule is in its own setting | That it resembles another case |
| **Comparison** | Examines similarities **and** differences | Shared causes or responsibilities |
| **Analogy** | Uses a relationship in one setting to illuminate another | Identity of the settings |
| **Translation** | Carries a question, concept or model into another setting and tests it | That its meaning survives unchanged |
| **Equivalence** | Claims sufficient sameness under defined criteria | Sameness in every respect |

A sixth operation often gets smuggled into the fifth: **classification**. Calling an event a famine, a forced removal, a crime, a genocide or an act of solidarity involves definitions and evidence specific to the claim. An analogy can suggest that a classification should be investigated; it cannot do the classificatory work by itself.

Before asking whether two things are the same, establish what each thing actually is. It sounds painfully obvious. Apparently this remains a growth area.

## 4. 🧳 What Are We Actually Carrying Across?

A comparison can transport very different intellectual cargo:

- **A question:** Who controls access to food?
- **A concept:** Effective entitlement, as distinct from the physical existence of a resource.
- **A hypothesis:** Loss of wages or purchasing power may have worsened hunger.
- **A mechanism:** A relief rule may make assistance conditional on surrendering an asset.
- **An institutional model:** Reports reach government, policy is chosen, consequences are observed, and the policy may or may not change.
- **A moral judgement:** A particular decision was unjustifiable.
- **A legal classification:** A specified rule or offence applies.
- **A political proposal:** Authority, land or resources ought to be redistributed in a particular way.

These are not synonyms. The question can travel very cheaply; the conclusion cannot. Each additional claim requires evidence from the place to which it is being applied.

For example, learning about a restrictive relief condition in nineteenth-century Ireland may prompt us to examine eligibility rules in a contemporary humanitarian programme. It does not prove that the contemporary programme has the same rule, motive, legal basis or effect. Those must be established separately.

A practical discipline is to state the cargo aloud: **I am borrowing a question**, or **I am testing a causal mechanism**, or **I am proposing a legal classification**. This reduces the chance of a modest observation arriving at its destination wearing the uniform of a settled verdict.

## 5. ⚙️ Mechanisms Are More Informative Than Familiar Labels

Large labels compress complicated histories. *Colonialism*, *dispossession*, *famine*, *occupation*, *reconciliation* and *restitution* can be useful terms, but they do not disclose every mechanism operating underneath them.

Consider dispossession. It might involve military conquest, coerced treaties, removal, confiscation, the reclassification of customary tenure, debt, eviction, exclusion from a resource, restrictions on movement, or the destruction of a home. Some cases involve several. Others involve one in a distinctive legal or political setting.

Two histories can share a mechanism without sharing an entire political structure. They can also share a label while the mechanisms that matter most differ.

To compare responsibly, identify the **actor**, **power exercised**, **instrument**, **people affected**, **material consequence**, **time period**, and **available remedy**. Then ask which parts are actually comparable.

> **A shared mechanism is evidence of a possible relationship between cases, not proof that the cases are identical.**

## 6. 🧪 Translation Is A Test, Not A Verdict

The working procedure for this node is deliberately reusable:

1. **Reconstruct the source context.** What happened, where, when, to whom, and under which institutions?
2. **Name the transferable element.** Is it a question, concept, mechanism, model, classification or proposal?
3. **Reconstruct the target context independently.** Do not populate its history with facts imported from the first case.
4. **Specify the resemblance.** Which properties are claimed to match, and why might they matter?
5. **Investigate the differences.** Which legal, material, social, temporal and political differences could change the explanation?
6. **Test the claim.** Does independent evidence support the proposed relationship?
7. **Keep, narrow, revise or discard it.** A failed analogy is useful information when it corrects the model.

```text
SOURCE HISTORY
      ↓
IDENTIFY QUESTION / CONCEPT / MECHANISM
      ↓
TARGET HISTORY — RECONSTRUCT INDEPENDENTLY
      ↓
TEST SIMILARITIES AND RELEVANT DIFFERENCES
      ↓
CHECK EVIDENCE AND SCOPE
      ↓
RETAIN / NARROW / REVISE / REJECT
      ↺
```

The target history is not obliged to fit our model. We do not get to discipline the territory for failing to resemble the map.

## 7. 🧭 Differences Are Not Embarrassing Exceptions

A weak analogy accumulates similarities and treats differences as inconvenient paperwork. A strong comparison treats differences as data.

Two crises may both involve hunger. In one, households may lose purchasing power amid continuing trade. In another, food movement may be restricted by armed actors. In a third, production and distribution infrastructure may both have collapsed. The embodied result can resemble itself while the causal and legal explanations differ substantially.

The differences determine what interventions are possible, who has authority, which obligations apply and what accountability requires. If a familiar explanation fails in the second case, the comparison has still improved our knowledge—provided we allow the failure to teach us something.

> **Sometimes the most valuable result of a comparison is discovering why the explanation that worked over there does not work over here.**

## 8. 🥔 Worked Example: The Potato Died; Why Did The People?

The blight affected potato crops. That biological event matters enormously. It does not, alone, explain why particular households became destitute, why some regions suffered differently, why disease killed so many, why relief took particular forms, or why people left.

Those questions require reconstruction of the social system onto which the blight landed: tenure, rents, wages, subsistence dependence, access to alternative food, commercial agriculture, markets, taxation, relief, eviction, migration and state administration.

The cluster's central formulation is worth retaining:

> **The potato blight hits the potato. The social system determines what happens to the human being whose potato has died.**

That sentence is a model, not a substitute for historical research. It tells us to look for mediating mechanisms between crop disease and human death. It does not say that every death can be attributed to one policy or that all Irish households occupied the same economic position.

The deeper substrate belongs in [🌱 Before The Blight](./🌱_before_the_blight.md), while [📐 English Land Controls](./📐_english_land_controls.md) traces how political and legal arrangements reshaped landholding and access.

## 9. 🌾 Food Existing Is Not Food Accessible

One of the most useful questions to carry away from An Gorta Mór is the difference between **availability** and **entitlement**.

The existence of food in a country does not mean every household owns it, can purchase it, can reach it, or is legally entitled to receive it. A person can be surrounded by resources from which they are effectively excluded.

But the converse shortcut is also unsound. The observation that food existed does not, on its own, prove that all food was deliberately withheld, that every official held the same intention, or that a single export figure explains every death.

The investigation must follow production, imports, exports, prices, wages, property rights, transport, relief and timing. Those relationships changed across the years of the catastrophe.

[🌾 There Was Always Food In Ireland](./🌾_there_was_always_food_in_ireland.md) develops the historical distinction. Here we are interested in its portability:

**What is the relationship between the existence of a resource and a person's effective capacity to access it?**

That question can be asked in many settings. The answer must be rebuilt in each.

## 10. 🏛️ What Was Known, Possible, Chosen—and Learned?

Another transferable structure is institutional feedback:

```text
WHAT WAS KNOWN — AND WHEN?
           ↓
WHAT OPTIONS WERE AVAILABLE?
           ↓
WHAT WAS CHOSEN, BY WHOM?
           ↓
WHAT CONSEQUENCES FOLLOWED?
           ↓
WHAT INFORMATION RETURNED?
           ↓
WHAT CHANGED — OR DID NOT?
```

In Ireland, the relevant record includes government reports, correspondence, relief administration, political debates and testimony from affected communities. We must distinguish the date a report was produced from the date a particular decision-maker received or acted on it. We must also distinguish awareness of suffering from accurate knowledge of its scale, causes and remedies.

[🇬🇧 What Britain Knew](./🇬🇧_what_britain_knew.md) follows the information. [🏛️ What Britain Did](./🏛️_what_britain_did.md) follows policy and implementation. [🇬🇧 Implications For Unionism](./🇬🇧_implications_for_unionism.md) examines what institutional continuity and constitutional responsibility mean for the ability to learn.

The structure can travel to other cases. The historical answers cannot be copied across with it.

## 11. 🪶 Two Ties, Two Different Relationships

The [🪶 Tied Twice To Land Back](./🪶_tied_twice_to_land_back.md) node develops two connections that must not be collapsed.

**The first is historical and relational:** Choctaw people contributed to relief for Ireland during An Gorta Mór; the gift was remembered, commemorated and invoked by Irish donors supporting Navajo and Hopi communities during the COVID-19 pandemic. These are acts and memories involving particular communities. Choctaw, Navajo and Hopi are not interchangeable identities.

**The second is political and comparative:** Indigenous Land Back movements raise questions about land, sovereignty, jurisdiction, restitution, treaty obligations and governance that can illuminate questions in Irish history. Their political programmes are not automatically Irish programmes, and Irish land reform is not a ready-made answer to Indigenous claims.

The first tie does not prove that the histories of dispossession are equivalent. The second does not depend on the first tie having existed. Their coexistence is interesting precisely because they are different kinds of relationship.

**Giving is not equivalence. Political learning is not appropriation.**

## 12. 🌱 Land Back: Learn The Questions Without Taking The Vocabulary Hostage

Land Back is not a decorative synonym for feeling strongly about ancestral land. Indigenous organisers use the language in relation to concrete, sometimes distinct demands involving land, sovereignty, authority, access, treaty rights, ecological stewardship and restitution. Their own accounts must lead any discussion of what a particular campaign seeks.

Irish land history invites comparison because conquest, plantation, confiscation, tenure, rent and later land reform all raise questions about who had the authority to define legitimate possession and who could live from the land.

Useful questions include: Who made the rules? Which relationships to land did those rules recognise? Who could enforce them? What happened to people whose relationships were excluded? What would repair mean: remembrance, access, ownership, jurisdiction, sovereignty, compensation, or some combination?

These questions do not authorise us to declare the political situations identical. They also do not permit a memorial to stand in automatically for material restitution.

> **Learning from another people's political vocabulary does not give us authority to redefine it.**

## 13. ⚖️ Similar Harm Does Not Confer Shared Legal Status

Two peoples may experience displacement without having the same relationship to the state that displaced them. One may possess treaty rights under a particular constitutional arrangement; another may have been governed through a landlord–tenant system under a different sovereign order. Different legal relationships create different duties, remedies and political possibilities.

Likewise, a description of historical harm is not automatically a legal classification. A causal explanation is not identical to a moral judgement. A moral judgement is not identical to proof of a particular offence.

For any strong legal claim, specify the governing law, relevant period, jurisdiction, legal elements and evidence. Later legal categories can be used retrospectively as analytical frameworks, but their temporal and doctrinal limits must be made explicit.

The point is not to hide behind law when people have suffered. It is to avoid telling people that a remedy or classification follows automatically when the actual legal architecture says otherwise. That architecture may itself deserve criticism or reform; that is a separate, necessary argument.

## 14. 🫀 Similar Bodies, Different Histories

Hunger, dehydration, disease, injury, displacement and death recur across human catastrophes. The body has a limited repertoire of ways to register certain kinds of harm. Images, testimony and memorials can therefore be powerfully recognisable across distance and time.

This is a feature of embodied information, not proof of historical identity. A body can show the presence and severity of harm without disclosing every cause. It may not tell us which official knew what, which rule governed access to food, whether an institution could have intervened, or what intention a particular actor possessed.

Conversely, abstracting every event into separate administrative categories can conceal the embodied consequences that ought to be central to analysis. The historical person is not merely a data point in the policy machine.

**Embodied consequence and causal explanation belong together. Neither replaces the other.**

## 15. 🩸 Genocide: Analogy Cannot Supply The Elements

The question explored in [🩸 Why Call It Genocide](./🩸_why_call_it_genocide.md) provides a demanding test of this method.

Mass death, foreseeable mass death, reckless policy, deliberate deprivation, intentional destruction and the legal crime of genocide are not interchangeable descriptions. Genocide has specific legal elements, including a particular intent requirement under the applicable convention. Historical inquiry into events preceding the modern legal framework also raises questions about retrospective terminology.

A resemblance to a catastrophe widely classified as genocide may prompt serious investigation. It does not independently prove that the second case meets the classification. Nor does a difference between cases settle the matter against the classification. The legal and historical evidence must be examined on its own terms.

We should neither promote every analogy into a verdict nor prohibit every analogy because the verdict is contested. **Ask which elements are alleged, what evidence bears on them, and where uncertainty remains.**

## 16. 🪞 Your Dead And My Dead

**YOUR DEAD + MY DEAD ≠ LESS MEMORY.**

Recognising another community's catastrophe does not subtract from one's own. Nor does acknowledging two catastrophes require describing them as the same kind of event.

The distinction matters when historical memory becomes a contest over who may speak, who may recognise, and whose experience counts as a legitimate starting point for understanding. A comparison can be inaccurate. A political claim can be challenged. Neither requires pretending that the person making it arrived without a history.

> **We can tell each other what happened without first assuming that the person listening has never had anything happen to their people.**

This is not an argument for equalising every claim. Power, responsibility, scale, intent and legal status remain available for careful analysis. Recognition makes those differences discussable; it does not abolish them.

## 17. 🍉 Palestine: Historical Knowledge Is A Prompt For Investigation

In this cluster, the author's reflections on Ireland, Jewish diasporic memory, Indigenous land politics and Palestine belong to an explicitly situated intellectual history. They explain why certain questions become salient. They do not substitute for evidence about contemporary events.

Questions that can travel include: Who controls land and movement? Who controls food, water, housing and medical access? Which authorities receive information about harm? What options are materially and legally available? Which duties apply? What remedies can affected people invoke? What would distinguish negligence, recklessness, intentional deprivation and other forms of wrongdoing in the relevant legal setting?

Every answer requires contemporary, case-specific evidence. Ireland's nineteenth-century political economy is not a template that can simply be laid over Gaza or the wider Palestinian experience. Indigenous sovereignty claims cannot be converted into Palestinian legal claims by analogy alone. Jewish histories are not interchangeable with either.

The question is whether previous knowledge helps us **notice what needs investigating**, while preserving the facts and voices of the people now affected.

[🍉 What October 09 Meant](./🍉_what_oct_09_meant.md) carries the author's contemporary reflection; this node supplies the method for keeping recognition distinct from proof.

## 18. 🧱 Cable Street: Coalition Is Not Identity

The 1936 mobilisation against the British Union of Fascists at Cable Street involved people and organisations from different communities and political traditions. Historical accounts of participation require attention to who actually organised, attended, resisted and subsequently narrated the event.

Its use here is deliberately limited. Cable Street was not a direct consequence of the Choctaw gift, and it is not a substitute for Irish or Indigenous history. It offers a separate question: **How can people who do not share ancestry or every political commitment identify a common immediate concern and act together?**

Shared action does not imply shared identity. Nor does it require that every participant remember the event in the same way. Coalition is an activity, not a magical ceremony in which everyone's history becomes one history.

## 19. 🇬🇧 Understanding Is Not Constitutional Conscription

The method also matters for Ireland's constitutional history. A person can learn about An Gorta Mór and criticise British historical governance without thereby adopting a particular present-day constitutional position. A Unionist can investigate Irish dispossession; a Nationalist can investigate Unionist experiences. Neither act of historical curiosity is, by itself, disloyalty to a community.

The legitimacy of contemporary constitutional positions and procedures must be examined through the relevant political and legal arrangements, including the principle of consent in Northern Ireland's settlement. Historical analogies can inform public discussion, but cannot replace those arrangements or the agency of people living under them.

[🇬🇧 Implications For Unionism](./🇬🇧_implications_for_unionism.md) develops this question through constitutional continuity, accountability, reconciliation and institutional learning.

**Understanding is not endorsement. Comparison is not constitutional conscription.**

## 20. 🕸️ Information Does Not Enter An Empty System

A historical fact is not received independently of the person or institution interpreting it. Prior knowledge, access to evidence, social position, professional categories, incentives and the questions being asked all shape what becomes salient.

A historian, a lawyer, a descendant, a civil servant and a person currently facing hunger may encounter the same account and ask very different questions. Those questions can all be intelligible without all subsequent claims being equally well supported.

This is where Embodied Information Ecology becomes particularly useful. Information has a route; interpretation has a context; decisions have material consequences. We need to know not only **what information existed**, but who encountered it, how it was processed, and what happened next.

The comparison itself is an intervention into that process. It changes what an observer may notice. That is a reason to use it carefully, not a reason to abandon it.

## 21. 🔄 Model Failure Is Useful Information

A model confronted with a mismatch has choices. The evidence may be unreliable or incomplete. The model may omit an important variable. The proposed analogy may have exceeded its scope. Or the analogy may simply be wrong.

```text
PRIOR MODEL
     ↓
NEW OBSERVATION
     ↓
PROPOSED COMPARISON
     ↓
EVIDENCE TEST
     ↓
MISMATCH?
     ↓
REVISE MODEL / NARROW CLAIM / DISCARD ANALOGY
     ↺
```

The important feature is the return path. A system that only absorbs confirming information is not learning very much. A state that records criticism but cannot alter a mistaken policy is not necessarily learning either.

The model must remain answerable to the people and material circumstances it purports to explain. When the second history tells us the first model does not fit, that is not insubordination from the second history.

## 22. 🧭 False Equivalence And False Exceptionalism

There are at least two ways to lose the plot.

**False equivalence** inflates a resemblance until relevant differences disappear. It may misstate responsibility, obscure legal distinctions or appropriate another community's experience.

**False exceptionalism** treats a case as so singular that no external question, concept or comparison can ever help us understand it. It can shield familiar institutions from scrutiny by declaring their history beyond comparison.

These errors are not automatically equally prevalent or consequential in every discussion. We have to examine what is actually being claimed and what evidence exists.

The alternative is not a mushy compromise in which everything is a little bit the same. It is **specificity with permission to learn**.

## 23. 🧰 The Five-Second Comparison Check

Before carrying an analogy into another history, ask:

1. **What exactly am I comparing?**
2. **What evidence establishes the resemblance?**
3. **Which differences could change the explanation?**
4. **Am I transferring a question, mechanism or conclusion?**
5. **What evidence would make me revise or abandon it?**

And then the human question:

**Have I allowed the people in the second history to remain themselves?**

If not, the model needs work. If yes, proceed—and keep checking.

Apparently the options are not limited to *everything is identical* and *nobody is ever allowed to learn anything from anybody else*. Historians may yet recover from this development.

## 24. 🗺️ The Map Is Useful Because It Can Be Corrected

The point of translation is not to make every place resemble somewhere we already know. It is to use what we have learned to approach an unfamiliar place with better questions.

We may find a shared mechanism. We may find a relevant difference. We may discover that the model needs another variable, that the analogy was too broad, or that the people whose history we are examining have been explaining the problem in terms we should have listened to in the first place.

We can carry a question without carrying away somebody else's sovereignty over their story. We can recognise suffering without assigning it a classification we have not established. We can compare political arrangements without compelling anyone into a constitutional allegiance. We can learn from an act of solidarity without pretending that generosity erased the differences between the giver and recipient.

> You can learn something over here that helps you recognise a question over there.
>
> You can carry the question with you.
>
> You can test it against another history.
>
> You can discover similarities, differences, and the limits of your own model.
>
> None of that requires the people in the second history to become the people in the first.
>
> **Translation is not equivalence.**
>
> **And when the translation fails, that is information too.**

## 🧪 Evidence And Method Ledger

This node sets out a method and uses other nodes as worked examples. It does **not** independently verify every underlying historical or contemporary claim. Where a comparison is promoted into a substantive conclusion, return to the underlying evidence and record the scope of the claim.

| Domain | What needs establishing | Principal error to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Cognition and models | What a particular theory actually supports | Treating a metaphor as an empirical law |
| Irish famine | Crops, access, tenure, relief, mortality, chronology | Blight as complete explanation |
| Food access | Production, ownership, prices, wages, distribution | Availability as entitlement |
| British response | Dated knowledge, options, decisions, feedback | Hindsight as proof of contemporaneous knowledge |
| Choctaw–Irish giving | Participants, records, transmission, later memory | Invented motives or interchangeable Indigenous nations |
| Land Back | Specific Indigenous organisations and demands | Political appropriation or a single universal programme |
| Genocide | Relevant definition, intent, acts, historical/legal context | Analogy as classification |
| Palestine | Contemporary, independently sourced facts and law | Importing nineteenth-century conclusions |
| Cable Street | Participation, organisation, historical context | Coalition as identity |
| Unionism | Constitutional history, consent, present legal arrangements | Historical understanding as mandatory allegiance |
| Cybernetics | Model, information route, observation and revision | Information collected as proof of learning |

### Research direction

For a fully sourced expansion, use historical scholarship and primary documents on An Gorta Mór; Indigenous-authored accounts of Choctaw history and Land Back; the relevant legal texts for any genocide or constitutional claim; and case-specific contemporary reporting and institutional records. A claim sourced in one historical case is **not** automatically a source for another. The linked cluster nodes provide starting points, not evidentiary shortcuts.
