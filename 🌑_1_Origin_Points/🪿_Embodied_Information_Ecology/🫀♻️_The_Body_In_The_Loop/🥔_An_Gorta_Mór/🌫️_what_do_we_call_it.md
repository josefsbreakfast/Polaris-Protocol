# 🌫️ What Do We Call It?

**First created:** 2026-10-06 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-07  
*Potatoes died. People died. Governments governed. Markets operated. Land changed hands. People left. What we call the catastrophe determines which of those facts enters the sentence.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

There is no perfectly neutral name for a catastrophe.

There are, however, names that are useful for different questions, names that carry different histories, and names that make stronger or weaker claims about causation, responsibility and intent.

This cluster has already encountered several:

- **the Irish Potato Famine**;
- **the Great Famine**;
- **the Great Irish Famine**;
- **the Great Hunger**;
- **An Gorta Mór**;
- **An Drochshaol**;
- **the Famine**;
- **Black ’47**;
- **a colonial famine**;
- **a colonial catastrophe**;
- **genocide**.

They do not all mean the same thing.

They do not all originate at the same time.

They do not all identify the same causal mechanism.

And disagreement over terminology does not necessarily mean disagreement over the underlying historical facts.

The governing question is therefore:

> **What does each name tell the reader happened; what does it leave outside the frame; when and where did that terminology develop; and what evidentiary claim are we making when we use it?**

This is not merely semantics.

```text
EVENT
  ↓
DESCRIPTION
  ↓
CAUSAL MODEL
  ↓
MORAL / LEGAL INTERPRETATION
  ↓
MEMORY
```

But the arrows also run backwards.

```text
MEMORY
  ↓
TERMINOLOGY
  ↓
WHAT THE READER EXPECTS
  ↓
WHICH EVIDENCE APPEARS RELEVANT
```

Names are information architecture.

A name can help us find the mechanism.

A name can also become a substitute for finding it.

This node therefore refuses two temptations at once:

```text
ONE PERFECT NAME
        ✗
```

and:

```text
ALL NAMES ARE EQUALLY PRECISE
        ✗
```

Better resolution does not necessarily produce one bigger, stronger word.

Sometimes it produces several more precise ones.

## 🥔 1. The Potato Has Some Explaining To Do

**Irish Potato Famine** is probably the most immediately intelligible name for many readers outside Ireland.

There is a reason.

The potato was not incidental.

A large section of the poorest population depended extraordinarily heavily on it for subsistence. *Phytophthora infestans* devastated the crop. The first great disturbance in this particular catastrophe was biological.

Without the potato failure, this catastrophe does not happen in this form.

That makes the potato causally important.

It does not make the potato causally sufficient.

The phrase can quietly teach this model:

```text
POTATO FAILED
      ↓
FOOD DISAPPEARED
      ↓
PEOPLE STARVED
```

But the rest of this cluster exists because the middle of that diagram contains rather more machinery.

```text
POTATO FAILURE
      ↓
HOUSEHOLD LOSES SUBSISTENCE
      ↓
LAND + RENT + WAGES + PRICES
+ MARKETS + CREDIT + RELIEF
+ PROPERTY + PUBLIC WORKS
+ POOR LAW + STATE POLICY
      ↓
WHO CAN STILL OBTAIN FOOD?
      ↓
BODY
```

The potato explains why a crucial crop died.

It does not by itself explain why a human being died.

> **Putting the potato in the title can accidentally promote the disturbance into the explanation.**

That is a problem with the phrase when it is used as a complete causal model.

It is not a reason to pretend the potato did not matter.

Cross-reference:

- [`🥔_phytophthora_infestans.md`](./🥔_phytophthora_infestans.md) — the pathogen, crop chronology and biological disturbance.
- [`🌾_there_was_always_food_in_ireland.md`](./🌾_there_was_always_food_in_ireland.md) — the difference between calories existing in a territory and people possessing an entitlement to eat them.

## 🦠 2. A Pathogen Is Not A Political Economy

*Phytophthora infestans* did not understand:

- rent;
- religion;
- nationality;
- property law;
- wages;
- Poor Law districts;
- Treasury doctrine;
- landlord debt;
- public works;
- imperial history.

It infected plants.

The blight affected potato crops beyond Ireland.

Human outcomes were not identical everywhere the organism appeared.

That gives us a useful causal distinction:

```text
BIOLOGICAL DISTURBANCE
        ≠
COMPLETE HUMAN CATASTROPHE
```

Or, in the line that has become the spine of this cluster:

> **The potato blight hits the potato. The social system determines what happens to the human being whose potato has died.**

Nature enters a human system.

The resulting disaster is produced by the interaction.

## 🌾 3. “Famine” Does Not Mean There Were Literally No Calories In Ireland

This semantic mistake causes an astonishing amount of historical confusion.

One side says:

> There was famine.

Someone replies:

> But there was other food in Ireland.

As though those propositions cannot coexist.

They can.

Food security is not simply an inventory of edible matter inside a geographic boundary.

The relevant chain is:

```text
FOOD EXISTS
     ↓
WHO OWNS IT?
     ↓
WHO CAN BUY IT?
     ↓
WHO HAS WAGES?
     ↓
WHO HAS CREDIT?
     ↓
WHO HAS AN ENTITLEMENT TO RELIEF?
     ↓
WHO CAN PHYSICALLY REACH IT?
     ↓
WHO EATS?
```

A field of grain is not automatically dinner for the person standing beside it.

A cow is not automatically milk or meat for the labourer who does not own it.

Food moving through a commercial system is not automatically available to a household whose subsistence crop has failed and whose income cannot replace it.

Therefore:

```text
FAMINE
≠
TOTAL ABSENCE OF EDIBLE MATTER
FROM A TERRITORY
```

The existence of food does not disprove famine.

It changes the question from:

> **Was there food?**

to:

> **Who possessed an effective entitlement to it?**

That is a much more interesting question.

## 🍽️ 4. The Great Famine

**The Great Famine** and **the Great Irish Famine** are conventional historical descriptions.

They foreground a population-level catastrophe:

- food insecurity;
- hunger;
- disease;
- mortality;
- migration;
- social disruption.

The term is broad enough not to identify the potato as the whole explanation.

But broad categories also have costs.

“Famine” can sound like a natural event that simply arrived.

It can make the governing machinery disappear unless the historian puts it back.

It can also make the event sound as though its defining fact was a shortage of food in aggregate rather than a catastrophic failure of access, entitlement, health and social protection.

So the term is useful.

It is not self-explanatory.

## 🫀 5. The Great Hunger

**The Great Hunger** changes the camera angle.

Compare:

```text
FAMINE
→ population / historical event
```

with:

```text
HUNGER
→ embodied condition
```

A famine is something historians can map.

Hunger is something a nervous system, stomach, immune system and exhausted body experience.

That distinction matters inside Embodied Information Ecology.

Hunger reaches:

- strength;
- cognition;
- temperature regulation;
- immune defence;
- pregnancy;
- childhood development;
- ability to work;
- ability to walk;
- ability to obtain the next meal.

The phrase therefore restores something that administrative language can lose.

But it can obscure things too.

The catastrophe was not only hunger.

People died through interacting pathways of malnutrition, fever, dysentery, diarrhoeal disease, exposure, crowding and other infections.

People were evicted.

People migrated.

People entered workhouses.

People crossed oceans.

People lost households and communities.

The body was hungry.

The system was larger than hunger.

## ☘️ 6. An Gorta Mór

The phrase **An Gorta Mór** is now widely used for the catastrophe, including by Ireland’s National Famine Commemoration.

It is tempting to tell a very neat story:

> The English called it the Famine.  
> The Irish called it An Gorta Mór.  
> Therefore An Gorta Mór is the original authentic name.

The archive is more interesting than that.

The National Famine Commemoration itself lists multiple terms: the great Irish famine, the great hunger, the Irish potato famine, *An Gorta Mór*, and, in some Irish-language communities, **blianta an droch-shaoil** — the years of the bad or hard life.

The Schools’ Collection of the 1930s preserves material headed **An Gorta Mór**.

It also preserves substantial material headed **An Drochshaol**.

So:

```text
GAEILGE
   ≠
ONE TIMELESS LABEL
```

And:

```text
AN GORTA MÓR USED NOW
        ≠
PROOF THAT EVERY IRISH SPEAKER
IN 1847 CALLED IT THAT
```

That does not make *An Gorta Mór* false.

It makes its history worth knowing.

A retrospective name can be culturally meaningful, analytically useful and historically real without having been the universal vocabulary of the people living through the event.

---

## 🌫️ 7. An Drochshaol

**An Drochshaol** deserves to stand beside *An Gorta Mór*, not underneath it as an embarrassing footnote.

Ó Dónaill’s *Foclóir Gaeilge–Béarla* gives *drochshaol* as bad or hard life, hard times, and specifically gives **An Drochshaol** historically for the Famine.

The Schools’ Collection preserves the term repeatedly.

One Irish-language entry begins:

> **Bhí an Droch Saoghal in gach conndae in Éirinn beagnach i 1845.**

The historical spelling is retained in the archive.

The term is revealing because it does something different from a neat event label.

It can hold:

```text
BAD LIFE
HARD TIMES
HUNGER
DISEASE
SOCIAL BREAKDOWN
A PERIOD PEOPLE LIVED THROUGH
```

That is conceptually fascinating.

A later historian wants an event with boundaries.

A person remembering catastrophe may remember a **condition of life**.

The Irish state’s commemoration material also records **blianta an droch-shaoil**:

> the years of the bad life.

Not merely the failed crop.

Not merely the year of hunger.

Years in which life itself had become bad.

That is information.

## 🗣️ 8. What Did People Actually Call It?

This question cannot be solved by finding the prettiest Gaeilge phrase and putting a shamrock beside it.

The surviving evidence is multilingual, regional and temporally layered.

We need to distinguish:

```text
WHAT PEOPLE SAID DURING THE EVENT
                ↓
WHAT SURVIVORS CALLED IT LATER
                ↓
WHAT CHILDREN / GRANDCHILDREN HEARD
                ↓
WHAT FOLKLORE COLLECTORS RECORDED
                ↓
WHAT HISTORIANS CALL IT
                ↓
WHAT STATES / MUSEUMS / DIASPORA
CALL IT NOW
```

Those are related information systems.

They are not identical.

The Schools’ Collection is particularly valuable because it preserves later community memory, including Gaeilge material.

But material collected in the 1930s is evidence of what was being remembered and transmitted in the 1930s.

It is not automatically a stenographic transcript of 1847.

That distinction does not reduce its value.

It tells us what kind of evidence it is.

## 🗃️ 9. The Archive Has A Language

Much of the administrative archive comes to us through English.

Its categories include:

- pauper;
- occupier;
- tenant;
- ratepayer;
- inmate;
- emigrant;
- relief recipient;
- able-bodied;
- destitute.

Those categories are useful.

They are also products of institutions.

A human being enters an administrative system and becomes legible to it as a category.

```text
PERSON
  ↓
INSTITUTIONAL OBSERVATION
  ↓
CATEGORY
  ↓
RECORD
  ↓
ARCHIVE
  ↓
HISTORIAN
```

The category is evidence.

It is not the whole person.

And the language in which a catastrophe is archived is not necessarily the language in which it was experienced.

## ☘️ 10. Gaeilge Is Not Decorative Evidence

There is a bad way to respond to the dominance of English-language archives:

write the same English causal model, add a few Irish words, and announce that history has been decolonised.

No.

Gaeilge matters when it changes the information available to us.

It can preserve:

- different names;
- different semantic boundaries;
- local memory;
- kinship;
- prayer;
- blessing;
- humour;
- grief;
- hunger;
- migration;
- place;
- ways of organising experience.

The point is not authenticity theatre.

The point is resolution.

## 🕰️ 11. When Does The Event Begin And End?

Even the dates are a naming problem.

Are we naming:

- the first appearance of blight in 1845?
- the catastrophic crop loss of 1846?
- Black ’47?
- repeated crop failures into the later 1840s?
- the mortality crisis?
- the relief regime?
- the migration crisis?
- the demographic transformation continuing long afterwards?

Different institutions and historians use different periodisations.

That is not necessarily sloppiness.

The system contains different clocks.

```text
BLIGHT CLOCK
HARVEST CLOCK
HUNGER CLOCK
DISEASE CLOCK
RELIEF CLOCK
EVICTION CLOCK
MIGRATION CLOCK
DEMOGRAPHIC CLOCK
POLITICAL-MEMORY CLOCK
```

The pathogen does not end when the workhouse crisis ends.

The mortality crisis does not end when a particular harvest improves.

The demographic scar does not end when the pathogen retreats.

There may be no single ending that answers every question.

## 🖤 12. Black ’47

1847 became **Black ’47**.

That name is itself a warning against treating the plant chronology as the human chronology.

Blight had been catastrophic in 1846.

In 1847 there was less blight, but the human catastrophe intensified. Seed had been consumed or lost; little acreage had been planted; reserves were exhausted; disease and displacement were spreading.

The state, household and body were carrying accumulated damage.

```text
SHOCK 1
  ↓
RESERVES CONSUMED
  ↓
SYSTEM ENTERS NEXT PERIOD
ALREADY DAMAGED
  ↓
SHOCK / DISRUPTION CONTINUES
  ↓
HUMAN OUTCOME WORSENS
```

A body does not reset on New Year’s Day.

Neither does a household.

Neither does a population.

The Schools’ Collection later preserved “Black 47” as a famine-year name in community memory.

So the name foregrounds consequence.

The worst year for the human system does not have to be the worst year for the pathogen.

## 🧠 13. Retrospective Does Not Mean False

Historians use retrospective categories constantly.

People living inside an event do not necessarily know:

- when it began;
- when it will end;
- which developments will prove decisive;
- which events will later be grouped together;
- what future institutions will call it.

Therefore:

```text
RETROSPECTIVE
≠
INVALID
```

But also:

```text
RETROSPECTIVE
≠
CONTEMPORARY
```

The solution is not to ban retrospective terminology.

It is to label it.

## 🌧️ 14. Was It A Natural Disaster?

At one causal layer, a naturally occurring plant pathogen is indispensable to the story.

But this inference fails:

```text
PATHOGEN IS NATURAL
        ↓
HUMAN OUTCOME IS NATURAL
```

The relevant structure is closer to:

```text
HAZARD
  ↓
EXPOSURE
  ↓
PRE-EXISTING VULNERABILITY
  ↓
INSTITUTIONAL RESPONSE
  ↓
DISTRIBUTED HUMAN OUTCOME
```

Disaster studies has long examined the interaction between hazards and socially produced vulnerability.

The important point here is simpler:

**nature does not allocate rent, relief, wages or property rights.**

Calling the blight natural tells us something about the organism.

It does not exonerate the human system through which the organism’s effects travelled.

## 🏴 15. What Does “Colonial” Claim?

“Colonial” should not be used as a volume knob meaning:

> Britain, but more evil.

It is an analytical claim.

Relevant historical features may include:

- conquest;
- plantation;
- confiscation and land redistribution;
- political domination;
- restructuring of enforceable property;
- religious hierarchy;
- cultural and linguistic domination;
- metropolitan decision-making;
- racialised or ethnic stereotypes;
- unequal political power;
- extraction;
- integration into an imperial political economy.

The work is to identify which mechanisms operated and how.

```text
COLONIAL
        ↓
WHICH RELATIONSHIP?
WHICH INSTITUTION?
WHICH POWER?
WHICH MATERIAL CONSEQUENCE?
```

If we cannot answer those questions, the word is doing too much work.

## 🇬🇧 16. Ireland Was Formally Part Of The United Kingdom

By the Famine, Ireland was formally part of the United Kingdom under the Acts of Union.

That matters.

Ireland was not being administered in precisely the same constitutional form as every overseas colony.

That also does not erase the much longer history through which the relationship had been made:

- conquest;
- plantations;
- confiscations;
- Protestant Ascendancy;
- Penal-era structures;
- land reorganisation;
- political subordination;
- linguistic and cultural pressure.

So both of these models are too simple:

```text
IRELAND WAS IN THE UK
        ↓
COLONIAL ANALYSIS IMPOSSIBLE
```

and:

```text
IRELAND HAD A COLONIAL HISTORY
        ↓
EVERY INSTITUTIONAL DISTINCTION
IS IRRELEVANT
```

History is allowed to be inconvenient.

## 🏛️ 17. “Colonial Famine” Is A Question Historians Actually Ask

This is not terminology Polaris has invented because it feels rhetorically satisfying.

Historian Peter Gray explicitly asks:

> **Was the Great Irish Famine a Colonial Famine?**

His 2021 review traces debates over the colonial relationship, pre-Famine economic structures, blight and food availability, and especially the British state response.

His conclusion is useful precisely because it refuses to collapse categories.

Gray identifies colonial stereotypes and social-engineering assumptions in the response and argues that humanitarianism was ultimately subordinated to perceived British national interest, while rejecting an intentionally genocidal interpretation of the British state response.

That gives us an important methodological result:

```text
COLONIAL DIMENSIONS
        ≠
AUTOMATIC PROOF OF GENOCIDE
```

One classification does not mechanically produce another.

The questions overlap.

They are not the same question.

## 🌍 18. Colonial Comparison Needs Mechanisms

Calling this a colonial famine opens comparison with other famines under imperial rule.

That can be useful.

It can also become mush very quickly.

The comparison should ask:

- who controlled land?
- who controlled taxation?
- what food existed?
- who possessed entitlement to it?
- what transport systems existed?
- what information reached government?
- what relief doctrine operated?
- what role did markets play?
- what racial or civilisational assumptions shaped interpretation?
- what political representation did the affected population possess?
- what did the governing state consider its responsibility?

Then:

```text
CASE A
↕
SPECIFIED MECHANISM
↕
CASE B
```

Not:

```text
BOTH COLONIAL
   ↓
SAME EVENT
```

Cross-reference:

- [`🗺️_translation_is_not_equivalence.md`](./🗺️_translation_is_not_equivalence.md)

Comparison should increase the resolution of both histories.

## 🩸 19. And Then There Is “Genocide”

This node is not where the full genocide argument belongs.

That is the job of:

- [`🩸_why_call_it_genocide.md`](./🩸_why_call_it_genocide.md)

But terminology cannot avoid the word entirely.

The first discipline is simple:

> **Genocide is not a synonym for an event in which an appalling number of people died.**

Under the 1948 Genocide Convention, the legal category requires specified acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such.

That means the evidentiary problem is not:

> Was the suffering sufficiently horrific?

The suffering can be horrific regardless.

The legal question includes a particular mental element.

## ⚖️ 20. Keep The Variables Separate

For this cluster, the following distinctions are non-negotiable:

```text
KNOWLEDGE
≠
FORESEEABILITY
≠
INDIFFERENCE
≠
RACISM
≠
RECKLESSNESS
≠
INTENT TO DESTROY
```

Those variables can interact.

Evidence of one may contribute to an argument about another.

They are not synonyms.

Similarly:

```text
PREVENTABLE MASS DEATH
≠
AUTOMATICALLY GENOCIDE
```

and:

```text
NOT PROVEN GENOCIDE
≠
NATURAL / HARMLESS / BLAMELESS
```

That second distinction matters enormously.

Otherwise a debate over one legal category becomes a bizarre switch:

```text
GENOCIDE
or
NOTHING TO SEE HERE
```

Those are not the available historical choices.

## 🧠 21. Why Want The Word?

People who argue for genocide terminology are not necessarily confused about dictionaries.

The word can be asked to perform several functions:

- recognition of enormous harm;
- resistance to natural-disaster framing;
- recognition of agency;
- recognition of colonial power;
- recognition of racialised contempt;
- acknowledgement by institutions;
- connection to a wider history of mass atrocity;
- a refusal to let administrative language make the dead disappear.

Those needs are intelligible.

But an evidentiary question remains:

> **Which of those truths require the legal or historiographical classification genocide, and which can be demonstrated directly without making that additional claim?**

Sometimes a more precise description is stronger because it tells us exactly what happened.

## 🧱 22. Precision Is Not Minimisation

We can investigate and, where evidence supports it, state:

- mass preventable mortality;
- hunger and disease;
- extensive governmental knowledge;
- policies continued after harmful consequences became visible;
- ideological commitments shaping relief;
- racialised or ethnic contempt;
- coercive property relations;
- eviction;
- colonial history;
- unequal political power;
- institutional decisions that worsened vulnerability.

None of those facts becomes less serious because we refuse to treat them as synonyms.

Indeed, separating them lets us ask who did what.

```text
ACTOR
  ↓
POWER
  ↓
INFORMATION
  ↓
DECISION
  ↓
CONSEQUENCE
```

That is accountability with handles.

## 🧠 23. Names Can Smuggle In A Theory Of Mind

This is one of the most dangerous functions of terminology.

A word can quietly move us from:

```text
THE POLICY PRODUCED THIS
```

to:

```text
THEY KNEW THIS WAS HAPPENING
```

to:

```text
THEY WANTED THIS TO HAPPEN
```

Those are different propositions.

Cross-reference:

- [`🇬🇧_what_britain_knew.md`](./🇬🇧_what_britain_knew.md) — information, interpretation, power and chronology.
- [`🏛️_what_britain_did.md`](./🏛️_what_britain_did.md) — policy and its material requirements.

The evidence should determine which proposition we can make.

The desired classification should not determine what evidence we pretend exists.

## 🔁 24. Feedback Changes Responsibility

One of the strongest principles from the first causal unit remains:

> **The first decision is evaluated against the information available before it. Continued policy is evaluated against the information generated by its own consequences.**

This matters whether or not we ever settle on a maximal label.

A government can make an initial mistake.

Then people suffer.

Reports arrive.

Mortality becomes visible.

Workhouses fill.

Public works fail.

Evictions occur.

Disease spreads.

The system now contains new information.

```text
POLICY
  ↓
CONSEQUENCE
  ↓
REPORT
  ↓
GOVERNMENT
  ↓
NEXT POLICY
```

The responsibility question changes with every loop.

## 👤 25. Trevelyan Cannot Carry The Whole Catastrophe On His Back

Charles Trevelyan matters.

His ideas matter.

His administrative role matters.

His writing matters.

But this is not an adequate causal diagram:

```text
BAD MAN
  ↓
FAMINE
```

The machine contains:

- Cabinet;
- Treasury;
- Parliament;
- Poor Law institutions;
- landlords;
- estate finance;
- local guardians;
- relief committees;
- markets;
- property law;
- administrative doctrine;
- religious and moral assumptions;
- local implementation;
- institutional incentives.

A system can produce catastrophic outcomes without every participant possessing the same intention.

That is not a defence of the system.

It is an explanation of why replacing institutional analysis with a villain can actually make accountability worse.

## 🧮 26. Dehumanisation Can Be Administrative

A person does not need to be explicitly described as subhuman for an institution to lose information about their humanity.

Watch the abstraction:

```text
PERSON
  ↓
OCCUPIER
  ↓
TENANT
  ↓
PAUPER
  ↓
RATE BURDEN
  ↓
ADMINISTRATIVE PROBLEM
```

Likewise:

```text
LAND
  ↓
HOLDING
  ↓
RENT
  ↓
INCOME STREAM
  ↓
ASSET
```

Each category can be useful.

Each can also hide information.

The question becomes:

> **Which variables remained visible to the institution when it made the decision?**

If “does this person survive?” is not attached to anybody’s operational responsibility, catastrophe can be produced lawfully and administratively.

## 📰 27. Who Gets To Name It?

Naming is power, but not in the simplistic sense that one authority invents the official word and everyone else obeys.

Names emerge through:

- administration;
- journalism;
- scholarship;
- family memory;
- folklore;
- political movements;
- diaspora;
- museums;
- schools;
- states;
- commemorations;
- literature.

Different information environments can stabilise different names.

A term can become ordinary in one population while sounding ideologically marked in another.

That is itself historical evidence.

## ☘️ 28. Irish Public Memory Does Not Use One Name

Ireland’s National Famine Commemoration currently uses **An Gorta Mór** prominently.

Its own explanatory material simultaneously acknowledges:

- the great Irish famine;
- the great hunger;
- the Irish potato famine;
- An Gorta Mór;
- *blianta an droch-shaoil*.

That plurality is useful.

The state does not need to pretend one phrase contains the whole event.

Neither do we.

## 🎓 29. Historiography Has Its Own Naming History

The terminology of Famine scholarship has changed alongside the scholarship itself.

Modern research includes works using:

- Great Famine;
- Great Irish Famine;
- Great Hunger.

Christine Kinealy has explicitly discussed the variety of designations and the interpretive emphasis they carry.

Peter Gray’s work makes **colonial famine** itself an object of historiographical analysis rather than simply assuming the category.

This means a future source corpus could map:

```text
AUTHOR
DATE
DISCIPLINE
TERM USED
DEFINITION
CAUSAL EMPHASIS
```

Not to hold an election for Best Famine Name.

To observe how historical models change.

## 🌍 30. Diaspora Has A Naming History Too

Migration did not merely move people.

It moved memory.

```text
IRELAND
  ↓
MIGRATION
  ↓
FAMILY / COMMUNITY ABROAD
  ↓
STORY

  ↓

```

Terminology can therefore develop differently across Ireland, Britain, the United States, Canada, Australia and elsewhere.

That is not necessarily corruption of an original memory.

It is what happens when memory enters new institutions and generations.

## 🧠 31. Memory Is Not A Recording

Memory can preserve extraordinary information.

Memory can also:

- compress;
- select;
- combine;
- simplify;
- mythologise;
- forget;
- reinterpret.

So:

```text
EVENT
  ↓
EXPERIENCE
  ↓
MEMORY
  ↓
TRANSMISSION
  ↓
SELECTION
  ↓
INTERPRETATION
  ↓
NAME
```

That does not make memory useless.

It makes memory a dynamic information system.

---

## 🔦 32. Every Name Is A Flashlight

This is the centre of the node.

A name points attention.

Point the flashlight at the potato and we see plant disease.

Point it at famine and we see a population-level food crisis.

Point it at hunger and we see bodies.

Point it at *An Drochshaol* and we may see a remembered period in which life itself became hard or bad.

Point it at colonialism and we see the historical architecture of power.

Point it at genocide and we ask a narrower question about group destruction and intent.

```text
PLANT PATHOLOGY
→ POTATO BLIGHT

FOOD SYSTEM
→ FAMINE

BODY
→ HUNGER / DISEASE

REMEMBERED LIFE
→ AN DROCHSHAOL

IRISH-LANGUAGE PUBLIC MEMORY
→ AN GORTA MÓR

POLITICAL ECONOMY
→ DISTRIBUTION OF VULNERABILITY

IMPERIAL HISTORY
→ COLONIAL ANALYSIS

GENOCIDE LAW / STUDIES
→ GROUP DESTRUCTION + INTENT QUESTION
```

> **A useful name is a flashlight, not a replacement for the room.**

No flashlight illuminates every wall at once.

---

## 🧩 33. Multiple Names Can Be True At Different Analytical Levels

This is not indecision.

It is variable discipline.

The same event can simultaneously involve:

- potato blight;
- famine;
- hunger;
- epidemic disease;
- eviction;
- migration;
- demographic collapse;
- colonial relations;
- political responsibility.

The fact that one word cannot encode every mechanism is not a defect in language.

The defect appears when we demand that it do so.

## 🏛️ 38. Naming Failure: Relief Existed, Therefore Relief Was Adequate

Bad model:

```text
STATE ACTED
  ↓
STATE SOLVED PROBLEM
```

A policy can exist and fail.

A relief programme can save lives and remain inadequate.

Different interventions can work differently at different times.

The correct question is functional:

> **What did the intervention require, who could access it, what did it provide, and what happened next?**

## 😈 39. Naming Failure: Villain Theory

Bad model:

```text

  ↓
CATASTROPHE
```

It feels satisfying because it gives the event a face.

It can also let the machinery escape.

## 😇 40. Naming Failure: No Proven Extermination Plan, Therefore No Serious Responsibility

This is the reverse mistake.

```text
NO PROVEN INTENT TO DESTROY
        ↓
NO RESPONSIBILITY
```

does not follow.

A catastrophe can involve grave:

- negligence;
- ideological rigidity;
- discriminatory assumptions;
- foreseeable harm;
- inadequate relief;
- harmful policy;
- property enforcement;
- indifference;
- institutional failure;

without every one of those variables becoming genocidal intent.

Precision cuts both ways.

## 🪦 42. Classification Comes After The Body

Our evidentiary order should be:

```text
WHAT HAPPENED TO THE BODY?
        ↓
WHAT MECHANISM PRODUCED IT?
        ↓
WHO POSSESSED WHAT POWER?
        ↓
WHO KNEW WHAT, WHEN?
        ↓
WHAT DID THEY DO?
        ↓
WHAT RELATIONSHIP STRUCTURED IT?
        ↓
WHICH HISTORICAL / LEGAL
CLASSIFICATIONS FIT?
```

Not:

```text
CHOOSE CLASSIFICATION
        ↓
RECRUIT EVIDENCE
```

> **Do not choose the word first and recruit the dead as evidence afterwards.**

The dead have already done enough work.

## 🫂 43. Descendants Hear The Name Too

Terminology is not emotionally inert.

For descendants, names can concern:

- recognition;
- family memory;
- cultural legitimacy;
- historical responsibility;
- grief;
- belonging;
- anger;
- acknowledgement.

Those responses are real information.

They do not automatically determine empirical classification.

Both statements can coexist:

```text
THIS NAME MATTERS DEEPLY TO ME
```

and:

```text
THIS HISTORICAL CLAIM STILL
REQUIRES EVIDENCE
```

That is not disrespect.

It is how we stop memory and evidence being forced to destroy one another.

---

## 🌫️ 44. Fog Is Not Ignorance

Terminological disagreement can create a false impression that nothing is known.

But we can know a great deal while still disputing the best category for the whole.

We can establish that:

- potato blight devastated the subsistence crop;
- vulnerability was profoundly unequal;
- enormous numbers suffered hunger and disease;
- mass mortality occurred;
- mass migration occurred;
- food other than potatoes continued to exist;
- access to that food was unequal;
- property relations shaped vulnerability;
- relief policy shaped survival;
- British governing institutions received extensive information;
- the catastrophe occurred inside a much longer history of conquest and unequal rule;
- the population and land system were permanently transformed;
- later generations remembered and interpreted the catastrophe politically.

And still ask whether a particular larger classification is the most accurate one.

```text
UNCERTAINTY ABOUT CLASSIFICATION
              ≠
UNCERTAINTY THAT PEOPLE SUFFERED
```

Nor does disagreement over one word require vagueness about everything else.

## ☘️ 45. What Should Polaris Call It?

We do not need one word for every job.

### **An Gorta Mór**

Useful when discussing the catastrophe as a whole within modern Irish-language and commemorative framing.

Do not falsely claim it was the one universal contemporary Irish-language name.

### **An Drochshaol**

Use where the Irish-language historical/folkloric tradition itself is under discussion, especially remembered “hard times” or “bad life.”

Preserve historical spellings when quoting archival material.

### **Great Famine / Great Irish Famine**

Useful conventional historiographical terminology.

### **Irish Potato Famine**

Useful for recognisability and when discussing the crop disturbance, but do not let “potato” become the whole causal explanation.

### **Great Hunger**

Useful for embodied and memorial framing.

Do not pretend hunger is the only mortality mechanism.

### **potato blight**

Use for the plant disease.

### **colonial famine / colonial catastrophe**

Use when explicitly analysing demonstrated colonial mechanisms rather than as a rhetorical intensifier.

### **genocide**

Use when explicitly entering the legal, historical or genocide-studies argument and stating which definition is being tested.

Route the full argument to:

- [`🩸_why_call_it_genocide.md`](./🩸_why_call_it_genocide.md)

This is not terminological cowardice.

It is refusing to make one variable impersonate the entire model.

The desire for a single correct name is understandable.

One word feels like closure.

But this project is not trying to close the box.

It is trying to see what is inside it.


## 🚧 48. Evidentiary Guardrails

- `potato blight ≠ complete explanation of famine`
- `food existed ≠ starving people could access it`
- `famine ≠ territory contains zero food`
- `natural pathogen ≠ natural human outcome`
- `An Gorta Mór ≠ proven universal contemporary Gaeilge terminology`
- `An Drochshaol ≠ the only historical Gaeilge terminology`
- `Irish-language terminology ≠ decorative authenticity`
- `folklore collected in the 1930s ≠ verbatim record of 1847`
- `retrospective term ≠ false term`
- `retrospective term ≠ contemporary term`
- `event periodisation ≠ one self-evident date range`
- `Black ’47 ≠ year of greatest blight`
- `colonial analysis ≠ identical administration to every colony`
- `colonialism ≠ complete causal explanation`
- `colonial famine ≠ automatically genocide`
- `mass death ≠ automatically genocide`
- `racism ≠ automatically genocidal intent`
- `knowledge ≠ intent`
- `foreseeability ≠ intent`
- `indifference ≠ intent`
- `failure to prove genocide ≠ failure to prove responsibility`
- `state intervention ≠ adequate intervention`
- `political memory ≠ literal recording`
- `administrative category ≠ whole human being`
- `emotional significance ≠ empirical classification`
- `terminological disagreement ≠ factual equivalence`
- `comparison ≠ equivalence`

And:

> **Do not choose the word first and recruit the dead as evidence afterwards.**

Nor should we demand that descendants surrender the words through which their families learned to remember.

The work is to know what each word is doing.

## 📚 49. Working Sources

These are working research anchors rather than a claim that the terminology history is finished.

- [National Famine Commemoration — “An Gorta Mór”](https://www.irishfamine.ie/an-gorta-mor/) — current Irish state commemoration material explicitly recognises multiple names, including *An Gorta Mór* and *blianta an droch-shaoil*.
- [President of Ireland — “Reflecting on the Gorta Mór: The Great Famine of Ireland”](https://president.ie/en/media-library/speeches/reflecting-on-the-gorta-mor-the-great-famine-of-ireland) — terminology, periodisation and public-memory framing.
- [Dúchas — “An Drochshaol”, Uachtar Árd](https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/4613696/4609642/4626251) — Schools’ Collection material including Irish-language use of *an Droch Saoghal*.
- [Dúchas — Schools’ Collection results containing “An Gorta Mór”](https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/stories?ChapterID=4427981) — evidence that *An Gorta Mór* was also present in collected twentieth-century folklore material.
- [Teanglann / Ó Dónaill — “drochshaol”](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/drochshaol) — lexical evidence for *An Drochshaol* as a historical term for the Famine.
- [Peter Gray — “Was the Great Irish Famine a Colonial Famine?”](https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/was-the-great-irish-famine-a-colonial-famine/) — review of the colonial-famine historiography and the relationship between colonial structures, state response and genocide claims.
- [Christine Kinealy — “A Modest Proposal: New directions in researching and understanding Ireland’s Great Famine”](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/hic3.12726) — modern Famine historiography and renewed research since the 150th anniversary.
- [United Nations — Definitions of Genocide and Related Crimes](https://www.un.org/en/node/218413) — Article II definition and the specific-intent requirement.

### Research still open

The node should not yet claim a securely established first use for:

- **An Gorta Mór**;
- **The Great Hunger**;
- **Black ’47**;
- **Irish Potato Famine**.

The present evidence securely establishes plurality of later usage and strong twentieth-century attestation for both *An Gorta Mór* and *An Drochshaol*.

A proper earliest-attestation search requires a larger nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century corpus.

404 THE OBVIOUS NOT FOUND is an acceptable archival result.

Inventing an etymology is not.
