# 🌱 Before the Blight

**First created:** 2026-10-06 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-12
*Before the potato failed, Ireland already had a history.*

## 🛰️ Orientation

This node establishes the Ireland into which *Phytophthora infestans* arrived in 1845.

It does not begin with a rotten potato.

That is deliberate.

The organism matters. The potato matters. But neither arrived in an empty landscape. By 1845, Ireland's population, landholding, agriculture, poverty, political institutions, religious divisions, languages, landscapes, regional economies and relationship with Britain had histories extending centuries backwards.

The land was not merely an arrangement of fields and rents. It was also woodland, rivers, animals, languages, stories, labouring bodies and the administrative categories through which governments attempted to understand them. Some relationships had already been broken; others were being remade.

The blight entered that system.

It did not create it.

This node therefore stays mostly on the **before** side of the catastrophe. Detailed land mechanics belong in [📐 English Land Controls](./📐_english_land_controls.md). The organism belongs in [🥔 *Phytophthora infestans*](./🥔_phytophthora_infestans.md). Food production, ownership and access belong in [🌾 There Was Always Food In Ireland](./🌾_there_was_always_food_in_ireland.md). British knowledge and policy come later.

The job here is to make one thing impossible to forget:

> **The blight arrived in a system with a history.**

## 1. 🌿 Ireland Did Not Begin With England

Medieval Ireland was not an empty, politically structureless or economically inert place waiting for English institutions to arrive.

Before the Anglo-Norman intervention of the twelfth century, Ireland contained multiple kingdoms and political territories rather than a single centralised state in the modern sense. Gaelic systems of lordship, kinship, law and landholding existed alongside ecclesiastical institutions, agriculture, pastoralism, trade and settlement. Norse settlement had already transformed parts of Ireland and contributed to the development of towns including Dublin, Waterford, Wexford, Cork and Limerick.

None of this requires us to invent a pre-conquest egalitarian paradise.

Gaelic Ireland had hierarchy.

It had violence.

It had inequalities of wealth, status and power.

It had property and exchange.

We do not need the society that existed before conquest to have been perfect in order to ask what conquest changed.

Nor do we need to imagine a pristine island waiting for the first English axe. Ireland's woods, wetlands, pastures and settlements had been shaped by people for thousands of years. Woodland decline did not begin with the Tudors; neither farming nor animal persecution was introduced by colonisation. But later governments and landowners could redirect those older processes through new powers, markets and incentives.

Wolves lived here long before the institutions whose records later describe their destruction. They were predators, sometimes feared, sometimes hunted, and also inhabitants of stories and place names. The Irish *mac tíre* — conventionally rendered *son of the land* or *son of the country* — makes a particularly arresting reminder that a creature can occupy a cultural landscape as well as a physical one. The phrase is not proof that everybody loved wolves. The annals themselves record attacks and scavenging. The point is that the animals belonged to the world people understood, argued about and lived within.

That distinction matters throughout this cluster. The question is not:

> Was everything fine before England arrived?

It plainly was not.

The useful question is:

> **What political, legal and material relationships changed as English
> power expanded, and whose conception of authority could increasingly
> be enforced?**

## 2. 🏰 The Anglo-Normans And The Pale

Anglo-Norman intervention began in 1169, followed by Henry II's arrival in Ireland in 1171 and the development of the English lordship of Ireland.

But this did not produce a neat, permanent conquest of the whole island.

Over subsequent centuries, Anglo-Norman lordships became embedded in Irish political life. Families intermarried. Alliances crossed supposed ethnic boundaries. Some settler families adopted Irish language, customs and political practices. English governmental control expanded and contracted.

By the later medieval period, the area under the most effective direct English governmental control had contracted substantially around Dublin and became associated with **the Pale**.

That is important because the Pale and the later plantation system are not the same thing.

There was already a centuries-old population descended partly from earlier settlers before the more systematic plantation projects of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries began.

Irish history therefore refuses the convenient diagram:

``` text
IRELAND
  ↓
ENGLAND ARRIVES
  ↓
EVERYTHING IS NOW ENGLISH
```

Control was uneven.

Identities changed.

People crossed boundaries.

The state repeatedly discovered that drawing a line on a map was considerably easier than making human beings remain tidily on their assigned side of it.

## 3. 🫀 A Population Is Not A Clean Category

This is worth establishing before we go any further.

Terms such as *Irish*, *English*, *Scottish*, *Gaelic*, *settler*, *Catholic* and *Protestant* are historically useful.

They are not impermeable biological populations.

Nor do they provide complete descriptions of individual allegiance, ancestry, experience or coercion.

Men are disproportionately visible in records of conquest because armies, property systems and administrative institutions recorded them. Even among men recorded as soldiers or settlers, participation alone cannot tell us the precise mixture of choice, recruitment, obligation, economic necessity, patronage or coercion that brought every individual into an invading or expanding force.

We should not invent motives where the evidence cannot recover them.

But focusing exclusively on the documented men produces another, larger distortion.

**Women and children present a different archival problem.**

Conquest and settlement move populations through more than armies and formally recorded migration. They also move them through marriage, relationships across communities, unequal relationships, sexual coercion and sexual violence.

The surviving record for seventeenth-century Ireland contains explicit evidence of sexual and reproductive violence during war, including rape and gang rape. The 1641 Depositions preserve unusually important testimony from women, but the archive itself was created for particular political and legal purposes: principally to record Protestant losses and support later retribution. Historians therefore have to read both what it contains and what its structure makes difficult to see.

The absence of a complete record is not evidence that the absent experience did not occur.

Nor can we calculate an unrecorded pregnancy rate backwards from silence.

What we can say is simpler.

Pregnancy makes these histories demographic.

A child conceived through an ordinary relationship, an unequal relationship, coercion or rape does not necessarily remain identifiable in later records by the circumstances of conception. A child may instead become legible through a surname, household, religion, legal status, property relationship or community affiliation.

Their descendants inherit none of the archival footnotes we might wish existed.

> **The category can survive more cleanly than the bodies that produced
> it.**

So when this cluster later discusses changing proportions of *Irish*, *English*, *Scottish*, *Catholic* or *Protestant* populations, those categories must not be mistaken for a complete reproductive history of Ireland.

Women whose experiences were never recorded, and children whose origins became administratively invisible, are also inside the population.

## 4. 👑 Tudor Conquest And The Attempt To Remake Ireland

The Tudor period brought a more sustained attempt to extend central government across Ireland and to remake political authority on English terms.

Henry VIII's government moved from the older lordship towards the **Kingdom of Ireland** in 1541. Policies including surrender and regrant attempted to incorporate Gaelic and Gaelicised lords into a political and legal order recognised by the English Crown.

But incorporation existed alongside coercion.

English law and administration expanded. The Reformation added an increasingly important religious dimension. Language, clothing and custom also became objects of government attention. The Irish parliament's 1537 legislation concerning English order, habit and language is a particularly useful point of entry: its precise clauses, intended subjects and enforcement need to be read in the statute itself, rather than reduced to the claim that speaking Irish was simply banned everywhere. A government could try to make conduct *legible* as loyalty or disloyalty without succeeding in making the population conform.

Later sixteenth-century schemes for reform and plantation similarly joined questions of education, religion, settlement and cultural practice. Proposed measures were not always implemented measures; a letter advocating a policy is evidence of an intention, not proof of its universal execution. Resistance, rebellion and military campaigns followed. Plantation experiments sought not merely to defeat opponents but to reorganise territory through confiscation and settlement.

This is where political conquest increasingly becomes something larger:

``` text
MILITARY POWER
      ↓
POLITICAL AUTHORITY
      ↓
LAW + ADMINISTRATION
      ↓
LAND
      ↓
SETTLEMENT
      ↓
ECONOMIC RELATIONSHIPS
```

Contemporary English colonial language could describe these transformations through ideas of **civility**, improvement, order and reform.

That matters.

Systems of domination are not always administered by people privately thinking:

> I am doing an evil thing today.

People can understand coercive transformation as improvement.

They can also materially benefit from a system whose moral story tells them that the system is making things better.

We will meet that problem again.

## 5. 🌳 Plantation Was Not One Plantation

There was no single Irish plantation.

There were different projects, in different places, at different times, under different political conditions.

### Munster

Following rebellion in the later sixteenth century, large areas of confiscated land in Munster were allocated for plantation by English settlers.

Implementation was incomplete and contested. War disrupted settlement. Some plantation families remained; others did not.

### Ulster

The Ulster Plantation followed the defeat of the major Gaelic northern lordships and the Flight of the Earls in 1607.

Under James VI and I, confiscated lands were reorganised through a large plantation involving English and Scottish settlers, new towns and institutions, alongside surviving and displaced Irish populations.

Religion, land, political authority and settlement became deeply entangled.

The plantation's published conditions and subsequent administration deserve attention as *design documents*. They distinguished classes of grantees, imposed conditions on occupation and building, and sought to influence which people could hold or occupy particular lands. English and Scottish migration, surviving Irish tenancy, evasion of rules and local accommodation did not always match the scheme drawn up by officials. We should not turn a proposed exclusion into a claim that no Irish tenant remained, or mistake the presence of settlers for an uncomplicated replacement of one population by another.

But we should resist projecting later identities backwards.

A seventeenth-century Scottish Presbyterian settler is not simply a twentieth-century Unionist who has accidentally travelled through time.

Nor is every person subsequently born into a community descended partly from plantation settlers individually responsible for the political project that originally moved some of their ancestors there.

Historical continuity matters.

So does historical change.

### Elsewhere

Plantation and confiscation also occurred elsewhere and through other mechanisms.

The important point is that there was no single afternoon upon which:

> **THE ENGLISH TOOK IRELAND**

Instead there were repeated processes of conquest, rebellion, confiscation, settlement, accommodation, resistance and reconquest.

That messiness does not make the transformations unreal.

It makes them historical.

## 6. 🐺 The Wolves Were Here First

A history of land cannot stop at the humans holding title to it.

Long before the Anglo-Norman intervention, wolves inhabited Ireland. Archaeological remains place them in the island's deep prehistory, although individual dates and interpretations must be checked against the excavation literature. Their presence also survives in place names, stories, annals and accounts of encounters with people. The *mac tíre* was not an ornamental character invented for a modern conservation campaign. It was a living animal with teeth, prey, territory and an inconvenient habit of refusing to recognise property boundaries.

That last inconvenience matters.

Across these islands, wolves were persecuted for centuries. They could threaten livestock and sometimes people; hunters and rural communities had reasons of their own to fear or kill them. Their decline cannot be explained by one conquest, one law or one landlord. Woodland loss likewise had a long history. But Ireland's later wolf records reveal something distinctive about the political and economic administration of the landscape.

### 🌳 A changing habitat

Woodland clearance, agricultural expansion, grazing, settlement and timber extraction altered habitats and the relationships between predators, prey, livestock and people. The timing and extent varied by region. Plantation and commercial land use formed part of that history, not its beginning. A field was not simply a field: it was land made available for particular uses, to particular people, under particular rules, often at the expense of other possible relationships with that place.

### 🪙 Making the killing payable

Accounts of Tudor and seventeenth-century administration describe organised campaigns against wolves. Measures associated with John Perrot in the 1580s precede the larger body of bounty records from the Cromwellian period. A frequently reproduced 1652 schedule gives different rewards for female wolves, males, younger animals and pups. Those figures, and the claimed sums paid, require checking against the original orders and accounts before we treat them as settled archival facts.

Even with that caution, the mechanism is clear enough to investigate: the authorities could attach money to the removal of living animals. They did not need to persuade every hunter to share a philosophy of environmental improvement. A bounty could recruit labour to produce an ecological result.

And there is a further, extraordinarily revealing claim in the secondary literature: Cromwellian authorities reportedly worried about permitting armed Irish people to roam the countryside as wolf hunters, and favoured hunters from England. **Who was allowed to carry weapons and move across the land became part of the question of who could kill its predators.** That political-security rationale needs tracing to its contemporary source; until then it is a reported interpretation, not a verbatim government instruction.

```text
LAND USE + LIVESTOCK INTERESTS
              ↓
      WOLVES AS A PROBLEM
              ↓
    BOUNTIES + HUNTING
              ↕
   ARMS + MOVEMENT + TRUST
              ↓
   CHANGES IN THE LANDSCAPE
```

War could complicate the pattern. Accounts from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries describe wolves among ruined settlements, battlefields and places where the dead went unburied. Those passages are not a census of wolf numbers, nor proof that every reported human death was caused by a wolf. They show animals inhabiting the aftermath of human violence. Disrupted settlement could temporarily create opportunities for wolves even while longer-term persecution and land-use change helped eliminate them.

### 🐺 A disappearance before the blight

The killing of a wolf near Mount Leinster in **1786** is conventionally cited as Ireland's last wild wolf. It is safer to call this a *traditionally reported final record* than a definitively established date on which the species vanished. Extinction is seldom so obliging as to leave a signed departure notice.

By 1845, however, wolves were no longer part of Ireland's wild fauna. Their disappearance did not cause the potato blight, and wolves must not be made into interchangeable stand-ins for dispossessed people. They have a history of their own.

But that history belongs here because it shows that the centuries preceding the Famine transformed **living relationships**, not only legal ownership. An animal that had inhabited Ireland for millennia could be reclassified as a threat to property, assigned a price for its death and ultimately removed from the landscape.

> **The map recorded who could claim the land. The bounty helps us ask what was permitted to live on it.**

## 7. ⚔️ Parliament Was Not the People

The English Civil Wars are often introduced as a contest between King and Parliament. That is a useful beginning, provided we do not mistake it for the whole argument.

Charles I's disputes with Parliament concerned taxation, religion, royal prerogative, military authority and the limits of lawful government. The conflicts became entangled with political and religious struggles in Scotland and Ireland. Historians therefore also speak of the **Wars of the Three Kingdoms**. Even that phrase needs care: three kingdoms shared a monarch, but their peoples did not share one constitutional position or one political interest.

Nor did English people divide into two tidy camps after reading a constitutional pamphlet and deciding whether they preferred a king or a democratic legislature. Local antagonisms mattered. So did kinship, religious commitments, patronage, regional economic interests, the behaviour of nearby garrisons, fear of violence and the obligations of tenants or dependants. Allegiances could change. People might resist one army because of what its soldiers had done locally rather than because they had settled the theory of sovereignty for all future generations.

And Parliament was not **the people**. Its members came from restricted, overwhelmingly male and propertied political worlds. Many Parliamentarians defended particular liberties, but those liberties were not a universal programme of political equality. The New Model Army contained competing visions; the Levellers pressed arguments about representation and rights that alarmed more conservative allies. The Diggers challenged property relations more radically still. The existence of those movements is evidence that the political future was contested, not that their programmes won.

### 🌾 Who fought, who paid, who governed?

Consider the ordinary person whose crops were requisitioned, whose household sheltered troops, whose wages disappeared or whose relatives went to war. A constitutional transformation could be real without distributing its rewards in proportion to its costs. Some people entered the struggle with strong political and religious commitments; others faced pressures that left very little room for philosophical preference.

```text
PEOPLE FIGHT + PAY + ENDURE
             ↓
   THE POLITICAL ORDER CHANGES
             ↓
WHO CONTROLS THE NEW INSTITUTIONS?
             ↓
WHO CAN VOTE, OWN, WORK, WORSHIP,
   HOLD LAND, OR REFUSE AN ORDER?
```

The execution of Charles I in January **1649** and the abolition of the monarchy were momentous. They did not create modern parliamentary democracy. Cromwell later became Lord Protector; military power remained central to politics; Parliaments could be summoned, challenged and dissolved. The monarchy returned in **1660**. The settlement of **1688–89**, so often narrated as a destination, belonged to a later struggle and did not confer equal political participation on the population.

Constitutional limitations on a monarch can matter enormously. They are not the same thing as an equitable distribution of political or economic power. Nor should later democratic gains be retrospectively assigned to everyone who fought for Parliament in the 1640s.

> **A redistribution of authority among governing institutions is not necessarily a redistribution of authority to the people they govern.**

And there is another problem. The parliamentary cause did not confine its claims to England. Its armies and financial arrangements reached across the Irish Sea.

## 8. 🏰 Ireland Was Not Waiting for Cromwell

Ireland was already entangled with English royal authority, conquest and settlement long before Cromwell was born. Henry VIII assumed the title **King of Ireland** in the sixteenth century. But the Crown's legal claims and its ability to govern particular districts were not the same thing, and the island contained competing jurisdictions, confessions, land interests and political loyalties.

The **1641 rebellion** began amid accumulated conflicts over land, religion and power. Violence against Protestant settlers and civilians was real and consequential; its scale and circumstances require careful reconstruction rather than the uncritical repetition of contemporary atrocity propaganda. Catholic civilians also suffered violence during the wider wars. The **1641 Depositions** preserve testimony, accusation, memory and political pressure. They are indispensable sources, but they are not a transparent numerical ledger of everything that happened.

From **1642**, the Irish Catholic Confederation developed political and military institutions, centred at Kilkenny, and governed significant territory. Its members were not one undifferentiated party. Disputes over religion, land, negotiations with the king and military strategy divided them. Royalist, Confederate, Scottish Covenanter and English parliamentary interests intersected and changed over the decade. Many Irish Catholics sought religious and property protections within a continuing monarchy; that is not the same thing as an island collectively voting for Charles I.

Ireland in 1649 was therefore not a blank space into which an English constitutional dispute wandered. It had already endured years of war, negotiations, sieges, shifting alliances and contested governments.

### 💰 The land had been promised before the army arrived

The English Parliament's **Adventurers' Act of 1642** helped raise funds for suppressing rebellion by promising investors interests in Irish land expected to be confiscated. The exact statutory entitlements and their subsequent implementation require reference to the legislation and accounts. The timing is crucial: **the idea of using Irish land to finance English parliamentary military objectives preceded Cromwell's 1649 campaign**.

That does not mean every soldier fought for a future estate or every investor received what was promised. It means land redistribution was already embedded in the financial and political planning of the parliamentary cause. A territory could be represented as a security problem, a religious problem and a source of assets capable of meeting state obligations — all at once.

## 9. ⚔️ Cromwell Comes to Ireland

**Oliver Cromwell landed at Dublin in August 1649** to lead a parliamentary expedition. The English Commonwealth had executed Charles I; supporters of his son and surviving Royalist forces could still threaten the new regime. The alliance of Royalist and Confederate interests in Ireland made the island strategically important. Cromwell's campaign sought to defeat those forces and impose effective Commonwealth control.

Security was one explanation. It was not the only one. English Protestant fears and memories of 1641 shaped the political atmosphere; Cromwell's own religious language presented the struggle in providential terms. The parliamentary regime also inherited financial commitments tied to Irish land. The motives of Cromwell, the Council of State, investors, officers and individual soldiers cannot simply be collapsed into one intention.

### 🩸 Drogheda, September 1649

Cromwell's army besieged **Drogheda** and stormed the town after its defenders refused surrender terms. The assault was followed by extensive killing. Cromwell's letters provide unusually important evidence of how he represented and justified the action, but a commander's account is not a neutral report from every person inside the walls. Historians dispute details of the numbers killed, the composition of the victims and the application of contemporary siege conventions. The violence itself is not in doubt.

The siege should not be reduced to either a reassuring claim that everything was normal for the period or an invented precision about every casualty. We can examine the military context and still recognise that the fate of those inside the town was part of what the conquest meant.

### 🌊 Wexford, October 1649

At **Wexford**, the storming of the town and associated killings again became central to later Irish memory of Cromwell. The sequence of negotiations, the army's entry and the conduct of troops require close attention to contemporary sources. Civilian suffering and the destruction of the town's military and maritime capacity belong in the account; so do the limits of what can be securely counted or attributed to an individual order.

The stories of Drogheda and Wexford are not interchangeable, and neither can stand in for the entire conquest.

### 🏰 The campaign did not end when Cromwell left

Cromwell continued campaigning through the winter and spring, but **left Ireland in May 1650**. The Commonwealth conquest continued under other commanders until **1653**. Further sieges, warfare, displacement, disease and the disruption of agriculture shaped the population's experience. Different regions and communities suffered in different ways; estimates of mortality and population loss must be tied to the evidence and methods used to produce them.

The distinction between **Cromwell's personal command**, **the parliamentary army**, and **the subsequent Commonwealth government** is not an attempt to absolve anybody. It is how we identify who made which decisions. The military conquest enabled later confiscation and settlement, but Cromwell did not personally sign every lease, design every survey or administer every subsequent policy.

And a question remains after the guns: what did the victorious government intend to do with the authority it had secured?

## 10. 🗺️ The Same War, Two Constitutional Histories

From the centre of English parliamentary power, the invasion could be narrated as defeating Royalists, preventing a Stuart restoration, punishing rebellion and securing a threatened Commonwealth. Those were meaningful political objectives to its supporters. They also provided language through which coercive action could be represented as the restoration of lawful order.

From Ireland, the same sequence included **military conquest, imposed government, confiscation and the loss of control over land and political institutions**. The English constitutional story and the Irish colonial story are not competing universes. They describe different positions within one political system.

The colonial character of the project matters. Earlier Tudor conquest and plantation had already established patterns of settlement, coercion and land redistribution. The Commonwealth's campaign extended the effective power of an external governing regime and enabled another sweeping transformation of property. That does not make every action across centuries identical; it locates this conquest within a longer history of English colonial rule in Ireland.

### 👑 Who counted as the political community?

A movement could challenge the arbitrary authority of the English Crown while denying Irish Catholic institutions an equivalent claim to decide how Ireland would be governed. The language of *liberty* operated within political and religious boundaries. It was not necessarily experienced as hypocrisy by every contemporary supporter: many did not believe that the populations involved possessed equal political standing in the first place.

But that boundary is precisely what we must investigate.

```text
FROM THE GOVERNING CENTRE       FROM THE GOVERNED TERRITORY
       "SECURITY"                         INVASION
       "SETTLEMENT"                       DISPOSSESSION
       "REWARD"                           CONFISCATED LAND
       "LAWFUL TITLE"                     LOSS OF A CLAIM
```

The paired terms are **different descriptions and experiences**, not a suggestion that every legal transfer had precisely the same effect on every household. English labourers did not all become landlords because an English army won. Irish society included Catholic and Protestant elites, tenants, labourers, merchants, women whose claims were obscured by property law, and people whose allegiances changed. Some gained, some lost, and many were excluded from the decisions either way.

That complexity does not dissolve the colonial structure. It makes the question of **who could command the machinery of government** more exact.

A confiscation could appear in a record as the lawful reassignment of an estate. For a household, it could mean altered tenancy, displacement, lost income or a new authority over the land on which it depended. Neither a survey nor a parliamentary speech captures all those consequences by itself.

> **The history of an institution acquiring power is not necessarily the history of a population acquiring power.**

And the constitutional history of the governing state cannot tell us, on its own, what happened to the people it governed through conquest.

## 11. 💰 Ireland Pays for the Parliamentary Victory

The Commonwealth's victory created the conditions for a major redistribution of Irish land. Its legal machinery did not spring fully formed from Cromwell's personal preferences. Parliament had already linked financing to expected confiscations in **1642**. After conquest, legislation and administrative orders determined who would be punished, whose property could be forfeited, who might be exempted and who could receive land.

The **Act for the Settlement of Ireland (1652)** distinguished categories of persons and offences and provided for extensive forfeitures. Its categories cannot be summarised responsibly as *all Catholics were expelled* or *every Protestant kept everything*. The provisions, exceptions, petitions and implementation must be read together. Yet the overall outcome was a profound transfer of land away from Irish Catholic ownership towards interests favoured by the victorious regime.

### 🌾 Transplantation, removal and the limits of an order

Policies associated with transplantation towards **Connacht and Clare** are among the period's most consequential and most easily oversimplified measures. Some landholders and their dependants faced orders to move or relinquish holdings; others sought exemptions, negotiated, resisted or remained under different arrangements. Formal deadlines and the geographical language of official orders are not a census of who actually moved. The slogan *to Hell or Connacht* captures a remembered coercive logic, not a substitute for checking individual instruments and outcomes.

Women, children, tenants and workers could experience the consequences of a settlement without appearing as principal owners in its records. The documentary system could be extraordinarily precise about a parcel of land and much less complete about the lives sustained by it.

### 🪙 Soldiers, investors and state debt

The state owed money. Soldiers had arrears. Investors had claims arising from wartime finance. Confiscated land could be used to discharge obligations that could not readily be met in cash. The resulting allocations were not merely abstract changes to a constitutional chart. They moved control over productive land, rent and the terms on which other people might remain.

The distribution was contested, administratively difficult and subject to further change. But the central mechanism is unmistakable:

```text
MILITARY AND FINANCIAL CLAIMS
             ↓
    STATE-DEFINED FORFEITURE
             ↓
    LAND MEASURED AND VALUED
             ↓
     TITLE REALLOCATED
             ↓
  RENT, TENURE AND INHERITANCE
```

A government that claimed to protect property and liberty for its political constituency could also define other people's property as forfeitable. **Whose property the constitutional order protected, and whose it authorised itself to take, were not incidental questions.**

## 12. 📐 The Down Survey: When Conquest Becomes a Map

The conquest and settlement required more than military victory and legal declarations.

They required information.

The **Down Survey of 1656–58**, associated with William Petty, mapped lands intended for redistribution. It helped make parcels identifiable, measurable and administratively available for allocation. Surveying was not invented in the 1650s, nor is every map an act of dispossession. This survey matters because of **what its measurements were being used to accomplish**.

```text
WAR
 ↓
CONQUEST
 ↓
CONFISCATION
 ↓
SURVEY
 ↓
MAP
 ↓
TITLE
 ↓
REDISTRIBUTION
```

The map is not merely a picture drawn after the violence has finished.

It can become part of the mechanism by which the consequences of conquest are made administratively durable.

Land becomes measurable. Boundaries become legible to government. A territorial relationship becomes a parcel. A parcel becomes a recognised title. That title can be inherited, leased, mortgaged and sold. The violence does not need to be repeated every morning once its outcome has been translated into ordinary administration.

The contrast with the wolf bounties above is instructive, provided we do not pretend they were the same policy. One administrative process made land available for reassignment; another rewarded the destruction of particular animals. Both show how a decision made through rules, records and incentives can become a change in the living landscape. Claims about armed Irish wolf hunters still require direct verification and should not be mistaken for a confirmed order of Cromwell himself.

The survey's records tell us what administrators sought to identify and distribute. They cannot, alone, tell us everything about a displaced family, a tenant's continuing obligations, a damaged community or a wolf no longer moving through the same fields.

This is a central principle of Embodied Information Ecology: **a description can also be an instrument of intervention**.

## 13. 📐 When Conquest Becomes Ordinary Property

The Restoration after **1660** modified the Cromwellian settlement; later Williamite confiscations and settlements, estate sales, inheritances, debts and changing leasehold arrangements modified Irish landownership further.

It did not simply restore the previous distribution of land. The eighteenth-century landed order was neither a carbon copy of 1652 nor a clean break from conquest.

Over time, confiscation, settlement, inheritance, sale, lease, debt and law produced a landed order whose everyday operation could become increasingly detached from the violence through which earlier distributions had been created.

Someone living generations later does not necessarily encounter:

> **LAND TAKEN DURING A SEVENTEENTH-CENTURY WAR**

They encounter:

-   an estate;
-   an owner;
-   an agent;
-   a lease;
-   a rent;
-   a recognised title;
-   an inheritance;
-   a mortgage;
-   a court.

This distinction matters enormously.

Historical coercion can become **ordinary property**.

That does not mean every later owner personally committed the original confiscation.

It does not mean every subsequent transaction was fictitious.

It means that law can preserve a distribution after the people who produced it are dead.

And that brings us to another rule for this cluster.

## 14. 🕸️ People Inherit Systems; They Are Not Interchangeable With Them

Colonial systems are built and maintained by people.

Individual responsibility should not disappear into the convenient abstraction of *the system*.

But the reverse mistake is also possible.

Historical systems can outlive the people who created them.

Property arrangements become ordinary property.

Administrative categories become ordinary categories.

Institutions inherit powers from earlier institutions.

Laws can disappear while distributions produced under them remain.

Wealth and disadvantage can reproduce themselves unevenly.

And people can be born into identities which once occupied particular positions within a historical conflict without personally choosing the history that produced those identities.

It is therefore easy to find a living person who appears to **represent** a historical system.

That does not necessarily mean changing, blaming or removing that person changes the system.

The more useful questions are:

-   What arrangement produced the harm?
-   Does that arrangement still exist?
-   If not, what replaced it?
-   Where does the relevant power sit now?
-   Who can exercise it?
-   Who is excluded from it?
-   What material consequences are still being inherited?
-   Which continuities are demonstrable, and which are merely assumed
from identity?
-   What would actually have to change for the relationship to become
different?

> **If the problem is structural, finding somebody who resembles the
> historical beneficiary is not the same thing as finding the
> mechanism.**

This is especially important when we later reach modern arguments about Britishness, Irishness, Protestantism, Catholicism and Unionism.

Nor were these mechanisms uniquely Irish. Enclosure in England, clearance and estate transformation in Scotland, cultural marginalisation in Wales, and colonial settlement in Ireland belong to overlapping but non-identical histories. The useful comparison asks which authorities could compel movement, enforce title, regulate custom or make subsistence dependent on another person's permission. It should not erase the specifically colonial dimensions of conquest and plantation in Ireland, or pretend every practice moved neatly in one direction from these islands to overseas empires. If we claim an administrative technique travelled, we should trace the people, laws or institutions that carried it.

There is also a problem of **historical viewpoint**. An account of the English Civil Wars organised around the later authority of Parliament may leave its reader with a story of constitutional progress. An account beginning with Irish landholding and political autonomy will foreground different consequences of the same victorious regime. Both histories need their own evidence; neither should be reduced to a national personality test. The crucial task is to ask what the institutions actually did, and for whom.

The purpose of reconstructing historical power is not to manufacture hereditary guilt.

It is to understand what the power **did**, what survived it, and what---if anything---still requires changing.

---

## 15. ⛪ Religion, Land And The Protestant Ascendancy

By the eighteenth century, political power, established religion and landownership had become strongly associated with a Protestant elite.

The **Protestant Ascendancy** is useful shorthand, but shorthand can flatten.

The Church of Ireland was the established church. Penal legislation imposed serious restrictions on Catholics and, in different ways, Protestant dissenters. Those restrictions changed over time and were gradually modified; they were not one static code operating identically for a century.

Education, inheritance, political participation and religious practice could each be regulated through different rules. Language requires a separate chronology. The Tudor state's attempts to impose English usage, eighteenth-century religious disabilities and the nineteenth-century national-school system were not a single uninterrupted statute against Irish speech. Yet together with economic and political pressures they formed part of the environment in which languages gained or lost institutional standing. The question is what a person could do in Irish, in English, or only with an intermediary — and who had the power to decide.

Nor does *Protestant* describe a single political population.

Presbyterian communities in Ulster had their own conflicts with the established church and state.

Catholics differed enormously by class and circumstance.

Religious category, political loyalty, ethnicity, landownership and wealth frequently overlapped.

They were never simply the same thing.

That complexity becomes particularly visible in the political upheavals at the end of the eighteenth century.

---

## 16. ⚔️ 1798 And The Union

The **Society of United Irishmen** emerged from the radical political world of the late eighteenth century, influenced by the American and French revolutions and drawing support across religious backgrounds, although the politics and violence of the period became increasingly sectarian in important places.

The rebellion of **1798**, its suppression, French intervention and British security concerns formed part of the immediate political context in which Union was pursued.

Then comes a constitutional fact we need to keep visible for everything that follows.

### 1 January 1801

The Acts of Union created the:

**United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland.**

The Irish Parliament ceased to exist.

Ireland sent **100 MPs** to the House of Commons at Westminster and 28 representative peers to the House of Lords. Day-to-day administration in Ireland nevertheless continued through institutions centred on the Lord Lieutenant and Dublin Castle.

This matters.

Because when the potato crop began to fail in 1845:

> **Ireland was constitutionally part of the United Kingdom.**

That fact does not make the preceding colonial history disappear.

Calling the relationship colonial does not make the constitutional Union disappear either.

The tension between those two facts is itself part of the history.

We will return to it directly in:

[🇬🇧 Implications For Unionism](./🇬🇧_implications_for_unionism.md)

---

## 17. 🗳️ The Union Did Not Settle The Irish Question

Union did not remove conflict over religion, political representation, land or sovereignty.

The decades before the Famine saw major political mobilisation.

Daniel O'Connell built a mass movement around **Catholic emancipation**, achieved in 1829, and subsequently campaigned for repeal of the Union.

The period also contained tithe conflict, agrarian organisation and violence, campaigns over representation and reform, and the emergence of **Young Ireland** before the Famine.

Emancipation itself demonstrates why a reform cannot be assessed from its headline alone. The 1829 settlement removed major Catholic political disabilities while raising the Irish county voting qualification, disenfranchising many forty-shilling freeholders. A measure could widen access to Parliament for some Catholics while narrowing electoral participation for poorer voters. The body politic did not move as one unit.

The **1831 national-school system** and **1838 Irish Poor Relief Act** also belong in this pre-blight chronology. They are not evidence that famine relief had already been planned; they are institutions through which education, poverty and eligibility for assistance were being defined and administered before 1845.

This chronology matters because later history can otherwise become suspiciously convenient:

``` text
FAMINE
  ↓
IRISH PEOPLE BECOME ANGRY WITH BRITAIN
  ↓
NATIONALISM
  ↓
INDEPENDENCE
```

No.

Political arguments over British government in Ireland existed before the blight.

Irish nationalism existed before the blight.

Agrarian conflict existed before the blight.

Debates about sovereignty existed before the blight.

An Gorta Mór would enter an existing political system and profoundly affect what followed.

It did not invent the dispute.

---

## 18. 🏭 Ireland Was Not One Economy

Pre-Famine Ireland was overwhelmingly rural and agricultural.

It was not economically uniform.

Eastern Ulster had a substantial linen economy.

Other areas contained different combinations of tillage, commercial grazing, dairying, fishing, ports, towns, domestic industry and wage labour.

Farm size varied.

So did access to cash.

So did the ability to purchase food rather than grow it.

So did dependence on potatoes.

That means there is no single representative **Irish household** whom we can place on the board and move through the catastrophe.

A substantial farmer producing for market and a labouring family cultivating potatoes on a tiny plot could inhabit the same island while living inside very different relationships to land, food and risk.

Diets varied accordingly. Potatoes, often eaten with milk or buttermilk, could provide substantial nourishment for poorer households; fish, oats, vegetables and animal products also mattered, but access differed by region, class and season. Potato reliance should not be treated as proof that everyone consuming potatoes was already starving. Its danger lay partly in how much household survival could depend on a crop grown on a small plot, with little cash available to buy alternatives.

Those differences will become lethal.

But they are not yet lethal.

---

## 19. 👨‍🌾 Ireland Was Not One Class

By the eve of the Famine, rural society included:

-   landlords;
-   substantial tenant farmers;
-   smaller farmers;
-   middlemen in some places;
-   cottiers;
-   agricultural labourers;
-   landless labourers;
-   domestic servants;
-   artisans;
-   merchants;
-   urban workers;
-   clergy;
-   professionals;
-   and many positions that refuse our desire for a tidy hierarchy.

Cottiers and labouring households require particular care here. A cottier might obtain a small potato plot through an arrangement involving rent, labour or both, but the terms differed. A family could have enough ground to produce subsistence calories without having secure tenure, savings or sufficient wages to purchase food if that crop failed. **Access to land is not the same as ownership of land; growing food is not the same as commanding the market for food.**

Nor should a person missing from a parish register or estate ledger be presumed absent from the census. Archives have different purposes. A landlord's list, a baptism register and a population return do not record the same relationships.

The distinction matters because one of the easiest ways to misunderstand An Gorta Mór is to say:

> **The Irish depended on the potato.**

Which Irish people?

With how much land?

Under what tenure?

With what wages?

Growing what else?

Selling what?

Owning what?

Owing what?

Able to move where?

Able to claim what?

The closer we get to the Famine, the less useful *the Irish* becomes as an economic unit.

---

## 20. 🗺️ There Was More Than One Rural Ireland

National descriptions can conceal radically different local systems.

Land quality, holding size, crop mix, access to markets, wage labour and dependence upon the potato varied across Ireland.

A household living largely from potatoes grown on a small or marginal western holding was not economically interchangeable with a substantial commercial farmer producing grain or livestock elsewhere.

Nor should east, west, north and south be treated as four internally uniform economies.

The important point is structural:

> **The same crop disease would eventually enter different human vulnerability systems.**

Regional variation therefore belongs inside the explanation before the blight arrives. Later nodes can ask how those differences mapped onto hunger, disease, eviction, emigration and population loss.

---

## 21. 🏚️ Poverty Was Already Visible

The 1841 Census counted more than **eight million people** in Ireland — approximately **8.18 million**. The number is important. So is the kind of information the state was collecting.

It also attempted to classify houses and families, leaving an unusually important statistical picture of living conditions immediately before the catastrophe.

The census was conceived as more than a bare headcount. Its published reports and associated inquiries classified houses and families, occupations, literacy and other social conditions. This matters because poverty was not simply a condition experienced privately by the poor. Some of its material signs were being converted into information legible to government.

### 🏚️ Houses as information

The census's housing classifications included a lowest category often described as **fourth-class houses**, typically one-room mud cabins. A category is not a person's entire life, and its distribution was not uniform across Ireland. But it provides evidence of the material circumstances in which many households lived. Housing also shaped exposure to illness, crowding and the ability to survive a shock. Later comparisons with the 1851 population must separate deaths from emigration and other movement; a correlation between poor housing and population decline is not, by itself, a count of famine deaths.

### 📖 What counted as literacy?

Literacy returns deserve particular care. Published summaries distinguish people who could read and write, those who could read only, and those recorded as unable to read. Before reproducing percentages, we need to check the original question, population denominator and instructions: **what language or languages could the enumerators actually recognise?** An administrative classification cannot automatically tell us everything about someone's ability to speak Irish, read a text in Irish or navigate an oral culture. Neither should we assume Irish-language literacy existed wherever Irish was spoken.

### 🩺 What was known about health and mortality?

William Wilde's work on mortality and the census material provides a further route into the conditions officials could describe before the Famine. Here, too, the chain of evidence matters: what was recorded, when it was published, which officials received it and what conclusions they drew. The existence of a published report is not proof that every minister understood its implications.

```text
LIVED CONDITIONS
       ↓
OBSERVATION + ENUMERATION
       ↓
CATEGORIES + PUBLISHED REPORTS
       ↓
OFFICIAL INTERPRETATION
       ↓
ACTION / INACTION / MISDIRECTED ACTION
```

A state can count a vulnerable population without providing that population with the means to survive. **Information gathered is not the same as protection delivered.**

But population growth should not be converted into a morality tale.

We should avoid:

> Ireland had too many people.

And we should avoid treating a large poor population as though it had already become a pile of future corpses.

Population becomes vulnerable in relation to systems:

``` text
POPULATION
     +
LAND ACCESS
     +
EMPLOYMENT
     +
WAGES
     +
FOOD
     +
HOUSING
     +
HOUSEHOLD RESERVES
     +
MARKET ACCESS
     +
RELIEF
     =
RESILIENCE OR FRAGILITY
```

The relevant question is not simply why Ireland had millions of people.

It is why so many households had so little room for a major subsistence shock.

---

## 22. 🌾 A Precarious Agricultural System

For many poor rural households, access to a small plot of land could function as part of the wage and subsistence system.

The household supplied labour.

The plot supplied potatoes.

The potatoes supplied an extraordinary quantity of food from a small area of ground.

That relationship could look something like:

``` text
LABOUR
  ↓
ACCESS TO LAND
  ↓
POTATO CULTIVATION
  ↓
HOUSEHOLD SUBSISTENCE
```

At the same time, other agricultural production could participate in a different circuit:

``` text
LAND
 ↓
GRAIN / LIVESTOCK / BUTTER / OTHER PRODUCE
 ↓
MARKET
 ↓
CASH
 ↓
RENT / INCOME / COMMERCE
```

These were not two sealed economies.

They overlapped.

But they did not give every person the same command over the food being produced around them.

This distinction will become central in:

[🌾 There Was Always Food In Ireland](./🌾_there_was_always_food_in_ireland.md)

For now, it is enough to see the vulnerability.

For a household whose subsistence depended heavily on potatoes grown on a very small amount of land, successful cultivation was doing an enormous amount of work.

It was converting tiny land access into calories.

That arrangement could sustain life.

It also meant that if the crop failed, the household might not possess an equivalent income with which simply to purchase a replacement diet.

---

## 23. 🔁 Ireland Had Met Subsistence Stress Before

The catastrophe beginning in 1845 was unprecedented in scale and duration.

It was not Ireland's first encounter with harvest failure or severe food stress.

Earlier crises included the devastating subsistence crisis of 1740–41 and serious shortages in the early nineteenth century, while localised potato failures had occurred before *Phytophthora infestans* arrived.

That history matters for two opposite reasons.

First, it prevents us treating agricultural vulnerability as something invented in 1845.

Second, it prevents us treating mass death as the automatic consequence of every bad harvest.

Previous shocks had occurred without producing the same sequence of mortality, displacement and demographic rupture.

The severe **1740–41 crisis**, often called the *Year of Slaughter*, deserves attention alongside later shortages and local potato failures. The point is not to collapse these events into one repeated famine. Their causes, scale and administrative settings differed. They do, however, complicate any claim that Irish subsistence vulnerability was a wholly new discovery in 1845. By then the 1838 Poor Law had also established a workhouse-centred relief framework, whose assumptions and limitations would become consequential.

That leaves a question for the later administrative nodes:

> **What had governing institutions learned from previous subsistence shocks, and what did they believe those precedents meant when a new kind of crop disease arrived?**

The existence of earlier crises is context.

It is not an argument that the Great Hunger was inevitable.

---

## 24. 🥔 The Potato Is Not Yet Dead

And this is where this node stops.

Not with catastrophe.

With ordinary life.

In 1841, more than eight million people were living in Ireland.

Children were being born.

People were marrying.

People were working.

People were paying rent.

People were emigrating.

People were protesting.

People were growing oats.

People were spinning linen.

People were raising cattle.

The wolves were gone, although the stories and names remained.

People were still living among woodlands, fields, bogs, rivers and coasts that had changed over centuries.

People were digging potatoes.

People were speaking Irish and English.

People were practising different religions.

People were arguing about the Union.

People were trying to keep their families alive.

Some were wealthy.

Some were comfortable.

Many were poor.

Some households had reserves.

Many had frighteningly little.

The political system was unequal.

The land system left millions exposed to decisions and claims made by people with greater legal and economic power.

The history of conquest was already embedded in ordinary institutions.

But none of these people knew they were living in a chapter called:

**Before the Famine.**

The potato crop was still growing.
