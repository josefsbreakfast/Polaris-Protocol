# 🚢 Where Everybody Went
**First created:** 2026-10-06 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-07  
*People did not simply disappear from Ireland. They moved through routes made by hunger, money, family, policy, empire, language, fear, survival — and the people who had already gone before them.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

The population fell.

That sentence is true and almost useless on its own.

Some people died. Some moved within Ireland. Some crossed to Britain. Some crossed the Atlantic. Some entered British North America. Some went to the United States. Some went to Australia. Some travelled through other parts of the British imperial world. Some paid their own passage. Some were helped by relatives. Some were assisted by landlords or institutions. Some moved through workhouse-linked schemes. Some left before eviction. Some left after losing their homes. Some travelled towards people they knew. Some travelled towards work. Some travelled towards places about which they knew almost nothing. Some expected to return. Some understood that they probably never would.

And once one person survived somewhere else, the information environment changed for everybody connected to them.

This node therefore asks:

> **What converted catastrophe into particular routes of human movement; who travelled through those routes; how did policy and empire shape the available destinations; and how did one generation's constrained movement become another generation's diaspora?**

It also asks a second question which becomes more important the further the person travels:

> **What happens to somebody's structural position when they move from one system into another?**

A person can flee poverty, dispossession, famine or colonial government and arrive somewhere in which the institutions offering them survival are themselves implicated in somebody else's dispossession.

That does not erase what happened to the migrant.

It changes the system in which they are now standing.

## 🪦 1. Absence Is Not A Destination

This node begins where [🪦 How And Why People Died](./🪦_how_and_why_people_died.md) leaves us: with people who cease to appear where we expect them.

```text
PERSON NO LONGER PRESENT
          ↓
         WHY?

├── death
├── internal migration
├── emigration
├── temporary migration
├── household fragmentation
├── administrative disappearance
└── unknown
```

Do not infer emigration from disappearance.

Do not infer death from disappearance.

Do not infer destination from a family story merely because the story is cherished.

And do not mistake uncertainty for evidence that nothing happened.

The archive can lose a person while the person remains alive. A family can lose a person while an administrative system continues counting them somewhere else. A descendant can know that a branch vanished from a townland without knowing whether the missing person died in Ireland, crossed the Irish Sea, boarded an Atlantic vessel or simply moved ten miles and fell into another record system.

The first discipline is therefore very simple:

> **Follow the person, not merely the absence.**

## 🧮 2. Population Loss Is Not A Death Count

The demographic transformation of Ireland contains several mechanisms which must not be collapsed into one number:

```text
EXCESS DEATHS
    +
EMIGRATION
    +
REDUCED BIRTHS
    +
CHANGED MARRIAGE PATTERNS
    +
CONTINUING POST-FAMINE MIGRATION
    =
DEMOGRAPHIC TRANSFORMATION
```

The detailed population consequences belong in [🌿 The Demographic Scar](./🌿_the_demographic_scar.md).

Here we follow the people who moved.

That matters because a person who dies and a person who migrates are both absent from the next Irish census, but they create radically different downstream systems. The migrant may write home, send money, purchase another person's ticket, marry abroad, have children, create an institution, join a political movement, preserve Gaeilge, lose Gaeilge, or become the beginning of a regional diaspora cluster thousands of miles away.

Absence can become network.

## 🚶 3. Irish Migration Did Not Begin In 1845

Do not manufacture a sedentary pre-Famine Ireland followed by a sudden explosion of movement.

Migration already existed.

Before the catastrophe, Irish people travelled for seasonal labour, moved between rural and urban economies, crossed to Britain, joined military and maritime systems, and crossed the Atlantic. University College Cork's EMIGRE history estimates that between 1815 and the beginning of the Famine, roughly 800,000 to one million Irish people sailed for North America, with substantial movement to both Canada and the United States.

The Famine therefore enters an already moving human system.

```text
PRE-EXISTING ROUTE
        +
CATASTROPHIC PRESSURE
        ↓
ROUTE CHANGES / SCALES
```

This distinction matters enormously.

A migration corridor is infrastructure even when nobody has formally designed it as infrastructure. It contains information, transport knowledge, trusted contacts, habitual ports, employers, landlords, clergy, relatives, letters, money and expectations.

When catastrophe arrives, those existing channels can suddenly carry far more people than they did before.

So the question is not merely:

> Did Irish people migrate before the Famine?

Obviously they did.

The question is:

> **What happened to existing migration systems when remaining became dramatically more dangerous?**

## 🧭 4. “Emigrant” Is An Administrative Category, Not An Explanation

Two people can appear in a dataset as emigrants while having experienced completely different events.

```text
MOVED
 ↓
├── ordinary economic migration
├── seasonal work
├── family reunification
├── chain migration
├── independently financed passage
├── prepaid family passage
├── landlord-assisted passage
├── Poor Law-assisted migration
├── workhouse-linked scheme
├── imperial recruitment
├── labour recruitment
├── post-eviction departure
├── famine flight
├── immediate destitution
└── mixed / uncertain
```

The statistical category tells us that movement occurred.

It does not tell us why.

It does not tell us whether the person wanted to leave.

It does not tell us whether they chose the destination freely.

It does not tell us whether they had a meaningful alternative.

It does not tell us whether the journey was supposed to be temporary.

It does not tell us whether the person survived it.

## 🫀 5. Choice Exists On A Gradient

“Voluntary” and “forced” are often too blunt to describe catastrophic migration.

```text
WIDE PRACTICAL CHOICE
        ↓
economic preference
        ↓
family pressure
        ↓
limited livelihood
        ↓
severe poverty
        ↓
loss of subsistence
        ↓
destitution
        ↓
loss of shelter
        ↓
leave or face extreme bodily danger
        ↓
EXTREME CONSTRAINT
```

A person can legally consent to a journey while possessing almost no acceptable alternative.

A person can sign their own name, board the ship under their own power and still be travelling because the systems around them have made staying intolerable.

> **A person can choose a ship because every alternative has become worse.**

That is not the same mechanism as capture and forced transportation.

It is also not ordinary free movement merely because nobody physically chained the passenger to the deck.

The practical question is always:

> **What alternatives did this particular person actually possess at the moment the decision was made?**

## 🏠 6. Somebody Had To Leave A House

Maps make migration look clean.

An arrow leaves Ireland and lands somewhere else.

Human beings do not travel as arrows.

Ask:

- Who decided?
- Who paid?
- Who remained?
- Who travelled together?
- Who was separated?
- What could be carried?
- What had to be sold?
- Who possessed an address at the destination?
- Who could read the letter containing it?
- Who expected to return?
- Who understood that goodbye might mean goodbye?

```text
HOUSEHOLD
   ↓
WHO LEAVES?
   ↓
WHO STAYS?
   ↓
WHAT MONEY MOVES?
   ↓
WHAT INFORMATION MOVES?
   ↓
WHAT RELATIONSHIPS SURVIVE?
```

A state document may call this *emigration*.

A family experiences somebody walking out of the door.

### 🕯️ Ag Dul ar Turas — Going On A Journey

The Schools' Collection preserves an explicitly journey-related Gaeilge blessing under the heading **Ag Dul ar Turas**. The historical spelling is retained here rather than silently modernised.

> **Gaeilge:**  
> *Ar choimrighe Dé is Mhic Duach is Mhic Dara thú, is go dtugaidh siad slán abaile thú.*
>
> **Phonetic — broad English-speaker approximation:**  
> *Er khim-rih-guh Jay iss Vick Doo-akh iss Vick Dah-ruh hoo; iss guh dig-ee sheed slawn uh-bal-yuh hoo.*
>
> **English:**  
> *May you be under the protection of God, Mac Duach and Mac Dara, and may they bring you safely home.*

— recorded in the National Folklore Collection's Schools' Collection; the phonetic line is an accessibility approximation, not a claim that Gaeilge has one canonical accent.

The final words matter here.

**Safely home.**

Not everybody came home.

## 🇬🇧 7. Britain Is Not Merely Another Destination

Britain occupies several positions in this history simultaneously.

```text
BRITAIN
  ↓
├── governing centre
├── neighbouring island
├── labour market
├── destination
├── transport infrastructure
└── onward migration node
```

That makes movement across the Irish Sea analytically strange and extremely important.

A person could leave a part of the United Kingdom undergoing catastrophic hunger and arrive in another part of the same state where wages, relief systems, housing, policing and public health operated differently.

Britain was also often the cheapest or most reachable escape route for people who could not finance a transatlantic passage.

Charles Trevelyan's own 1848 account — a deeply interested source which must be read as such — records 278,005 arrivals from Ireland at Liverpool between 13 January and 1 November 1847. He stated that only 122,981 of those people subsequently sailed from Liverpool to foreign countries. His account also records major Irish movement through Glasgow, Ardrossan, Port Patrick, Fleetwood, Welsh ports, Bristol, Plymouth, Southampton and London.

The figures are useful precisely because they break the lazy diagram:

```text
IRELAND → AMERICA
```

The actual routes can look more like:

```text
IRELAND → LIVERPOOL → STAY IN BRITAIN
```

or:

```text
IRELAND → LIVERPOOL → ATLANTIC
```

or:

```text
IRELAND → SCOTLAND → WORK → FAMILY FOLLOWS
```

or any number of other sequences.

By 1851, substantial Irish-born populations were living in British industrial cities. The regional geography was already becoming visible.

## ⚓ 8. Liverpool — Destination, Choke Point, Junction

Liverpool deserves to be treated as a system rather than a dot on a map.

```text
IRELAND
   ↓
LIVERPOOL
   ├── remain
   ├── move elsewhere in Britain
   └── cross Atlantic
```

The enormous influx placed pressure on housing, relief and health systems. Trevelyan's contemporary account describes relief stations, expanded medical provision and temporary fever hospitals, and records relief officers themselves dying of fever.

This is not merely a story about where Irish people went.

It is a story about **what happens when a damaged population enters another urban system faster than that system can safely absorb them**.

The body remains in the loop.

Malnutrition travels.

Infection travels.

Bereavement travels.

Information travels.

So do relatives, employers, priests, landlords, officials, dock workers, nurses and people trying to help.

Liverpool can therefore be:

- refuge;
- danger;
- destination;
- transit point;
- information hub;
- disease environment;
- employment market;
- beginning of another journey.

A later American family history may contain Liverpool even when nobody in the family remembers it.

## 🌊 9. The Sea Is Part Of The Environment

The term **coffin ship** has become closely associated with some vessels carrying Irish emigrants during An Gorta Mór.

It describes something real.

People crossed the Atlantic already malnourished, sick and exhausted. Overcrowding, inadequate provisions, poor sanitation, infectious disease and dangerous conditions could make the voyage itself another stage of the catastrophe.

But it must not become a universal label for every Famine-era vessel.

Ask which ship.

Which year.

Which route.

Which regulations.

Which mortality.

Which passengers.

```text
LEFT IRELAND
     ≠
SURVIVED THE FAMINE

BOARDED SHIP
     ≠
ARRIVED ALIVE
```

And the imagery carries us towards another Atlantic history.

We should stop there and look at it.

## ⛓️ 10. This Atlantic Already Had Ships Of Death

For centuries before An Gorta Mór, European and American slave traders had forcibly transported captive African people across the Atlantic.

There are visual and bodily similarities which can make these histories collide in human memory:

```text
SHIP
 ↓
CONFINEMENT
 ↓
OVERCROWDING
 ↓
DISEASE
 ↓
HUNGER / DEHYDRATION
 ↓
DEATH
 ↓
BODY ENTERS THE ATLANTIC
```

Those similarities are real.

The systems were not equivalent.

The transatlantic slave trade forcibly transported people who had been enslaved within a commercial system which treated human beings as saleable property. SlaveVoyages estimates approximately 12.5 million captive Africans embarked on transatlantic slave ships and about 10.7 million disembarked in the Americas over the history of the trade. Those figures do not include the entire human cost of capture, forced marches, confinement before embarkation, violence, family destruction and the slave systems into which survivors were delivered.

An Irish famine emigrant choosing among catastrophic options was not occupying the same legal or material position as a captive person who had been enslaved and forcibly transported as property.

```text
IRISH FAMINE-ERA PASSAGE

catastrophe / displacement
        ↓
migration under radically constrained choices
        ↓
dangerous commercial passage
        ↓
disease / exposure / death


TRANSATLANTIC SLAVE TRADE

capture / enslavement
        ↓
human being made saleable property
        ↓
forced transportation
        ↓
systematic confinement and coercion
        ↓
violence / disease / death
```

Both histories contain human beings dying aboard Atlantic ships.

That does not make the systems equivalent.

And remembering Irish suffering must not require us to reduce the much larger, systematically coercive history of African people transported through the transatlantic slave trade.

Likewise, recognising the particular extremity of the Middle Passage does not make the Irish dead disposable.

> **One set of dead does not need to become smaller so another can be remembered accurately.**

This is not competitive suffering.

It is historical resolution.

## 🧠 11. Similar Image, Different Mechanism

Historical memory works associatively.

A reader encounters:

> overcrowded Atlantic ship  
> hunger  
> disease  
> bodies at sea

and another history may arrive immediately in the mind.

Good.

Now investigate the mechanism.

For every catastrophic movement ask:

- Who controlled whether this person travelled?
- Could they legally refuse?
- What alternatives actually existed?
- Were they a passenger, migrant, prisoner, captive or property?
- Who profited from their movement?
- Who controlled their body during the journey?
- What happened if they resisted?
- What provisions were supplied?
- What happened if they became sick?
- What happened if they died?
- What awaited them if they survived?

The vessel can look superficially similar.

The answers tell us what system we are actually looking at.

> **Similarity tells us where to investigate the mechanism. Difference tells us what history we are actually looking at.**

## 🧱 12. Coffin Ship Is Not Concentration Camp

Another association needs to be handled directly.

Large numbers of people confined in unhealthy conditions, processed administratively, inadequately fed and exposed to predictable disease and death can remind us of later systems of mass confinement.

That resemblance is worth noticing.

It is not enough to establish a direct institutional lineage.

```text
SIMILAR DESIGN PRINCIPLE
        ↓
ASK:

Did A directly influence B?
Did both inherit an earlier model?
Did administrators study one another?
Did similar constraints produce similar designs?
Did later institutions deliberately adapt an older practice?
Did the purpose fundamentally change?
```

The term **concentration camp** came into widespread use around the end of the nineteenth century, including in connection with Spanish policy in Cuba and British civilian internment during the South African War. Nazi concentration camps later developed into a centralised system of extrajudicial detention, terror and forced labour. Nazi killing centres were a different institutional category again: sites created for systematic mass murder.

So:

```text
FAMINE EMIGRANT SHIP
purpose: transportation
possible output: foreseeable mass illness / death
but death is not the defining institutional purpose

CONCENTRATION CAMP
purpose: confinement / repression / exploitation
functions vary by regime and period

NAZI KILLING CENTRE
purpose: systematic mass murder
murder is the institutional output
```

A British concentration camp in South Africa is not Auschwitz.

A Soviet camp is not Treblinka.

A Famine emigrant vessel is not a concentration camp.

A slave ship is not a Famine emigrant vessel.

And none of those distinctions require us to pretend there are no recurring technologies of classification, confinement, deprivation, forced labour, administrative abstraction or bodily control.

The useful question is not:

> Which atrocity was basically another atrocity?

It is:

> **Which design principles recur, which were transmitted, which arose independently, what changed their purpose, and what warning signs become visible before a system becomes more destructive?**

We do not need a fabricated straight line from one institution to another.

We need enough resolution to identify the parts which actually travel.

## 🪞 13. Comparison Is Not A Special Accommodation

Nothing about this method is peculiar to Irish, Black, Jewish or Palestinian history.

Human institutions repeatedly encounter similar problems of labour, movement, territory, extraction, classification and control.

We can compare British imperial uses of coerced South Asian labour in African infrastructure with Japanese imperial systems of coerced labour used for railway and other construction in occupied Asia.

That does not make the labour regimes identical.

The comparative question is:

> **What problem was the institution trying to solve, what human beings did it use to solve it, how were those people obtained and controlled, what choices did they possess, what happened to their bodies, and where do the mechanisms diverge?**

```text
CASE A                    CASE B
  ↓                         ↓
        MECHANISM
            ↓
      compare evidence
            ↓
SIMILARITIES ← → DIFFERENCES
            ↓
   BETTER MODEL OF BOTH
```

A useful comparison should increase the resolution of both histories.

If comparison makes either population disappear into a generic category of suffering, the comparison has become too coarse.

## 🇨🇦 14. British North America — Arrival Is Not Settlement

The Canadian route makes the distinction between departure, arrival and survival painfully visible.

The 1847 migration to Canada produced extraordinary mortality. Contemporary British figures reproduced by Trevelyan counted 89,738 British emigrants embarked for Canada and 15,330 deaths across the passage, quarantine station and subsequent hospitals and towns — a source which should be checked against modern scholarship when final figures are used, but which demonstrates how officials themselves could already see the scale of mortality.

Grosse Île became one of the great sites of this migration catastrophe.

```text
IRELAND
   ↓
SHIP
   ↓
GROSSE ÎLE / QUARANTINE
   ↓
QUÉBEC / ST LAWRENCE SYSTEM
   ↓
ONWARD MOVEMENT
```

And again:

> **Port of arrival ≠ final destination.**

Some people continued deeper into British North America.

Some crossed later into the United States.

Some died before either became possible.

The final country listed in a descendant's family tree can conceal the route which produced it.

## 🪦 15. The Famine Travels With The Body

The environmental disturbance does not remain politely behind at the Irish shoreline.

People arrive elsewhere carrying the consequences.

```text
IRELAND
 ↓
MALNUTRITION / DISEASE / DISPLACEMENT
 ↓
SHIP
 ↓
QUARANTINE
 ↓
RECEIVING CITY
```

A person may technically have escaped the place where the potato failed while still dying from the physiological and epidemiological system produced by the catastrophe.

This is why [🪦 How And Why People Died](./🪦_how_and_why_people_died.md) and this node cannot be cleanly separated.

Migration is itself an embodied environment.

## 🇺🇸 16. The United States — Do Not Flatten The Route

The United States became the dominant destination for huge numbers of Irish migrants, but even here the route matters.

Distinguish:

```text
IRELAND → UNITED STATES
```

from:

```text
IRELAND → BRITAIN → UNITED STATES
```

and:

```text
IRELAND → BRITISH NORTH AMERICA → UNITED STATES
```

Then keep following the person after arrival.

New York is not necessarily the end of the journey.

Boston is not necessarily the end of the journey.

Ports feed cities; cities feed labour markets; railways, canals, relatives and employers feed further movement.

The diaspora has geography inside the geography.

## 🇦🇺 17. Australia — Survival Through Another Colonial System

Australia makes the relationship between vulnerability, policy and colonial routing unusually visible.

The Earl Grey scheme moved thousands of young women from Irish workhouses to Australian colonies between 1848 and 1850. The scheme did not represent all Irish migration to Australia. It represents a particularly legible policy pathway:

```text
FAMINE
 ↓
DESTITUTION
 ↓
WORKHOUSE
 ↓
BRITISH ADMINISTRATIVE SYSTEM
 ↓
ASSISTED MIGRATION
 ↓
AUSTRALIAN COLONY
```

For an individual young woman, the British imperial system could therefore be simultaneously implicated in the political economy surrounding her destitution and capable of offering a route out of the workhouse.

That is uncomfortable.

Good.

The discomfort contains information.

It does not make her the architect of the British Empire.

It does place her inside another colonial system.

## 🪃 18. The Land Was Not Empty When They Arrived

Australia, Canada, South Africa, New Zealand and other settler-colonial destinations must not be drawn as blank maps waiting for Irish people.

Ask:

- Whose land was this?
- What Indigenous political orders already existed?
- What was British settlement doing there?
- What legal position did an arriving Irish migrant occupy?
- What opportunities became available to them?
- At whose expense, if anyone's, were those opportunities produced?
- How did that position change in the next generation?

> **Being routed through a colonial system is not the same thing as designing that system.**

And:

> **Being harmed by one part of an imperial system does not guarantee that somebody cannot benefit from, participate in, or become differently positioned within another part of it.**

A person can be structurally subordinated in system A and acquire relative advantage in system B.

Their earlier suffering remains real.

Their new position is also real.

---

## 🌍 19. South Africa And The Wider Imperial Network

Do not let the diaspora map stop at Britain, the United States, Canada and Australia because those are the routes most familiar to modern anglophone readers.

Irish people also moved through wider British imperial systems, including southern Africa, New Zealand, India, the Caribbean and other colonial or military networks.

But movement type matters.

A soldier, administrator, settler, labourer, missionary, transported prisoner, impoverished migrant and assisted migrant are not interchangeable categories.

Nor are the receiving societies interchangeable.

The point is not to make a giant map covered in shamrocks.

The point is to ask why **this** Irish population appears **here**, in **this** period, through **this** route.

## 🗺️ 20. The Diaspora Has A Geography Inside The Geography

It is not enough to ask why Irish populations became substantial across Britain, North America, Australia, South Africa and other parts of the former British imperial world.

They are not distributed evenly inside those places.

The better question is:

> **Why did particular Irish populations become concentrated in particular cities, regions and communities?**

```text
PARTICULAR COUNTY / TOWNLAND / COMMUNITY
                  ↓
PARTICULAR SHOCK / ECONOMIC CONDITION / POLICY
                  ↓
AVAILABLE DEPARTURE ROUTE
                  ↓
PARTICULAR PORT
                  ↓
SHIPPING / RAIL / LABOUR / IMPERIAL NETWORK
                  ↓
PARTICULAR DESTINATION
                  ↓
WORK + HOUSING + FAMILY / RELIGIOUS NETWORK
                  ↓
LETTERS + MONEY + INFORMATION HOME
                  ↓
MORE PEOPLE FROM CONNECTED COMMUNITIES FOLLOW
                  ↓
REGIONAL DIASPORA CLUSTER
```

This is path dependence.

A national map conceals the mechanism.

## 🧠 21. History Has Routing Tables

Once a route exists, the next person is not choosing from the whole planet.

One destination contains:

- a sister;
- an uncle;
- somebody from the parish;
- a bed;
- a church;
- an employer;
- an address;
- perhaps somebody who speaks Gaeilge;
- somebody who knows which ship to take;
- somebody who knows which streets are dangerous;
- somebody who can lend the fare;
- somebody who can write home afterwards.

Another theoretically available destination contains nobody.

Those are not equivalent options.

```text
FIRST MIGRANT
     ↓
INFORMATION
     ↓
REDUCED UNCERTAINTY
     ↓
NEXT MIGRANT
```

> **History has routing tables.**

That is why Irish areas exist.

Not because an ethnicity was sprinkled evenly across an empire.

Because humans followed information, relationships, transport and opportunity through routes that became easier to reuse once somebody else had survived them.

## ✉️ 22. Letters Are Migration Infrastructure

A letter can carry:

- an address;
- a wage;
- a warning;
- a death;
- a marriage;
- a job lead;
- shipping advice;
- a ticket;
- money;
- an invitation;
- news that the destination is not safe after all.

```text
IRELAND
   ↕
LETTER
   ↕
DIASPORA
```

The diaspora is partly an information network.

A letter can change who migrates next.

A missing letter can change who does not.

A person who cannot read may depend upon another human being to process the information for them.

The network is social before it is digital.

## 💷 23. Remittances Change Who Can Leave Next

Migration can become self-reinforcing.

```text
MIGRANT SURVIVES
      ↓
EARNS
      ↓
SENDS MONEY / TICKET / INFORMATION
      ↓
HOUSEHOLD OPTIONS CHANGE
      ↓
ANOTHER PERSON CAN MOVE
```

The first migration may require the sale of almost everything.

The second may have a prepaid ticket.

The third may arrive to an established household.

The fourth may be born there.

A catastrophe can therefore create a migration route whose later users are no longer fleeing the original catastrophe at all.

## 🔗 24. Chain Migration Produces Regional Clustering

```text
MIGRANT A
   ↓
NETWORK
   ↓
MIGRANT B
   ↓
LARGER NETWORK
   ↓
MIGRANTS C + D
   ↓
COMMUNITY
   ↓
INSTITUTIONS
   ↓
REGIONAL DIASPORA
```

What looks 150 years later like a stable ethnic geography may have begun with a remarkably specific set of routes and decisions.

This is why we need regional rather than merely national analysis across:

- Britain and Ireland;
- the United States;
- Canada;
- Australia;
- South Africa;
- New Zealand;
- the wider former British imperial world.

“Lots of Irish people live in this country” is the beginning of the question, not the answer.

## 🏛️ 25. Westminster Policy Can Appear In A Family Tree

Do not make the crude claim that Westminster personally selected every migrant.

Ask instead:

> **Which policies altered the material conditions under which particular people decided, were encouraged, were assisted, or were effectively compelled to leave?**

Relevant systems include:

- relief policy;
- Poor Law administration;
- the quarter-acre rule;
- workhouse conditions;
- estate incentives;
- landlord decisions;
- assisted emigration;
- imperial labour and settlement requirements;
- military structures;
- later land and economic policy.

```text
POLICY
   ↓
MATERIAL CONDITIONS
   ↓
AVAILABLE OPTIONS
   ↓
WHO MOVES
   ↓
AVAILABLE ROUTE
   ↓
DESTINATION
   ↓
DESCENDANTS
```

The point is not that every diaspora cluster was consciously engineered from Westminster.

The point is that policy changes the feasible set.

And when policy repeatedly changes the feasible set, it can become visible generations later as geography.

Or, less academically:

> **Which fucking policy happened to your great-great-granny?**

Sometimes the answer will be none in any simple sense.

Sometimes the answer will be surprisingly legible.

## 🧬 26. Policy Cohorts

“The Irish diaspora” is too large a category to explain itself.

Test distinct cohorts:

```text
PRE-FAMINE MIGRATION

FAMINE-ERA FLIGHT

BLACK '47 MOVEMENT

WORKHOUSE-LINKED MIGRATION

LANDLORD-ASSISTED MIGRATION

IMPERIAL ASSISTED MIGRATION

POST-FAMINE CHAIN MIGRATION

LATER AGRARIAN / ECONOMIC MIGRATION

TWENTIETH-CENTURY LABOUR MIGRATION

MODERN MOBILITY
```

Different shocks select different people.

Different policies create different routes.

Different routes create different descendant populations.

So ask:

> **Which historical mechanism put this generation of this population there?**

## 👩 27. Migration Selects People

Migration is not demographically neutral.

Track where the evidence permits:

- sex;
- age;
- occupation;
- marital status;
- household position;
- county;
- class;
- language;
- religion;
- health;
- access to money.

A scheme selecting young women produces a different descendant population from a labour route selecting young men.

A route requiring substantial passage money selects differently from a route subsidised by an institution.

A route through a particular port selects differently from a route opened by a particular employer.

The diaspora map is therefore partly a map of **selection mechanisms**.

## 👶 28. Migration Makes Generations

```text
IRISH-BORN MIGRANT
       ↓
CHILD BORN ELSEWHERE
       ↓
GRANDCHILD
       ↓
GREAT-GRANDCHILD
       ↓
IRISH ANCESTRY
WITHOUT IRISH BIRTH
```

Diaspora is a process, not a static ethnic box.

The first generation remembers the journey.

The second may remember the person who made it.

The third may remember the story.

The fourth may remember only “we're Irish”.

The fifth may discover the route again in an archive.

Or none of that may happen.

Information can survive brilliantly.

It can also fall out of a family in one generation.

---

## 🧬 29. Every Generation Inherits A Different Ireland

### First generation

Ireland is lived memory.

### Second generation

Ireland is parental memory plus receiving-country childhood.

### Third generation

Ireland may be family story, neighbourhood, church, political identity, surname, food, music or inherited silence.

### Later generations

Information can be:

- maintained;
- transformed;
- romanticised;
- politicised;
- partially forgotten;
- completely lost;
- rediscovered.

Therefore:

> **Two Irish-descended people can inhabit radically different relationships to the same ancestral history.**

And two people living in the same modern country can have arrived there through completely different historical machines.

---

## 🗣️ 30. Anglophone Destination Does Not Mean Anglophone Origin

A person ending up in an English-speaking society does not prove that English was their original, preferred or strongest language.

```text
GAEILGE-SPEAKING / BILINGUAL PERSON
              ↓
MIGRATION
              ↓
ANGLOPHONE LABOUR ENVIRONMENT
              ↓
CHILDREN REQUIRE ENGLISH
              ↓
LANGUAGE TRANSMISSION CHANGES
```

The global geography of Irish ancestry is also partly a geography of language disruption.

Famine, emigration, schooling, labour markets, administration and social pressure all matter to the larger history of language shift.

Do not reduce that history to one cause.

Do not remove coercive power from it either.

---

## ☘️ 31. Gaeilge Went On The Ship Too

Language does not vanish at the dock.

People carried:

- prayers;
- songs;
- stories;
- names;
- jokes;
- curses;
- religious formulas;
- ways of describing weather;
- ways of describing hunger;
- ways of blessing somebody who was leaving.

Some of that survived overseas.

Some did not.

Some survived in English translation.

Some became fragments descendants no longer recognised as Gaeilge.

The person does not leave their information ecology behind merely because the state changes the box in which they are counted.

### 🪬 Dia romhainn agus Dia linn

Another prayer in the Schools' Collection contains the protective formula:

> **Gaeilge:**  
> *Dia romhainn agus Dia linn.*
>
> **Phonetic — broad English-speaker approximation:**  
> *Jee-uh roh-in agus Jee-uh linn.*
>
> **English:**  
> *God before us and God with us.*

— recorded in a Gaeilge prayer collection from County Galway. Here it is used as an attested protective formula, not claimed as a Famine-emigration prayer specifically.

That distinction matters.

We can allow a surviving Gaeilge information system to speak beside the journey without pretending every prayer was uttered on every ship.

## 🧳 32. What People Carried

Not just luggage.


Some are deliberately transmitted.

## 🪞 33. Structural Position Can Change Without Erasing The Past

A person can be subordinated in one system and differently positioned in another.

```text
PERSON IN SYSTEM A
       ↓
MIGRATION
       ↓
PERSON IN SYSTEM B
       ↓
DIFFERENT RELATION TO POWER
```

Their previous suffering remains real.

Their new structural position is also real.

> **People inherit histories. They do not inherit one permanent position in every system they subsequently enter.**

This matters enormously when discussing Irish people inside the British Empire and later inside racial systems in North America and elsewhere.

A family history can contain both vulnerability and later proximity to power.

That is not hypocrisy in the blood.

It is what happens when humans move between systems.

## ⚙️ 34. Survival Can Create Uncomfortable Incentives

A receiving society can offer a vulnerable migrant:

- employment;
- citizenship;
- policing roles;
- military service;
- land;
- political membership;
- legal status;
- racial classification;
- economic mobility.

Those opportunities may themselves be embedded in systems that disadvantage somebody else.

This does not mean:

> the migrant was secretly always the oppressor.

It means:

> **institutions can make participation in somebody else's exclusion materially useful to somebody seeking security for themselves.**

That mechanism is not peculiar to Irish history.

It is precisely why historical comparison matters.

---

## 🇺🇸 35. Irish America, Whiteness And The Lost Possibility Of Solidarity

Noel Ignatiev's *How the Irish Became White* made an influential argument that Irish incorporation into American whiteness involved participation in anti-Black racial ordering and a lost possibility for interracial working-class solidarity.

The thesis must be handled critically rather than repeated as scripture.

Later scholarship has stressed that nineteenth-century Irish people were generally already classified as white even while Irish poverty, Catholicism and political identity were intensely racialised and caricatured.

So the more precise question is often not:

> When did Irish people literally become white?

but:

> **How did Irish migrants and their descendants acquire changing proximity to the protections, institutions and advantages associated with whiteness in the United States, and what incentives accompanied that movement?**

That means examining:

- labour competition;
- party politics;
- policing;
- political machines;
- anti-Black discrimination and violence;
- military participation;
- housing;
- citizenship;
- class mobility;
- interracial organising;
- moments when solidarity succeeded;
- moments when it failed.

Do not compare Irish racialisation with Black enslavement or Jim Crow as though they were equivalent institutions.

They were not.

The comparison is about changing structural position, incentives and the political possibilities available to populations seeking greater safety.

---

## ✡️ 36. Diaspora, Conditional Safety And Proximity To Power

This is also where comparison with Jewish diaspora histories can become useful if it is kept at the correct level.

Not:

```text
IRISH EXPERIENCE = JEWISH EXPERIENCE
```

Instead:

```text
DIASPORA A
   ↓
HISTORICAL INSECURITY
   ↓
MIGRATION
   ↓
CHANGING RELATION TO
DOMINANT RACIAL / POLITICAL SYSTEM

DIASPORA B
   ↓
DIFFERENT HISTORICAL INSECURITY
   ↓
DIFFERENT MIGRATION
   ↓
CHANGING RELATION TO
DOMINANT RACIAL / POLITICAL SYSTEM
```

In particular North American contexts, Irish and Jewish populations have both had histories in which greater proximity to dominant whiteness could increase security while not making older histories of prejudice, persecution or possible scapegoating disappear.

The histories are not interchangeable.

The comparative question is:

> **What happens when a historically insecure population is offered greater safety inside an existing hierarchy?**

Sometimes the answer includes solidarity.

Sometimes assimilation.

Sometimes communal separation.

Sometimes participation in somebody else's exclusion.

Usually the history is messier than one branch.

## 🤝 37. Solidarity Is A Possible Branch, Not An Automatic Outcome

```text
HISTORICAL INSECURITY
        ↓
NEW SOCIETY
        ↓
NEED FOR SAFETY
        ↓
     OPTIONS

├── proximity to dominant power
├── assimilation
├── communal separation
├── political mobilisation
├── coalition with other minorities
├── participation in exclusion
└── mixtures of the above
```

No branch is genetically predetermined.

No population is born naturally solidaristic.

No population is born naturally oppressive.

Institutions alter incentives.

Fear alters incentives.

Material security alters incentives.

Relationships alter incentives.

People alter institutions.

## 🫂 38. Somebody Else May Understand The Mechanism

Historical particularity does not require human incomprehensibility.

```text
YOUR EVENT ≠ MY EVENT

but perhaps:

your displacement ↔ something I recognise

your inherited fear ↔ something I recognise

your fear for your children ↔ something I recognise

your uncertainty about neighbours ↔ something I recognise

your temptation to seek absolute security ↔ something I recognise
```

> **We do not need to have lived the same event to recognise the human problem you are trying to solve.**

That is true when speaking to Americans about the fear produced by 9/11 without pretending another ally's terrorist attack was “the same”.

It is true when speaking across Jewish and Palestinian histories without pretending the histories are the same.

It is true when descendants of colonialism, enslavement, famine, partition, war or displacement recognise a mechanism in another population's fear without claiming ownership of that fear.

There are very few completely unique human problems.

There are many unique historical configurations of them.

## 🌉 39. Not Everybody Has To Hold The Bridge

People experiencing immediate violence cannot be required to perform reconciliation work.

A person who has just buried somebody does not owe anybody an elegant seminar in comparative historical empathy.

A survivor does not owe the person they believe is harming them reassurance.

A Palestinian living through current destruction does not have to perform emotional labour to make an Israeli feel safe before Palestinian life deserves protection.

An Israeli or Jewish person in acute grief or fear does not have to be the person capable of hearing every argument at every moment either.

Human networks allow different people to perform different functions.

```text
SOME PEOPLE CANNOT
HOLD THE BRIDGE TODAY
        ↓
THEY DO NOT HAVE TO

OTHER PEOPLE CAN
        ↓
LISTEN
        ↓
TRANSLATE
        ↓
CHALLENGE
        ↓
REMAIN PRESENT
```

The bridge does not require everybody to stand on it simultaneously.

---

## 👂 40. Listening Does Not Mean Agreement

There is a form of listening which says:

> Tell me what you believe will happen to you.

> Tell me what you believe will happen to your children.

> Tell me what history makes that fear credible to you.

> Tell me what you believe you require in order to be safe.

And then still asks:

> **Does the solution you are proposing require somebody else to remain permanently unsafe?**

Understanding the causal origin of violence does not require accepting violence.

```text
LISTENING ≠ ACQUIESCENCE

UNDERSTANDING ≠ ENDORSEMENT

EMPATHY ≠ IMMUNITY FROM ACCOUNTABILITY
```

Even brutal violence can be produced partly by fear.

That makes the fear relevant to prevention.

It does not make the violence acceptable.

---

## ⏱️ 41. Protection And Reconciliation Run On Different Clocks

This distinction must remain explicit.

```text
IMMEDIATE HARM
      ↓
REQUIRES IMMEDIATE PROTECTION
```

while:

```text
FEAR
MEMORY
CONSTITUTIONAL ARRANGEMENTS
TRUST
RECONCILIATION
      ↓
MAY REQUIRE YEARS / GENERATIONS
```

> **Dialogue cannot become the price somebody has to pay before ongoing harm stops.**

Nor can:

> this will take time

become permission for indefinitely continuing preventable harm.

Urgent protection and patient reconciliation operate on different clocks.

Both can be necessary.

They are not substitutes for one another.

---

## 🕊️ 42. An Olive Branch Is Labour

Do not expect every harmed population to perform it.

Sometimes the people capable of holding open communication will be:

- members of the same community with greater emotional distance;
- diaspora members;
- people from another traumatised population;
- neighbours;
- mediators;
- historians;
- lawyers;
- organisers;
- friends;
- people who simply happen to have enough capacity today.

There are Jewish people who may be too hurt, frightened or exhausted to perform that work in a particular moment.

There are other Jewish people prepared to listen closely to Israeli fears while still insisting upon Palestinian safety and opposing violence against Palestinians.

Those are not contradictory positions.

Likewise, Palestinians cannot be required to supply the emotional bridge while Palestinians are themselves in immediate danger.

The existence of somebody capable of listening does not create a duty for the person currently bleeding to become the mediator.

---

## 🧠 43. A Safe Room Is Not A Room Without Correction

People need spaces in which they can:

- reveal fear;
- expose an assumption;
- say something clumsy;
- discover prejudice;
- be corrected;
- reconsider;
- remain human afterwards.

```text
PERSON SPEAKS
      ↓
BIAS / FEAR / ERROR
BECOMES VISIBLE
      ↓
CHALLENGE
      ↓
PERSON CAN PROCESS
THE CHALLENGE
      ↓
MODEL CHANGES
      ↓
BEHAVIOUR CAN CHANGE
```

But:

```text
ROOM TO MAKE MISTAKES
        ≠
ROOM TO KEEP HARMING PEOPLE
WITHOUT CONSEQUENCE
```

Call this **accountable safety**.

If every disclosure of unconscious prejudice produces immediate permanent exile, people become extremely good at concealing prejudice and extremely bad at examining it.

If every disclosure is endlessly excused, the room becomes unsafe for everybody harmed by it.

The useful space does both:

**stay in relationship where possible; keep accountability alive.**

## 🗺️ 44. Self-Determination Includes Knowing When The Decision Is Not Yours

Northern Ireland provides a useful methodological example without becoming a template to copy onto somebody else's conflict.

The Belfast/Good Friday Agreement embeds the principle that Northern Ireland's constitutional status rests on the consent of the people of Northern Ireland, while protecting the right to identify as British, Irish or both.

That creates a useful discipline:

```text
I CARE DEEPLY
     ≠
I GET TO DECIDE

I AM MATERIALLY AFFECTED
     ≠
I GOVERN THE PEOPLE
MOST DIRECTLY AFFECTED

I HAVE STRONG FEELINGS
     ≠
I REPLACE LOCAL
SELF-DETERMINATION
```

A person elsewhere in Britain can have intense emotional opinions about the constitutional future of the six counties and still recognise that the people living with the border, institutions and risk of renewed violence possess a different political relationship to the decision.

That is not indifference.

It is restraint around somebody else's self-determination.

## 🧩 45. Translation Is Not Equivalence

This node relies heavily on the method developed in [🗺️ Translation Is Not Equivalence](./🗺️_translation_is_not_equivalence.md).

Historical comparison can ask:

- what is the fear?
- what is the resource?
- what is the coercive mechanism?
- what is the security requirement?
- what institution mediates it?
- who bears the cost?
- what alternatives exist?
- who decides?

without claiming:

- identical history;
- identical law;
- identical intent;
- identical moral responsibility;
- identical power;
- identical outcome.

```text
COMPARE MECHANISMS
        ↓
IDENTIFY DIFFERENCES
        ↓
ONLY THEN ASK
WHAT TRAVELS
```

> **Comparison should increase the resolution of both histories, not blur one of them.**

## 🛡️ 46. Safety That Requires Somebody Else's Permanent Insecurity

A recurring political failure mode looks like this:

```text
GROUP A FEARS HARM
       ↓
SECURITY SYSTEM
       ↓
GROUP B MUST REMAIN
WEAKER / EXCLUDED / DISPLACED
       ↓
GROUP B BECOMES INSECURE
       ↓
ANGER / RESISTANCE / FEAR
       ↓
GROUP A PERCEIVES
ADDITIONAL THREAT
       ↓
DEMAND FOR MORE SECURITY
```

The loop can reinforce itself.

The liberatory engineering problem is therefore not:

> stop being frightened.

It is:

> **How do we satisfy legitimate human requirements for safety without making another population's permanent insecurity the mechanism through which safety is produced?**

That is a much harder question.

It is also considerably more useful.

## 🫱🏻‍🫲🏾 47. Solidarity Is Defensive Infrastructure

Relationship changes the information environment.

```text
RELATIONSHIP
      ↓
DIRECT INFORMATION
      ↓
LESS DEPENDENCE ON
THIRD-PARTY REPRESENTATION
      ↓
HARDER TO TURN
OTHER PERSON INTO ABSTRACTION
```

Solidarity does not mean compulsory agreement.

It does not mean historical equivalence.

It does not mean abandoning boundaries.

It does not mean trusting everybody.

It does not mean denying actual conflicts of interest.

It means maintaining enough human connection that fear does not automatically become somebody else's weapon.

> **It is harder to convince me that you are an abstraction when I already know you as a human being.**

## 🧩 48. Divide And Conquer Travels With Diaspora Too

Displaced populations do not arrive without vulnerabilities.

A population seeking safety may be particularly sensitive to messages saying:

> those people threaten the safety you have finally acquired.

That vulnerability can be deliberately cultivated, opportunistically exploited or reproduced without a single mastermind.

Ask:

```text
WHAT AM I AFRAID OF?

WHO IS BEING PRESENTED AS THE THREAT?

WHAT EVIDENCE SUPPORTS THAT?

WHAT ACTION AM I BEING ASKED TO TAKE?

WHO GAINS POWER IF I TAKE IT?

DOES IT REQUIRE ME TO ABANDON
A POTENTIAL ALLY?
```

> **Strong emotion is information — not necessarily instruction.**

Historical trauma can make a threat model understandable.

It cannot make the threat model automatically accurate.

## 🌍 49. There Are Very Few Completely Unique Human Problems

Do not write:

> everybody has suffered equally.

Do not write:

> all atrocities are the same.

Do not write:

> everybody understands everything.

Instead:

> **Somewhere in human history there is often another population which has encountered part of the mechanism you are struggling with.**

That may be:

- displacement;
- famine;
- persecution;
- terrorism;
- coerced migration;
- religious exclusion;
- racialisation;
- state violence;
- colonialism;
- enslavement;
- forced labour;
- assimilation;
- partition;
- diaspora;
- fear of recurrence;
- conditional acceptance;
- participation in another hierarchy as the price of safety.

This is why comparison is not an attack on historical particularity.

It can create more possible interlocutors.

## 🫀 50. The Descendants Are In The Room

Historical comparison is not being performed in an empty laboratory.

The descendants are alive.

Their nervous systems are alive.

Their families may contain missing people, inherited stories, silence, photographs, names, destroyed archives, rediscovered ancestry, religious memory, political fear or material inequality produced by these histories.

Someone says:

> famine  
> slavery  
> pogrom  
> partition  
> Holocaust  
> Nakba  
> colonialism  
> terrorism

and another human body can immediately process:

> **this concerns the survival of people like me.**

That is not stupidity.

That is part of what collective memory does.

The challenge is to build enough historical resolution that the next step does not automatically become:

> therefore your memory threatens mine.

It can sometimes become:

> **Different history. Different mechanism in important ways. But I recognise part of the problem you are describing. Tell me more.**

## 🕯️ 51. You Do Not Have To Be Uniquely Understood To Be Understood

Your dead remain yours.

Your history remains particular.

Your fear remains worthy of being heard.

Recognition of another person's history does not reduce yours.

```text
YOUR DEAD
   +
MY DEAD
   ≠
LESS MEMORY
```

Another person does not need to have lived your exact catastrophe to sit beside you while you explain why you are frightened.

Sometimes they will disagree with what you believe should happen next.

Sometimes they will tell you no.

Sometimes they will recognise a prejudice you cannot yet see.

Sometimes you will do the same for them.

That is not necessarily a failure of solidarity.

That can be what solidarity is for.

## 🙏 52. Bless The Traveller

The blessings in this node are not decorative Celtic wallpaper.

They restore the scale which administrative language removes.

Policy calls it assisted emigration.

Demography calls it population movement.

Empire calls it settlement.

The person at the door says:

> **go safely.**

### 🏠 Leaving home

> **Gaeilge:**  
> *Ar choimrighe Dé is Mhic Duach is Mhic Dara thú, is go dtugaidh siad slán abaile thú.*
>
> **Phonetic:**  
> *Er khim-rih-guh Jay iss Vick Doo-akh iss Vick Dah-ruh hoo; iss guh dig-ee sheed slawn uh-bal-yuh hoo.*
>
> **English:**  
> *May you be under the protection of God, Mac Duach and Mac Dara, and may they bring you safely home.*

### 🪬 Protection on the way

> **Gaeilge:**  
> *Dia romhainn agus Dia linn.*
>
> **Phonetic:**  
> *Jee-uh roh-in agus Jee-uh linn.*
>
> **English:**  
> *God before us and God with us.*

The first formula is explicitly recorded as **Ag Dul ar Turas** in the Schools' Collection. The second is an attested protective prayer formula from the same wider folklore archive. Historical spelling and dialect matter; phonetics here are deliberately broad accessibility aids rather than linguistic transcription.

And for the people who did not come home, this node returns us to the prayers for the dead used in [🪦 How And Why People Died](./🪦_how_and_why_people_died.md).

The journey does not cancel the mourning.

## 🔬 56. Research Architecture

### A — Baseline movement

Establish pre-1845 seasonal, British, Atlantic and imperial migration patterns so the Famine does not falsely appear to invent mobility.

### B — Britain

Map regional settlement and transit: Liverpool, Lancashire, Manchester, Glasgow and western Scotland, London, the North East, ports and industrial districts.

### C — Atlantic passage

Track fares, vessels, regulations, provisioning, disease, voyage time, mortality and changing passenger protections.

### D — British North America

Separate Grosse Île and Québec arrival from onward settlement and later US movement.

### E — United States

Separate direct arrival from routes through Britain and Canada; follow internal migration after port arrival.

### F — Australia

Separate ordinary migration from assisted schemes, especially workhouse-linked and Earl Grey migration.

### G — South Africa and wider empire

Distinguish military, administrative, labour, settler, religious and ordinary migration rather than aggregating them as “Irish abroad”.

### H — Policy

Test Poor Law, relief, land, estate and assisted-emigration policies for their effects on who could or had to move.

### I — Regional clustering

Build origin → route → destination → network chains rather than merely national totals.

### J — Household networks

Letters, remittances, prepaid passages, addresses, marriage, sponsorship and family reunification.

### K — Demographic selection

Sex, age, class, occupation, language, religion and household position.

### L — Gaeilge

Track language movement, language loss, surviving prayer and oral traditions, and attested travel/protection formulas.

### M — Structural position

Ask what position a migrant left, what position they entered, and how that changed over generations.

### N — Comparative method

Where another history is evoked, specify the dimension of comparison and the point of divergence. Never use another population's suffering as a loose metaphor when its actual history can be named.
