# 🌳 Old Man Willow Is Just A Fairy
**First created:** 2026-10-24 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-24  
*Not a hobbit-murderer. Hear me out.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

There is a persistent question about Tolkien's Old Man Willow that I find rather bewildering: how could a man who loved trees write such a wicked one?

Have you motherfuckers heard fairy tales? 🤨

I am not entirely sure what to tell you. Tolkien wrote an ancient woodland, inhabited by beings with memories and opinions, which has been subjected to enclosure, felling and burning by its neighbours. Four hobbits walk into it, discover that its paths do not behave as expected, succumb to enchantment, and are nearly swallowed by a tree. A local supernatural authority arrives, addresses the tree, and makes it release them.

This is not an ontological emergency. This is a **fairy-related incident**.

More specifically, Old Man Willow's conduct is interesting because Tolkien does not merely put a frightening spirit inside an arbitrary tree. He makes the spirit act *through a willow*: a tree with a real material ecology, a long medicinal history, and overlapping associations in British and Irish traditions with water, protection, healing, mourning, and sometimes supernatural significance. Comfort, rest, enclosure, guardianship and the riverbank become the very means by which the hobbits are endangered.

**Old Man Willow has not forgotten how to be a willow. The hobbits have forgotten that the willow might have opinions about them.**

This is a defence of a culturally recognisable supernatural neighbour, not a claim that Tolkien explicitly classified Old Man Willow as one of the *aos sí*, or that every association below was a consciously encoded authorial reference. It is also not an argument that Merry and Pippin deserved to be eaten. The question is whether a reader familiar with the fairy traditions of these islands would necessarily find the encounter as morally or ontologically baffling as some later readings appear to.

And then there is the wild twin. Because I am not going to reduce an undomesticated woodland being to a badly behaved version of Treebeard just to make everybody more comfortable.

---

## ✨ Key Features

- **Living folklore, not a dead code:** in many communities, the practical advice about fairy places was simply *don't bother the fairies*. The conventions could be recognisable without exposition.
- **A body that matters:** Tolkien's early draft describes an earth-bound spirit bound up with a willow; the published encounter operates through the tree's physical form and its riverside environment.
- **Correspondences in action:** the willow's medicinal, protective, watery and funerary associations illuminate *how* the hobbits are enchanted, sheltered, trapped and threatened.
- **History in the loop:** the Old Forest has experienced hobbit enclosure and burning. Its inhabitants are not meeting humans in a historical vacuum.
- **The wild twin is not the evil twin:** Martin Shaw offers a rawer, more relational folkloric lens than simply treating the tree as somebody's Jungian shadow.
- **Tom knows the neighbours:** Bombadil does not deny Old Man Willow's agency or destroy him. He addresses him, rather firmly, and retrieves the hobbits.

In Irish traditions concerning the *aos sí*, and in many other fairy traditions across Britain and Ireland, particular places are not necessarily empty simply because no human being is visibly occupying them. A mound, ringfort, spring, grove, tree or stretch of ground may have inhabitants. The inhabitants may be powerful. They may be easily offended, or simply uninterested in human explanations for why their homes have been disturbed.

Sometimes people leave offerings. Sometimes they avoid cutting a particular tree, building on a particular place, or interfering with a feature that a neighbour has warned them about. Sometimes the correct response is not to investigate further. These practices vary enormously by locality, period and tradition; they are not a universal Celtic rulebook. Their shared practical grammar is nevertheless recognisable: **the landscape is inhabited, and human access is not the same thing as permission**.

Marion Dowd's work on Irish fairy archaeology, folk magic and traditional medicine is particularly useful here. It documents the association of fairy beings with identifiable archaeological places and records practices and fears surrounding their disturbance. UCD's discussion of holy wells and sacred trees likewise draws on folklore collections recording misfortune associated with damage to significant trees. This is not simply a set of decorative symbols. It is a body of accounts in which human actions toward a place have consequences because the place is socially and supernaturally significant. [1][2]

There is a reason euphemistic names such as the *Good Neighbours* can be so funny to outsiders. The name is not a customer-satisfaction rating. It is a way of conducting oneself in relation to beings one would rather not antagonise.

☘️: Did you make an offering?

🇺🇸: Why would we make an offering?

☘️: To the inhabitants.

🇺🇸: WHAT INHABITANTS?

The offerings are not a secret canonical toll booth at the entrance to Tolkien's Old Forest. **They are part of the interpretive equipment** that a reader steeped in these traditions might bring to an encounter with a territorial supernatural being. The deeper issue is not whether the hobbits have the right flowers or milk. It is that they do not seem to understand that they have entered somebody else's world.

And when somebody has warned you not to bother the fairies, asking whether the fairies are *technically* fairies before deciding whether to bother them is not, historically speaking, a brilliant survival strategy.

## 📖 2. It Was 1954, Cousin

🇺🇸: But Tolkien never tells us the rules.

🇬🇧: Why would he have to?

🇺🇸: How else would anybody know?

🇬🇧: ...Darling. **It was 1954.**

Old Man Willow appeared in Tolkien's poem *The Adventures of Tom Bombadil* in 1934, before the publication of *The Fellowship of the Ring* in 1954. The Bombadil material therefore has its own imaginative history, not merely the function of delaying Frodo on his journey. [3]

The dates matter because the relevant fairy traditions were not just remote fragments of an ancient religious past. They circulated through oral accounts, family warnings, local custom, folklore collecting, literature and the everyday reputations of particular places. Their prevalence was uneven. They were not universally believed. But they were sufficiently present that a reader in the middle of the twentieth century **might not have needed an explanatory lecture** before recognising a story about an inhabited, dangerous place and an unfortunate visitor.

This is a question of cultural reception, not a demand that every reader in 1954 possessed the same knowledge. A literary text can rely on conventions shared by some of its audience without announcing that it is doing so. The text can also continue to work when those conventions are unfamiliar—although the unfamiliar reader may diagnose a different problem.

The modern question *Why is Tolkien's tree evil?* may sometimes be less a discovery of inconsistency than a symptom of changed interpretive context. A reader familiar with warnings about fairy trees may instead ask, *Why did the hobbits sit down there?*

It would be useful to test this further against documented reception of the Old Forest in the 1950s; the existence of widespread traditions alone cannot prove how particular readers interpreted Tolkien. But the possibility is substantial enough that we should stop pretending the only available framework is a contemporary fantasy bestiary.

## 🌳 3. There Is A Spirit In That Tree

The inhabiting-spirit interpretation is not a fanciful rescue attempt invented for the defence.

In material preserved by Christopher Tolkien in *The Return of the Shadow*, an early version of the Old Forest episode describes a **“grey thirsty earth-bound spirit”** imprisoned in the willow. The tree's own life and decay, and the spirit's influence through earth and water, become entangled. This is evidence of Tolkien's thinking during composition; it does not require us to pretend that every draft formulation is a definitive statement of the final published ontology. [4]

The distinction is nevertheless consequential. There is a tree, and there is a spirit, and over time the relationship between them becomes difficult to disentangle. The spirit is not simply wearing a willow costume. The willow's trunk, roots, hollow, shade, location and surrounding environment furnish the means by which it acts.

In the published *Fellowship*, the forest itself appears capable of frustrating the travellers' movements. The route seems to draw them toward the Withywindle. At the riverbank, the willow offers an inviting place to stop; sleep overtakes the hobbits; the tree physically confines Merry and Pippin. These are connected stages in an encounter with an inhabited environment, not a random monster attack dropped into neutral scenery. [5]

Is Old Man Willow an Ent? A Huorn? A nature spirit? A fairy? Tolkien's wider tree-beings make the taxonomic question interesting, and Verlyn Flieger has examined how Tolkien develops the idea of enspirited trees. [6] But a folkloric reading does not depend on settling a species label. The claim is that **his behaviour and his mode of inhabitation are recognisable alongside traditions of dangerous, place-bound supernatural beings**.

## 🌿 4. Why A Willow? Because He Acts Like One

This is the centre of the argument, not an appendix of vaguely relevant symbolism.

**Tolkien's willow acts through a set of experiences and relationships that overlap strikingly with the real willow's medicinal history, physical ecology and recorded cultural associations.** Some correspondences are stronger than others. None requires the claim that willow bark literally contains a hobbit tranquilliser. The point is how a writer can make a supernatural encounter feel *of its body*.

### 💊 4.1 Medicine, relief and the surrender of vigilance

Willow bark has a documented medicinal history. Edward Stone reported on powdered white willow bark as a treatment for ague in a communication published by the Royal Society in 1763. Willow contains salicin and related salicylates; salicin is metabolised through pathways yielding salicylic acid, while aspirin is the distinct compound acetylsalicylic acid. Later histories of salicylate medicine trace the route from plant remedies to chemical pharmaceuticals. [7][8]

The willow's association with relief from bodily distress therefore has a real historical basis. Folk uses and scientific pharmacology should not be conflated, and salicylates are **not** a plausible explanation for magical sleep. Yet the *form* of the enchantment matters. The hobbits are not first met by an openly charging beast. They become weary. The riverside offers repose. They accept the invitation of shade and shelter. Their bodies relinquish alertness.

The medicinal correspondence is not *willow bark causes sleep*. It is more subtle: **a tree associated with alleviating bodily discomfort becomes the place where bodily relief turns into vulnerability**.

🇺🇸: THE TREE DRUGGED THEM.

🇬🇧: Well, willow does have a medicinal history.

🇺🇸: THAT IS NOT A DEFENCE.

🇬🇧: I didn't say he was prescribing responsibly.

### 🌿 4.2 Protection, shelter and whose interests count

Recorded British and Irish willow traditions include protective, fortunate and domestic uses of willow branches or rods. University College Cork's account of grey willow gathers examples involving journeys, dairy practices and seasonal custom; these need to be treated as locally documented traditions, not proof of a single timeless Celtic willow theology. [9]

Old Man Willow also offers shelter—or what the hobbits experience as shelter. His body becomes the resting place and then the enclosure. The same trunk that appears to support and protect a tired traveller becomes a trap.

But perhaps the protection has not been inverted. Perhaps the problem is **the direction in which it operates**.

A guardian is not obliged to guard everybody equally. A protective boundary that shelters one community may exclude another. If Old Man Willow is functioning as a territorial guardian, the forest may be the beneficiary of his protection, while the hobbits are the disturbance from which it is being protected.

The hobbits are endangered; that is not in dispute. But treating danger to visitors as proof of an essentially wicked guardian assumes that visitors are the measure of the place. **Protective** and **dangerous** may describe the very same act from opposite sides of a boundary.

### 🌊 4.3 Water, roots and the material landscape

Willows grow readily in wet places and along waterways. Old Man Willow stands beside the Withywindle; his power is narrated through the environment of river, roots, earth and woodland. This is ecological placement as well as atmosphere. [5][9]

The early manuscript's emphasis on an earth-bound spirit drawing influence through its physical setting makes this more interesting still. Agency is not sealed inside a discrete supernatural personality. It is distributed through a living organism and its relationships with place.

The riverbank is not scenery. It is part of the apparatus.

### 🪦 4.4 Mourning, burial and the threshold

The weeping willow became a recognisable emblem of grief and remembrance in Georgian and Victorian visual culture, including funerary and memorial imagery. That is not the same claim as saying every cemetery contained living willows, or that ancient Druids regarded willow trees as gates to the dead. Those stronger claims require their own evidence. [10]

Nevertheless, a reader may encounter a willow already resonant with mourning and death. The inviting hollow of Old Man Willow then becomes an almost grotesque threshold: a place of apparent rest which might become a place of permanent disappearance.

The folkloric comparison with supernatural captivity strengthens that reading, though it should remain a comparison rather than a demonstrated source for Tolkien's particular scene.

### 🧿 4.5 Correspondences in action

| Recorded property or association | Action in the Old Forest | Interpretive consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Willow bark used in pain and fever remedies | The hobbits experience relief, drowsiness and surrender of vigilance | Healing/rest become a language of vulnerability; **not** literal salicylate sedation |
| Protective or fortunate willow customs | The tree offers shelter but closes around visitors | Protection may serve the forest, not its guests |
| Riparian ecology | The encounter centres on the Withywindle | The body and environment jointly shape action |
| Willow in mourning imagery | The inviting trunk threatens to become a place of death | Refuge shades into burial and supernatural captivity |
| Traditions of inhabited or significant trees | A spirit acts through a particular tree | The organism is not an interchangeable monster costume |
| Fairy-place taboos and territoriality | Outsiders enter a woodland with an antagonistic history | The encounter is legible as an intrusion into an inhabited world |

These are **literary correspondences**, with different evidentiary strengths—not a checklist of proven intentions. Their value lies in their pattern. The tree's body is not incidental to the attack. Its properties and associations help make the attack intelligible.

**Old Man Willow has not forgotten how to be a willow.**

## 🧾 4.6 Exhibit A: How the willow conducts his business

The correspondences become clearer when the encounter is read **in order**, rather than as a catalogue of willow symbols pasted onto an evil-tree episode.

**First, the landscape brings the hobbits down to the water.** The forest's paths and apparent preferences frustrate the travellers' chosen direction. The Withywindle is not just an attractive backdrop for the next monster: it is the wetland setting in which a willow's roots and bodily presence make ecological sense. The travellers are gradually moved from their own navigational system into the tree's environment.

**Second, the tree makes itself comfortable.** Shade, grass, a bank, the sounds of water, the invitation to stop: these are not incidental comforts. The enchantment reaches the hobbits through bodily need. They are tired. Rest seems reasonable. This is where the willow's association with medicinal relief becomes literarily interesting. Not because salicin is a sedative—it isn't—but because **relief is the entry point**.

**Third, sleep reduces their capacity to respond.** In the medicinal imagination, rest may be part of recovery. In the fairy-story encounter, the same surrender of vigilance exposes the travellers to a being whose purposes they have not understood. What heals in one relationship can incapacitate in another. That is not a contradiction in the willow's powers; it is a change of recipient, intention and context.

**Fourth, the body that offered support becomes enclosure.** A trunk is ordinarily something against which a person may lean. Here the hollow and living wood become the means of capture. The tree does not exchange its willow body for generic monster equipment. **It makes a willow's own architecture do the work.**

**Fifth, the place that appeared to shelter visitors becomes a defended interior.** The reader who sees only a hospitable tree suddenly becoming hostile experiences betrayal. The reader who recognises a territorial, inhabited fairy place can also see a boundary being enforced. The same body offers rest to one party and serves as a means of controlling intrusion for another.

**Sixth, Tom speaks to the inhabitant, not to the scenery.** The resolution confirms that the event has involved an agent whose conduct can be addressed. There is no need to burn the tree, excavate its roots, or pretend that it was a malfunctioning plant.

This is the sharper claim: **the willow's medicinal, protective, riparian and supernatural correspondences are enacted in the mechanics of the scene.** They are not merely visual decorations in the background. The evidence supports a reading of purposeful literary patterning; it does not prove Tolkien consciously selected every individual correspondence.

🇺🇸: HE'S STILL EATING THEM.

The Old Forest's relationship with Buckland is not a blank slate. Tolkien describes a boundary contested between hobbits and trees, including the cutting and burning of woodland after trees encroached toward the hedge. The hobbits have a story about a threatening forest; the forest might reasonably have a story about threatening hobbits. [5]

It would be unfair to assign personal responsibility for all earlier tree burning to Frodo, Sam, Merry and Pippin. Their immediate innocence matters. So does the forest's history. Those facts can coexist without cancelling each other.

Katrine L. A. Hjulstad's *The Tale of the Old Forest* is useful precisely because it takes the forest's history, perspective and relation to Faërie seriously. The apparently malevolent woodland need not be read exclusively through the visitors' assumptions about what a tree ought to do. [11]

The hobbits think they are moving through landscape. The woodland may experience them as members of a species—or neighbouring community—with a record of violence against its inhabitants. The same event is processed through different histories.

This is where a simple moral binary becomes unhelpful. A legitimate grievance does not license unlimited retaliation against strangers. But a dangerous response does not become historically inexplicable merely because the person experiencing it did not initiate the conflict.

**The forest has memory. The visitors have an itinerary. These are not equivalent descriptions of the situation.**

---

### 🪵 5.1 The hedge is not the whole history

The Buckland boundary is a particularly good example of two communities describing the same line differently. From the hobbits' perspective, a hedge is a reasonable defensive measure against trees that move too close. From the forest's perspective, the hedge marks the edge of a human settlement whose inhabitants have cut and burned trees when the boundary became contested. The published narrative gives us the hobbits' account of that conflict; it does not give us a deposition from the forest.

That absence matters. We should not manufacture Old Man Willow's personal recollections of particular burnings. But nor should we quietly elevate the hobbits' account into the only available moral history. **The story makes the forest's hostility intelligible before the willow ever closes his trunk.** The question of who began the conflict may be less simple than the inhabitants of Buckland would like.

And the four travellers arrive in the wake of it. They do not have to be personally guilty of felling a single tree for the forest to perceive them through a longer history of human intrusion. That is a description of the informational asymmetry, not a verdict of collective guilt.

### 🧚 5.2 Fairy-place cases the defence should recognise

The comparative evidence is not one universal fairy code. It is a set of **recurring narrative relationships** documented in particular places and periods:

- **A marked or inhabited place:** Irish ringforts and fairy-associated trees appear in recorded accounts as places whose significance is not visible to every passer-by. Dowd's archaeological-folkloric research examines precisely this overlap between landscape features, supernatural attribution and human conduct. [1]
- **A warning against interference:** The UCD discussion of sacred trees draws on recorded traditions in which damage to particular trees is followed by reported illness or misfortune. The force of the account lies in the *relationship* between a person, a tree and the community's knowledge about it—not in a botanist's ability to identify an invisible resident. [2]
- **An appropriate courtesy or avoidance:** Offerings are attested in some Irish fairy-place customs, but simple non-interference is itself a recognised form of conduct. The practical instruction can be as unceremonious as leaving a particular place alone. [1][2]
- **A consequence experienced as disproportionate:** Folk narratives frequently allow the supernatural party to retaliate without submitting to the visitor's account of innocence. That is one reason the warnings exist. The comparison explains the *story logic*; it does not make every harm morally admirable.

A later draft could use named, dated narratives from the National Folklore Collection for closer case comparison. We should not invent an exact historical tale of a willow swallowing a traveller simply because it would make excellent evidence for the defence.

## 🪞 6. Courting The Wild Twin — Not The Evil Twin

And now I would like to bring in Martin Shaw, because reducing this to a Jungian shadow reading feels altogether too tidy.

Jung's shadow can be useful for discussing rejected or unacknowledged aspects of the self. But **Shaw's *Courting the Wild Twin*** approaches the undomesticated counterpart through myth, story, estrangement and relationship. It is rawer than a neat diagram of psychological integration. The wild twin is not simply the socially unacceptable part waiting to be cured and brought back into polite company. [12]

That matters when the figure under discussion is a dangerous woodland being.

Treebeard and Old Man Willow offer a suggestive comparison. Treebeard is ancient and formidable, but he can explain himself to hobbits. He has a language of memory, community, deliberation and political action that visitors can recognise. Old Man Willow is ancient and formidable too, but his agency is embedded in place, bodily capture and refusal. He does not stop to make himself administratively legible.

🇺🇸: Treebeard is a good tree.

🇬🇧: Treebeard is a tree you can negotiate with.

🇺🇸: Exactly.

🇬🇧: Those aren't synonyms.

🇺🇸: Old Man Willow doesn't want to talk.

🌳: CORRECT.

🇬🇧: Well, at least we've established his preferences.

This is not a claim that Tolkien intentionally wrote Treebeard and Old Man Willow as a matched psychological pair, nor that Shaw—writing much later—influenced Tolkien. It is a way of asking what modern readers do to folklore when they insist on making every dangerous being either a villain to eliminate or a wounded aspect of themselves to rehabilitate.

**What if the wild twin is allowed to remain wild?**

What if the point of meeting an undomesticated other is not to make it nice, assimilate it, or announce that its refusal to cooperate proves it is evil? What if it is a being with its own needs, territory, history and capacities, whose relationship with us requires negotiation of boundaries rather than erasure of difference?

The wild twin also sharpens the willow correspondence. The cultivated, helpful willow and the dangerous, enchanted willow need not possess opposite properties. They may express the *same* embodied capacities within different relationships. Rest can be healing or disabling. Shelter can protect or confine. Guardianship can save one inhabitant and endanger another.

**The wild twin is not the evil twin.**

And, to be fair, the wild twin may be absolutely sick of your shit.

---

### 🪞 6.1 What Shaw's wild twin actually adds

Shaw's usefulness is not simply that his language sounds earthier than Jung's. The *wild twin* shifts the direction of interpretation. A psychological shadow reading can invite the reader to ask which unacceptable part of **me** the frightening figure represents. A wild-twin reading asks what it would mean to meet a figure who has not agreed to become the socially acceptable version of **us**. The encounter is a relationship, not merely a diagnostic exercise.

That difference is vital for a story in which the other party has an actual territory and an actual body. Old Man Willow is not obliged to function as a therapeutic symbol for the hobbits. He need not be a failed Treebeard, a broken personality or a moral caution sign. He can be encountered as a being whose ways of inhabiting the world are not organised around the travellers' comfort.

There is a danger of romanticising the wild as well. The wild twin is not an automatic certificate of virtue, and dangerous conduct remains dangerous. But **the demand that the wild become pleasant before we will recognise it as real is itself a form of domestication**. That is the point of keeping Shaw's folkloric rawness in the node.

In that sense Treebeard and Old Man Willow are not simply *good tree* and *bad tree*. They show how much more readily visitors recognise agency when it speaks in a manner they understand. Treebeard can explain himself. Old Man Willow communicates through shade, sleep, roots, obstruction and refusal. Tom can address both kinds of being; the hobbits cannot.

We should not put invented quotations into Shaw's mouth: the comparison is our reading of his framework, and page-specific engagement with *Courting the Wild Twin* should be checked against a copy of the book.

Tom's arrival matters because he does not behave as if the encounter is an incomprehensible anomaly. He recognises the agent involved. He knows how to address him. He secures the hobbits' release, and Old Man Willow remains standing. [5]

It would overstate the scene to call this a friendly negotiation. Tom commands; his speech and song have authority. Yet that is precisely the point: **recognition of another being's agency does not mean consenting to its conduct**. Tom can know the tree, understand the territory and still insist that the captives be released.

The local supernatural environment is not a moral free-for-all. Nor does every conflict require the destruction of whichever inhabitant frightened the visitors. There are relationships, authorities, limits and interventions available to someone who knows where they are.

This is not merely an argument for being nicer to trees. It is an argument for learning how to recognise and address the agency of beings whose existence does not revolve around us.

### 🌼 7.1 The local authority already knows the defendant

Tom's role is more interesting than that of a convenient rescuer. He is not discovering an inexplicable tree monster for the first time. In the chapters that follow, he knows the forest, tells its history, and treats its inhabitants as beings with whom one can have dealings. His command to Old Man Willow is firm, but its very intelligibility matters: the tree can be **addressed**, can **respond**, and can be **left in place** once the immediate danger is resolved. [5]

That is the difference between a fairy-related incident and a war of extermination. Tom does not tell the hobbits that Old Man Willow is harmless. He does not suggest they should return to sit under him tomorrow. He enforces a boundary around what the tree may do to these particular travellers, then allows the woodland to remain woodland.

The defence is not claiming that Tom delivers a judicial acquittal in Tolkien's text. It is observing that **the story's resolution does not require a verdict that the supernatural resident has no right to exist**.

## 🫀 8. The Body In The Loop

Why does this belong in Embodied Information Ecology rather than a folder of Tolkien criticism?

Because the encounter illustrates how an agent's body, environment, history and relationships shape what its behaviour means—and how observers can misunderstand one another when they do not share those contexts.

**Embodiment:** the spirit's powers are enacted through a willow, not through an interchangeable magical avatar. Trunk, shade, hollow, roots, riverbank and the sensation of rest all participate.

**Situated information:** the hobbits receive information about an apparently hospitable place to stop. The forest may receive information about intruders from a hostile neighbouring community. Tom receives information about an inhabitant with whom he already has a relationship. They are not interpreting the same signals against the same background.

**Historical feedback:** enclosure, tree-felling, burning, encroachment and defensive responses may produce escalating mutual hostility. Neither side's present conduct can be fully understood without the preceding loop.

**Boundary conditions:** what appears to a traveller as open countryside may be somebody else's home, body or defended territory. An absence of visible human ownership is not an absence of relations.

**Protective systems:** the same response can protect one system and harm another. Its protective *function* does not settle its moral *proportionality*.

**Cultural memory:** the text a reader encounters in 2026 is not materially different from the text a reader encountered in 1954. The available interpretive ecology may be. A fairy-story convention familiar to one audience can look like an inexplicable fantasy inconsistency to another.

**The wild twin:** the impulse to domesticate every difficult other into familiar psychological or moral categories may destroy the very information that the encounter is offering.

Put simply:

> **Before declaring another being's behaviour inexplicable, examine its body, environment, history, relationships, and the assumptions you have brought to the encounter.**

And before diagnosing a hostile system, establish whether you are the thing it believes it is protecting itself against.

This is not a universal vindication of hostile systems. It is a demand for a better model before acting.

☘️: Quite.

🇺🇸: And their people burned the forest?

🌳: THANK YOU.

🇺🇸: BUT HE TRIED TO EAT THEM.

🇬🇧: Yes, cousin. We have all understood that part since chapter six.

🇺🇸: THEN WHAT ARE WE SUPPOSED TO LEARN?

🇬🇧: **DON'T BOTHER THE FAIRIES.**

🌳: AND STOP BURNING MY RELATIVES.

🌼: There we are. Everybody's learning.

The willow is dangerous. The hobbits need rescuing. Tolkien can describe malice without requiring the reader to forget the forest's injuries, the tree's inhabiting spirit, the body's particular powers, or the older narrative traditions in which supernatural neighbours are not obliged to be safe.

We do not have to pretend Old Man Willow is secretly nice. We do not have to explain him away as a pathology. **And yes, the defence is asking for an acquittal on the charge of being an inherently wicked tree.** The evidence of dangerous conduct is not evidence that his entire nature is evil.

We can recognise that a guardian may be frightening, that a place may be inhabited, and that *what is good for the forest* may not be especially good for hobbits.

**Old Man Willow has not forgotten how to be a willow. The hobbits have forgotten that the willow might have opinions about them.**

**Finding on the principal charge:** the defence submits that *inherently evil tree* has not been established. The conduct is dangerous; the context is inhabited, territorial and historically contested; the method is peculiarly willow-like; and the fairy-story grammar is recognisable without a sign explaining the rules at the forest gate.

The prosecution has established that the defendant attempted to digest two hobbits. It has not established that the defendant was obliged to welcome them into his home.

🇺🇸: HE STILL TRIED TO EAT MERRY.

☘️: And Tom stopped him. Nobody is proposing a second visit.

🌳: I REQUEST THAT THE RECORD REFLECT MY EXEMPLARY RESTRAINT SINCE THE INCIDENT.

🌼: It will reflect that you let them out when told.

**Acquitted of being an inexplicably wicked tree. Advised against further hobbit consumption.**

*The defence rests. Preferably somewhere other than underneath the defendant.*

- [1] Marion Dowd, [“Bewitched by an Elf Dart: Fairy Archaeology, Folk Magic and Traditional Medicine in Ireland”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-archaeological-journal/article/bewitched-by-an-elf-dart-fairy-archaeology-folk-magic-and-traditional-medicine-in-ireland/7EF2D9BD63A34CAA405A42E120C4D421), *Cambridge Archaeological Journal*. Accounts and folklore-collection references concerning significant trees and the consequences of interference.

**Literary interpretation and the wild twin**

- [6] Verlyn Flieger, [“How Trees Behave—Or Do They?”](https://dc.swosu.edu/mythlore/vol32/iss1/3/), *Mythlore*. On Tolkien's enspirited trees and their literary development.
- [11] Katrine L. A. Hjulstad, [“The Tale of the Old Forest”](https://scholar.valpo.edu/journaloftolkienresearch/vol10/iss2/7/), *Journal of Tolkien Research*. On the forest's history, perspective and relationship to Faërie.
- [12] Martin Shaw, *Courting the Wild Twin* (2020). A **modern interpretive companion**, not a proposed source of Tolkien's influence. Direct quotation and page-specific claims should be checked against the book before publication.

**Evidence boundaries:** Documented willow medicinal uses do not establish a sedative mechanism; funerary willow imagery does not establish universal ancient Celtic willow theology; fairy offerings in historical communities do not establish a canonical Tolkienian requirement; cultural familiarity in 1954 does not prove every reader's interpretation; an early draft is not automatically final canon. These distinctions refine the reading without extinguishing the joke.
