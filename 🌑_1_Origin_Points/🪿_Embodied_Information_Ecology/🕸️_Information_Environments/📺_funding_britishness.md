📺 Funding Britishness

* Working question:
    * Britain clearly retains a significant desire for a recognisable British cultural settlement.
    * The policy question should not be reduced to:
        * “How do we save the BBC?”
        * “How do we preserve the TV licence?”
    * Instead:
        * What parts of British cultural production do we actually value?
        * What institutions and conditions allow them to exist?
        * How should they be funded in the 21st century?
        * How can Britain monetise international demand for British culture without allowing the mechanisms of monetisation to flatten the thing people actually want?

⸻

📺 The TV licence problem

* The TV licence remains politically and emotionally contentious.
    * Cost-of-living pressures make a compulsory household charge particularly visible.
    * There is an unresolved distinction between:
        * believing public-service broadcasting is valuable;
        * believing the existing licence-fee architecture is the best way of financing it.
    * Those propositions do not have to stand or fall together.
* Streaming makes the existing settlement feel increasingly strange.
    * The consumer can theoretically escape the universal bundle.
    * In practice, they may instead acquire multiple recurring private subscriptions.
    * Greater choice can therefore mean greater fragmentation and more recurring liabilities.
    * “Shangri-La as a service”:
        * unprecedented access;
        * accompanied by an expanding collection of direct debits.
* Personal consumer dilemma as useful miniature:
    * wanting to watch Slow Horses;
    * not wanting to acquire/pay for services unnecessarily;
    * discovering that an intensely British cultural product is controlled internationally through an American platform.
    * The competing loyalties are revealing:
        * support British cultural production;
        * dislike a particular domestic funding mechanism;
        * enjoy American culture;
        * resist unnecessary dependence on American cultural infrastructure.
    * Much of this political instinct operates through refusal:
        * “I am not paying X for that.”
        * “I am not giving that institution/company/person my money.”
    * Consumer choice is therefore also cultural and political choice.

⸻

🏗️ Stop treating “British culture” as synonymous with the BBC

* The BBC is one component of a much larger British cultural-production ecosystem.
* Relevant institutions/mechanisms include:
    * BBC;
    * BFI;
    * Channel 4;
    * independent production companies;
    * film and television production;
    * theatre;
    * music;
    * publishing;
    * regional cultural institutions;
    * public investment;
    * tax incentives;
    * licensing;
    * international co-production;
    * archives and heritage.
* British cultural policy could start from desired capabilities, rather than defending individual institutions as ends in themselves.
* Questions:
    * What do we want Britain to remain capable of producing?
    * What cultural infrastructure is required?
    * Which parts require public support?
    * Which parts can generate commercial returns?
    * How should successful publicly supported work recycle value into future cultural production?

⸻

🎞️ You can tell when something is culturally specific

* BFI-associated film provides an intuitive example:
    * something can immediately feel different to a British viewer.
    * That feeling can be decomposed into actual production/storytelling characteristics:
        * pacing;
        * narrative structure;
        * greater tolerance for ambiguity;
        * greater emphasis on nuance;
        * different assumptions about audience knowledge;
        * different treatment of class;
        * different humour;
        * regional and linguistic specificity.
* This is not:
    * “British culture good; American culture bad.”
* American mass-market cultural production is shaped partly by the enormous scale of the American domestic audience.
    * America is a very large country.
    * Products designed for very large heterogeneous audiences face different commercial pressures.
* Smaller cultural markets can therefore possess comparative advantages in:
    * specificity;
    * niche production;
    * regionality;
    * eccentricity;
    * linguistic variation;
    * stories that do not need to appeal to everyone.

⸻

🇺🇸 America is both enormous opportunity and structural challenge

* Britain loves American culture.
    * American language has become normalised in some British contexts.
    * American music, television, film and internet culture have enormous influence.
* The concern is not:
    * “stop American culture.”
* It is:
    * cultural exchange should not require cultural convergence.
* Americans also demonstrably like British cultural products.
    * Britishness itself can be part of the attraction.
    * The product often travels because it is different.
* Useful analogy:
    * Americans can enjoy the Clash without needing to own, domesticate or redesign the cultural conditions that produced the Clash.
    * British people similarly enjoy American cultural forms without needing them to become British.
* Cultural exchange allows:
    * appreciation;
    * influence;
    * experimentation;
    * learning how other people live;
    * enjoying someone else’s “cool stuff.”
* It does not inherently require:
    * ownership;
    * interference;
    * homogenisation.
* Core proposition:
    * America already has America.
    * There is little export advantage in Britain producing a weaker approximation of American mass culture.
* Americans buying British culture frequently want:
    * British actors;
    * British accents;
    * British humour;
    * British class dynamics;
    * British institutions;
    * British regional cultures;
    * British weirdness.
* Therefore:
    * sell the product without optimising away the factory that makes it strange.

⸻

💷 Cultural sovereignty without protectionism

* Useful distinction:
    * enjoying one another’s culture is not the same proposition as saying ownership and distribution infrastructure do not matter.
* Cultural sovereignty does not have to mean:
    * hostility to foreign culture;
    * isolationism;
    * cultural purity;
    * Little Englandism.
* It can instead mean retaining sufficient domestic capacity to:
    * finance;
    * commission;
    * make;
    * own;
    * archive;
    * distribute;
    * experiment;
    * train;
    * discover talent.
* Strategic question:
    * How does Britain export British distinctiveness without selling away the conditions that produce it?

⸻

💸 Follow the international money

* A recurring problem in cultural debates is funding.
    * representation costs money;
    * experimentation costs money;
    * regional production costs money;
    * safeguarding costs money;
    * maintaining archives and infrastructure costs money.
* But Britain also produces cultural assets with significant international value.
* Rather than treating international demand as incidental:
    * cultural exports could be considered explicitly within cultural funding strategy.
* Existing mechanisms already include licensing.
    * A programme can be licensed for foreign broadcast/distribution under particular terms.
    * Rights have territorial, temporal and platform dimensions.
* Possible wider toolkit to examine:
    * international licensing;
    * co-production;
    * format sales;
    * rights retention;
    * equity participation;
    * production incentives;
    * public investment;
    * international distribution;
    * limited exclusivity windows;
    * archive monetisation;
    * subscription products;
    * levies;
    * cultural export support.
* Potential principle:
    * public money can absorb some early-stage risk where markets underprovide experimentation;
    * successful internationally valuable assets should, where possible, return some value to the ecosystem that enabled their creation.
* Question:
    * If publicly supported British culture creates substantial global commercial value, how much of that value currently returns to British cultural production?

🏴 Distinctiveness is an export asset

* Britain contains multiple cultural traditions rather than one homogeneous “British” voice.
* Potential sources of internationally valuable specificity include:
    * Black British culture;
    * British Asian cultures;
    * queer British culture;
    * Welsh culture;
    * Scottish culture;
    * Northern Irish culture;
    * regional English cultures;
    * class cultures;
    * dialects and accents;
    * immigrant and diaspora cultures;
    * countercultures.
* These should not merely be treated as:
    * representation obligations;
    * diversity targets;
    * worthy things that cost money.
* They can also be:
    * sources of artistic innovation;
    * internationally distinctive cultural products;
    * soft-power assets;
    * commercially valuable precisely because they cannot be replicated elsewhere.
* Black British culture is not interchangeable with African-American culture.
    * It has its own history.
    * Its own relationship with Britain, empire, migration, class and region.
    * Its own language and cultural forms.
* That specificity is valuable rather than inconvenient.

🐭 Rastamouse question

* Rastamouse provides a useful research hook around:
    * Jamaican Patois;
    * Black British children’s culture;
    * institutional anxiety about language;
    * assumptions about what children can understand;
    * who is permitted to sound “normal” on British television.
* 🔎 Need to verify the precise history and reasons for the programme ending before making a causal claim that it was removed because of anxiety about Jamaican Patois.
* Broader question remains:
    * why should British children’s television not expose children to language varieties genuinely present in British life?
* Commercial inversion:
    * the feature considered too specific domestically may be exactly what makes a programme interesting internationally.

🌍 Representation versus plurality of authorship

* “More representation” can still leave the underlying cultural gatekeeping structure intact.
* Representation can mean:
    * placing a wider range of people inside an existing commissioning system.
* A stronger question is plurality of authorship:
    * Who gets to decide what stories are made?
    * Who determines what is “professional”?
    * Who determines what audiences will understand?
    * Who controls commissioning?
    * Who controls budgets?
    * Who owns resulting IP?
    * Who can take artistic risks?
    * Who is permitted to fail and try again?
    * Which accents/languages/styles are considered commercially viable?
    * Which communities are allowed to depict themselves rather than being interpreted by someone else?
* Cultural survival may require a genuine meritocracy of creative opportunity rather than a narrow inherited gatekeeping network.

⸻

🚪 Post-Savile cultural institutions

* British cultural policy cannot simply romanticise historic broadcasting institutions.
* Jimmy Savile is part of the history of British broadcasting.
    * His cultural power was intertwined with institutional access and audience power.
    * He appears to have been highly interested in control.
    * Music, celebrity and access to audiences formed part of the environment through which he accumulated influence.
* This is not solely a BBC problem.
    * Hollywood provides parallel examples, including Harvey Weinstein.
    * Show-business structures can allow people with commercial/cultural power to exploit:
        * workers;
        * aspiring performers;
        * vulnerable people;
        * fans.
* Post-Savile reform therefore cannot mean merely:
    * better vetting;
    * identifying individual “bad people.”
* Institutional questions include:
    * How does someone accumulate cultural gatekeeping power?
    * How dependent do other people’s careers become upon them?
    * How contestable is their authority?
    * What happens when an institution believes somebody is commercially indispensable?
    * Can junior/freelance workers safely challenge powerful people?
    * Who hears complaints?
    * Who can remove access?
    * Does everybody’s next job depend upon the same small network?
* A post-Savile cultural settlement should ask both:
    * Who gets access to cultural power?
    * How contestable is cultural power once somebody has it?
* Relevant mechanisms to examine:
    * safeguarding;
    * whistleblowing;
    * freelance protections;
    * transparent commissioning;
    * employment structures;
    * decentralised cultural power;
    * regional production;
    * routes for independent creators;
    * ownership;
    * accountability.

🏴‍☠️ Piracy, VPNs and broken distribution

* People highly interested in film/television are often:
    * comfortable with VPNs;
    * knowledgeable about regional catalogues;
    * aware of torrenting/piracy;
    * frustrated by territorial licensing.
* Even purchasing many subscriptions does not necessarily provide lawful access to everything someone wants to watch.
* Problems include:
    * territorial licensing;
    * rights windows;
    * fragmented catalogues;
    * inaccessible archives;
    * platform exclusivity;
    * cost of multiple subscriptions.
* Piracy cannot be reduced entirely to:
    * people refusing to pay.
* Some piracy represents free-riding.
* But some also represents market information:
    * people are actively seeking a cultural product;
    * they may be willing to pay;
    * the rights/distribution system gives them no reasonable lawful way to do so.
* Example question:
    * if somebody torrents an old British documentary because there is literally nowhere lawful to pay to watch it, what does that tell policymakers?
* Possible answer:
    * there is revealed demand for a product that the rights architecture has made impossible to sell.
* The internet reduced technological distribution barriers.
    * Territorial rights structures can recreate geographical scarcity contractually.
* 🔎 Research:
    * scale of inaccessible British archive material;
    * international demand;
    * archive rights complexity;
    * potential revenue lost through non-availability.

📢 Things worth preserving from public-service broadcasting

* The objective should not be to discard the features that worked simply because the financing architecture needs reconsideration.
* Examples to examine:
    * absence/reduction of advertising interruptions;
    * universal access;
    * public-interest programming;
    * children’s programming;
    * educational content;
    * news;
    * minority-interest programming;
    * regional production;
    * cultural archives;
    * willingness to commission work that does not immediately maximise commercial return.
* Separate:
    * the desired public-service outcome
    * from
    * the institution or tax/fee mechanism historically used to deliver it.

🧮 A 21st-century cultural industrial strategy

* Potential reframing:
    * British cultural policy is industrial policy as well as arts policy.
* The country possesses:
    * talent;
    * recognised cultural brands;
    * language advantage;
    * production expertise;
    * archives;
    * institutions;
    * international audiences;
    * soft power.
* Funding debate should therefore include:
    * domestic cultural value;
    * economic value;
    * export value;
    * soft-power value;
    * skills and employment;
    * regional development;
    * ownership of IP;
    * long-term cultural capacity.
* Core strategic objective:
    * remain open enough to absorb other cultures while retaining enough domestic capacity to continue producing something recognisably our own.
* This is compatible with enthusiastic cultural exchange.
* Indeed, international cultural exchange may work better when participating cultures remain distinctive.

🇺🇸 Note for American audiences

* Britain does not need America to stop liking or buying British culture.
* Quite the reverse:
    * please buy it.
* But there is a useful distinction between:
    * consuming another country’s cultural production;
    * financing it;
    * distributing it;
    * owning it;
    * reshaping it for your own market.
* American audiences themselves often value British work precisely because it offers something unavailable in American mass culture.
* Same principle works in reverse:
    * Britain imports American cultural products because they provide things British culture does not.
* Counterculture often travels particularly well.
    * Britain imports substantial American:
        * Black culture;
        * queer culture;
        * feminist culture;
        * music;
        * film;
        * political/countercultural ideas.
    * Americans similarly consume distinctive British counterculture.
* Irony worth exploring:
    * some of the American cultural influence Europeans particularly value comes from precisely the Black, queer, feminist and countercultural production attacked domestically as “woke.”
* Cultural diversity is therefore not merely a domestic social-policy concern.
    * It contributes to international cultural attractiveness.

🧠 Working propositions

* Protect capabilities, not merely institutions.
* Public-service broadcasting and the licence fee are separate policy questions.
* Cultural exchange does not require cultural convergence.
* Cultural sovereignty does not require cultural protectionism.
* Distinctiveness can be commercially valuable.
* Representation is not identical to plurality of authorship.
* Post-Savile cultural reform must address concentrated gatekeeping power, not merely individual misconduct.
* Piracy can sometimes reveal unmet demand and distribution failure.
* Public investment should consider mechanisms for recycling international commercial success into future cultural production.
* Britain should not attempt to defeat America by becoming a smaller America.
* Americans often buy British culture because Britain remains irritatingly British.
* Sell the product without optimising away the factory that makes it strange.
