# 🌼 Friends of Tom Bombadil
**First created:** 2026-10-26 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-28  
*On cunning folk, protective magic, ecological competence, and the terrible institutional implications of a man who minds his own business.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

What *is* Tom Bombadil?

This is one of those questions Tolkien readers keep asking, partly because Tolkien has provided us with a spectacularly unhelpful man in yellow boots who sings at trees, knows more than he is inclined to explain, and handles the One Ring as though someone has handed him an interesting bit of jewellery found behind the sofa.

The usual response is to try to fit Tom into a hierarchy. Is he one of the Ainur? Something older? More powerful than Sauron? The world's most cheerfully underutilised strategic asset?

But what if this is the wrong question?

What if, instead of beginning with a hierarchy of supernatural power, we begin with the British and Irish cultural repertoire of **cunning folk**: local practitioners of healing, charms, protection, divination, counter-witchcraft and sometimes dealings with fairies or other supernatural beings? What if we understand Tom not as an unclassified military asset but as someone whose unusual competence comes from long practice, cultivated boundaries, intimate knowledge of place, and relationships he does not attempt to own?

This is **a comparative reading**, not a claim that Tolkien explicitly identified Bombadil as a historical cunning man, nor that the Ring canonically bounces off a magical ward. It asks what that cultural frame illuminates—and, especially, what happens when an observer brings the *wrong model of power* to someone else's body and behaviour.

Tom might be very difficult to classify because the thing he is good at isn't a lesser version of what Sauron does.

It may be the opposite.

---

## ✨ Key Features

- **Folkloric legibility:** Tom's songs, local knowledge, protective interventions and relations with an animated landscape resemble aspects of British and Irish cunning-folk traditions without making him a one-to-one historical specimen.
- **Maintained protection:** Warding provides an interpretive model in which resistance may be cultivated and contingent, not necessarily an immutable attribute.
- **Ecological competence:** Tom intervenes within a living landscape rather than reorganising it into a dominion.
- **Observation is not mechanism:** One successful encounter with the Ring cannot, by itself, specify the mechanism or establish performance under every condition.
- **Institutional projection:** An insecure observer may mistake *resistance to domination* for *capacity and intention to dominate*.
- **Structural incompatibility:** In this reading, converting Tom's protective competence into a rival Mordor might dismantle—or even immediately interrupt—the conditions that sustain it.

## 🧙‍♂️ 1. Strange Men We Used to Know

The cunning man is not a job title with a standardised competency framework. Cunning women and men operated in varied places and periods, with different religious beliefs, reputations and methods. Some found lost goods. Some offered remedies. Some identified suspected witchcraft or supplied countermeasures against it. Some claimed special knowledge through fairies, spirits, prayers, charms or familiar beings. Some were respected; some were distrusted; some could be both, depending on who was asking and what had gone wrong.

These people were not necessarily self-described witches, and historical authorities did not always make distinctions that map neatly onto modern categories. Their practices could be Christian, vernacular, commercial, communal, protective, accusatory or contradictory. Nor does a tradition of healing and protection mean every practitioner was a pacifist or ecological philosopher. We do not need to invent a uniform religion of woodland niceness.

We do, however, have a recognisable repertoire of **situated supernatural competence**: knowing which remedy, person, place, charm or intervention is appropriate; being called upon because other people cannot solve a problem; and acquiring an uneasy reputation for abilities that may be difficult to explain to a magistrate.

### John Walsh: Dorset, 1566

The printed examination of John Walsh describes a man questioned about magical knowledge, healing and a relationship with a fairy-like familiar. His examiners are concerned not merely with what he says he can remedy, but with where the ability comes from and whether the knowledge associated with healing might also imply capacity to harm. The surviving record is a hostile, interrogative document, not a transparent autobiography. It tells us a great deal about **what authorities wanted to know** as well as about what Walsh claimed.

### Andro Man: Aberdeen, 1598

Andro (Andrew) Man's witchcraft case includes reported dealings with the Queen of Elphame and other fairy-world figures. Here, again, supernatural knowledge is filtered through the language and coercive conditions of prosecution. A story of contact or healing can become a story about illicit power when the listener has the authority to make it one.

### John Stewart: Irvine, 1618

Accounts of John Stewart's prosecution describe a juggler and fortune-teller associated with encounters involving a fairy king. He is useful as another example of how practices and stories about fairies could enter judicial scrutiny. The underlying trial record should be checked directly before adding fine-grained claims about his exact words or the prosecution's reasoning.

**The comparison with Tom is structural, not genealogical.** A locally knowledgeable person helps with dangerous things. Other people struggle to identify the source and limits of that help. Their uncertainty can become curiosity, gratitude, suspicion—or, where authority and law permit, coercion.

## 🧚 2. When Fairy Research Was Nerd Culture

Tolkien did not write in a cultural vacuum. British and Irish fairy traditions had been recorded, reinterpreted and argued over for centuries; they were still part of serious, eccentric and popular intellectual life around his childhood and adulthood.

Robert Kirk's *The Secret Commonwealth* described a world of fairy beings and second sight; its nineteenth-century publication and Andrew Lang's editorial interest helped keep it in circulation. W. Y. Evans-Wentz's *The Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries* (1911) assembled testimony and interpretation across Celtic-speaking regions. Arthur Conan Doyle's *The Coming of the Fairies* (1922) illustrates how fairy belief could be entangled with photography, spiritualism, credulity and supposedly modern inquiry. The late Victorian and Edwardian occult revival supplied another, not identical, repertoire of ritual, esotericism and supernatural speculation.

These writers disagreed with one another. Some recorded belief, some endorsed it, some transformed it, and some helped make very poor evidential judgements. They should not be blended into a single ancient folk tradition, and **availability in Tolkien's cultural milieu is not proof that Tolkien read a particular book**.

The point is simpler: an English writer born in 1892 had access to a cultural world in which fairy contact, protective magic, extraordinary local specialists and the peculiar powers of song were familiar narrative materials.

### From cultural availability to a documented Bombadil

There are three different evidential questions here. Did the material circulate in Tolkien's cultural environment? Did Tolkien encounter a particular version? And did that encounter shape this character? The first is relatively easy to establish; the latter two require evidence and should not be quietly inferred from resemblance.

Bombadil's history gives us a better anchor than free-floating speculation. Tolkien's 1934 poem, **“The Adventures of Tom Bombadil,”** already places Tom among Goldberry, Old Man Willow and a Barrow-wight. The character did not originate as an emergency solution to the One Ring's plot mechanics. He arrived in the larger story carrying a pre-existing set of relationships, local encounters and an unruly narrative identity. Tolkien's essay **“On Fairy-stories”** also makes his own thinking about fantasy and fairy-story available for comparison; it is not a licence to claim that he consciously modelled Tom on John Walsh or any named practitioner.

The question, then, is not whether we have uncovered a secret Tolkien source. It is whether this older folkloric vocabulary helps us recognise something that a modern power-ranking vocabulary tends to miss.

### 🎶 The Pied Piper has entered the chat

The Pied Piper of Hamelin offers a useful *motif comparison*: conspicuous clothing, music that has effects other people cannot reproduce, and a figure whose apparent ridiculousness is a poor guide to his capacity. The historical Hamelin disappearance tradition predates the later elaboration involving rats and disputed payment; Robert Browning's nineteenth-century poem is another important route by which British readers encountered the story.

The Piper is not Tom, and this is not a demonstrated line of influence. The Piper's actions also make him a much darker figure. But the comparison helps establish that a brightly dressed singing man with extraordinary local authority was hardly unimaginable within the stories circulating in Britain.

Britain had, in other words, an established cultural tradition of men in strange clothes turning up, singing at things, and causing administrative difficulties.

The Piper also offers a useful *contrast*, not just a resemblance. Similar costume and extraordinary music do not imply similar ethics. In familiar retellings, the Piper's response to a broken bargain is devastating. Tom's interventions, by contrast, have to be judged individually: what does he stop, whom does he protect, and what does he leave intact? Appearance and technique alone cannot tell us whether the practitioner is a guardian, a coercer or something else. That is already an Observed Body problem.

## 🎶 3. The Man Who Sings at Things

Tom sings when travelling, when greeting his guests, when intervening in danger, and when moving through the world he knows. His singing is not a detachable special effect that activates only in combat. It belongs to his ordinary conduct.

That matters for a cunning-folk reading. Charms, prayers, repeated words, ritual acts and embodied routines are not always best understood as isolated spells with an offensive damage rating. They may be ways of organising attention, rehearsing relationships, asking for protection, marking a boundary or participating in a world believed to contain other agencies.

Some practices could also offer reassurance, agency or a means of regulating fear. This is an analytical possibility, not permission to reduce historical religious and magical beliefs to modern psychotherapy. Nor is every repeated song a documented ward.

But **what looks effortless in the encounter may be the visible outcome of a long-maintained practice**.

Tom's competence is inseparable from how he lives. His home, his marriage to Goldberry, his knowledge of the Old Forest, his routines and his songs are not background decoration to the supposedly important question of how many magical hit points he possesses.

They may be the important thing.

---

### 🌊 Goldberry is not a household accessory

If we are proposing that Tom's competence is relational, Goldberry cannot merely be the attractive evidence that he has sorted out his domestic arrangements. She has her own presence, speech and association with the river. The household is not a one-man defensive installation staffed by a decorative water-lily department.

In **“In the House of Tom Bombadil,”** Frodo asks whether the land belongs to Tom, and Goldberry describes him as *Master* while rejecting the implication that mastery means ownership of the creatures and places around him. Her answer matters enormously to this reading: the distinction between **being able to act within a place** and **possessing that place** is voiced from inside the household itself. It is not an ethical principle imported solely by the modern commentator.

Goldberry also receives and hosts the hobbits, participates in the home's rhythms and is more than a supporting character in Tom's personal resilience plan. We should resist the temptation to translate a relationship into a tool that one partner uses to generate magical power. The interpretive proposition is subtler: Tom's way of living is constituted through relationships that have meaning and agency of their own. A healthy relationship is not a battery.

This is also why the home matters. Hospitality, rest, food, speech, song and familiarity can be understood as part of an ecology of safety without declaring that Tolkien has written a technical manual for ward maintenance. The home is neither a fortress nor a command centre. It is a place where people can recover enough to continue their journey.

**Goldberry is not the reward for Tom's mastery. Her answer helps tell us what that mastery does *not* mean.**

## 🌳 4. Old Man Willow Was Wronged by the Record

To be clear, Old Man Willow really does attempt to trap and consume the hobbits. The case for his acquittal on that particular charge is not strong.

**But the record has a context.**

The Old Forest and the hobbits of Buckland have a history of antagonism, including tree-cutting and burning. The forest is not merely an empty obstacle course inhabited by malicious vegetation. It has inhabitants, memory, territorial interests and forms of agency the hobbits do not fully understand.

Tom knows more of that history than his guests do. He rescues Merry and Pippin from Willow, but he does not uproot or annihilate the tree. He orders the immediate harm to stop. Willow remains where he is.

This is not proof of a formally codified woodland legal system. It is an observable difference in *style of intervention*.

A conqueror might see an unruly tree and decide that no such tree should remain. A custodian can recognise that a being is dangerous in one encounter without concluding that its continued existence is intolerable.

Tom is **boss, but not a boss**. He has situated authority and knowledge, but he is not presented as the proprietor of every living thing around him.

His response is proportionate: **release the hobbits; stop eating people; carry on being a tree**.

> 🌳 **OLD MAN WILLOW:** The hobbits have a history of unlawful woodland clearance.
>
> 🌼 **TOM:** You still cannot eat them.
>
> 🌳 **OLD MAN WILLOW:** I wish to register an objection.
>
> 🌼 **TOM:** Noted.
>
> 🌳 **OLD MAN WILLOW:** WILL IT BE ENTERED INTO THE RECORD?
>
> 🌼 **TOM:** The record is already quite complicated.

The wider lesson is not that every participant in an ecology is benevolent. **Coexistence does not require everyone to be nice.** It requires ways of managing the fact that they aren't.

---

### 🪦 The Barrow-wight is not Old Man Willow

The second rescue is a necessary stress test for any argument that Tom always negotiates gently with every inhabitant of the landscape. In the Barrow-downs, the hobbits are in mortal danger, and Tom intervenes decisively through song and action. He drives out the wight's hold on the barrow and brings the hobbits back into the light. He does **not** simply ask everybody to respect the wight's perspective and schedule another mediation meeting.

That difference is useful. A relational approach is not an obligation to treat every danger identically. Willow remains a living tree in the forest after being compelled to release his captives. The barrow episode concerns an invasive, deadly occupation of a burial place, with a different intervention and outcome. Tom also has the hobbits discard the barrow's enclosing enchantment and distributes recovered treasure rather than claiming it as his spoils.

We needn't decide that one entity is intrinsically entitled to coexistence and the other is not. We can say that **Tom's response changes with the nature of the harm, the setting and the beings involved**. Ecological competence includes discrimination. It is not a blanket rule of woodland pacifism.

> 🌳 **OLD MAN WILLOW:** I note that the Barrow-wight received substantially less procedural accommodation.
>
> 🌼 **TOM:** You were asked to stop eating visitors. He was trying to keep them dead.
>
> 🌳 **OLD MAN WILLOW:** I would like that distinction entered into the minutes.

## 🌿 5. Ecological Competence Is Not Ecological Control

In this interpretation, Tom's competence depends on his position *inside* a living environment. He understands relationships and limits. He recognises that other beings have their own agency. He knows when intervention is required and when leaving something alone is the more intelligent act.

This is not a claim that every cunning practitioner historically held a modern ecological philosophy. It is an interpretive model built by bringing protective folk practice into conversation with Tom's depicted conduct.

The model makes a distinction that a military or administrative hierarchy can easily miss:

**Knowing how to maintain a system is not the same as having the power—or the right—to own and command every component of it.**

A person can know a river without owning the water. They can negotiate a dangerous woodland without being sovereign over its trees. They can protect themselves from an influence without being able to annihilate the source of that influence.

And the practitioner is not outside the ecology. Their body, attention, energy, relationships and health are part of the conditions under which their competence operates.

Which brings us to wards.

---

## 🧿 6. Wards: The Art of Not Letting Things In

Protective practices across Britain and Ireland have included charms, prayers, amulets, protective marks, household deposits, witch bottles, blessings and repeated ritual actions. Their meaning varied enormously. The word *ward* is a useful modern shorthand here, not a universal historical term or proof of a single technical system.

Our question is what happens if we read Tom through a repertoire in which **protection is something a person does and maintains**, not necessarily an invulnerability they possess by birth.

A cunning practitioner may need to attend continually to their own condition. Their work may involve safeguarding a home, a threshold, another person—and themselves. Their capacity to act may depend on routines, attention, ritual, relationships and the practical possibility of continuing those activities.

This is a broader understanding of self-care than the commercialised version involving a candle and an expensive bath product. It is sustained care for the conditions that allow a person to remain capable of acting in the world.

Within the reading proposed here, Tom's songs, his home, his habits and his ecological relationships might be part of that protective maintenance. **Tolkien does not explicitly say that Tom maintains mental wards.** We are testing what the folkloric analogy makes thinkable.

A boundary does not have to mean hostility to everything beyond it. Often, boundaries are what make relationships possible.

The point of a door is not to declare war on the rest of the world.

---

### What can we actually document about protective maintenance?

The historical examples are varied, and the variation matters. A **witch bottle** might be prepared and concealed as counter-magic in a household; protective marks might be cut into timber or placed near thresholds; a charm might be spoken or written; a prayer or blessing might be repeated. Some objects were installed and left in place, while some verbal and ritual practices were performed again as circumstances required. None proves that all cunning practitioners spent every morning renewing a shield around their minds.

Owen Davies's work on English cunning folk provides the practitioner context; scholarship on material protective practice, including Brian Hoggard's *Magical House Protection*, helps distinguish durable household deposits from acts that require repetition. The evidence supports **a repertoire of protection**, not one universal theory of psychic warding. A concealed shoe or marked beam is not, by itself, evidence of the protective technique we are imagining for Tom.

This distinction actually improves the argument. The move from historical practice to Tom's mental boundary is an **explicit analogy**: people have used material, verbal, religious and embodied means to mark and maintain safety; we can therefore ask what it would mean to read Tom's habits as protective work. We cannot claim the historical record has already supplied a recipe for resisting Sauron's Ring.

### Place is part of the mechanism, if the mechanism is relational

Tom's knowledge is not interchangeable with a portable offensive spell. He knows particular paths, trees, waters, histories and beings. He has a recognisable range in which his interventions occur. **A boundary in the story is also a geographical fact**, not merely a metaphor for emotional boundaries.

This adds a different vulnerability from influenza. Even a perfectly healthy practitioner may be less capable when displaced from familiar places, deprived of local allies, or required to act in conditions where their knowledge is no longer situated. A journey to Mordor would not simply be Tom performing his usual work for longer; it would be a radically different task in a radically different ecology. That does not establish that his Ring resistance would fail outside the forest. It shows why a brief encounter at home cannot settle the question.

## 💍 7. The Ring Has Encountered a Boundary

When Tom handles the One Ring, the familiar signs of its influence do not appear. He puts it on without vanishing, treats it lightly, and returns it. Frodo is understandably astonished.

The tempting conclusion is that Tom must therefore possess greater power than the Ring—and, by extension, perhaps greater power than its maker.

But that inference assumes every encounter with magical influence is a contest along a single scale.

**What if the Ring has encountered a boundary rather than a stronger opponent?**

Under the cunning-man interpretation, Tom's protection might consist in preventing an unwanted influence from establishing purchase upon him. The Ring cannot readily recruit his desires or reorganise his attention around possession and command. That does not mean he can command the Ring, destroy it or defeat Sauron.

It means that, **in the observed encounter**, the Ring does not appear to get in.

The analogy is a locked door. A person who can keep an intruder out is not thereby capable of defeating every intruder in open combat. Nor does a successful lock make the whole building indestructible.

Tolkien's own discussion of Bombadil in Letter 144 emphasises renunciation of control, enjoyment without possession and the limits of Tom's place in the struggle. That offers a meaningful point of contact with our non-domination reading. It **does not establish warding as Tolkien's explanation**.

The distinction we need is:

- **Observed:** Tom appears unaffected by the Ring in this encounter.
- **Interpreted:** A protective-folk-magic framework makes that outcome intelligible as a failure of access rather than a failed contest of strength.
- **Not established:** That Tom possesses literal wards, that they cause the Ring's behaviour, or that they would always work.

The Ring has not been defeated. It has failed to acquire this particular customer.

---

### One Ring, several distinct effects

The Ring's effects should not be compressed into a single generic substance called *power*. Invisibility is one visible phenomenon; possessiveness, temptation and long-term corruption are others. Its relation to Sauron's will and to a bearer seeking command is not reducible to the disappearance trick. Tom's brief handling shows something striking about **that encounter**, including the absence of the expected invisibility. It does not amount to an experiment in years of possession, a trial of every possible temptation, or a demonstration that he could unmake the Ring.

The distinction also guards against an easy error in our own reading. A ward against unwanted influence, if such a ward existed, need not explain every observed effect by the same mechanism. The hypothesis is productive because it changes the question, not because it closes the case.

## 🤒 8. Has Anyone Checked Whether Tom Has the Flu?

We meet Tom in a particular state, in a particular place, for a limited period. We do not conduct a longitudinal assessment of Bombadil under every conceivable set of conditions.

We do not observe him ill for a fortnight. We do not observe him displaced from home for months. We do not observe him exhausted, deprived of familiar routines, or required to carry the Ring continuously across Middle-earth.

And that matters if we are entertaining a model of **maintained protective competence**.

There are at least two possible kinds of vulnerability within that model:

1. **Maintenance vulnerability:** protection might weaken when the practitioner cannot sustain the activities that support it.
2. **Structural vulnerability:** protection might depend on relationships, dispositions or environmental conditions that can be disrupted.

Neither is canonically established for Tom. In particular, we cannot claim that influenza would actually make him vulnerable to the Ring. The hypothetical is useful because it reveals the limit of the inference drawn from one encounter.

**An observed capacity is not necessarily an unconditional capacity.**

This is an important *Observed Body* principle. A person who performs a task successfully on Tuesday has not thereby demonstrated that they can perform it under every condition on every Tuesday thereafter. A person who struggles on Friday has not necessarily lost their knowledge or changed their character.

Capacity can be supported, situated, embodied and variable. The observer sees the performance; the observer may not see its maintenance history.

> 🧪 **PROPOSED RESEARCH QUESTION:** Is Tom Bombadil categorically immune to the Ring?
>
> 🩺 **METHODS REVIEW:** Have you controlled for seasonal influenza?
>
> 🧪 **RESEARCH TEAM:** ...

The joke is silly. The epistemological problem is not.

---

### Three hypotheses, not one convenient magical explanation

The flu example, displacement example and rival-Mordor example test **different variables**. They should not be blurred together merely because all three could alter an outcome.

| Proposed dependency | What changes | What it would test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Repeated maintenance | Illness, fatigue or interrupted routine | Whether protection requires ongoing practice |
| Situated ecology | Removal from familiar place or relationships | Whether competence depends on environmental supports |
| Structural non-domination | A decision to possess or subjugate | Whether the protective mode is incompatible with domination itself |
| Unknown mechanism | No known change | Whether our entire interpretation is mistaken or incomplete |

The third possibility is the most dramatic: the decision to dominate might change the very orientation through which Tom remains protected, perhaps abruptly. But it is also the most speculative. Neither the Tolkien text nor the historical cunning-folk record establishes a general rule that a practitioner instantly loses magical protection when entertaining an authoritarian thought. We are tracing the internal implications of **this proposed model**, not reporting a historical magical law.

A change in performance would not automatically tell us which variable had changed. That is why *The Observed Body* insists on separating observation, mechanism and interpretation.

## ⚖️ 9. A Witch Is What Someone Else Calls You

The folk practitioner may understand a charm as protection. A neighbour may understand it as help. An interrogator may understand it as evidence of forbidden supernatural dealings.

**The behaviour has not necessarily changed. The observer has.**

The John Walsh material is particularly revealing because questions about healing and protective knowledge become questions about whether the same source of power might permit harm. The institution does not simply observe an act; it translates the act into a theory of what the practitioner *could* do.

That theory may be right, wrong, partial or impossible to test. But if the observer is a magistrate rather than a curious neighbour, the classification can acquire coercive force.

Witch prosecutions cannot be explained by classification anxiety alone. They arose within specific legal, religious, social and interpersonal circumstances, often involving accusations of actual misfortune or harm. The records also contain coercion and unequal power. We should not flatten that history into a parable about eccentric people being misunderstood.

Nevertheless, the institutional failure mode is clear:

**Unfamiliar protective competence can be reinterpreted as potential offensive capability, especially when the observer's model of power assumes that anyone who can avert harm must also be able to cause it.**

An ability to resist something can look, to an insecure authority, like evidence of an ability to dominate it.

The step from *I don't understand how you did that* to *therefore you are dangerous* is not a deduction. It is a projection requiring evidence of its own.

---

### John Walsh: what the examiner's question does

Walsh's printed examination is valuable not because it offers a clean transcript of an independent healer's worldview, but because it shows an interrogation *organising* supernatural expertise into questions of legitimate and illegitimate capacity. The pamphlet presents his claims about acquiring knowledge through a fairy-associated familiar, divination and remedies in a form shaped by the people questioning him and by the conventions of print.

The critical movement is from **the services someone says they provide** to **the dangerous things the examiner thinks those capacities might entail**. The examiner is entitled to ask whether harm has occurred; the mistake would be to treat a speculative offensive counterpart as established simply because a protective practice has been described. Nor should we flatten the history: many witchcraft accusations originated in local allegations of specific misfortunes, not in a state bureaucracy hunting unclassifiable healers. Neighbours, clergy, clients, magistrates and printers might all interpret the same person differently.

We should also remember that the examination is not a neutral recording device. What survives is already an *observed body* mediated by coercion, editorial selection and a legal-religious vocabulary. The source lets us study those processes; it does not grant unfiltered access to Walsh's interior life.

## 🧙‍♂️ 10. The White Council Has a Threat Matrix

We should distinguish the actual narrative from the institutional thought experiment. Gandalf already understands that the Ring is dangerous and refuses Frodo's offer before the Bombadil chapters. He also knows something of Tom. The **White Council** is not identical to the order of the **Istari**, and Tolkien does not depict a Council prosecution of Bombadil.

### The actual Council of Elrond has already considered the operational question

Before inventing an institutional reaction, we have to attend to the one Tolkien wrote. At **the Council of Elrond**, the possibility of sending the Ring to Bombadil is raised. The discussion does not end with *Tom is stronger than Sauron, therefore appoint him*. Gandalf distinguishes Tom's unusual relationship to the Ring from his willingness or suitability to guard it: the Ring has little hold on him, but that does not make him a dependable custodian for the collective mission. The Council also recognises that his territory and safety would not endure indefinitely if Sauron prevailed elsewhere.

This is unusually good evidence for our central distinction. **Not being captured by a danger is not identical to being able to contain that danger for everyone else.** The Council's actual discussion is therefore more discriminating than the imagined anxious threat assessment below. It tests suitability, attention, geography and the larger strategic environment rather than assuming that immunity automatically implies superior war-making capacity.

It also complicates any simplistic claim that *institutions always fear Tom*. Tolkien depicts figures who are able to recognise an exceptional capability **and** its limits. Our following scenario asks what a less secure or less perceptive institution *might* do with the same information.



But imagine an authority encountering the story of Tom's apparent immunity without knowing how it works.

It knows that the Ring is dangerous. It knows that powerful people may be tempted. It has organised a great deal of its thinking around the Ring's capacity to corrupt and dominate.

Then someone reports that a man in yellow boots put it on, remained visible, laughed and gave it back.

Some observers might be delighted. Some would want to investigate. Others might be unsettled—not because Tom has threatened anyone, but because **his behaviour does not fit their model**.

An insecure authority might reason:

*If the Ring can influence the mighty but cannot influence Tom, Tom must be mightier still.*

From there it might imagine a rival sovereign, a strategic asset, an unregulated source of magical power or a threat to the established order.

But it has quietly imported several unsupported assumptions:

- Resistance to influence implies offensive superiority.
- Offensive superiority can be scaled into military capability.
- Someone who has that capability wants to use it.
- Someone who resists control intends to control others.
- A person can retain the same competence after adopting an entirely different relationship to power.

None follows from Tom handing the Ring back.

**The institution imagines what it would do with Tom's apparent capacity, then attributes that imagined project to Tom.**

This is the real threat-matrix problem: the observer's ambitions have been smuggled into the description of the observed person.

> 🧙‍♂️ **THE COUNCIL:** We are concerned about your capacity to establish a rival dominion.
>
> 🌼 **TOM:** I have a wife and a garden.
>
> 🧙‍♂️ **THE COUNCIL:** That does not address the strategic balance.
>
> 🌼 **TOM:** It addresses mine.
>
> 🧙‍♂️ **THE COUNCIL:** WHAT ARE YOUR LONG-TERM OBJECTIVES?
>
> 🌼 **TOM:** Water-lilies.

## 🏭 11. Tom Is Not Building a Second Mordor

This is the crucial step beyond *Tom is a nice man who doesn't want power*.

**What if the very conditions that make Tom powerful are incompatible with building a Mordor?**

Mordor represents the concentration of will, the subordination of other beings, extraction, command and the reorganisation of a landscape around domination. Tom's competence, under our ecological reading, is maintained through situated relationships, protective boundaries, recognition of other agency, and the refusal to possess everything he encounters.

These are not simply weaker and stronger versions of the same skill.

The capacity to live with a forest is not an undeveloped form of the capacity to turn that forest into a war machine.

If Tom tried to convert his relational competence into conquest, he would begin treating the beings and relationships that sustain it as objects to be overridden. He might gradually dismantle his own supports. More radically, **the act of adopting domination as his organising principle might immediately interrupt the disposition or relationships on which his protection depends**.

This is a *hypothesis within the interpretive model*, not a documented rule of Tolkien's metaphysics or a universal law of historical folk magic. It has two possible forms:

- **Gradual erosion:** the supports of competence are damaged as relationships and practices are neglected or destroyed.
- **Immediate incompatibility:** the protective orientation itself changes when the practitioner chooses domination; the very mechanism they would try to weaponise ceases to function as before.

The second possibility is particularly interesting in relation to the Ring. If Tom began desiring to impose his will upon others, he might be adopting the kind of desire through which the Ring ordinarily tempts its bearers. **Trying to exploit his resistance as a route to domination might be precisely what makes him vulnerable.**

We do not know whether that would happen. We are asking whether the proposed mechanism permits the institutional fantasy in the first place.

And the answer might be no.

**Tom is not merely declining to build a rival Mordor. He may be unable to build one *while remaining powerful in the particular way that makes the authority nervous*.**

> 🧙‍♂️ **THE COUNCIL:** But suppose you turned your powers towards conquest.
>
> 🌼 **TOM:** Then they would not be these powers anymore.
>
> 🧙‍♂️ **THE COUNCIL:** Explain.
>
> 🌼 **TOM:** If I destroy the forest, I no longer have a forest.
>
> 🧙‍♂️ **THE COUNCIL:** We understand that, but the strategic advantages—
>
> 🌼 **TOM:** I don't think you understand what a forest is.

This is the argument's strongest reversal: **the authority fears that Tom might become another Sauron, while failing to consider that becoming another Sauron might be the one thing that destroys the competence it fears.**

---

## 🪖 12. Ronald Has Seen Enough of the Fucking Somme

Tolkien's experience of the First World War, including the Somme and the deaths of friends, matters to the cultural and moral world in which his fiction developed. It does not give us a simple code for decoding every character, and it would be a mistake to claim that Tolkien rejected martial courage. His stories contain genuine bravery in battle.

They also contain other kinds of courage and masculine competence.

Sam fights and gardens. Aragorn fights and heals. Faramir is an accomplished commander who does not wish to seize the Ring. Gandalf's task is to guide and encourage resistance, not to replace Sauron with another tyrant. Saruman shows what happens when knowledge and responsibility are converted into the desire to command.

And then there is Tom.

Tom is confident, flamboyant, affectionate, domestic, musical and very good at intervening when someone needs help. His authority does not depend on proving that he could win a war against every other man in Middle-earth.

He is not an unsuccessful soldier. He is not an unfinished wizard. He is not an uncommissioned general waiting for the correct paperwork.

Tolkien's Letter 144 famously treats Tom's renunciation of control as significant, while acknowledging that this position does not provide a military solution to Sauron. The non-domination reading therefore has a genuine point of contact with Tolkien's own explanation, even though our specific cunning-folk and warding mechanism remains interpretive.

**RONALD HAS SEEN ENOUGH OF THE FUCKING SOMME.**

The joke has a serious edge. A world in which all masculine competence must ultimately be validated through conquest is a world that has already surrendered too much of its imagination to the logic of war.

---

### The different masculinities of competence

There is also a historical trap in assuming that a man who does not display military ambition must be a failed version of a commander. A local healer, a household protector, a counsellor and a general may all exercise serious responsibility, but the responsibilities are not interchangeable. A competent commander cannot simply sing a relationship with a river into being; a skilled local practitioner cannot necessarily organise an army. Their different kinds of authority are not ranked by how readily they can be weaponised.

Tom's conspicuous clothes, affection, songs and domesticity are therefore not evidence against his competence. They expose the narrowness of the test that asks whether he can convert it into conquest. His power does not need to be made masculine by being made militarily useful. **Ronald has supplied a man in yellow boots. The rest of us keep demanding a defence procurement plan.**

---

## 🫀🧿 13. The Observed Body: Who Gets to Decide What Tom Is?

Tom's conduct does not arrive with a single self-evident label attached. Different observers can interpret the same behaviour through different models:

| Observer position | What is noticed | Possible interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A rescued hobbit | Tom sings and intervenes effectively | Strange but useful protector |
| A folkloric practitioner | Repeated, situated, protective conduct | Maintained competence and boundaries |
| A power-ranking reader | The Ring fails to produce its ordinary effects | Superior magical strength |
| An insecure institutional observer | An exception to a known threat model | Potential rival capability |
| An early modern interrogator | Unfamiliar supernatural knowledge | Possible illicit or harmful magic |
| An ecological observer | Knowledge of relationships and proportionate intervention | Custodianship without domination |

Some of these are hypothetical analytical standpoints, not documented responses by Tolkien's characters.

The important distinction is between **what has been observed**, **what mechanism is inferred**, and **what motives are projected**.

Tom's apparent resistance to the Ring is an observation. The maintained-warding account is an interpretation. The fantasy of a second Mordor is a projection unless independently supported by evidence about Tom's capabilities and intentions.

The observer may also fail to see the history of maintenance: years of practice, routines, relationships, bodily conditions, attention, rest and adaptation. The competence looks innate because its labour is not visible at the moment of assessment.

And that is not merely a Tolkien problem.

It is a problem wherever institutions observe people under limited conditions, classify them through pre-existing categories, and mistake the classification for a complete account of the person. It is particularly consequential when the observer can attach legal, medical, professional or security consequences to the label.

**The observer does not merely record a capability. The observer interprets it through a model of what capability is for.**

If that model assumes all power exists to dominate, then resistance to domination can become legible only as a rival form of domination.

Tom's refusal to participate in that logic may be the very thing the observer cannot recognise.

---

### The model may be the problem

A suspicious observer need not begin as a villain. A responsible official may have genuine obligations to investigate a dangerous object and any apparent exception to its effects. The failure begins when the official's categories become immune to revision: when resistance must mean superior force, when privacy must mean concealment, when an unknown method must imply a hidden weapon, and when every form of competence is assumed to have a military conversion rate.

An adequate assessment would ask what was actually observed, under what conditions, over what duration, and with what evidence of motive or transferable capability. It would distinguish **protecting oneself** from **coercing others**, and **not being susceptible** from **being able to destroy the source of susceptibility**. It would also notice what it does not know.

The issue is not simply whether the authority is nice enough to tolerate Tom. It is whether its model of power is capable of describing a person whose strength is partly constituted by not seeking dominion. **When the subject does not fit the model, inspect the model before treating the subject as the defect.**

---

## 🌼 14. The Man Who Was Already Home

There is a temptation to treat Tom as an unsolved puzzle whose value lies in the answer to a classification question. But perhaps the more interesting question is why we are so dissatisfied when the answer is simply *Tom Bombadil*.

His home is not a headquarters. His marriage is not a political alliance. His songs are not necessarily weapons. His knowledge of the forest does not make him its owner. His resistance to the Ring does not establish that he could destroy it, carry it indefinitely or conquer its maker.

He has a place in the world, and he knows how to inhabit it.

In the cunning-man reading, that competence might require sustained care: for his body, his mind, his relationships, his boundaries and his environment. It may have limits. It may be vulnerable to changed conditions. It may even be incompatible with the domination an anxious authority imagines he could exercise.

Tom does not need to be more powerful than the Ring.

**He may simply know how not to let it in.**

And if that knowledge comes from the relationships, routines and boundaries through which he maintains his life, then asking whether he could build another Mordor rather misses the point.

He might lose everything that makes him Tom in the attempt.

The extraordinary thing is not necessarily that he possesses more power than everyone else. It is that he may have spent a very long time learning how to live without needing to possess everyone else.

🌼 And anyway, Goldberry is expecting him home.

## 📚 Sources, Comparisons & Evidential Boundaries

### Tolkien: primary texts and authorial commentary

- J. R. R. Tolkien, *The Fellowship of the Ring* (1954): “The Old Forest”, “In the House of Tom Bombadil”, “Fog on the Barrow-downs”, and “The Council of Elrond”. Primary basis for the encounters, Buckland/Old Forest conflict and later discussion of Tom's limits.
- J. R. R. Tolkien, “On Fairy-stories” (lecture 1939; published 1947; subsequently collected). Context for Tolkien’s thinking about fairy-story, not a direct statement that Bombadil is a cunning man.
- J. R. R. Tolkien, “The Adventures of Tom Bombadil” (1934), later collected in *The Adventures of Tom Bombadil* (1962). The earlier poem establishes that Tom, Goldberry, Old Man Willow and the Barrow-wight predate the *Lord of the Rings* narrative. [Tolkien Estate: *The Adventures of Tom Bombadil and Other Poetry*](https://www.tolkienestate.com/writing/the-adventures-of-tom-bombadil-and-other-poetry/).
- J. R. R. Tolkien, *The Letters of J. R. R. Tolkien*, ed. Humphrey Carpenter (1981; expanded edition ed. Wayne G. Hammond and Christina Scull, 2023), especially Letter 144. Authorial commentary on Tom's renunciation of control, the wartime relevance of that perspective and its limits. Consult the edition used before inserting direct quotations.

### Historical folk practice and prosecution

- *The Examination of John Walsh* (1566), early printed examination. [Bodleian Oxford Text Archive transcription](https://ota.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/repository/xmlui/bitstream/handle/20.500.12024/A14696/A14696.html?isAllowed=y&sequence=5). A mediated and adversarial source; distinguish the printed pamphlet's dating from the examination date.
- University of Edinburgh, *Survey of Scottish Witchcraft*: database and case materials for Andro Man and Scottish prosecutions. [Survey of Scottish Witchcraft](https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/).
- Ronald Hutton, *The Witch: A History of Fear, from Ancient Times to the Present* (2017). Comparative scholarship on witchcraft, fairies and European beliefs; useful for Walsh, Andro Man and Stewart, with page-specific references to be added against the edition used.
- Owen Davies, *Cunning-Folk: Popular Magic in English History* (2003). Historical overview of practitioners, their services and the distinction between helpful magic and accusations of harmful witchcraft.
- Brian Hoggard, *Magical House Protection: The Archaeology of Counter-Witchcraft* (2019). Material evidence for concealed deposits and protective marks; not evidence of literal mental wards.
- John Stewart of Irvine (1618): retain as a case for further primary-record verification before quoting examination or trial language.

### Folklore and cultural milieu

- Robert Kirk, *The Secret Commonwealth* (written late seventeenth century; published in nineteenth-century editions, including Andrew Lang's 1893 edition).
- W. Y. Evans-Wentz, *The Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries* (1911).
- Arthur Conan Doyle, *The Coming of the Fairies* (1922): evidence of contemporary interest, **not** reliable evidence that photographed fairies existed.
- Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm, *Deutsche Sagen* (1816–1818); Robert Browning, “The Pied Piper of Hamelin” (1842). Comparative traditions; no direct influence on Bombadil claimed.

### Interpretive status: do not collapse these categories

1. **Textually established:** Tom's conduct in the Old Forest, at home and with the Ring; his limited role in the wider struggle.
2. **Historically supported comparison:** British and Irish protective folk practice, cunning practitioners, fairy traditions, and the legal reinterpretation of supernatural knowledge.
3. **Our proposed reading:** Tom as an ecologically situated protective practitioner; songs and routines as possible ward-like maintenance; resistance as failure of access.
4. **Counterfactuals, not canon:** Tom with influenza; an insecure White Council threat assessment; instantaneous loss of protective power if he adopts domination.

The counterfactuals are here to test the limits of inference and reveal the observer's assumptions—not to pretend that Tolkien wrote scenes he did not write.
