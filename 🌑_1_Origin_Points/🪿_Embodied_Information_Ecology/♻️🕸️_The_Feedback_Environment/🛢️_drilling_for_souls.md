# 🛢️ Drilling For Souls
**First created:** 2026-09-20 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-20  
*Human beings became extraordinarily valuable information deposits. Unfortunately for the extraction industry, the deposits are alive, adaptive, political, tired, and capable of noticing the drill.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

For most of industrial history, enormously valuable resources had one extremely convenient property.

**The oil did not object.**

It did not change its behaviour because somebody measured it.

It did not become anxious because Exxon knew where it slept.

It did not start speaking in euphemisms because the derrick was listening.

It did not unionise with the neighbouring oil field.

It did not develop an opinion about the purposes for which it was being extracted.

Human beings are a much less cooperative natural resource.

Unfortunately, we are also fantastically information-rich.

We produce information almost continuously.

We write.

Talk.

Move.

Search.

Read.

Buy things.

Make friends.

Fall in love.

Make art.

Take photographs.

Develop expertise.

Build communities.

Argue about politics.

Write software.

Tell jokes.

Get frightened.

Change our minds.

Teach one another.

Leave records of where we went, what we wanted, what interested us, what upset us, what we made, what we know and who we know.

Increasingly sophisticated information systems have discovered that these traces are extraordinarily valuable.

Commercially valuable.

Administratively valuable.

Politically valuable.

Strategically valuable.

Scientifically valuable.

Sometimes socially valuable.

The obvious response has been to build better machinery for extracting them.

```text
HUMAN ACTIVITY
↓
PRODUCES INFORMATION
↓
INFORMATION ACQUIRES VALUE
↓
INFRASTRUCTURE DEVELOPS TO EXTRACT MORE
↓
GREATER EXTRACTION INCREASES CAPABILITY
↓
COMPETITION REWARDS GREATER EXTRACTION
↓
HUMANS EXPERIENCE / ADAPT TO EXTRACTION
↓
THE INFORMATION ENVIRONMENT CHANGES
↺
```

And there is the complication.

**The deposit responds to the drill.**

This is not a node arguing that information is literally petroleum.

It is a node about what happens when systems begin treating increasingly intimate dimensions of human life as extractable informational resources while forgetting one fairly important engineering detail:

> **The resource is part of the feedback loop.**

---

## 1. 🛢️ We Found Oil

The first deposits were comparatively obvious.

Clicks.

Purchases.

Searches.

Demographics.

Browsing behaviour.

Location.

Social connections.

Advertising platforms discovered that apparently mundane behavioural traces could be aggregated into extraordinarily valuable systems for targeting, prediction and optimisation.

The individual datum might be trivial.

The pattern was not.

Then the wells got deeper.

Language.

Voice.

Images.

Video.

Movement.

Professional knowledge.

Creative work.

Political behaviour.

Social relationships.

Entire public archives.

The relationships between concepts.

Patterns that can be inferred from combinations of information which appear relatively uninteresting on their own.

Generative AI makes the scale of the informational deposit particularly obvious because the resource is no longer merely:

> *What advertisement is this person likely to click?*

It can also be:

> *What have millions of human beings learned, written, photographed, coded, painted, argued, translated, discovered, documented and explained?*

That is an extraordinary resource.

It is also culture.

It is labour.

It is history.

It is expertise.

It is communication between human beings.

It was not originally produced as one enormous homogeneous seam waiting for whichever future computational system happened to develop the machinery to extract it.

But once the machinery exists, the incentive to drill is obvious.

## 2. 🤑 Oh Fuck, The Souls Are Valuable

Information can produce capability.

Where additional information improves a system, the operator has a reason to acquire it.

```text
MORE USEFUL INFORMATION
↓
GREATER CAPABILITY
↓
BETTER TARGETING / MODELLING /
PREDICTION / RETRIEVAL / INTELLIGENCE
↓
COMMERCIAL OR STRATEGIC ADVANTAGE
↓
INCENTIVE TO ACQUIRE MORE
↺
```

This does not mean that more data is always better.

Bad data can degrade systems.

Irrelevant data can add noise.

Biased data can produce distorted outputs.

Some information is useless for a particular objective.

But where information **does** confer advantage, competition can make voluntary informational restraint expensive.

The exact advantage differs between operators.

An advertiser wants to know enough about people to predict what might persuade them.

A retailer wants to predict demand.

An employer may want to predict performance or retention.

An AI developer may want enormous quantities of high-quality human-generated material.

A police force may want to identify people, vehicles, locations or behavioural patterns.

An intelligence service may want to understand networks, intentions and threats.

A government may want to understand its population.

Different wells.

Different drilling rights.

Different purposes.

Different legal regimes.

But the same systems question appears repeatedly:

**What happens once deeper extraction produces advantage?**

## 3. 🪤 The Extraction Trap

For creators, this becomes particularly perverse.

The contemporary economy repeatedly tells people that visibility is a prerequisite for opportunity.

```text
NEED WORK / RECOGNITION / AUDIENCE
↓
MUST BE DISCOVERABLE
↓
PUBLISH MORE WORK / MORE SELF
↓
BECOME MORE EXTRACTABLE
↓
INTERMEDIARIES CAN DERIVE VALUE
↓
VALUE / PROVENANCE MAY DETACH
FROM PRODUCER
↓
CREATOR'S ECONOMIC POSITION
MAY WEAKEN
↓
GREATER NEED FOR VISIBILITY
↓
PUBLISH MORE
↺
```

The individual behaviour is perfectly comprehensible.

If employers will not take your word that you can write, publish writing.

If clients need evidence that you understand something, demonstrate your expertise publicly.

If nobody knows your music exists, post the music.

If publishers want an existing audience, build an audience.

If your wages are inadequate, develop a side hustle.

If the side hustle does not make enough money, build a brand around the side hustle.

If the algorithm stops showing people your work, produce more material for the algorithm.

For people entering adulthood around and after the financial crash, the message was difficult to miss:

**Monetise yourself harder.**

Your hobby could be income.

Your expertise could be content.

Your personality could be a brand.

Your spare room could be an asset.

Your social relationships could become networking.

Your leisure could become professional development.

Your breakfast could presumably be content if you photographed the bastard attractively enough.

The resulting public internet contains an extraordinary quantity of human competence precisely because people were repeatedly encouraged to make themselves discoverable.

Then discoverability became extractability.

The shop window became part of the mine.

## 4. ⚙️ Do Capitalism Faster Machine

Artificial intelligence is not inherently a capitalism machine.

It is not inherently an advertising machine.

It is not inherently a surveillance machine.

It is not inherently a labour-substitution machine.

The technology can be deployed within many possible institutional arrangements towards many possible objectives.

Which is precisely why the surrounding incentive environment matters.

Take an extremely capable technology and deploy it inside markets already rewarding:

- scale;
- speed;
- engagement;
- market capture;
- labour substitution;
- lower marginal costs;
- greater prediction;
- greater personalisation;
- greater informational advantage;

and it should not be terribly surprising when some organisations ask:

**Can this help us do those things faster?**

Hence one possible description of part of the present deployment environment:

> **We invented a machine capable of doing extraordinary things and immediately asked whether it could do capitalism faster.**

The interesting question is not whether capitalism is secretly hiding inside the transformer architecture.

It is not.

The interesting question is:

> **Which optimisation problems do we hand increasingly capable machinery?**

If the surrounding system rewards extraction, better extraction machinery does not automatically produce restraint.

It may simply produce better extraction.

## 5. 🎰 Nobody Ordered This Outcome

The resulting ecology does not require a mastermind.

That is one of the easiest ways to misunderstand complex systems.

Large organisations divide work.

```text
TEAM A
"Improve retrieval."

TEAM B
"Increase engagement."

TEAM C
"Acquire useful data."

TEAM D
"Reduce operating costs."

TEAM E
"Manage legal exposure."

TEAM F
"Improve safety."

INVESTOR
"Increase growth."

STATE
"Don't lose the technology race."

USER
"Why does the combined system feel like this?"
```

Each instruction can make sense locally.

The aggregate outcome can still be strange.

A recommendation engineer does not need to want people to become addicted to a platform.

A researcher working on model capability does not need to want illustrators to lose income.

A procurement officer does not need to want an entire profession restructured.

An investor does not need to understand every informational consequence of demanding growth.

A government official worried about strategic competition does not need to want the public to live inside permanently accelerating technological churn.

Complex systems can produce outcomes which nobody ordered as a complete package.

> **Distributed responsibility can produce concentrated consequences.**

This is useful because it prevents two equally bad analyses.

The first imagines that every harmful emergent outcome must have been secretly planned.

The second assumes that because nobody planned the complete outcome, nobody can be responsible for changing it.

Neither follows.

> **Emergence explains how an outcome can occur without central intent. It does not make the outcome ungovernable once observed.**

Once the feedback becomes visible, somebody has to decide whether to keep feeding it.

## 6. 🧾 Congratulations, You Have Rediscovered Externalities

None of this is actually a new problem in political economy.

Industrial systems have spent centuries discovering new arrangements for producing:

```text
PRIVATE VALUE
+
DISTRIBUTED COST
```

A factory produces something useful and profitable.

The pollution travels downstream.

An employer reduces costs.

Workers absorb greater risk.

A financial institution takes profitable risks.

The wider system absorbs part of the failure when those risks collapse.

The informational economy has its own versions.

A platform becomes more engaging.

Users supply the attention.

A model becomes more capable.

Human intellectual production supplies part of the informational substrate.

An employer becomes more efficient.

Workers absorb the consequences of algorithmic management.

A surveillance system becomes more powerful.

The observed population absorbs the change in informational conditions.

The analogy is not that data is literally petroleum or AI literally pollution.

It is about the architecture of incentives.

**The actor capturing the value does not necessarily bear every cost created in capturing it.**

And if the cost never appears in the operator's own accounting, the system can describe it as external.

The human being living downstream may use a different term.

As explored in [🤖 The Great Plausible Deniability Machine](../🫀🕸️_Information_Is_Experienced/🤖_the_great_plausible_deniability_machine.md):

> **An externality is an accounting description from the system's perspective. From the person's perspective, it is an experience.**

## 7. ⚖️ Why Doesn't The Company Just Stop Drilling?

Sometimes it does.

Companies are not devoid of people, ethics, foresight or institutional learning.

Nor is every commercial incentive pointed towards harm.

Sometimes preventing harm is entirely compatible with commercial self-interest.

Consider an activity which produces:

```text
HARM
+
REPUTATIONAL DAMAGE
+
LEGAL LIABILITY
+
CUSTOMER LOSS
+
INVESTOR CONCERN
+
NO IMPORTANT COMMERCIAL BENEFIT
```

The firm already has powerful reasons to stop it.

Now consider:

```text
HARM
+
SUBSTANTIAL COMMERCIAL ADVANTAGE
+
COMPETITORS MAY CONTINUE
+
UNCERTAIN LEGAL COST
+
RESTRAINT REDUCES CAPABILITY
```

That is a harder problem.

Even an organisation whose leadership would prefer a better collective outcome may fear imposing costs upon itself while competitors continue.

This is one reason regulation exists.

Regulation can alter:

- permissions;
- liability;
- price;
- transparency;
- bargaining power;
- minimum standards;
- available actions;
- competitive conditions.

In cybernetic terms, regulation changes the feedback environment in which the operator is optimising.

The interesting opposition is therefore not simply:

```text
REGULATION
vs
INNOVATION
```

It is:

> **What objective function are we collectively permitting innovation to optimise?**

And where preventing harm materially conflicts with commercial advantage, voluntary restraint has a structural reliability problem.

Sometimes the rules exist precisely so that the person who wants to behave responsibly does not have to lose to the person who does not.

## 8. 🎸 Mate, She's Doing Her Cookie Banner

There is a recurring lament about culture.

Where is the next Beatles?

Where is the next Clash?

Why do we not get another enormous musical scene?

Why does so much contemporary culture sometimes seem polished, optimised, market-tested and strangely exhausted?

There are hundreds of possible explanations.

But culture has material inputs.

People need time.

Housing.

Venues.

Rehearsal rooms.

Social spaces.

Disposable attention.

Privacy.

Economic security.

Access to other creative people.

The ability to experiment.

The ability to fail.

The ability to be embarrassingly mediocre for three years while becoming good.

The ability to fuck about without immediately transforming the fucking-about into a financial product.

Now consider the contemporary creator stack:

```text
MAKE ART
+
BUILD BRAND
+
POST CONTENT
+
MAINTAIN AUDIENCE
+
OPTIMISE PLATFORM
+
ANALYSE METRICS
+
MONETISE
+
LEARN NEW PLATFORM
+
LEARN AI
+
DEFEND PROVENANCE
+
ANSWER EMAIL
+
HAVE DAY JOB
```

And then somebody asks:

**Why can't we have another Clash?**

Mate.

She's doing her cookie banner.

The joke is stupid.

The allocation of human attention is not.

A culture cannot indefinitely demand that its creative population convert increasing amounts of attention, personality, social life and leisure into economically useful signals while assuming that cultural production itself will remain unaffected.

> **A culture cannot indefinitely treat human attention, expression and experience as extractable inputs and then be surprised when the humans are too exhausted to make culture.**

That does not require romanticising the past.

Record labels exploited musicians.

Publishers exploited writers.

Artists were poor.

Gatekeeping was enormous.

Cultural industries have always contained concentrated ownership, abusive contracts and wildly unequal bargaining power.

The question is whether digital systems have taken familiar forms of extraction and equipped them with:

**greater scale;**

**finer-grained observation;**

**more automation;**

**more intermediaries;**

and

**considerably faster machinery.**

## 9. 🌎 The Drill Is American; The Oil Field Isn't

Information systems cross borders more easily than political accountability does.

A commercial decision can be made in one jurisdiction and alter informational conditions in dozens of others.

```text
COMMERCIAL DECISION
MADE IN ONE JURISDICTION
↓
INFORMATION ENVIRONMENT
DEPLOYED GLOBALLY
↓
COSTS / BENEFITS
EXPERIENCED ACROSS JURISDICTIONS
↓
POLITICAL RECOURSE
REMAINS SUBSTANTIALLY TERRITORIAL
```

This matters because many globally dominant technology companies emerged from the United States and operate within an American commercial and legal environment while serving populations governed by different political settlements.

People in Britain do not vote in American elections.

People in France do not write Californian privacy law.

European creators do not determine American copyright doctrine.

Yet all can live inside information systems substantially shaped by American firms.

This does not make the United States uniquely responsible for every digital harm.

It does not mean American technology produces no enormous benefits.

It does not mean other states or companies are passive.

It does create a transnational governance problem.

A population can remain politically and strategically allied with the United States while becoming deeply irritated by particular externalities produced by American corporate power.

Those positions are not contradictory.

From inside the United States, the environment can look like:

```text
EU REGULATION

UK REGULATION

STATE REGULATION

COPYRIGHT LITIGATION

PRIVACY DEMANDS

COMPETITION ENFORCEMENT

FOREIGN GOVERNMENTS COMPLAINING

CREATORS COMPLAINING
```

Why is everybody suddenly regulating us?

From elsewhere, the chronology may feel rather different.

**Some populations have been absorbing parts of the externalised cost for years.**

The regulation did not necessarily arrive before the problem.

It may be arriving because the problem did.

## 10. 🐉 China Also Found Oil

China demonstrates a different part of the same information problem.

Commercial information extraction exists there too.

But an authoritarian state has additional reasons to value population legibility.

Information can contribute to:

- administration;
- public-order management;
- censorship;
- political control;
- intelligence;
- identification of organised dissent;
- understanding social conditions.

The objective function therefore differs substantially from that of an advertising platform or American technology company.

These systems should not be collapsed into political equivalence merely because both value information.

But they share one awkward engineering constraint.

**The humans remain human.**

Authoritarianism can increase the cost of saying something.

It can restrict publication.

It can censor terminology.

It can punish organisation.

It can suppress visible opposition.

It can make particular forms of communication dangerous.

What it cannot safely assume is:

```text
LESS OBSERVABLE DISSENT
=
LESS UNDERLYING DISSENT
```

The state can alter the signal.

That does not necessarily mean it has altered the underlying state by the same amount.

> **Authoritarianism does not remove the organism from the feedback loop.**

## 11. 👀 The Oil Field Has Noticed The Drill

Observation is not always passive.

People behave differently according to what they believe is observable, by whom, for what purpose and with what consequences.

That creates a basic loop:

```text
STATE OBSERVES POPULATION
↓
POPULATION PERCEIVES OBSERVATION
↓
POPULATION ADAPTS EXPRESSION
↓
OBSERVABLE SIGNAL CHANGES
↓
STATE INTERPRETS CHANGED SIGNAL
↓
STATE MAY ALTER / INCREASE OBSERVATION
↓
POPULATION ADAPTS AGAIN
↺
```

People can self-censor.

Move conversations.

Develop euphemisms.

Use coded language.

Perform compliance.

Avoid searchable terminology.

Communicate through trusted relationships.

Stop telling the observer what they actually think.

None of these strategies makes a population completely illegible.

Nor does greater surveillance necessarily mean the state knows less.

The problem is subtler:

> **Greater capacity to observe does not automatically produce proportionately greater capacity to know.**

Because the sensor can change the system being sensed.

That is not peculiar to China.

A worker behaves differently when the manager is watching.

A child behaves differently when the teacher enters the room.

A politician behaves differently in front of a camera.

A social-media user behaves differently according to what they believe the platform will reward or punish.

A population can behave differently when political expression carries risk.

The information environment is part of the behaviour-generating environment.

There is no clean observational position outside the system.

## 12. 🙈 “Everyone Says Everything Is Fine”

This creates another problem.

Systems need bad news.

An organisation which cannot receive information about its own failures becomes less capable of correcting them.

But bad news can become costly to produce.

```text
BAD NEWS
↓
COSTLY TO REPORT
↓
LESS BAD NEWS REPORTED
↓
SYSTEM APPEARS HEALTHIER
↓
DECISION-MAKER RECEIVES
DISTORTED MODEL
↓
BAD DECISIONS BECOME MORE LIKELY
```

Again, this is not uniquely authoritarian.

Corporations do it.

Political parties do it.

Militaries do it.

Bureaucracies do it.

Families do it.

Abusive organisations do it.

Teams do it when everybody knows the boss hates hearing that the project is fucked.

But authoritarian systems can increase the stakes because unwelcome political information can carry much greater consequences for the person producing it.

The interesting question is therefore larger than:

**Is surveillance oppressive?**

It is:

> **What happens to a governing system when the machinery it uses to increase population legibility simultaneously changes the population's incentives to provide truthful signals?**

The oil field has started lying to the geologist.

Perhaps because the geologist keeps arresting the oil.

## 13. 🦅🐉 Congratulations, You're Both In The Loop

The United States and China have substantially different political systems, legal environments, information regimes and relationships between state and citizen.

Those differences matter.

They should not be flattened into an easy equivalence.

But strategic competition can still place both states inside the same higher-level feedback loop.

If American decision-makers believe restraint will allow China to gain strategically important AI capability, restraint becomes more expensive.

If Chinese decision-makers believe restraint will allow the United States to gain strategically important AI capability, restraint becomes more expensive.

```text
SIDE A
FEARS LOSING ADVANTAGE
↓
ACCELERATES
↓
SIDE B
OBSERVES ACCELERATION
↓
FEARS LOSING ADVANTAGE
↓
ACCELERATES
↓
SIDE A
OBSERVES SIDE B
↓
PERCEIVED COST OF RESTRAINT INCREASES
↺
```

Nobody needs to desire maximal acceleration.

The competitive environment can reward it anyway.

This is the international version of the extraction problem.

A company can think:

> *If we don't exploit this commercially useful resource, a competitor will.*

A state can think:

> *If we don't develop this strategically useful capability, an adversary will.*

At both scales, unilateral restraint can become costly.

The system consequently selects for drilling.

## 14. 🤝 This Is Why You Have Diplomats

This is also why diplomacy exists.

Diplomacy is not what states do when everybody trusts one another.

If everybody already agreed, there would be considerably less to negotiate.

Diplomacy is one of the technologies humans developed for altering adversarial feedback environments.

It can create:

- communication;
- signalling;
- shared terminology;
- incident protocols;
- reciprocal commitments;
- scientific exchange;
- verification;
- minimum standards;
- negotiated limits;
- predictable consequences.

Not every proposed form of AI cooperation between strategic competitors will be achievable.

Some areas may remain irreconcilable.

Verification can be difficult.

States can defect.

Agreements can be exploited.

Political systems can have fundamentally different objectives.

None of that changes the underlying collective-action problem.

> **When the cost of unilateral restraint is too high, restraint may require reciprocity.**

Domestic regulation changes the incentive environment between companies.

International agreements can sometimes change the incentive environment between states.

Both are attempts to intervene in a feedback loop rather than merely asking one operator to become nicer.

Some of us will hold your fucking hand through this.

But, guys.

You do actually need to start playing ball.

Strategic advantage.

National competitiveness.

From inside the population, the same ecology can feel rather different.

Platform churn.

Algorithmic management.

Attention competition.

Surveillance.

Creator precarity.

Repeated demands for content.

Repeated demands for data.

Opaque automated decisions.

Weak provenance.

Weak recourse.

Another privacy notice.

Another platform to learn.

Another algorithm to appease.

Another profession being told that automation will liberate it shortly before everybody starts wondering which jobs will still exist.

Another fucking cookie banner.

People do not need to become anti-American, anti-technology or anti-AI for that environment to become exhausting.

Nor does irritation with American corporate power imply rejection of political or security alliances with the United States.

An allied population can simultaneously think:

**yes, obviously we remain allies**

and

**could your fucking companies stop doing that please.**

The distinction matters.

Strategic alignment does not erase embodied experience.

If allies repeatedly report that parts of a shared technological environment are producing unacceptable costs, treating those complaints merely as obstacles to innovation means throwing away feedback.

And systems which habitually throw away unwelcome feedback eventually become surprised by things they had plenty of opportunities to notice.

## 16. 🪿 THE DEPOSIT IS ALIVE

This is the mistake underneath the whole node.

Human information looks wonderfully extractable if the human is treated as a static source.

But humans are not static.

They are:

- adaptive;
- reflexive;
- social;
- embodied;
- creative;
- strategic;
- political;
- capable of withholding;
- capable of performing;
- capable of resistance;
- capable of regulation;
- capable of changing platforms;
- capable of organising;
- capable of leaving;
- capable of becoming fucking exhausted.

So the extraction itself becomes part of the environment producing the next round of information.

```text
EXTRACTION
↓
CHANGES HUMAN EXPERIENCE / INCENTIVES
↓
HUMANS ADAPT
↓
BEHAVIOUR CHANGES
↓
INFORMATION CHANGES
↓
SYSTEM OBSERVES CHANGED INFORMATION
↓
SYSTEM ADAPTS
↺
```

That is why the analogy eventually breaks.

Oil does not update its threat model.

Coal does not self-censor.

Natural gas does not deliberately provide misleading telemetry because it has worked out what the pipeline operator wants to hear.

A human population can.

A creative population can also stop creating.

A workforce can disengage.

Users can abandon a platform.

Voters can demand regulation.

Creators can withhold work.

People can poison data.

Communities can develop norms against observation.

Countries can impose laws.

Allies can get pissed off.

The resource is not merely being acted upon.

**It acts back.**

## 🪶 Maybe Stop Treating People Like A Seam Of Coal

Industrial civilisation became extremely good at asking:

**How much can we extract?**

Eventually it also had to learn to ask:

**What happens to the surrounding ecology if we do?**

The information age is encountering a similar conceptual correction.

Not because information is petroleum.

Not because data processing is automatically exploitation.

Not because every act of observation is illegitimate.

Not because every use of publicly available information requires compensation.

Not because every AI system has the same incentives.

And not because every society should draw the same regulatory boundary.

The correction is simpler.

**Extraction cannot be adequately understood by measuring only the output of the extractor.**

Human beings produce extraordinary informational value.

Our attention is valuable.

Our creativity is valuable.

Our movements are valuable.

Our relationships are valuable.

Our preferences are valuable.

Our expertise is valuable.

Our culture is valuable.

Our fears are valuable.

Our language is valuable.

Our experiences are valuable.

Our responses to being observed are valuable.

Increasingly sophisticated systems can drill deeper into all of them.

But human beings are not passive mineral deposits.

The deposit observes the drill.

The deposit responds to the drill.

The deposit changes because of the drill.

The deposit talks to the other deposits.

The deposit elects governments.

The deposit writes regulations.

The deposit invents euphemisms.

The deposit gets tired.

The deposit occasionally tells the drilling company to fuck off.

And eventually the people running around demanding ever-better drilling technology may discover that they have spent years measuring the efficiency of extraction while failing to measure what was happening to the field.

**Bad news, lads.**

**The oil field had a nervous system.**
