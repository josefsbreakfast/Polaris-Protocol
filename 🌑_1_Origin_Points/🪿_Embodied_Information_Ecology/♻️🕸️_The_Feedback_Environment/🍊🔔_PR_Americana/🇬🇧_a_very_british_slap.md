# 🇬🇧 A Very British SLAPP

**First created:** 2026-10-03 \| **Last updated:** 2026-10-03\
*Britain says it wants women to speak. The harder question is whether
the information environment can still hear them when the alleged
perpetrator is expensive to confront.*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🛰️ Orientation

There is something uniquely galling about being told, with great
seriousness, that violence against women and girls is a national
emergency; that victims and survivors must be heard; that perpetrators
must be relentlessly pursued; that government will use the full
machinery of the state to make women safer --- while the legal and
institutional environment still contains routes by which information
about violence can become extraordinarily difficult to transmit when it
touches somebody powerful.

This is not an argument that every confidentiality agreement is
sinister.

It is not an argument that privacy law is illegitimate.

It is not an argument that national-security secrecy is fake.

It is not an allegation that every powerful man accused of wrongdoing
has deployed these mechanisms, nor that every institution encountering
such an allegation has deliberately protected him.

It is a systems question.

> **What happens to information about violence when the alleged
> perpetrator, an associated institution, or an implicated network
> possesses unusually high legal, political, financial, diplomatic or
> national-security value?**

That question matters because the British government's own current VAWG
strategy says that misogynistic attitudes have historically been present
inside state agencies and institutions, promises the relentless pursuit
of perpetrators, says perpetrators should not operate with impunity, and
commits government to continuing to hear survivor voices.

Fine.

Then the system has to survive the difficult test.

Not only:

> **What happens when Bob from down the road is the perpetrator?**

But:

> **What happens when the alleged perpetrator has money, lawyers,
> reputation-management capacity, institutional access, social capital,
> political relationships, intelligence adjacency, or some combination
> of the above?**

Those are not the same information environments.

And unfortunately, pretending they are produces policy calibrated
against the wrong threat model.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. 🔔 Britain Would Like Women To Speak Now

The government's *Freedom from Violence and Abuse* strategy, published
in December 2025, sets a national ambition to halve violence against
women and girls within a decade.

Its stated architecture is unusually strong.

It promises:

-   prevention and early intervention;
-   relentless pursuit of perpetrators;
-   support for victims and survivors;
-   a whole-of-government and whole-of-society response;
-   trauma-informed justice reform;
-   accountability through cross-government governance;
-   continued engagement with victims and survivors;
-   enforcement intended to prevent perpetrators operating with
    impunity.

The strategy also acknowledges something important: the attitudes and
cultures enabling violence have not merely existed somewhere outside
government. They have existed inside agencies and institutions of the
state --- the places in which victims are supposed to be able to place
their trust.

This matters.

Because once the state says that, the feedback problem cannot be reduced
to:

> Why don't women report?

The next question is:

> **What happens to the information when they do?**

A survivor-centred system cannot measure itself solely by the
availability of an intake route.

A mouth opening is not the end of the information process.

It is the beginning.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2. 👂🏽 What Happens When She Tells You?

Take one piece of information:

> **A woman says that a man subjected her to serious violence or
> abuse.**

That information may need to travel through several systems before it
can produce accountability:

**experience**

→ trusted person

→ specialist support

→ lawyer

→ police

→ investigator

→ corroborating witness or record

→ prosecutor

→ journalist

→ editor

→ publisher

→ court

→ public record

Not every case follows that route.

Not every allegation should be published.

Not every allegation can be proved.

But every transition changes the information.

Every receiving system has its own rules, incentives, thresholds,
liabilities and protected interests.

The relevant question is therefore not merely:

> **Was she legally allowed to speak?**

It is:

> **Could the information survive transmission?**

That is an Embodied Information Ecology question.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 3. 🤐 Permission To Speak Is Not Public Transmissibility

Non-disclosure agreements are an obvious place to start because they
make the distinction unusually visible.

Section 17 of the Victims and Prisoners Act 2024 created statutory
protection for specified disclosures by victims or people who reasonably
believe themselves to be victims. The operative change took effect on 1
October 2025.

Among the protected routes are disclosures to law enforcement for
relevant functions, qualified lawyers for legal advice, regulated
professionals for professional support, and other specified recipients
and purposes.

That is important protection.

It is also recent.

Government guidance expressly states that the changes apply to relevant
NDAs signed on or after 1 October 2025, while agreements signed before
that date remain subject to the previous rules and may still be
enforceable in some circumstances.

So a historical survivor cannot simply be placed inside the 2025 legal
environment.

More importantly:

**police is not public.**

**lawyer is not public.**

**therapist is not public.**

**permission to make a protected disclosure is not the same thing as the
ability to establish a durable public record.**

This distinction is easy to lose when policy speaks about whether a
victim is "allowed to disclose".

The information ecology asks something else:

> **To whom? For what purpose? At what cost? With what consequences? And
> what happens at the next node?**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4. 🧱 Parliament Is Still Altering The Wall

The law is still moving.

The Victims and Courts Act 2026 contains a broader replacement
architecture for the 2024 section 17 regime. Its enacted wording reaches
beyond the underlying criminal conduct to information concerning another
contracting party's response to that conduct or disclosure.

As at 3 October 2026, that replacement provision has been enacted but
has not yet been commenced.

That does not prove that previous agreements were abusive.

It does establish something more modest and more useful:

> **Parliament has continued to identify the boundaries around victim
> confidentiality as requiring reform.**

That is exactly why historical analysis must not read today's safeguards
backwards into yesterday's environment.

The question is not:

> Look, there is a protection now. What is the problem?

The question is:

> **What information environment did the survivor actually inhabit at
> the relevant time?**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 5. 🥊 A Very British SLAPP

Then there is strategic litigation.

The Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 introduced a
statutory anti-SLAPP mechanism requiring Civil Procedure Rules to permit
early strike-out of qualifying SLAPP claims and providing costs
protection in qualifying cases.

The remaining provisions came fully into force on 18 June 2025.

But the statutory architecture is tied to the economic-crime definition
in the Act.

That produces an uncomfortable contrast.

Information concerning economic crime may fall within the statutory
anti-SLAPP framework.

An allegation of sexual violence does not become an economic-crime SLAPP
merely because the alleged perpetrator is wealthy, powerful or
litigation-happy.

Oh.

**Cool.**

The problem is not that sexual-violence defendants possess some special
statutory right to bring abusive litigation.

They do not.

The problem is that the statutory anti-SLAPP architecture does not yet
provide a general equivalent shield against every form of abusive
litigation deployed against public-interest speech.

And a SLAPP does not need ultimately to win in order to alter an
information environment.

The burdens can themselves matter:

-   solicitors;
-   counsel;
-   correspondence;
-   evidence preservation;
-   disclosure;
-   hearings;
-   interim applications;
-   adverse-costs risk;
-   insurance questions;
-   publisher risk;
-   time;
-   stress;
-   uncertainty.

For an individual survivor, the relevant calculation may occur long
before a judge decides who is right.

The system can therefore produce chilling without producing a final
judgment.

That is why the title is **A Very British SLAPP**.

The analytical object is not merely the statutory definition of a SLAPP.

It is the wider environment in which the credible possibility of
expensive civil process can change whether information moves.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 6. 💷 Bob From Down The Road Is Not The Correct Threat Model

A great deal of institutional safeguarding is implicitly calibrated
against an ordinary perpetrator.

Call him Bob.

Bob may be controlling.

Bob may lie.

Bob may intimidate witnesses.

Bob may manipulate family members.

Bob may exploit weaknesses in policing or support systems.

Those are serious problems.

But now hold the alleged conduct constant and change only the
perpetrator's resources.

The second hypothetical person has:

-   enormous disposable capital;
-   elite legal representation;
-   professional reputation-management capacity;
-   extensive personal networks;
-   institutional access;
-   relationships with people who themselves possess power;
-   the ability to generate credible litigation risk;
-   access to private investigators or specialist advisers where
    lawfully retained;
-   control over organisations or records;
-   unusually strong opportunities to shape narratives;
-   possible proximity to diplomatic, political, security or
    intelligence interests.

This does **not** mean that powerful people are inherently guilty.

It means that, where a powerful person *has* committed wrongdoing, the
capability profile available for resisting scrutiny can be radically
different from that of an ordinary offender.

That is a distinct threat model.

A high-control personality with extraordinary access, capital and
institutional reach may be unusually well positioned to exploit every
lawful ambiguity, procedural delay, asymmetry and protective boundary
available.

The policy mistake is to ask:

> Would this safeguarding mechanism work against Bob?

and then infer:

> Therefore it is adequate against everybody.

No.

**That is not the same world.**

A serious VAWG architecture needs a **high-power perpetrator stress
test**.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 7. 👑 High-Value Nodes

Some people sit at unusually valuable points in institutional networks.

Examples may include:

-   senior politicians;
-   members of royal households;
-   major business figures;
-   significant donors;
-   senior officials;
-   intelligence personnel;
-   intelligence sources or assets;
-   people embedded in strategically important industries;
-   foreign officials;
-   individuals connected to diplomatic relationships;
-   individuals whose exposure may reveal information about other
    protected systems.

Again, institutional value does not establish guilt.

Nor does it establish a cover-up.

The systems problem is different.

> **Information about one person can become entangled with independently
> protected information about another system.**

That creates the possibility of **protection by adjacency**.

The law does not need to say:

> Protect Person A.

The operative protection may instead say:

> Protect source B.

> Protect operation C.

> Protect liaison relationship D.

> Protect confidential record E.

> Protect private information F.

> Protect the integrity of proceeding G.

If evidence about A cannot easily be separated from B--G, A may receive
substantial incidental protection.

That may be legitimate.

It may also have severe consequences for accountability.

Both things can be true.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 8. 🕵️ National Security Changes The Topology

National-security material creates an especially difficult version of
this problem.

The relevant safeguards exist for reasons.

States genuinely need to protect intelligence sources, capabilities,
operations, foreign liaison relationships and information whose
disclosure could cause serious harm.

But once potentially probative information becomes entangled with those
interests, the route to public scrutiny changes.

There are at least four analytically distinct possibilities:

### A. Necessary secrecy

The protected information genuinely cannot safely be disclosed.

### B. Incidental protection

Necessary secrecy also conceals information that would embarrass or
implicate an individual.

The secrecy is not imposed *for* that person's benefit, but they benefit
from it.

### C. Excessive protection

A legitimate protected interest exists, but the secrecy boundary is
drawn more widely than necessary, taking potentially separable
information with it.

### D. Pretextual or abusive protection

A protected-interest claim is invoked principally to shield wrongdoing,
embarrassment or institutional reputation rather than the interest the
protection is supposed to serve.

The existence of A, B or C is not evidence of D.

That distinction matters.

But so does another condition:

### E. The public cannot tell

The material necessary to determine whether the situation is A, B, C or
D may itself be inaccessible.

That is the epistemic problem.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 9. 🕳️ The State Has Reasons

This is where insufficiently bounded secrecy can become materially
devastating even without proving deliberate bad faith.

From outside the protected system, the exchange can become structurally
recursive:

> **Why can't I see the evidence?**\
> National security.
>
> **How can I test whether withholding it is actually necessary?**\
> You cannot see the sensitive evidence.
>
> **How do I know the boundary is proportionate?**\
> An authorised institution has reviewed it.
>
> **Can I examine what that institution examined?**\
> Not where doing so would disclose the protected material.

There are meaningful safeguards against executive assertion.

Courts can scrutinise government claims.

Special advocates can operate in some closed proceedings.

Parliamentary and statutory oversight mechanisms exist.

Investigations, inquiries, journalism, later disclosure and
declassification can sometimes reveal information unavailable at an
earlier stage.

Those checks matter.

But:

> **independent scrutiny is not the same thing as public
> falsifiability.**

A government claim can be genuinely reviewed while remaining impossible
for the survivor or wider public independently to verify.

The state does not possess an unlimited legal power to "deny everything
forever".

The more precise problem is worse in a different way:

> **The state's claim about why information cannot safely be disclosed
> can itself become part of the reason outsiders cannot obtain enough
> information to test the claim.**

That is an epistemic closure risk.

And it needs boundaries.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 10. 🪢 Disentanglement Is A Safeguarding Function

A functioning system cannot treat:

> allegation + national-security material

as if the only available output were:

> **SECRET. NEXT.**

The safeguarding task is **disentanglement**.

Where possible:

**allegation + sensitive material**

→ identify the genuinely protected component

→ protect the source, method, capability, operation or relationship that
genuinely requires protection

→ preserve non-sensitive evidential information

→ independently investigate alleged wrongdoing

→ construct an open or appropriately protected accountability route

→ record why remaining information was withheld

→ provide meaningful independent review

→ revisit secrecy where circumstances change

The point is not to publish intelligence material recklessly.

The point is that **protecting legitimate secrets and investigating
violence are two state functions, not one function and an
inconvenience**.

If the architecture cannot separate them, legitimate secrecy can
accidentally manufacture impunity.

That is not a theoretical side issue for VAWG policy.

It is a safeguarding design problem.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 11. 🫥 Nobody Has To Order The Cover-Up

This is where systems analysis matters.

A containment outcome does not necessarily require a room in which
somebody says:

> Silence her.

Imagine instead:

A lawyer identifies substantial litigation risk.

A publisher demands stronger corroboration.

A witness fears being sued.

A civil servant cannot disclose classified information.

A police officer encounters material governed by another process.

A department protects a confidential relationship.

A journalist cannot independently verify a key component.

An editor decides the evidential position cannot presently survive
publication.

A victim has already spent years learning that every new disclosure
produces another expensive, frightening process.

Every actor may possess an individually intelligible reason.

No single node necessarily contains an instruction to suppress the
allegation.

**System output: the information does not travel.**

That is why intentionality cannot be the only diagnostic.

If the state waits for proof that somebody explicitly ordered a cover-up
before recognising a structural containment problem, it will miss
systems capable of producing equivalent outputs through distributed
incentives and constraints.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 12. 📈 Power Is A Variable, Not Background Decoration

This is the point at which conventional policy modelling can become
dangerously naive.

Power changes the environment.

Capital changes the environment.

Institutional access changes the environment.

Legal capacity changes the environment.

Control over information changes the environment.

Social credibility changes the environment.

The number and status of people with reasons to avoid disruption changes
the environment.

Potential national-security adjacency changes the environment.

A high-control perpetrator with access to several of those resources
presents a different systems problem from an offender without them.

That does not justify reversing the burden of proof.

It does not justify presuming guilt.

It does justify refusing to model every alleged perpetrator as though
they possess identical capabilities for evading scrutiny.

Criminology and safeguarding already understand that offenders adapt to
control systems.

A sufficiently powerful offender may be uniquely capable of:

-   identifying weak interfaces;
-   exploiting jurisdictional boundaries;
-   increasing the cost of scrutiny;
-   fragmenting information across institutions;
-   benefiting from other people's confidentiality obligations;
-   generating competing institutional interests;
-   delaying resolution;
-   exhausting individual challengers;
-   benefiting from uncertainty that would be less consequential for
    somebody without equivalent resources.

So the test cannot be:

> **Would the rules catch Bob?**

It must include:

> **What happens when the person being scrutinised can afford to use
> every available rule?**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 13. ⚖️ Bounded Protection Or Recursive Impunity

Confidentiality needs boundaries.

Privacy needs boundaries.

Litigation needs procedural safeguards.

National-security secrecy needs boundaries.

Those boundaries cannot simply mean that an authorised institution has
internally concluded that the boundary is appropriate.

A credible high-power safeguarding architecture needs mechanisms capable
of asking:

-   Is the protected interest precisely identified?
-   Is the restriction no broader than necessary?
-   Can information about alleged wrongdoing be separated from the
    genuinely protected material?
-   Is there an independent route for investigating the allegation?
-   Can the survivor meaningfully challenge the boundary?
-   Is somebody outside the institution whose interests are implicated
    able to review it?
-   Is the restriction time-limited or periodically reconsidered where
    appropriate?
-   Is there an audit trail?
-   Can aggregate patterns be detected even where individual material
    remains protected?
-   What happens if the protected institution itself may be implicated?
-   Who checks the checker?

Without those questions, protection can become recursive.

Person A is difficult to scrutinise because revealing A exposes
relationship B.

Relationship B cannot be exposed because it reveals operation C.

Operation C implicates liaison partner D.

D's information is protected because disclosure could damage the
intelligence relationship.

Document E therefore remains unavailable.

Document E may be the thing capable of corroborating what the woman
originally said about A.

And around we go.

**Forever is not an acceptable safeguarding architecture.**

## 14. 📣 And Then Britain Says: Speak Up, Ladies

This is where the policy rhetoric lands differently.

The state says:

> We must listen to survivors.

The survivor says:

> Here is information about violence.

The state says:

> We will relentlessly pursue perpetrators.

The survivor says:

> Okay. This one is important.

And suddenly the system may have to reconcile:

-   victim protection;
-   due process;
-   privacy;
-   freedom of expression;
-   confidentiality;
-   civil justice;
-   institutional reputation;
-   diplomatic interests;
-   national security;
-   evidential thresholds;
-   public accountability.

Those interests are real.

But that is precisely why a serious VAWG strategy cannot be designed
only around cases in which accountability is institutionally cheap.

> **A VAWG system cannot be evaluated only by what happens when the
> perpetrator is disposable.**

Its difficult test is what happens when accountability conflicts with
another institutionally valuable interest.

That is the slap.

Not:

> VAWG policy is fake.

Something much harder:

> **Your VAWG policy is incomplete unless it remains functional when the
> allegation travels upwards.**

## 15. 🍊 PR Americana: The Feedback Failure

This is why the node belongs in **The Feedback Environment**.

The failure is not only injustice in an individual case.

It corrupts the information available to the state itself.

Imagine the loop:

**STATE:** Please report violence.

**SURVIVOR:** Here is information about violence.

**SYSTEM:** This information encounters confidentiality, litigation
risk, evidential fragmentation, protected interests or secrecy.

**OUTPUT:** Information does not reach a durable accountable endpoint.

**STATE DATA:** Low reporting. Weak evidence. No substantiated pattern.
Limited institutional record.

**POLICY RESPONSE:** We need to understand why women are reluctant to
report.

At that point, the system risks diagnosing **women's reluctance** when
part of the variable may actually be **transmission cost**.

That is feedback corruption.

The system observes the output of its own architecture and mistakes it
for an independent description of reality.

And because high-power cases may be precisely the cases most capable of
generating extraordinary transmission costs, they may be
disproportionately likely to disappear from the feedback environment
used to understand institutional performance.

The absence of information then begins to authenticate itself.

> No evidence of a problem.

Because the evidence did not travel.

> Why didn't it travel?

Insufficient evidence of a problem.

**Excellent.**

## 16. 🧪 The High-Power Perpetrator Stress Test

A VAWG system serious about power needs a specific stress test.

Hold the alleged conduct constant.

Change only the institutional power of the alleged perpetrator.

### Baseline

The alleged perpetrator is Bob from down the road.

### Stress condition

The alleged perpetrator possesses some combination of:

-   exceptional wealth;
-   political access;
-   institutional authority;
-   elite legal capacity;
-   extensive reputation-management resources;
-   control over relevant records or organisations;
-   significant social credibility;
-   diplomatic importance;
-   intelligence or security adjacency;
-   relationships with other high-value actors.

Then ask:

1.  **Does the survivor's ability to report change?**
2.  **Does the likelihood of effective investigation change?**
3.  **Does access to corroborating information change?**
4.  **Does the cost of public-interest publication change?**
5.  **Does the number of protected interests intersecting with the
    allegation increase?**
6.  **Does the survivor gain an independent route around institutions
    that may themselves have interests at stake?**
7.  **Can genuinely sensitive information be disentangled from evidence
    of wrongdoing?**
8.  **Can secrecy decisions be meaningfully challenged?**
9.  **Can patterns across otherwise compartmentalised reports be
    detected?**
10. **Can the system reach an accountable endpoint without requiring the
    survivor personally to overpower the defendant's resources?**

If the answers deteriorate as perpetrator power rises, then perpetrator
power is not incidental context.

**It is part of the threat model.**

## 17. 🧭 What This Node Does Not Establish

This framework must not eat its own epistemology.

The existence of an NDA does not establish wrongdoing.

The existence of litigation does not establish a SLAPP.

The existence of privacy protection does not establish improper
suppression.

The existence of classified information does not establish intelligence
complicity.

The existence of national-security secrecy does not establish a
cover-up.

A powerful person's proximity to protected institutions does not
establish that those institutions protected them.

An information gap is not automatically evidence of what lies inside it.

The point is not to fill inaccessible space with accusation.

The point is to stop treating inaccessible space as analytically
meaningless.

Where potentially probative information becomes inaccessible, the
correct questions are:

> **What mechanism restricted it?**

> **What protected interest was claimed?**

> **Who assessed that interest?**

> **What independent review existed?**

> **Could the evidence of alleged violence be disentangled from the
> protected material?**

> **Was the restriction broader or longer than necessary?**

> **What accountability route remained available?**

That is evidence work.

Not conspiracy.

Not institutional deference either.

## 18. 🦆 The Material Consequence Of Bad Boundaries

Poorly bounded protection does not merely make researchers cross.

It changes survivor behaviour.

It changes journalist behaviour.

It changes publisher behaviour.

It changes witness behaviour.

It changes institutional memory.

It changes what can be corroborated.

It changes what enters court.

It changes what enters official statistics.

It changes what later investigators know existed.

It changes what future policymakers believe the historic problem looked
like.

And it can do all of that without anybody needing to erase the original
event.

The event happened.

The survivor knows it happened.

Perhaps several other people know fragments.

But the information never becomes sufficiently connected, transmissible
and durable to acquire institutional force.

That is why:

> **The right to speak is not the same thing as an information
> environment capable of hearing you.**

And that is why boundedness matters.

When the person accused is unusually capable of resisting scrutiny, weak
boundaries are not neutral.

They are exploitable system properties.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 19. 🪞 The Standard Cannot Be Institutional Sincerity

Government may sincerely care about VAWG.

Officials may sincerely care about national security.

Courts may sincerely protect privacy.

Lawyers may sincerely advise clients about risk.

Publishers may sincerely apply responsible evidential standards.

All of those things can be true.

A systems analysis still has to ask what their interaction produces.

**Good intentions at individual nodes do not guarantee a safe network
output.**

That is the point.

The relevant standard is therefore not:

> Did everybody mean well?

Nor:

> Can we prove somebody secretly intended impunity?

It is:

> **Can the architecture continue to investigate violence when doing so
> becomes expensive for powerful systems?**

If not, fix the architecture.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 20. 🛠️ Design Requirement: Make Power Expensive To Abuse, Not Expensive To Report

A high-power VAWG architecture should aim for a simple inversion.

At present, concentrated power can increase the potential cost of
scrutiny.

The safeguarding objective should be to ensure that increased
perpetrator power produces **increased robustness of independent
scrutiny**, rather than increased fragility.

That may require policy work across multiple systems rather than one
magical statute:

-   broader and properly bounded anti-SLAPP protection;
-   robust victim-confidentiality safeguards;
-   independent review where protected state interests intersect with
    allegations of serious wrongdoing;
-   mechanisms for disentangling genuinely sensitive material from
    evidence capable of being disclosed;
-   reliable routes for pattern detection across institutional silos;
-   meaningful protection for lawful reporting and public-interest
    investigation;
-   periodic review of secrecy where appropriate;
-   clear records of why restrictions were imposed;
-   safeguards where the institution controlling information may itself
    have an interest in the outcome.

The design principle is:

> **The more power capable of being brought to bear against scrutiny,
> the less the system should depend upon the individual survivor's power
> to withstand it.**

That is what "support victims and survivors" means when taken seriously
as systems engineering.

## 🏮 Footer

*🇬🇧 A Very British SLAPP* is a living node of the **Polaris Protocol**.\
It maps the gap between formal permission to speak and the practical
transmissibility of survivor information when allegations intersect with
concentrated power, litigation capacity, confidentiality or protected
state interests. It does not infer wrongdoing from secrecy; it treats
the ability to preserve accountability under conditions of unequal power
as a systems requirement.
