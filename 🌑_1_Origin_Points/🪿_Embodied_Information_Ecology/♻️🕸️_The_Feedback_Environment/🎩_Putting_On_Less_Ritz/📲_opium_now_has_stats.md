# 📲 Opium Now Has Stats

**First created:** 2026-10-01 \| **Last updated:** 2026-10-01\
*The opium did not disappear. It acquired telemetry. On consolation, attention, behavioural feedback, political agency, information defence, and what happens when the delivery system can observe the person using it.*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🛰️ Orientation --- Metaverse Killed The Radio Star

We have built an extraordinary information environment.

It can entertain us, educate us, introduce us to people we would never
otherwise meet, preserve relationships across continents, expose
wrongdoing, organise political movements, translate languages, sell us
socks, find us obscure nineteenth-century philosophy, show us a leopard
gecko eating a worm, provide mutual aid, coordinate disaster response,
make us laugh when we are miserable, and allow several million people to
spend an afternoon collectively asking what the fuck just happened.

This is not a node arguing that technology is bad.

It is not even a node arguing that distraction is bad.

It is asking whether we have perhaps put **too much fucking technology
into one feedback loop**.

A large part of the modern Western technology industry has developed
around commercially valuable capacities to capture attention, observe
behaviour, infer preferences, personalise information, predict likely
responses, and return material to users in ways intended to keep them
participating. The US Federal Trade Commission reported in 2024 that
major social-media and video-streaming companies engaged in extensive
surveillance of users in order to monetise personal information, and
that targeted advertising accounted for most revenue for many of the
companies it examined.[^1]

None of this means that every recommendation is manipulation, every
algorithm is sinister, every platform is identical, or every person who
enjoys scrolling has been hypnotised by Mark Zuckerberg.

It does mean that **behaviour has become commercially valuable
information**.

At roughly the same time, the digital environment has become part of the
infrastructure through which societies conduct news, politics, social
relationships, public communication and democratic participation.

That combination deserves rather more scrutiny than:

> number went up 👍

The organising principle of this node is **harm reduction**.

Not abstinence.

Not prohibition as a universal answer.

Not the fantasy that individual willpower can carry the entire burden of
system safety.

Not the opposite fantasy that no practice should ever be prohibited,
constrained or made illegal.

Harm reduction asks a more useful set of questions:

-   What is this thing doing?
-   What benefit does it provide?
-   What harm can it produce?
-   For whom?
-   Under what conditions?
-   What information does it collect?
-   What behaviour does it reward?
-   What happens after the immediate reward?
-   What vulnerabilities does the protective intervention itself
    introduce?
-   What would reduce harm without destroying the useful function?
-   And, crucially, **what happens next?**

Because the thing we are interested in is not merely the object.

It is the loop.

## 1. 💊 Marx's Extremely Misfortunate Banger

There is a sentence by Karl Marx that has escaped its enclosure so
comprehensively that it now frequently arrives without the argument it
belonged to:

> "It is the opium of the people."

The surrounding passage matters.

In the introduction to *A Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's
Philosophy of Right*, written in 1843--44, Marx is not sitting down to
produce a free-standing universal theory called **Why Religion Is Bad,
By Karl Marx**.

He is writing in a particular German philosophical and political
context, moving through a critique of religion toward a critique of the
social and political conditions that produce the need for religious
consolation. Immediately before the famous line, he describes religious
suffering as simultaneously an expression of real suffering and a
protest against real suffering. Religion is the "sigh of the oppressed
creature", the "heart of a heartless world", and the "soul of soulless
conditions".[^2]

Then comes the opium.

The scissored version makes the sentence sound almost ontological:

**religion = opium = passivity.**

The surrounding passage is more interesting.

Marx is preserving information about the wound.

The consolation exists because something hurts.

The expression is not meaningless because it does not automatically cure
the condition. The protest is not unreal because it occurs in symbolic
form. The relief is not imaginary merely because the material
circumstances remain.

A huge paraphrase might therefore be:

> Something hurts.
>
> You find something that makes living with the hurt more bearable.
>
> The relief can be real.
>
> The thing causing the hurt can still remain.

That is a much more useful starting point for the present information
environment than **RELIGION BAD, COMMUNIST MAN SAID SO**.

It also suggests a correction.

### The opium is not necessarily the thing

Religion can perform an opiate function.

So can:

-   television;
-   books;
-   shopping;
-   exercise;
-   gambling;
-   work;
-   Stoicism;
-   political commentary;
-   social media;
-   activism;
-   community;
-   therapy;
-   writing several million words in a public repository because
    apparently we all have hobbies.

None of these things is therefore **the opium** in every context.

The relevant question is functional:

> **What is this practice doing inside this particular feedback loop,
> for this person or population, at this time?**

A practice may provide analgesia.

It may provide avoidance.

It may provide recovery.

It may build capacity.

It may create solidarity.

It may extract attention.

It may do several of those things at once.

The category becomes useful only when we stop treating it as a moral
sticker to place on objects.

## 2. 🩹 The Distraction Is Not The Disease

"Distraction" is often treated as though it means something frivolous.

Psychologically, distraction can be a completely sensible form of
short-term state management.

Sometimes the nervous system is carrying more than can usefully be
processed in the present moment.

The appropriate response may genuinely be:

> I cannot do anything productive with this at 2 a.m. I am going to
> watch rubbish, read a book, play a game, stroke the cat, stare at the
> ceiling, or otherwise stop feeding this feeling until I have enough
> capacity to deal with it.

That is not necessarily denial.

It can be regulation.

The problem appears when a temporary regulatory strategy becomes the
whole circuit:

**distress → distraction → temporary relief → distress returns →
distraction → temporary relief**

The signal is repeatedly quietened.

The generator of the signal remains.

This gives us at least three functionally different loops that may look
very similar from outside.

### Short-term regulation

**I need the intensity to come down before I can do anything useful.**

Good.

### Chronic avoidance

**Whenever the signal appears, I remove awareness of it and return to
the same conditions without processing or acting on what generated it.**

Potential problem.

### Strategic recovery

**I reduce the immediate intensity so that I recover enough capacity to
address the underlying condition.**

Potentially extremely useful.

This is why the simple question---

> Did this make you feel better?

---is not enough.

The more useful question is:

> **What did feeling better make possible next?**

That distinction matters politically too.

A population that rests is not necessarily a population that has
surrendered.

A person who stops looking at the news is not necessarily indifferent.

Someone who refuses to spend every waking hour emotionally activated by
conditions outside their immediate control may be conserving the exact
resources required to act where action is possible.

Relief is not resolution.

But relief is not failure either.

## 3. 🏛️ Stoicism Was So Close

This is where the Stoic distinction gets irritatingly close to being
correct as a self-protective practice.

You cannot safely make your internal state entirely contingent on
controlling other people, institutions, events or outcomes.

There are things you can influence.

There are things you cannot influence.

There are things you cannot influence **right now**.

These are not the same category.

The useful move is:

> **I will not allow an external system to consume unlimited emotional
> resources merely because I cannot immediately control it.**

The dangerous mutation is:

> **I cannot control this immediately, therefore I should not attempt to
> alter it.**

That second proposition does not logically follow from the first.

This distinction matters because a coping practice can look like
accommodation from the outside while functioning as resource
conservation from the inside.

The person has stopped screaming at the machine.

Perhaps they have given up.

Perhaps they have gone to sleep.

Those are not equivalent system states.

And regrettably for the machine, sometimes they wake up with executive
function.

## 4. 🐟 Thank You, Capitalist Realism

Mark Fisher is useful here less as one quotation than as a course of
work.

*Capitalist Realism* gave a name to the sense that capitalism had become
not merely an economic system but the apparently realistic horizon
within which alternatives had to be imagined. Across Fisher's writing,
this sits beside recurring concerns with depressive hedonia, reflexive
impotence, bureaucratic absurdity, the privatisation of stress, lost
futures, consciousness-raising, and eventually the unfinished movement
towards *Acid Communism*.

The point is not that capitalism makes everybody apathetic.

That would be much easier.

The contemporary information environment can produce enormous quantities
of political feeling, political speech and political activity.

A systematic review in *Nature Human Behaviour* covering 496 studies
found digital-media use associated with increased political
participation and information consumption in some contexts, while also
finding associations with declining political trust, increasing populism
and growing polarisation, particularly in established democracies.[^3]

That is much more interesting than:

**internet makes people not care.**

It suggests the superficially paradoxical possibility of:

**more political information + more political expression + more
political participation + less trust + more polarisation + an enduring
subjective sense that very little can be changed**

The population can be politically noisy while transformative agency
feels scarce.

This matters when we look at the extraordinary amount of attention
generated when politics becomes conspicuous inside mainstream commercial
culture.

Music has contained politics for a very long time.

Labour songs existed.

Anti-war songs existed.

Folk existed.

Punk existed.

Reggae existed.

Hip-hop existed.

Anti-apartheid music existed.

Benefit concerts existed.

Artists have taken political positions and sometimes paid material,
professional and reputational costs for doing so.

The interesting contemporary datum is therefore not:

> Musician says political thing.

It is the **amount of system disturbance that can occur when a political
act becomes conspicuous inside a large commercial entertainment
environment**.

The politics was already there.

Contracts were there.

Corporate risk was there.

Audience segmentation was there.

Venue ownership was there.

Advertising was there.

International politics was there.

Artist economics were there.

Speech norms were there.

The conspicuous political act does not necessarily introduce politics
into an apolitical system.

Sometimes it makes the politics already structuring the system
**legible**.

And then everybody starts fucking yapping.

## 5. 🗳️ The Interface Is Absolutely Full Of Democracy

The modern citizen is offered choices constantly.

Vote.

React.

Like.

Dislike.

Follow.

Unfollow.

Subscribe.

Cancel.

Rate.

Review.

Personalise.

Post.

Petition.

Boycott.

Buy.

Don't buy.

Select your preferences.

Tell us how you feel.

There is agency everywhere at the level of interface.

The more difficult question is:

> **Is agency correspondingly abundant at the level of system state?**

This is where language such as **"uniparty"** becomes useful as evidence
of a perception while remaining unhelpful as a literal description of
political systems.

People use the term in the United States and the United Kingdom to
express a complaint that conventional electoral choices do not feel
sufficiently different on the structural issues they care about.

That does not establish that the parties are actually interchangeable.

It establishes that some voters experience their available choices as
insufficiently consequential.

That distinction matters.

Perceived constrained agency can produce many responses:

-   disengagement;
-   protest;
-   union activity;
-   direct action;
-   community organising;
-   third-party politics;
-   left populism;
-   right populism;
-   mutual aid;
-   political withdrawal;
-   or attempts to break conventional political machinery altogether.

The right-populist route can be particularly dangerous where democratic
constraints themselves become framed as the enemy, but it is not the
only route through the problem.

The deeper information-ecology issue is that people can be surrounded by
opportunities to **express preference** while doubting their ability to
**change conditions**.

That is not absence of choice.

It is a possible mismatch between **interface agency** and **system
agency**.

And a motivational quote is not going to fix it.

The internet has approximately nine hundred million of those already.

## 6. 📱 Enter Han, Looking Extremely Pleased With Himself

Byung-Chul Han's *Psychopolitics: Neoliberalism and New Technologies of
Power* is unusually useful here because Han is interested in power that
works not simply by prohibiting action but through apparent freedom,
participation, self-optimisation and voluntary disclosure.

Verso describes the book as an account of neoliberalism discovering the
"productive force of the psyche", with Big Data central to Han's
analysis.[^4]

The important move for this node is not to treat Han as a complete
empirical description of every platform.

It is to use his conceptual knife.

A disciplinary system says:

**No.**

A psychopolitical system can say:

**Yes.**

Go on.

Express yourself.

Participate.

Optimise.

Disclose.

Measure.

Tell us what you like.

Tell us what you hate.

Tell us what frightens you.

Tell us what makes you horny.

Tell us what you bought.

Tell us who you love.

Tell us who you cannot fucking stand.

Tell us what you think of the government.

Tell us what you think of the people who think the opposite about the
government.

Tell us whether this content was useful.

Thank you for improving our systems.

This is where Marx and Han become extremely unpleasant company for one
another.

The contemporary opium does not necessarily tell you to stop
participating.

It may invite you to **participate continuously**.

And the participation itself may produce commercially valuable
information.

## 7. 🎰 The Casino Has A News Desk

We need to be careful with terminology here.

Not every recommender system is an addiction machine.

Not every personalisation system is behavioural modification.

Not every use of personal data is profiling.

Not every notification is coercion.

If we collapse distinct mechanisms into one spooky category, we make the
analysis worse.

So:

Where the evidence supports **profiling**, call it profiling.

Where it supports **targeting**, call it targeting.

Where it supports **personalisation**, call it personalisation.

Where it supports **behavioural inference**, say behavioural inference.

Where a regulator uses **addictive design**, we can say addictive
design.

And, unfortunately, regulators are now using it.

In February 2026, the European Commission preliminarily found TikTok in
breach of the Digital Services Act in relation to what it called
addictive design, focusing on features including infinite scroll,
autoplay, push notifications and a highly personalised recommender
system. The Commission said its preliminary investigation indicated that
these features could contribute to compulsive behaviour and reduced
self-control.[^5]

In July 2026, the Commission announced preliminary findings against Meta
concerning what it likewise described as the addictive design of
Instagram and Facebook, again identifying infinite scroll, autoplay,
push notifications and highly personalised recommender systems among the
features under investigation.[^6]

These are **preliminary regulatory findings**, not final adjudications.

That distinction stays in.

But the vocabulary is no longer confined to people shouting **PHONE
BAD** on daytime television.

Meanwhile, the FTC's 2024 staff report found that the business models of
many major social-media and video-streaming companies incentivised mass
collection of user data for monetisation, particularly targeted
advertising. It also found personal information being fed into
algorithms, data analytics and AI systems, with little or no ability for
users and non-users to opt out of some automated uses.[^7]

So we have built systems where:

-   attention is valuable;
-   behavioural information is valuable;
-   personalisation is valuable;
-   repeated participation is valuable;
-   and political, social and emotional life increasingly passes through
    the same infrastructure.

Perhaps the casino did not need a news desk.

Yet here we are.

## 8. 💨 Six Hours Of Yapping Later, The Landlord Still Owns The Building

This is where it is important not to become stupid in the opposite
direction.

Online political participation is not fake politics.

Digital systems can:

-   spread information quickly;
-   expose wrongdoing;
-   connect dispersed communities;
-   reduce barriers to participation;
-   coordinate protests;
-   support fundraising;
-   preserve evidence;
-   build political identity;
-   provide mutual aid;
-   create international solidarity;
-   and move information across borders that powerful institutions would
    rather keep contained.

The systematic evidence is mixed precisely because digital media can
support democratic participation while also creating or amplifying other
democratic risks.[^8]

So:

> **Posting can matter.**

The problem is not that posting happens.

The problem is that the same observable activity can perform very
different functions.

Imagine:

**distress → open platform → consume → express → receive
recognition/stimulation → temporary regulation → return**

That might be:

-   education;
-   organising;
-   solidarity;
-   community;
-   evidence gathering;
-   emotional processing;
-   avoidance;
-   compulsive use;
-   commercial extraction;
-   or six of those at once.

From the platform's telemetry, several versions can look remarkably
similar:

**click → read → comment → share → return**

But from the person's perspective, and from the perspective of
democratic capacity, they may be completely different loops.

This is why **engagement** is such a spectacularly impoverished measure
of what humans are doing.

It tells you that something happened.

It does not necessarily tell you what the thing meant.

## 9. 🕸️ The Opium Is Not A Thing. It Is A Loop.

This is the central cybernetic correction.

Do not ask only:

> Is social media opium?

Ask:

> **What is this social-media use doing in this loop?**

Do not ask only:

> Is religion opium?

Ask:

> **What does this religious practice make possible next?**

Do not ask only:

> Is Stoicism resignation?

Ask:

> **Is this conserving capacity or extinguishing action?**

Do not ask only:

> Is distraction avoidance?

Ask:

> **What happens when the distraction ends?**

The model is closer to:

**person → environment → signal → practice → immediate output → feedback
→ subsequent capacity/action → environmental consequence**

Then trace it.

### Loop A --- analgesia without change

**distress → relief → return to unchanged condition → distress**

Opiate function is a reasonable description.

### Loop B --- relief restores agency

**distress → relief → restored capacity → action → changed condition**

Very different.

### Loop C --- participation becomes extraction

**distress → expression → engagement → behavioural data → optimised
return stimulus → more engagement**

Han is now waving.

### Loop D --- participation becomes organisation

**distress → expression → recognition by others → coordination →
collective action → changed political environment**

Same platform.

Different loop.

### Loop E --- mixed system

**distress → community + useful information + advertising exposure +
behavioural profiling + political action + exhaustion + friendship +
three hours lost arguing with an egg account**

Welcome to the internet.

The point is not to purify human behaviour into perfectly emancipatory
actions.

That is not how people work.

The point is to become more capable of noticing **what function a loop
is currently performing**.

## 10. 🛡️ Unfortunately We Require The Population For The Country

And now the problem stops being merely personal.

NATO's 2024 approach to countering information threats states that
strategic competitors seek to exploit the openness, interconnectedness
and digitalisation of Allied societies. It says hostile information
activities may constitute a national-security threat and identifies
social media and online services as part of the environment in which
hostile information operations occur.[^9]

This matters.

Commercial platforms and hostile information operations are **not the
same thing**.

A recommendation system designed to keep somebody watching cooking
videos is not an enemy information operation.

A company trying to sell targeted advertising is not thereby conducting
cognitive warfare.

Do not collapse the categories.

The more defensible proposition is also more interesting:

> **Commercially constructed behavioural and attentional infrastructure
> can create capabilities and vulnerabilities that hostile actors may
> exploit.**

The attack environment does not have to be constructed from scratch.

Societies already contain:

-   highly networked populations;
-   rapid emotional transmission;
-   personalised information environments;
-   commercially valuable behavioural data;
-   recommendation systems;
-   influencer networks;
-   measurable engagement;
-   social graphs;
-   large-scale advertising infrastructure;
-   and populations accustomed to receiving political information
    through privately governed digital systems.

NATO's own framing makes clear that openness, interconnectedness and
digitalisation can be exploited by hostile actors.[^10]

So the information-defence question becomes:

> **Why would a society deliberately maintain unnecessary behavioural
> and attentional vulnerabilities inside infrastructure its own defence
> institutions regard as strategically consequential?**

"Because engagement makes money" is an explanation.

It is not a complete systems evaluation.

This is not:

**SOCIAL MEDIA → NEXT 9/11.**

That causal claim would be ridiculous without evidence.

It is:

**if information infrastructure is an attack surface, reducing
unnecessary exploitability is defence work.**

Congratulations on the quarterly results.

Unfortunately we require the population for the country.

## 11. 🧨 Local Optimisation, Global Vulnerability

This is a classic systems problem.

A subsystem becomes extremely good at producing the thing it is rewarded
for producing.

Suppose the rewarded variable is:

**engagement**

or:

**advertising conversion**

or:

**time on platform**

or:

**data useful for prediction**

The system optimises locally.

But the larger ecology contains other variables:

-   privacy;
-   child development;
-   psychological capacity;
-   social trust;
-   democratic participation;
-   pluralism;
-   shared informational environments;
-   information resilience;
-   cybersecurity;
-   national security;
-   commercial sustainability;
-   and the apparently radical proposition that humans might
    occasionally want to do something else.

A local optimisation can therefore become a global vulnerability.

No conspiracy is required.

No one has to sit in a room and announce:

> Gentlemen. Our objective is to make NATO populations easier to fuck
> with.

The system merely needs to reward one outcome strongly enough while
failing to price the costs imposed elsewhere.

This is why regulation is not automatically an external enemy of
innovation.

Sometimes regulation is the mechanism by which the larger system informs
the subsystem:

> **That metric is not the only thing that matters.**

## 12. 👶 "For The Children" Is Not A Threat Model

Child protection is important.

That does not make every intervention described as child protection
safe.

Age assurance is a particularly useful example because it demonstrates
why **the control mechanism itself belongs inside the threat model**.

Age assurance does not necessarily mean handing over a passport or
creating a biometric database.

The UK Information Commissioner's Office lists multiple approaches,
including self-declaration, AI or biometric systems, technical measures,
tokenised third-party checking and hard identifiers such as
passports.[^11]

Some approaches may use biometric recognition or biometric age
estimation; others do not.[^12]

The ICO's guidance explicitly warns against collecting more data than
necessary, repurposing age-assurance information for other uses, and
failing to conduct appropriate due diligence on third-party
providers.[^13]

Ofcom likewise states that all age-assurance methods involve processing
personal data and that services must satisfy both online-safety and
data-protection requirements rather than treating privacy and safety as
a trade-off.[^14]

Good.

That is the correct shape of the problem.

The question is not:

**AGE CHECKS BAD.**

It is:

> **What information does this safety intervention require, where does
> that information travel, how long is it retained, who can access it,
> what happens when the provider is compromised, and can the same safety
> function be achieved with less sensitive data?**

A safety intervention that creates a valuable new attack surface has not
finished its safety analysis.

"For the children" is a reason to do the threat modelling.

It is not permission to stop.

## 13. 🩹 Harm Reduction For The Information Environment

Harm reduction is useful because it refuses two equally unhelpful
fantasies.

The first:

> **BAN THE THING.**

The second:

> **THE USER CHOSE IT, THEREFORE THE SYSTEM HAS NO RESPONSIBILITY.**

Sometimes prohibition is appropriate.

Some practices should be illegal.

Some data should not be collected.

Some products should not be offered to children.

Some design practices may warrant restriction.

But prohibition is a tool, not a worldview.

The more useful toolbox includes:

-   safer defaults;
-   data minimisation;
-   strict retention limits;
-   meaningful user controls;
-   limits on sensitive profiling;
-   separation of datasets;
-   non-profiled recommender options;
-   restrictions tailored to children;
-   notification controls;
-   friction where friction is protective;
-   independent auditing;
-   researcher access;
-   incident reporting;
-   security requirements;
-   interoperability where appropriate;
-   transparency requirements;
-   defined liability;
-   and prohibition where the evidence supports prohibition.

The Digital Services Act already works in this general territory by
imposing systemic-risk duties on very large platforms and search
engines, including risks involving civic discourse, electoral processes,
public security and wellbeing.[^15]

This is not an argument for a single perfect regulator.

Regulators can create data hazards too.

Governments can overreach.

Identity systems can be breached.

Centralisation can create attractive targets.

Safety mechanisms can exclude people.

A badly designed regulatory system can reproduce the exact systems error
it is trying to fix.

So the rule applies recursively:

> **Evaluate the intervention as part of the environment it changes.**

Cybernetics is irritating like that.

This is not a demand that everybody delete Instagram and become chair of

This matters because people need experiences of efficacy.

Not constant efficacy.

Not omnipotence.

Not getting their own way every time.

Just enough evidence that:

**I did something → something happened**

The outside world needs feedback too.

## 15. 🔁 Democracy Needs Closed Feedback Loops

An engaged democracy requires more than elections.

It requires people to believe that participation can enter a system and
be processed.

A healthy democratic feedback loop looks something like:

**input → processing → decision → observable consequence →
explanation/accountability → new input**

The answer to public participation cannot always be:

> Thank you for your feedback. Your views are important to us.

and then the void.

Sometimes the democratic answer is **no**.

That is fine.

Democracy does not mean every citizen receives their preferred outcome.

But functioning democratic systems need intelligible routes through
which people can see:

-   where information went;
-   who considered it;
-   what evidence mattered;
-   what decision was made;
-   why;
-   how it can be challenged;
-   how errors are corrected;
-   and how officeholders can ultimately be replaced through lawful
    democratic mechanisms.

This is where a politics made mostly of messaging becomes inadequate.

If somebody's existing experience is:

**I express preferences constantly and nothing consequential appears
responsive to me**

then another branded exercise inviting them to **HAVE YOUR SAY ✨** may
simply reproduce the condition.

Political communication is not political efficacy.

You cannot Canva-template your way out of that.

## 16. 🧠 The Mental-Health Coincidence We Are Not Yet Allowed To Call Causation

There is an uncomfortable historical juxtaposition here.

Commercial systems became increasingly capable of monetising prolonged
attention and behavioural disclosure.

At the same time, many communities experienced severe reductions in
parts of the offline infrastructure that can support social connection,
prevention and early help.

In England, the Government's 2025 national youth strategy states that
local spending on youth services fell by 73% between 2010 and 2023, with
more than 1,000 youth centres closing and more than 4,500 youth-worker
roles lost.[^16]

The Institute for Fiscal Studies separately reports very large
reductions in English council spending on services for young people and
Sure Start over the post-2010 period.[^17]

These are real trajectories.

What we **do not presently have** is evidence establishing that
social-media or AI companies caused those public-service reductions in
order to obtain more exploitable populations or more valuable
behavioural data.

Chronology is not causation.

Convenience is not proof.

The defensible structural observation is:

> **A society can simultaneously underinvest in people's capacity for
> social support, prevention and distress regulation while building
> commercial information systems that benefit from prolonged attention
> and extensive behavioural disclosure.**

Those incentive structures can become mutually convenient without a
mastermind.

That is already enough to warrant examination.

We do not need to invent the bit we cannot prove.

## 17. 💰 Congratulations, You May Keep Capitalism

There is a strange tendency to discuss technology regulation as though
the available choices are:

**unregulated private innovation**

or:

**the state owns the server now**

This is not how most mature high-consequence industries work.

Private industries coexist with:

-   safety standards;
-   licensing;
-   reporting duties;
-   audit;
-   liability;
-   insurance;
-   capital requirements;
-   technical standards;
-   consumer protection;
-   and regulators.

The difficult questions are:

**which standards?**

**which regulator?**

**which evidence?**

**which liability?**

**which risks?**

**which unintended effects?**

not:

**regulation yes/no?**

The insurance point is especially useful.

Insurance depends on risk being sufficiently legible to price.

Cyber risk is difficult precisely because losses can be correlated,
systemic, fast-moving and difficult to bound. Regulation does not
magically solve that.

But clear security requirements, incident reporting, minimum controls,
auditability and defined responsibilities can help make some risks more
legible.

This matters commercially.

A mature regulatory settlement can make an industry easier to finance
and insure by reducing uncertainty about what responsible operation
requires.

That does not mean every risk becomes insurable.

It means **risk reduction is not automatically anti-market**.

Silicon Valley may, with appropriate supervision, retain capitalism.

## 19. 🎩 Putting On Less Ritz

The answer is not digital puritanism.

Humans are allowed pleasure.

Humans are allowed distraction.

Humans are allowed to waste time.

Humans are allowed to watch seventeen consecutive videos about a
capybara.

Not every minute of a life needs to be politically useful.

In fact, the demand for permanent political activation is itself
exhausting and potentially self-defeating.

The proposition is much smaller.

**Less unnecessary extraction.**

**Less unnecessary profiling.**

**Less unnecessary behavioural inference.**

**Less unnecessary engagement optimisation.**

**Less unnecessary concentration of sensitive information.**

**Less routing of essential social and democratic functions through
systems whose primary optimisation target is commercial engagement.**

Not **no Ritz**.

**Less Ritz.**

The FTC's findings about extensive commercial surveillance, the European
Commission's current enforcement work on addictive design, and NATO's
information-threat framework come from very different institutional
directions.[^18][^19][^20][^21]

Put beside one another, they do not prove a single grand causal theory.

They do establish enough of the component parts to justify a systems
question:

> **What happens when commercially optimised attention infrastructure
> becomes part of the democratic and security environment of a
> society?**

That is a perfectly reasonable thing to regulate.

## 20. 🔴 The Big Shiny Red Button

There is a small systems problem in repeatedly placing enormous shiny
red buttons on the floor and then becoming surprised when humans press
them.

The crude individual-responsibility model says:

**stimulus → person should exercise restraint**

The behavioural model says:

**salient stimulus → low-friction action → immediate reward → learned
behaviour → repeated exposure → repeat**

If a system repeatedly constructs high-salience, low-friction,
immediately rewarding affordances, individual restraint cannot carry the
entire burden of system safety.

This does not abolish personal agency.

It acknowledges that environments participate in behaviour.

That is the whole point of designing them.

Nobody advertises a product and then solemnly insists that advertising
cannot possibly affect choice.

Nobody designs a recommender system and then claims recommendations have
no effect on what people encounter.

Nobody adds push notifications because they hope users will ignore them.

Design matters.

Humans matter.

Feedback matters.

We are allowed to discuss all three at once.

## 21. 🧭 So What Do I Actually Want?

At the individual level, a small amount of mindfulness goes a long way.

Not optimisation.

Not self-surveillance.

Not turning your leisure into another productivity dashboard.

Just occasionally:

> **What did I come here for?**
>
> **Did I get it?**
>
> **What is this doing to me now?**
>
> **What does this make possible next?**

Sometimes the answer is:

> I came here to watch a stupid video and I have successfully watched a
> stupid video.

Excellent.

No notes.

Sometimes:

> I came here to find one piece of information and I have now been
> furious for four hours.

Perhaps leave.

Sometimes:

> I came here because something terrible happened and I found people who
> knew what to do next.

That matters.

At the societal level, the questions become:

> **What do we want this infrastructure to do?**
>
> **What are we rewarding it for doing?**
>
> **What else does it do because of those incentives?**
>
> **Which populations bear the externalities?**
>
> **What information is being collected because collection is profitable
> rather than necessary?**
>
> **What happens when somebody hostile discovers the same feedback
> loops?**
>
> **What safety measures create new information hazards?**
>
> **Which functions should never have been routed through this machinery
> in the first place?**

The strongest defence against political and social life collapsing
entirely into commercially mediated simulation is not abstinence.

It is maintaining enough consequential human agency outside the
interface that people have somewhere real to go with what they learn,
feel and decide.

Marx gives us the wound and the opium.

Han asks what happens when participation, freedom and disclosure
themselves become productive resources.

Fisher helps describe an environment in which people can recognise that
something is wrong while struggling to imagine effective alternatives.

Psychology reminds us that relief, avoidance, recovery and resolution
are not the same thing.

Cybernetics asks what the loop is actually doing.

Information defence asks who else can use it.

Harm reduction asks how to keep the useful functions while reducing the
damage.

We are merely suggesting that perhaps the casino does not also need to
operate the news desk.
