# 🎩 Putting On Less Ritz  
**First created:** 2026-09-16 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-20  
*Dear America: we need to have a little chat about what you keep selling your supposed best mates.*  

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Britain and America have a Special Relationship.

We know this because everybody keeps fucking telling us.

We are best friends.  
Closest allies.  
Cousins.  
Partners.  
Two nations divided by a common language.  
There are flags.  
There are state visits.  
There are extremely expensive dinners.  
Occasionally somebody mentions Churchill.

Lovely.

Unfortunately, being someone's best mate does eventually confer upon them the right to say:

**Mate. What the fuck are you doing.**

*Putting On Less Ritz* is a collection of British complaints about American technology, commercial logic, infrastructure, law, language and the recurring assumption that whatever America has most recently decided constitutes **THE FUTURE** must therefore be adopted by everybody else in roughly the form in which America has chosen to sell it.

It is not an argument that American technology is uniformly bad.

That would ruin the joke, because quite a lot of it is extremely fucking useful.

The complaint is that America has an extraordinary capacity to make something useful and then, somewhere between versions 2 and 14, decide that what the customer really wanted was a compulsory ecosystem, six subscriptions, an account, a new cable, another data centre, an AI assistant nobody requested and a Terms of Service document governed by the laws of California.

Britain would like to submit some feedback.

We have brought exhibits.

## 📋 The Exhibits

The prosecution would like to establish that this is not our first rodeo.

### 📋 Exhibit A — VLOOKUP

Sometimes the revolutionary technology is a spreadsheet function that lets Linda stop copying the same number into seventeen different places.

This is important.

Not everything useful has to **transform humanity**.

Sometimes it just has to make Tuesday less annoying.

### 📋 Exhibit B — Windows Vista

A foundational lesson in the difference between:

**we have improved your computer**

and:

**we have changed your computer quite substantially and would now like you to discover which bits of your life no longer work.**

The customer is allowed to notice this distinction.

### 📋 Exhibit C — iPhone Batteries

Welcome to the exciting technological future.

The future is extremely elegant.

Unfortunately, you cannot open it.

### 📋 Exhibit D — Fucking Cables

USB-A.

USB-B.

Mini-USB.

Micro-USB.

Lightning.

USB-C.

Thunderbolt.

DisplayPort.

HDMI.

Various things which look sufficiently similar that you will confidently take the wrong one to the meeting.

Humanity has placed machines on Mars.

We remain unable to guarantee that the cable in the drawer fits the thing on the desk.

Please reflect.

### 📋 Exhibit E — Printer Ink

At some point, somebody looked at the ordinary human desire to put words onto paper and thought:

**What if this became an ongoing financial relationship?**

No further questions.

### 📋 Exhibit F — AI v UK Law

And now we arrive at artificial intelligence.

America appears to believe that it is offering Britain the transformation of human intelligence.

Britain has reviewed the proposition.

There appears to have been a translation error.

We would instead like to purchase:

**less fucking admin.**

## 🇺🇸 Dear America

The recurring misunderstanding is that British scepticism means we do not want the technology.

Quite the contrary.

We would like the useful bit.

We would often like the useful bit **very much**.

We would like computers which work with the computers we already own.

We would like software which does not require an infrastructure programme before Margaret in Accounts can use it.

We would like tools that find documents, compare versions, extract information, update trackers, complete routine forms and draft routine correspondence.

We would like technology to identify the seventeen things Dave has been sent and tell him which three things actually require Dave.

We would like machines to absorb some of the enormous quantity of repetitive informational labour modern organisations have accidentally created for human beings.

This would be fantastic.

Please sell us that.

What we are rather less interested in purchasing is a philosophical revolution, an anthropomorphic synthetic colleague, an entirely new hardware estate, several million tonnes of compute infrastructure and **DISEMBODIED CLIPPY**.

We already have people.

The people are tired.

Help the people do the paperwork.

## 🧮 This Is What Productivity Actually Looks Like

There is a persistent technology-industry tendency to mistake **visibility** for **value**.

Replacing a human being is visible.

Generating something spectacular is visible.

Putting a humanoid interface on software is visible.

Announcing that an industry has been transformed is extremely visible.

Removing seven minutes of pointless labour from something somebody does forty times a week is less visible.

Unfortunately, the boring one may be the thing that actually improves productivity.

A useful system might:

- find the right document;
- notice that two versions disagree;
- extract the three relevant paragraphs;
- populate information already held elsewhere;
- identify duplicated work;
- remember where the fucking file went;
- translate between incompatible administrative formats;
- draft the standard response;
- flag the unusual case;
- leave the unusual case to a human being;
- and then **stop**.

No personality required.

No artificial friendship required.

No little bouncing thing asking whether we are writing a letter.

We remember Clippy.

Do not test us.

## 🗣️ We Already Speak English

There is also a smaller but surprisingly revealing problem.

Britain speaks English.

America also speaks English.

These are related products.

They are not, however, identical products.

British English carries information through register, understatement, indirectness, politeness structures, contextual phrasing and linguistic conventions which American-designed systems have a recurring tendency to interpret as unnecessary decoration requiring optimisation.

So the British user does not simply tell the machine what they want.

The British user sometimes has to remember how the machine expects an American to tell the machine what they want.

Then the British user translates themselves into American English.

Then the American software helpfully translates them back into what it thinks is clearer English.

Then the British user puts all the bits back because **THAT WAS WHY I WROTE IT LIKE THAT IN THE FIRST PLACE**.

This is not assistance.

This is additional admin wearing an assistant hat.

Please take the hat off.

## 🌳 Fuck Off Hobbiton

Britain is a relatively small, densely populated collection of islands.

This has consequences.

We do not have infinite land.

We do not have infinite electricity.

We do not have infinite water.

We also have landscapes, towns, farms, habitats, history, planning law and a population with quite strong opinions about what happens to the field over there.

Therefore:

**“Simply build an enormous quantity of new infrastructure” is not a frictionless proposition.**

This is particularly relevant when the infrastructure exists partly because software has been designed around the assumption that abundant compute is somebody else's problem.

No.

Efficiency includes resource efficiency.

A technology which saves fifteen minutes of office work by requiring Mordor behind the industrial estate has not necessarily completed the efficiency calculation.

Fuck off Hobbiton.

We have it good here.

We do not need Big Folk data centres everywhere.

**DID ANY OF YOU READ THAT BOOK OR NOT.**

## 💰 We Are Not Replacing Everything Again

There is another sentence with which the technology industry should become reacquainted:

**We already own things.**

Computers.

Phones.

Networks.

Printers, unfortunately.

Databases.

Servers.

Software.

Public-sector systems held together by several decades of procurement decisions and one employee called Steve who understands why the database gets upset on Thursdays.

A proposal which begins:

> Step 1: replace your existing hardware estate

has not yet demonstrated an efficiency saving.

It has demonstrated a sales opportunity.

These are not synonymous.

This matters particularly when technology is being sold to governments, public services, schools, universities, small businesses and ordinary people who do not possess an infinite refresh budget.

Also, America:

we remember 2008.

Maybe don't open the conversation with **give us loads more money**.

Read the room.

## 🕸️ The Actual Feedback Problem

Underneath the satire is a serious systems problem.

America has become extraordinarily good at building technologies and commercial systems upon which other countries depend.

That creates power.

It also creates feedback.

If your technology becomes our infrastructure, its failure modes become partly our problem.

If your companies become our suppliers, their incentives become partly our problem.

If your platforms mediate our information environment, their design choices become partly our problem.

If your AI systems operate inside British institutions, British law becomes your problem too.

This is what interdependence does.

You cannot spend decades constructing yourself as an indispensable ally, supplier, security partner, technology centre and financial power and then become offended when the countries entangled with you develop opinions about how you are behaving.

We have opinions because **you matter**.

This should not be surprising.

## 🎩 The Special Relationship Includes Complaining

The mythology of the Special Relationship sometimes seems to require Britain to demonstrate friendship through perpetual reassurance.

This is silly.

Have you met British people?

Complaining **is** relationship maintenance.

There is an enormous difference between:

**we would like America to fail**

and:

**America, for fuck's sake, could you please stop doing that.**

Indeed, the second sentence generally implies that the speaker retains some investment in America successfully stopping doing that.

A relationship which permits praise but interprets criticism as hostility is not especially special.

It is merely fragile.

Britain should be able to tell America when American systems are creating British problems.

America should be able to tell Britain when British systems are creating American problems.

Then both countries can argue about it for six months, produce an unreadable joint communiqué, quietly fix 70% of the problem and announce that the relationship has never been stronger.

**That** is diplomacy.

## 🪞 We Are Telling You Because We Still Expect Better

There is something considerably more affectionate underneath the irritation than the language initially suggests.

America's allies frequently judge it against standards America itself helped popularise.

Open societies.

Democratic accountability.

Free expression.

Rule of law.

Innovation.

Competition.

Individual liberty.

Government constrained by institutions.

Technology improving ordinary life.

The frustration does not necessarily arise because nobody believed any of it.

Sometimes the frustration arises because people believed enough of it to develop expectations.

And because the United States has made itself important enough that failures inside American systems do not reliably remain inside America.

That is the price of centrality.

You get influence.

You get markets.

You get allies.

You get enormous cultural reach.

You also get your supposed best mates occasionally turning up with a folder marked:

**EXHIBITS A–F.**

## 🧿 The Proposition

So here is the counter-offer.

Keep making clever things.

We like clever things.

Some of us are enormous nerds.

But:

make them interoperable;

make them repairable;

make them efficient;

make them legally deployable outside California;

make them capable of understanding that other countries exist;

make them work with things people already own;

make the boring functions excellent;

stop turning every useful tool into a compulsory ecosystem;

stop assuming human replacement is inherently more impressive than human enablement;

and, occasionally, when your allies say **this bit is causing us a problem**, consider the radical possibility that we are providing useful product feedback.

We do not require the transformation of human intelligence.

We require the document.

Sarah had it last.

Nobody knows where she saved it.

**Please start there.**
