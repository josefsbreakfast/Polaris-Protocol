## 1. 🎯 Sanctions Need An Objective

The first question is not:

> **What can we take away?**

It is:

> **What outcome are we trying to produce?**

Sanctions can perform several different functions.

They can attempt to:

- deter future behaviour;
- constrain the target's capacity to continue behaviour;
- increase the cost of continuing behaviour;
- disrupt a particular activity or network;
- deny access to specific resources, services or infrastructure;
- signal a legal or diplomatic boundary;
- induce compliance with a defined requirement;
- protect another system while remediation occurs.

Those functions are not interchangeable.

```text
DETER
→ make future behaviour less attractive

CONSTRAIN
→ reduce capacity to continue behaviour

DISRUPT
→ interrupt an active pathway

COERCE
→ increase costs until behaviour changes

PROTECT
→ reduce exposure while a vulnerability is unresolved

SIGNAL
→ communicate a boundary or collective position
```

A measure may do more than one.

But if nobody can state the primary objective, it becomes difficult to assess whether the sanction is proportionate, effective, counterproductive, obsolete, or merely still there because nobody wants to be the person who removes it.

## 2. 🧾 First, Show Me The Receipts

Sanctions are consequential interventions.

Therefore:

```text
CLAIM
↓
EVIDENCE
↓
ATTRIBUTION
↓
DECISION
```

Not:

```text
VIBES
↓
PRESS RELEASE
↓
ASSET FREEZE
```

The evidentiary burden will vary with the legal framework and measure involved.

But the systems principle is stable:

> **Do not destroy uncertainty merely because a policy response would be administratively convenient.**

Possible findings include:

```text
ESTABLISHED
PROBABLE
PLAUSIBLE BUT UNCONFIRMED
CONTESTED
INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE
NOT ESTABLISHED
```

These are different information states.

A government can sometimes need to act under uncertainty.

That makes accurate representation of uncertainty **more important**, not less.

The framework should therefore preserve:

- source provenance;
- confidence;
- competing explanations;
- attribution limits;
- evidentiary gaps;
- what would change the assessment;
- the legal threshold actually being applied.

This is the Frances Kelsey rule travelling abroad:

> **If the evidence does not support the proposition you are being asked to authorise, “not yet” remains an available answer.**

## 3. 🕸️ Sanction The Lever, Not The Vibe

The next problem is topology.

Suppose Actor A is doing something undesirable.

That does not mean every asset, industry, relative, citizen, customer, supplier, cultural product or institution associated with Actor A controls the behaviour.

Map the system.

```text
behaviour
   ↓
decision-maker
   ↓
authority
   ↓
resources
   ↓
dependencies
   ↓
intermediaries
   ↓
external enablers
```

Then ask:

> **Where can an intervention actually alter the pathway?**

This matters because badly targeted sanctions can impose substantial cost on people who have little capacity to change the behaviour being sanctioned.

A sanction should therefore be evaluated not merely by:

> **Does this hurt?**

but by:

> **Does the cost reach an actor or resource capable of changing the relevant system?**

Pain is not synonymous with leverage.

## 4. ⚖️ Proportionality Is An Engineering Requirement

Current UK sanctions enforcement policy explicitly treats proportionality and fairness as enforcement principles.

That is not softness.

It is control-system design.

If every breach receives the maximum response, the system loses gradation.

If every disagreement immediately produces total exclusion, there is little remaining escalation capacity.

A useful ladder might look like:

```text
0 — OBSERVE
    gather evidence / establish baseline

1 — NOTICE
    identify concern / request information

2 — REMEDIATION
    define corrective action + review period

3 — TARGETED RESTRICTION
    constrain specific person / service / transaction / capability

4 — SECTORAL MEASURE
    widen restriction where the relevant system requires it

5 — COORDINATED ESCALATION
    allied / multilateral measures

6 — COMPREHENSIVE RESTRICTION
    exceptional high-cost measure
```

Not every regime needs every rung.

The important thing is that the rungs exist conceptually.

**Escalation should contain information.**

A target should be able to distinguish:

> *This got worse because the underlying behaviour continued.*

from:

> *They hate us and will keep escalating regardless.*

Those messages produce different incentives.

## 5. 🚪 A Sanction Needs An Exit

This is perhaps the most neglected part of the popular sanctions imagination.

If the purpose is behavioural change, then the target needs to know what behavioural change would matter.

```text
TRIGGER
↓
MEASURE
↓
REMEDIATION CONDITIONS
↓
EVIDENCE OF COMPLIANCE
↓
REVIEW
↓
LIFT / MODIFY / RETAIN
```

A sanction with no credible off-ramp can stop functioning as an incentive.

If compliance does not alter the sanction, then compliance has less instrumental value.

That does **not** mean every sanction must disappear immediately when a target makes a promise.

Verification matters.

Durability matters.

Repeated behaviour matters.

Legal obligations matter.

But the framework should answer:

- What condition triggered the measure?
- What change is being sought?
- What evidence would demonstrate that change?
- Who evaluates that evidence?
- When is the decision reviewed?
- Can the measure be suspended?
- Can it be narrowed?
- Can it be reimposed?
- What happens after sustained compliance?

## 6. 🔄 Sanctions Are A Feedback Environment

A sanction changes the environment in which people behave.

That means the response becomes new information.

```text
sanction
   ↓
target adapts
   ↓
markets adapt
   ↓
intermediaries adapt
   ↓
allies adapt
   ↓
adversaries adapt
   ↓
new behaviour becomes visible
   ↓
sanctioning system updates
   ↺
```

Possible adaptations include:

- compliance;
- substitution;
- circumvention;
- concealment;
- rerouting;
- localisation;
- alliance formation;
- black-market development;
- legal challenge;
- political mobilisation;
- retaliation;
- genuine behavioural change.

A sanctions framework therefore cannot be:

```text
DESIGN
↓
IMPOSE
↓
NEVER LOOK AGAIN
```

The measure itself becomes part of the information environment.

**Observe what it teaches the system to do.**

## 7. 🪃 Measure The Unintended Effects Too

A sanction can advance one objective while damaging another.

Possible second-order effects include:

- civilian hardship;
- supply-chain disruption;
- humanitarian constraints;
- market concentration;
- circumvention industries;
- corruption opportunities;
- increased dependency on alternative powers;
- allied friction;
- retaliation;
- reduced information access;
- reduced civil-society capacity;
- technological substitution;
- incentives for domestic repression.

This does not mean:

> **Sanctions have side effects, therefore never use sanctions.**

It means:

> **Side effects are outputs of the system and therefore belong in the evaluation.**

A useful review asks:

```text
INTENDED EFFECT
What changed?

TARGET EFFECT
Who bore the cost?

BEHAVIOURAL EFFECT
Did conduct change?

SYSTEM EFFECT
What rerouted?

HUMAN EFFECT
Who else was affected?

STRATEGIC EFFECT
Did the wider objective become easier or harder?

REVERSIBILITY
What happens if the measure is lifted?
```

If the only metric is **sanction still exists**, the system is measuring itself rather than its objective.

## 8. 🪪 Licensing Is Not A Loophole

Real sanctions systems already contain licences and exceptions.

That is important.

A licence can allow a regime to distinguish:

```text
PROHIBITED ACTIVITY
from
LEGITIMATE / HUMANITARIAN / NECESSARY ACTIVITY
```

without abandoning the underlying restriction.

This is another reason sanctions should be understood as **information architecture** rather than merely punishment.

The system needs to represent distinctions.

Food is not necessarily weapons technology.

Humanitarian medicine is not necessarily luxury consumption.

A journalist is not necessarily a state institution.

A researcher is not necessarily a procurement intermediary.

A cultural work is not necessarily—

🇺🇸: Ah.

🦊: **Do not get ahead of yourself. We are not at Schedule 4 yet.**

Licensing can preserve legitimate exchange while maintaining pressure on the pathway actually being targeted.

That requires:

- clear criteria;
- usable application routes;
- competent decision-makers;
- reasonable response times;
- auditable decisions;
- emergency pathways where delay itself creates harm.

## 10. 🛠️ Remediation Before Escalation — Where Appropriate

Not every problem requires immediate punitive escalation.

Sometimes the useful first intervention is:

> **Mate. Something is broken. Fix it.**

This is especially relevant to cross-border technical systems.

Suppose Britain detects a reproducible computational anomaly involving an American provider.

The first useful pathway may be:

```text
observe anomaly
↓
preserve evidence
↓
notify counterparty
↓
reproduce
↓
root-cause analysis
↓
defined remediation
↓
deadline / SLA
↓
test
↓
close
```

If the problem is fixed:

**excellent.**

The objective was not to obtain the emotional satisfaction of sanctioning an American company.

The objective was to stop the fucking problem.

If the problem is ignored, concealed, repeatedly reproduced, or cannot be remediated through ordinary cooperation, then a sanctions or restriction framework may provide escalation options where a lawful basis exists.

That is why **debugging as diplomacy** and sanctions architecture belong near one another.

One tries to solve the problem cooperatively.

The other tells the system what happens if a sufficiently serious problem cannot be resolved that way.

## 12. 🧪 A Sanction Is A Hypothesis

Every sanctions measure contains an implicit causal claim.

For example:

```text
IF
access to resource X is restricted

THEN
actor Y will have less capacity / greater cost

WHICH WILL
alter behaviour Z

BECAUSE
X is materially connected to Z
```

That claim may be correct.

It may be partly correct.

It may become incorrect as the system adapts.

So write the hypothesis down.

Then test it.

This prevents the policy from quietly changing from:

> **We imposed this because we expected X.**

to:

> **The sanction still exists, therefore it must be doing something useful.**

A feedback system needs an observable output.

## 13. 🧯 Deterrence Requires Legibility

A deterrent works partly because another actor can anticipate consequences.

That requires some degree of legibility.

```text
IF YOU DO X
→ Y MAY FOLLOW

IF YOU STOP X
→ Y CAN BE REVIEWED

IF YOU ESCALATE X
→ FURTHER MEASURES ARE AVAILABLE
```

Perfect predictability is neither possible nor always desirable.

But a completely arbitrary sanctions environment can undermine the behavioural logic the measure is supposed to create.

The target needs enough information to understand:

- what boundary exists;
- what conduct crossed it;
- what consequences followed;
- what further consequences are possible;
- what corrective behaviour matters.

**Deterrence is partly an information problem.**

The sanction communicates through the cost.

This is why the Tolkien threat is, regrettably, pedagogically useful.

🇬🇧: **Fix your algorithms or we start explaining that the Shire is an argument for limits on technological power.**

🇺🇸 DEFENCE ESTABLISHMENT: **Jesus fucking Christ, get DARPA on the phone.**

A deterrent does not need to be used if the prospect of its use changes behaviour.

## 14. 🪜 The Escalation Ladder

A generic Polaris ladder:

| Stage | Function | Example form |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Observation | evidence gathering, monitoring |
| 1 | Notification | diplomatic/technical notice, information request |
| 2 | Remediation | corrective action plan, deadline, audit |
| 3 | Targeted measure | individual/entity/service restriction |
| 4 | Sectoral measure | selected technology, finance, trade or service restriction |
| 5 | Coordinated measure | allied or multilateral restriction |
| 6 | Broad restriction | exceptional comprehensive measures |
| R | Review | assess behaviour, effects and evidence |
| ↓ | De-escalation | narrow, suspend, license or lift |
| ↑ | Re-escalation | restore or strengthen if conduct resumes |

This is not a universal legal template.

Different regimes have different statutory authorities, thresholds and available measures.

It is a **reasoning scaffold**.

The important bit is the arrows.

A ladder that only travels upward is not much of a control system.

It does not.

```text

Who controls it?

1. DEFINE THE PROBLEM
2. PRESERVE THE EVIDENCE
3. REPRESENT UNCERTAINTY
4. ATTRIBUTE CAREFULLY
5. STATE THE OBJECTIVE
6. MAP THE LEVER
7. SELECT THE NARROWEST USEFUL MEASURE
8. PROTECT NECESSARY EXCEPTIONS
9. DEFINE REMEDIATION
10. DEFINE REVIEW
11. OBSERVE ADAPTATION
12. MEASURE EXTERNALITIES
13. ESCALATE IF REQUIRED
14. DE-ESCALATE WHEN WARRANTED
15. TERMINATE WHEN THE OBJECTIVE NO LONGER JUSTIFIES THE MEASURE
```

Or:

> 🦊 **Tell them what the problem is, show them the receipts, make the consequence legible, leave a route back, and keep checking whether the fucking thing works.**

That is a sanctions framework.

## 23. 🎩 Finding

Sanctions are not inherently effective because they are sanctions.

They are interventions in adaptive systems.

Their usefulness depends on relationships between:

- evidence;
- attribution;
- objectives;
- leverage;
- proportionality;
- coordination;
- implementation;
- exceptions;
- feedback;
- unintended effects;
- remediation;
- review;
- exit.

A good sanctions architecture does not merely ask:

> **How hard can we hit?**

It asks:

> **What are we trying to change, what intervention has a plausible pathway to changing it, what else will the intervention change, and how will we know when to stop?**

Which leaves the Special Relationship in an entirely manageable position.
