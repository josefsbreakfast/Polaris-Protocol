# 🎮 Capitalism Working as Designed
**First created:** 2026-10-12 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-12  
*Dear America: are we ready to come home and get the jam and mud cleaned off us, or are we still setting fire to the garden?*

## 🛰️ Orientation

Dear America,

We understand that you have had a very exciting afternoon.

You have invented some extraordinary technologies. You have encouraged entrepreneurship. You have celebrated disruption. You have promised to democratise the means of production, although we suspect some of you imagined the means would remain quite expensive and located primarily in your own data centres.

Unfortunately, some people have taken you at your word.

They have made software.

Some of it does things that your software does. Some of it reads your file formats. Some of it runs games whose original publishers have long since stopped selling or supporting them. Some of it emulates hardware you would rather everybody purchased again. Some of it is the work of very serious engineers. Some of it is the work of enthusiasts who have spent an absolutely deranged amount of time trying to make a computer pretend to be another computer.

And now coding agents are changing how quickly certain parts of this work can be attempted.

**Your system is working as designed.**

This is not a bug. It is a feature.

The question is what happens when the people who have spent years selling technological disruption find themselves among the disrupted.

And, cousin, whether we might discuss some generally applicable rules before everybody decides that the only available alternative is an expensive private army of lawyers.

## 1. 🎮 Look At All This Fucking Software

For readers who have not been watching the shed engineers: this is not one bloke making a Photoshop-shaped rectangle and declaring the software industry dead. There is an enormous, long-running ecosystem of alternative applications, compatibility layers, game-engine reimplementations, decompilation projects and emulators.

**Examples, not an exhaustive inventory:**

| Project | What it does | What it demonstrates | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| [GIMP](https://www.gimp.org/) | Free and open-source image editing | An established alternative to proprietary image editors, including Photoshop for some workflows | Not a recent AI reconstruction of Photoshop |
| [Krita](https://krita.org/) | Free and open-source digital painting | Independent creative software competing for artists' time and budgets | An independently developed product, not an Adobe clone |
| [Inkscape](https://inkscape.org/) | Free and open-source vector graphics | Alternatives to proprietary illustration workflows | Compatibility and functionality do not themselves prove copying |
| [Blender](https://www.blender.org/) | Open-source 3D creation suite | Sophisticated creative production outside proprietary licensing | Longstanding community and institutional development |
| [LibreOffice](https://www.libreoffice.org/) | Office suite supporting common document formats | A practical alternative to proprietary productivity suites | File compatibility is a different issue from copying source code |
| [Wine](https://www.winehq.org/) | Compatibility layer for running Windows applications on other operating systems | Reimplementation of interfaces can reduce dependence on a platform | Wine is not a Windows emulator in the usual hardware-emulation sense |
| [ReactOS](https://reactos.org/) | Open-source operating-system project aiming for Windows compatibility | The ambition to reproduce platform behaviour independently | Not a complete drop-in replacement for current Windows |
| [Dolphin](https://dolphin-emu.org/) | GameCube and Wii emulator | Software can reproduce the behaviour of older console hardware | Emulation is not the same as distributing copyrighted games |
| [RPCS3](https://rpcs3.net/) | PlayStation 3 emulator | Technically demanding platform reimplementation | Game files, firmware and circumvention raise separate questions |
| [OpenMW](https://openmw.org/) | Open-source engine for *Morrowind* | Rebuilding an engine can preserve and extend a game's playability | Typically requires legitimately obtained game assets |
| [OpenRCT2](https://openrct2.io/) | Open-source reimplementation of *RollerCoaster Tycoon 2* | Community engineering can extend an older commercial game | Original game assets are generally still required |
| [DevilutionX](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX) | Source-port/reimplementation work for *Diablo* and *Hellfire* | Community reconstruction can improve portability and preservation | Does not grant ownership of Blizzard's original assets |
| [Ship of Harkinian](https://www.shipofharkinian.com/) | PC port project for *Ocarina of Time* | Decompilation and reconstruction can enable new platforms and features | Requires separate handling of copyrighted game material |

Notice what this table does **not** establish. It does not establish that these projects were built using generative AI. Most predate the current coding-agent wave. It does not establish that any particular project infringes copyright. Nor does it establish that all of them are commercially equivalent to the products or platforms they replace.

What it establishes is that **the technical and social practice of building alternatives already exists at scale**. AI-assisted programming enters an environment full of people who know how to decompile, document, test, port, emulate, patch, maintain and share software.

And now those people have additional tools.

**🎩 America:** We have democratised software development.

**🎮 Internet:** Brilliant. I've made some software.

**🎩 America:** Wonderful. What sort?

**🎮 Internet:** Yours, sort of.

**🎩 America:** ...

**🎮 Internet:** Mine works on Linux.

**🎩 America:** THAT IS NOT THE POINT.

**🎮 Internet:** It is to me.

### 🧩 What about the new AI-assisted recreations?

This is the part requiring a **separately verified, dated release ledger**: named repositories; who published them; whether AI was actually used; whether the project contains copied source or assets; how much works; whether it was released; and whether the rights-holder responded. A viral demonstration is not a working replacement. A developer's claim is not an independent audit. An open-source licence attached to a repository does not automatically resolve the provenance of everything inside it.

The Photoshop-like project recalled in the surrounding discussion still needs its precise identity verified before being treated as an exhibit. Do not confuse it with GIMP simply because both involve image editing.

That caution isn't a retreat from the argument. It's how we make the argument survive contact with someone who can read a repository.

## 2. 💰 Congratulations, Comrades. Capitalism Has Happened.

The joke is not that the workers have organised a revolutionary council and seized a factory.

The joke is that the means of *reproducing* some forms of commercial functionality are becoming more accessible, and the people using them may have no particular political programme at all.

They might be preserving an abandoned game. They might be annoyed about a subscription. They might want software to work on a different computer. They might be curious. They might be competing for profit. They might simply be somebody called `xX_DragonSlayer_2007_Xx` who thinks this sounds like a laugh.

None of those motivations automatically makes the resulting work lawful or unlawful.

But the market proposition is recognisable: **lower barriers to entry permit new suppliers to challenge incumbents**.

This is the bit where we are apparently supposed to applaud.

**🎩 America:** We believe in the free market.

**🎮 Internet:** Excellent. We've entered it.

**🎩 America:** With whose permission?

**🎮 Internet:** Sorry. I thought you said *free*.

To be clear, a free market is not a copyright-free market. Property rights and enforceable contracts are among the rules that make markets possible. The point is not that the internet may take anything it likes. The point is that **incumbency is not itself a protected intellectual property right**.

The company may own its code, artwork, trade marks and other protected material. It does not necessarily own every useful idea, workflow, interface convention or function that a competitor might independently implement.

That boundary is where the actual law lives.

## 3. 🎨 Oh, NOW We're Discussing Intellectual Property?

For years, writers, illustrators, musicians, photographers, developers and other creators have raised questions about the use of their work in training and deploying generative models.

What was copied? Was permission required? Is the use lawful? Is the output substituting for the creator's work? What is the commercial value of the original? What evidence can the creator obtain? Who can afford to bring the case?

These are difficult questions. Different activities trigger different legal tests. Training a model on a dataset is not legally identical to copying a software binary, extracting game assets, reproducing protected artwork or independently writing a compatible application.

**But it is entirely reasonable to ask whether the principles being defended remain consistent when the affected rightsholder changes.**

If a major software company objects to unauthorised use of its work, what exactly is the principle? Does it also protect the independent illustrator? The small studio? The writer whose catalogue has been ingested? The musician who cannot finance five years of litigation?

We are not asking whether every claim should succeed.

We are asking whether **the rule is general**, or whether it becomes especially vivid when the complainant has a legal department.

**🇺🇸:** They're using AI to reproduce our intellectual property.

**🇬🇧:** Oh dear.

**🇺🇸:** Without permission.

**🇬🇧:** How dreadful.

**🇺🇸:** It threatens our commercial model.

**🇬🇧:** Does it?

**🇺🇸:** We need protection.

**🇬🇧:** Cousin.

**🇬🇧:** **WELCOME TO THE FUCKING CONVERSATION.**

## 4. ⚖️ Cousin, We Had a Framework

Not a perfect framework. Not a universal answer. And certainly not a British claim to have personally invented copyright while everybody else was out playing baseball.

**A framework.**

The UK has copyright legislation, including the [Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/contents), with specific rules and exceptions relevant to computer programs and some forms of text-and-data analysis. The US has the [Copyright Act](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/) and a context-sensitive fair-use doctrine, alongside rules and case law addressing software and interoperability. Both systems have unresolved questions about generative AI; neither is a legal vacuum.

That is precisely why we should discuss what existing rules do, where they are uncertain, and what changes would actually be useful.

The choice is not simply **no rules** versus **an enormous new Ministry of Computer Permission**.

It can be a choice between:

- defined lawful uses and clearly identified prohibited conduct;
- workable licensing and evidence arrangements;
- meaningful remedies accessible to creators who are not multinational corporations;
- protection for legitimate interoperability and independent competition;
- cross-border coordination where the activity is already cross-border;
- proportionate technical standards and security obligations;
- rules that apply to the people making frontier models **and** the people using them to build competitors.

**🇬🇧:** Cousin, we had a framework.

**🇺🇸:** We don't want excessive regulation.

**🇬🇧:** Neither do we.

**🇺🇸:** We want innovation.

**🇬🇧:** Lovely. So do we.

**🇺🇸:** We want to protect our intellectual property.

**🇬🇧:** **THEN PERHAPS WE COULD DISCUSS WHAT THE FUCK THE RULES ARE.**

## 5. 🧾 Congratulations. You've Invented a Subscription to Lawyers.

Imagine a large software company that is not itself one of the largest AI developers. It has a valuable catalogue, paying customers and shareholders who would prefer the catalogue to remain valuable.

A new wave of technically capable competitors arrives. Some are lawful. Some might not be. Some use AI. Some don't. Some are in countries where enforcement is difficult. Some disappear and reappear under different names.

The company can negotiate a favourable agreement with one model provider. Fine. That might be sensible.

But the agreement does not automatically bind every other provider, model, developer or jurisdiction. Nor does it make independent competition disappear.

So the firm buys monitoring. Lawyers. Licensing advice. Technical protections. Security. Compliance. Insurance. More lawyers. Perhaps a proprietary service layer designed to make leaving the ecosystem harder.

Not all of these are caused by deregulation. Some are ordinary business costs. Cybersecurity protects systems and data against intrusion; it does not stop somebody independently writing lawful competing software. Copyright remedies are not a substitute for secure engineering.

But **fragmented legal uncertainty can add repeated, defensive costs across an entire industry**.

One firm might find a private deal rational. If every firm must privately negotiate the same fundamental questions, the collective result can be absurdly expensive.

**🎩 America:** We don't want expensive regulation.

**🇬🇧:** Understandable.

**🎩 America:** We'll litigate.

**🇬🇧:** Against whom?

**🎩 America:** Everybody who infringes our rights.

**🇬🇧:** In every jurisdiction?

**🎩 America:** Where necessary.

**🇬🇧:** Indefinitely?

**🎩 America:** ...

**🇬🇧:** Darling, you've invented a subscription to lawyers.

And unlike Sandra's spreadsheet, this one does not appear to save anybody Thursday.

## 6. 🏚️ Move Fast and Break Things. You Live in the House.

There is a serious distinction between disrupting an incumbent business, reducing unnecessary bureaucracy and dismantling the institutions that make markets governable.

Successful investors and founders may have genuine expertise in scaling technology, identifying unmet demand and reorganising production. That does not establish that courts, administrative competence, public research, infrastructure, standards bodies and regulatory capacity were merely obstacles to their success.

Some of those institutions were part of the reason the success was possible.

Different accelerationist and anti-regulatory thinkers make different arguments. It would be lazy to pretend that all technology investors want the state abolished, or that advocacy of rapid innovation amounts to an endorsement of theft. The serious research task is to name actual thinkers, quote their actual positions and trace demonstrable relationships to policy—not turn a shared dinner invitation into a conspiracy diagram.

But we can ask the structural question without inventing a villain:

**How much of the house can you knock down before you discover that you needed the roof?**

Contracts need enforcement. Property rights need courts. Markets need payment infrastructure and credible information. Businesses need a degree of policy predictability. International trade needs rules even when the parties dislike one another.

If you profit from the stability of these systems, they are not merely somebody else's administrative problem.

**🇺🇸:** We're going to remove the unnecessary structural supports.

**🇬🇧:** Have you had a survey?

**🇺🇸:** We move fast.

**🇬🇧:** Yes, we've noticed.

**🇺🇸:** We break things.

**🇬🇧:** Cousin.

**🇬🇧:** **YOU LIVE IN THE FUCKING HOUSE.**

## 7. 🕸️ The Feedback Environment Has Entered the Chat

This is why the node belongs in *The Feedback Environment* rather than simply in a folder labelled *Things That Are Annoying About Software*.

The same technological capabilities that enable new products also change the environment in which existing products must compete. That competition changes commercial incentives. Those incentives influence demands for law, enforcement and institutional capacity. The responses then reshape the conditions under which the technology is developed and used.

```text
Commercial investment in frontier AI
               ↓
More accessible development capability
               ↓
Cheaper attempts at software reconstruction
               ↓
New competitors and disputed uses
               ↓
Pressure on established revenue and rights
               ↓
Demands for protection, litigation or clearer rules
               ↓
New costs, incentives and market structures
               ↺
```

This is a **proposed feedback mechanism**, not a claim that every arrow has already been measured across the software industry. The pace and effects vary dramatically by product and technical method.

A second loop matters just as much:

```text
Uncertain or fragmented rules
               ↓
Repeated private enforcement and negotiation
               ↓
High fixed legal and compliance costs
               ↓
Greater advantage for well-resourced firms
               ↓
Risk of concentrated market power
               ↓
Pressure for tailored rules or exceptions
               ↺
```

And here is the trap: regulation designed only to protect established companies can itself become a barrier to smaller competitors. **Good rules must protect creators without making lawful interoperability or market entry prohibitively expensive.**

The point is not to make the world safe for every incumbent business model.

It is to make the rules of competition intelligible enough that people can invest, create, challenge and defend their rights without requiring an army of solicitors merely to discover what the rules might be.

## 8. 🏛️ You Cannot Buy a Stable Future One Favour at a Time

A private agreement can settle a private dispute. An executive decision can change policy. A court can interpret existing law. A legislature can change the statute. These are different mechanisms with different reach and durability.

For a business deciding where to invest over the next decade, predictability matters. So does knowing whether a licence, exemption, regulatory permission or favourable interpretation will survive a change in personnel or policy.

It is tempting to secure a narrow concession protecting one's own catalogue. It may even be rational in the short term.

But it is not necessarily a settlement for the industry, let alone for the smaller creators who cannot negotiate from the same position.

A durable framework requires political institutions capable of doing the work. It need not require everyone to like one another, vote alike or agree on a philosophy of technological progress.

It requires them to identify the problem and negotiate rules that will still make sense when the next product arrives.

**🎩 America:** Can't we just make a deal?

**🇬🇧:** You can make *a* deal.

**🎩 America:** Excellent.

**🇬🇧:** With one party, about one set of rights, under one set of terms.

**🎩 America:** ...

**🇬🇧:** Would you like the rest of the market to know what the rules are?

## 9. 🍿 International Competition Has Not Gone Away

America is not conducting this experiment in an empty room.

Other states, firms, researchers and developers observe US choices and adapt to them. Some will compete through price. Some through technical capability. Some through regulation, procurement, standards or access to markets.

It would be careless to claim that China automatically wins whenever American policy becomes uncertain, or to claim that a particular foreign actor caused the problem. But it would be equally careless to imagine that American firms can indefinitely absorb rising coordination costs without consequences for international competitiveness.

**🇨🇳:** 🍿

**🇺🇸:** This doesn't concern you.

**🇨🇳:** 🧃

**🇬🇧:** Cousin, I fear the global market has failed to remain politely outside while you sort yourselves out.

## 10. 🦊 Cousin, We Have Ideas

At this point a small fox appears carrying a folder.

The fox does not propose a world government. The fox does not require universal agreement on the moral character of capitalism. The fox is not sweating. The fox has discovered that some administrative coordination is cheaper than everybody independently recreating the same administrative nightmare.

**🦊:** Good news, chaps. We have discovered a framework.

**🇺🇸:** We don't want more bureaucracy.

**🦊:** Neither do we.

**🇺🇸:** We don't want to surrender sovereignty.

**🦊:** Nobody asked.

**🇺🇸:** We want innovation.

**🦊:** Wonderful. So do we.

**🇺🇸:** We need to protect our businesses.

**🦊:** Yes. And the artists. And the developers. And the customers. And the people who need the software to continue working on Thursday.

**🇺🇸:** So what are you suggesting?

**🦊:** Establish which uses are lawful. Make licensing and remedies workable. Protect interoperability. Make the evidence auditable. Coordinate where rules collide. Stop using bespoke contracts to pretend a system-wide problem has been solved.

**🇺🇸:** Sounds expensive.

**🦊:** Have you priced the alternative?

The framework is a **proposal to investigate**, not an already-negotiated settlement. Its components would need legal, technical and economic assessment, including safeguards against regulatory capture and excessive compliance burdens.

But the fox has a point.

**You do not have to like one another to agree where the load-bearing walls are.**

## 11. 🧸 Are We Ready to Come Home?

Dear America,

We are not asking you to stop inventing things.

Quite the opposite. Some of us are enormous nerds. We like your clever things. We buy your clever things. We would very much like the clever things to work with the other clever things we already own.

We are also not suggesting that every independent software project is an act of theft, that every company deserves to retain its market position forever, or that copyright law should become a universal veto on competition.

We are suggesting that **you cannot spend years celebrating the productive power of disruption and then act astonished when the productive power of disruption becomes available to other people**.

If those people infringe your rights, the law should provide appropriate remedies.

If they compete lawfully, you may have to compete back.

If the law is unclear, perhaps it would be sensible to negotiate rules that work for more than the handful of organisations capable of buying their own private settlement.

And if you are thinking of removing the institutions that would make such a settlement possible, we would gently remind you that the courts, standards, public infrastructure and administrative capacity are not merely decorative features of the house.

You have had a very exciting afternoon.

You have made some extraordinary things.

There is jam on the doorframe. There is mud on the carpet. Somebody has taken the matches into the garden. An investor is explaining that the flames represent unprecedented opportunity.

We appreciate your enthusiasm.

But, cousin:

**Are we ready to come home and get the jam and mud cleaned off us?**

**Or are we still setting fire to the garden?**

Because this is not a bug.

**Capitalism is working as designed.**

And, before somebody burns the fucking house down—

**we had a framework.**

## 📚 Evidence Notes — What Is Established, and What Still Needs Checking

The named projects in Section 1 are established examples of alternative software, compatibility engineering, emulation or game-engine reconstruction. Their existence is not evidence that they were generated by frontier AI models. Their official project sites are linked directly so readers can inspect what each actually does.

The **newer AI-assisted replication wave** should be expanded only with verified examples that record: project name, publication date, developer claim, repository or demonstration, actual AI involvement, functional completeness, code and asset provenance, commercial status, and any documented rights-holder response. The earlier conversational research contained candidate projects that require verification; they are deliberately **not presented here as confirmed facts**.

For the legal discussion, the UK and US statutory links above are starting points, not substitutes for case-specific analysis. Before publishing an updated legal survey, check contemporary judgments, consultations, legislation and corporate positions, including any developments after the node's creation date.

For the political-intellectual thread, *The End of Reality* is a contextual reading lead, not a licence to attribute a single view to all Silicon Valley investors. Add named primary sources before making claims about individual advisers, donors or policymakers.

The economic feedback diagrams are analytical models. They identify mechanisms to investigate; they are not quantified forecasts.
