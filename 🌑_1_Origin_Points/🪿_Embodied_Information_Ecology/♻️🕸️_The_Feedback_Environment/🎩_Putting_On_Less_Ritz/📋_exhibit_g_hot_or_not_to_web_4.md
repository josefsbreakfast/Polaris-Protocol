## 🛰️ Orientation — Congratulations On Web 4. Have You Finished Web 2?

America has brought us another exciting technological proposition.

This one is quite large.

The proposition is that computing may be moving beyond the phone.

Instead of the internet being primarily somewhere we visit through a rectangle, the next layer may become increasingly:

- ambient;
- multimodal;
- spatial;
- wearable;
- AI-mediated;
- context-aware;
- agentic;
- and embedded in the ordinary physical environment around us.

Meta has spent years trying to build towards precisely this kind of future.

In 2014, Facebook bought Oculus for approximately $2 billion and described virtual reality as a possible next social and communications platform. In 2021, Mark Zuckerberg described the metaverse as an **“embodied internet”** and said Meta wanted to help build the next computing platform. By 2026, Meta was explicitly describing AI glasses as an all-day assistant capable of understanding the world from the wearer’s perspective.

This is not, in itself, stupid.

Annoyingly, quite a lot of it is interesting.

Some of it may be extremely useful.

Unfortunately, Britain has reviewed the previous work.

This exhibit is **not** an argument that Meta should never build another thing.

It is not an argument that a university website from 2003 determines the moral character of every technology built by Meta in 2026.

It is not an argument that Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Oculus, Quest, Horizon, Llama, AI glasses and whatever Mark has started constructing since breakfast are all the same product.

They plainly are not.

This is a design-provenance question.

> **If Meta wants to help build the next layer of the human information environment, what did it learn from the information environments it already built?**

More specifically:

> **What changed in the informational relationship between the system, the user, and the human being who is being observed?**

Because Web 4 — if we are going to use that imperfect but useful shorthand — is not merely a better website.

It potentially increases the sensorium.

It potentially moves computation closer to:

- vision;
- speech;
- memory;
- relationships;
- movement;
- physical surroundings;
- immediate intention;
- and action in the world.

So before the computer follows us outside, we would like to conduct a small regression test.

## 1. 🔥 Hot or Not

In October 2003, Harvard student Mark Zuckerberg built Facemash.

The contemporary account in *The Harvard Crimson* is unusually useful because there is no need to retrofit the origin story twenty years later.

Zuckerberg said he obtained photographs from Harvard House online facebooks, compiling student ID photographs into a website that presented two people at a time and asked users to choose which was “hotter”. The site also ranked students according to attractiveness.

The photographs had not been supplied by those students for that purpose.

The system was popular very quickly.

It was also contested very quickly.

Around 450 visitors reportedly generated at least 22,000 votes before the site came down. Individuals and student organisations objected. Fuerza Latina and the Association of Black Harvard Women were among the groups whose lists carried criticism. Zuckerberg apologised. Harvard’s Administrative Board subsequently considered allegations involving security, copyright and individual privacy arising from the unauthorised use of the photographs.

So there is an important correction to the simplified mythology here.

### 👩 The Women Did Actually File A Bug Report

The privacy problem was not discovered in 2018.

It was not discovered by Congress.

It was not discovered after somebody invented machine learning.

It was not discovered after Facebook acquired a few billion users.

**People noticed immediately.**

The feedback arrived.

In fact, the feedback was sufficiently legible that Zuckerberg himself told *The Crimson* that the privacy issues did not seem readily surmountable and shut the site down.

A few months later, thefacebook.com launched on a materially different basis. Users voluntarily registered. Zuckerberg emphasised extensive privacy controls. Contemporary reporting explicitly connected those controls to the reputational aftermath of Facemash.

Harvard’s later official electronic facebook likewise emphasised privacy and making information available when students permitted it.

So this is not a story in which nobody learnt anything.

That would be easier.

The much more interesting question is:

> **How much of the original correction became load-bearing?**

Because the underlying problem was visible before Facebook existed.

Information had been collected in one context.

The information was technically accessible.

A programmer acquired it.

The programmer changed its purpose.

Other people interacted with the new representation.

Their reactions generated additional information.

The people represented inside the system had not chosen the new informational relationship.

That is not merely an embarrassing anecdote about a nineteen-year-old man.

That is an information architecture.

And unfortunately for everyone, it scales beautifully.

## 2. ♻️ The Original Feedback Loop

Facemash was not Facebook.

Facebook is not Facemash with better CSS.

We are not doing historical phrenology for corporations.

What matters here is the **design primitive**.

```text
information about human
        ↓
acquisition
        ↓
computational representation
        ↓
ranking / comparison
        ↓
other humans respond
        ↓
behaviour becomes data
        ↓
system observes response
        ↺
```

This is a feedback loop.

The computer does not merely display information.

It changes the environment in which people encounter information, observes what they do next, and can use those responses to organise subsequent information.

There is nothing inherently evil about this.

Recommendation systems are feedback systems.

Search engines are feedback systems.

Spam filters are feedback systems.

Navigation systems are feedback systems.

A thermostat is a feedback system.

The problem is not **feedback**.

The problem is that feedback systems require decisions about:

- what is being observed;
- what counts as a signal;
- whose behaviour matters;
- what objective is being optimised;
- what can be inferred;
- who can inspect the model;
- who can contest the output;
- what happens to people represented by the system;
- and what happens when the person being represented is not the person operating the system.

That last distinction matters enormously.

### 👁️ User ≠ Observed Human

The person clicking is not necessarily the person being judged.

The person uploading is not necessarily the person depicted.

The person wearing the camera is not necessarily the person entering its field of view.

The person asking the AI is not necessarily the person the AI is being asked about.

This is where a very old problem begins following us into very new technology.

> **Technical accessibility is not contextual permission.**

A photograph can be visible and still have been supplied for a particular social purpose.

A voice can be audible and still not have been offered as training material.

A face can be present in physical space without becoming an invitation to extract every computationally inferable property from it.

A person can be observable without consenting to every possible transformation of that observation.

Or, more simply:

> **A photograph does not consent merely because a server can retrieve it.**

This is why Facemash belongs inside **Embodied Information Ecology**.

The pixels did not acquire a new moral property when they moved servers.

The **relationship** changed.

The purpose changed.

The audience changed.

The processing changed.

The power changed.

The information therefore changed in practical human meaning.

## 5. 🔨 Mark Zuckerberg Has Located A Hammer

Facebook/Meta’s institutional repertoire is broad, but a recurring pattern is architectural control.

A problem appears.

The organisation asks whether it can:

- build;
- buy;
- integrate;
- automate;
- rank;
- redesign;
- scale;
- vertically integrate;
- acquire more compute;
- or create a new technical layer around the problem.

Often this is exactly what an engineering organisation should do.

If the database is slow, please fix the fucking database.

If the model is inefficient, optimise the model.

If the hardware cannot do the thing, design better hardware.

If the product is inaccessible, improve accessibility.

No objection.

The difficulty begins when a governance problem is treated as though it is primarily an insufficiently sophisticated engineering problem.

```text
Problem: misinformation
        ↓
🔨 ranking changes / labels / fact-checking / classifiers

Problem: moderation at scale
        ↓
🔨 more automation

Problem: automation imperfect
        ↓
🔨 better automation

Problem: fact-checking contested
        ↓
🔨 different feedback mechanism

Problem: Apple controls important platform rules
        ↓
🔨 build next platform

Problem: next platform needs interface
        ↓
🔨 VR / AR / glasses

Problem: glasses need context
        ↓
🔨 AI

Problem: AI needs compute
        ↓
🔨 chips / datacentres

Problem: datacentres need enormous physical infrastructure
        ↓
🔨 slowly rises

🌍 EVERYBODY:
MARK.
```

There is nothing wrong with having a hammer.

The concern is what the institution classifies as a nail.

Administrative legitimacy is not an engineering deficit.

Consent is not a latency problem.

Political disagreement is not necessarily a classification error.

Cultural context cannot always be solved by increasing model parameters.

A right does not become obsolete because respecting it reduces engagement.

Sometimes the correct architecture is **external constraint**.

Sometimes the correct answer is **you do not get to decide this alone**.

This will become important later.

First, however, we must discuss the legs.

## 6. 🦵 We Regret To Inform You The Metaverse Has No Legs

The strategic proposition behind Meta’s metaverse investment was not inherently ridiculous.

This is important because the joke is much better if we are fair.

Facebook had already identified VR as a possible future platform when it bought Oculus in 2014. Zuckerberg explicitly described mobile as the platform of the present and Oculus as preparation for platforms of the future.

By 2021, the proposition had become much grander.

Meta described an embodied internet organised around presence, spanning VR, AR glasses, phones and computers. Zuckerberg also explicitly discussed what Facebook had learnt from building on platforms controlled by other companies.

That is a coherent strategic concern.

Then we saw the avatars.

```text
🧠 plausible strategic insight
        ↓
💰 staggering investment
        ↓
🥽 technological implementation
        ↓
✨ THE FUTURE ✨
        ↓
👩 woman examines future
        ↓
🤨

"Mark, where are its legs?"
```

The floating torsos became an almost perfect miniature of the wider problem.

A company can correctly identify an important technological direction while still producing an implementation that causes ordinary humans to ask an extremely basic question.

> **The man can identify a plausible next computing paradigm and still forget the fucking legs.**

These facts are not mutually exclusive.

They are, historically, distressingly compatible.

The lesson is not that VR is silly.

The lesson is not that every Meta engineer forgot humans have legs.

The lesson is that **strategic intelligence is not complete systems intelligence**.

A technically sophisticated organisation can optimise extremely difficult things while overlooking something obvious from another perspective.

This is why external observers are useful.

Sometimes the external observer is a regulator.

Sometimes the external observer is a researcher.

Sometimes the external observer is a journalist.

Sometimes the external observer is a woman staring at your expensive future and asking where you put the lower half of the body.

All are feedback.

## 8. 🌐 What The Fuck Is Web 4 Anyway?

**Web 4.0 is not a settled technical standard.**

There is no universally agreed committee-approved border where Web 3 ends, everybody changes badges, and Web 4 begins at 09:00 on Monday.

The term is useful here as shorthand for a family of changes that increasingly push computing away from a device we deliberately consult and towards an environment that can accompany us, perceive context and act with greater autonomy.

For educational purposes, Britain has prepared the following highly serious technical taxonomy.

```text
WEB 1
"Here is my website."

WEB 2
"Everyone is the website."

WEB 3
"Nobody agrees what this means,
but somebody has mentioned a blockchain."

WEB 4
"THE COMPUTER HAS FOLLOWED YOU OUTSIDE."
```

The useful concepts are less silly.

### Web 4-ish computing may combine:

- **ambient computing** — computation available throughout ordinary environments rather than confined to one deliberate device interaction;
- **multimodal AI** — systems able to process combinations of text, voice, images, video and other signals;
- **AI agents** — systems that do more than answer, increasingly carrying out sequences of actions;
- **wearables** — computing that travels on the body;
- **spatial computing** — digital information organised in relation to physical or simulated space;
- **persistent context** — systems retaining enough situational information to respond differently according to what is happening around the user;
- **embodied interfaces** — interaction that increasingly involves gaze, gesture, voice, movement and physical surroundings;
- **networked identity and relationships** — existing social and communication systems continuing into the new interface layer.

Meta’s language has moved around, but the continuity is visible.

In 2021 it called the destination an **embodied internet**.

In 2026 it described AI glasses as an ideal device for an all-day assistant that can understand the world from the wearer’s perspective.

The route has therefore evolved from:

```text
VR MAY BE NEXT PLATFORM
        ↓
THE METAVERSE IS NEXT PLATFORM
        ↓
MIXED REALITY + AR
        ↓
SMART GLASSES
        ↓
SMART GLASSES + MULTIMODAL AI
        ↓
CONTEXTUAL AI THAT TRAVELS WITH HUMAN
```

Which is, in practical terms:

> **the metaverse without requiring everybody to become a legless Mii in Mark Zuckerberg’s conference room.**

And this version is considerably more plausible.

Which is why we should pay considerably more attention.

## 9. 👁️ Mark, Why Does The Computer Have Eyes?

The phone already has cameras.

People already photograph strangers.

CCTV already exists.

Wearable cameras are not conceptually unprecedented.

None of that makes the design question disappear.

It makes the design question easier to identify.

Meta’s current AI glasses include cameras, microphones, speakers and AI capabilities. Meta says it has designed privacy safeguards for both wearers and people around them. Its 2026 explanation emphasises a visible capture LED for photos and video and says the camera is disabled when the system detects attempts to block or tamper with that indicator.

That is good evidence that Meta itself recognises a crucial fact:

> **The user is not the only human affected by a wearable observational system.**

Excellent.

Gold star for Mark. ⭐

Now we have follow-up questions.

```text
USER
chose device
accepted terms
interacts with AI
        ↓
receives service

        ≠

BYSTANDER
walked into room
```

If I am wearing AI glasses and I look at you, whose information environment is that?

Mine?

Yours?

Meta’s?

What can the system perceive?

What can it infer?

What is processed locally?

What is transmitted?

What is retained?

What can be associated with other information?

What does the wearer know?

What does the bystander know?

What is obvious from the physical interface?

What requires reading documentation?

What can the bystander refuse?

What happens when the system is not recording a photograph for the gallery but is nevertheless processing information in order to provide an AI function?

The answers will vary by feature and implementation.

That is precisely why the architecture matters.

> **Consent to being visible is not necessarily consent to being computationally processed.**

Walking into somebody’s field of vision has always revealed information.

Machine perception changes what can potentially be extracted from that visibility.

A human may forget.

A machine may store.

A human may notice one feature.

A model may infer another.

A human conversation disappears into ordinary memory.

A networked computational system may create records, metadata, associations or outputs that travel elsewhere.

Again: none of this means Meta’s glasses necessarily perform every imaginable processing operation.

Do not invent the machine.

Inspect the actual machine.

But that requirement itself produces the governance question:

> **Who is allowed to inspect the fucking machine?**

## 10. 👩 Unfortunately, The Deployment Environment Contains Women

A new information environment enters a social world in which **gender has already happened**.

This matters.

It does not mean women uniquely own privacy.

It does not mean men cannot be harmed by surveillance, image abuse, sexualisation, stalking, unwanted recording, inference or computational objectification.

It means the historical distribution of those experiences affects how technological propositions are received.

Women have an extremely long history of being:

- looked at;
- evaluated;
- compared;
- sexualised;
- photographed;
- represented;
- discussed;
- circulated;
- classified;
- and turned into information for other people’s use.

Often the woman’s participation in that process has been treated as a secondary consideration.

So yes.

From the perspective of a woman looking at the design lineage of Facebook, the fact that the prologue begins with student photographs obtained without permission for that use and turned into comparative attractiveness rankings is relevant.

Not because Facemash generated Meta’s later trillion-dollar value.

It did not.

Not because a nineteen-year-old’s conduct eternally contaminates every subsequent product.

It does not.

It matters because it gives us an unusually clean early example of the distinction between:

> **woman as participant in an information environment**

and

> **woman as information available inside somebody else’s environment.**

The women did not design the purpose.

The women did not choose the ranking.

The women did not choose the audience.

The women did not choose the computational transformation.

They were the object moving through everybody else’s feedback loop.

And crucially:

**they complained.**

The feedback arrived in 2003.

Which makes the relevant question in 2026 neither sentimental nor biographical.

It is an engineering question.

> **Show us where the observed human acquired substantially more power over the observer.**

Because if the proposed next interface can increasingly perceive physical and social surroundings, the non-user becomes technologically important.

The woman across the room.

The child walking past.

The employee speaking to a customer.

The stranger on the train.

The friend standing beside the wearer.

The person whose voice enters the microphone.

The person who never bought the product, never accepted the terms, never created an account and nevertheless exists inside its sensorium.

## 11. 🧪 You Have Already Supplied The Fucking Regression Tests

The useful thing about Facebook’s history is that we do not need to speculate about whether technologically mediated social environments can produce unexpected feedback.

We have twenty-plus years of field data.

This is not an insult.

It is an asset.

Facebook has operated one of the largest experiments in computationally mediated human social interaction ever constructed.

That experience has generated evidence about:

- privacy;
- consent;
- data repurposing;
- ranking;
- recommender systems;
- advertising incentives;
- political information;
- misinformation;
- child safety;
- moderation;
- language inequality;
- cultural context;
- differential enforcement;
- internal research;
- external research;
- whistleblowing;
- regulatory visibility;
- platform dependency;
- interoperability;
- and what happens when billions of unpredictable primates encounter a product manager’s objective function.

Excellent.

**Use the evidence.**

Before expanding the sensorium, regression-test the new architecture against the old failure classes.

```text
OLD QUESTION
Can information gathered for one purpose be repurposed?

WEB 4 QUESTION
What happens when ambient systems can gather far richer context?

---

OLD QUESTION
What can the platform infer about a user?

WEB 4 QUESTION
What can the system infer about the user AND the people around them?

---

OLD QUESTION
Can outsiders observe how ranking affects people?

WEB 4 QUESTION
Can outsiders observe how personalised AI interpretation affects
perception, recommendation and action?

---

OLD QUESTION
What happens when engagement and safety conflict?

WEB 4 QUESTION
What happens when an agent can act as well as recommend?

---

OLD QUESTION
Who can audit the feed?

WEB 4 QUESTION
Who can audit the environment?
```

This is why the Facebook Papers matter conceptually even when an individual allegation is disputed.

They demonstrated the importance of **observability asymmetry**.

A platform operator can possess information that the people living inside the platform’s effects cannot independently reconstruct.

The company can see experiments, rankings, internal metrics, enforcement systems, model behaviour and aggregate patterns that an individual user cannot see from their own screen.

The more personalised the environment becomes, the harder surface observation becomes.

Two people may use the same product and encounter different systems.

By the time the system is contextual, multimodal and agentic, merely opening the app and having a look may tell an external researcher very little about the decision architecture underneath.

Which means:

> **Somebody has to be allowed to look inside the fucking machine.**

This is not anti-innovation.

This is observability engineering.

## 12. ⚖️ Unfortunately, “Mark Will Get Distracted” Is Not A Regulatory Framework

Here we need to be adults for another four minutes.

Meta is entitled to challenge regulators.

Regulators can be wrong.

Regulators can exceed statutory powers.

Regulatory demands can be disproportionate.

Legislation can be badly drafted.

A judicial review is not evidence of corporate guilt.

A company disputing the interpretation of a statute is not, by itself, evidence that the company opposes safety.

Courts are themselves feedback mechanisms.

**Good. Keep them.**

As of September 2026, Meta is involved in several disputes around Ofcom’s implementation of the UK Online Safety Act. Ofcom’s categorisation register lists Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp as Category 1 services. Reporting also records Meta challenging aspects of categorisation, the regulator’s approach to fees and penalties based on qualifying worldwide revenue, and efforts by Ofcom to gather information under the Act.

Those disputes have legal questions that should be decided on their actual merits.

Do not solve litigation by vibes.

But there is a separate systems question.

If a platform is:

- enormous;
- personalised;
- algorithmically mediated;
- difficult to inspect externally;
- increasingly integrated with AI;
- and potentially moving towards more ambient interfaces;

then **information-gathering powers are not peripheral to regulation**.

They are part of the regulator’s sensorium.

A regulator that cannot observe a sufficiently complex system cannot meaningfully regulate that system.

This does not mean every information request is lawful.

It means the existence of an external information channel is architecturally important.

```text
🇬🇧 OFCOM:
We require information about the machine.

🇺🇸 META:
We dispute whether the statute authorises that request.

🇬🇧:
Entirely legitimate. Please proceed to court.

🇺🇸:
Thank you.

🇬🇧:
We will still require an answer about the fucking machine.
```

That is the balance.

Not:

> **META EVIL. OFCOM GOOD.**

Not:

> **REGULATION BAD. INNOVATION GOOD.**

The useful model is:

```text
platform
   ↕
users
   ↕
researchers
   ↕
civil society
   ↕
regulator
   ↕
courts
   ↕
legislature
```

Different actors possess different information.

Different actors have different powers.

Different actors can correct different failure modes.

A healthy feedback environment does not require any one of them to be omniscient.

It requires enough **independent routes for reality to get back into the system**.

Which is exactly why legal action by Meta matters in both directions.

Meta must be able to contest state power.

The state must be able to exercise lawful regulatory power over Meta.

Courts must be able to decide where the boundary actually lies.

And the public must be able to see enough of that contest to understand the architecture governing technologies increasingly embedded in ordinary life.

That is not friction outside the system.

**That is part of the system.**

Fortunately, history has already supplied a robust constitutional settlement.

There is also the powerful internal constraint whereby Mark Zuckerberg becomes interested in another extremely expensive thing.

This has historically provided meaningful friction.

It is also not a governance architecture.

> **Only Mark Zuckerberg with a hammer is protecting the world from Mark Zuckerberg with a hammer.**

This arrangement lacks several characteristics normally associated with constitutional design.

More importantly:

> **“Mark Zuckerberg will probably get distracted by another computing paradigm” is not an acceptable safety case.**

External constraint is not evidence that innovation has failed.

Sometimes external constraint is how an information system discovers that the rest of the world exists.

Apple gets a vote.

Governments get a vote within lawful authority.

Courts get a vote on the meaning of that authority.

Users get a vote by adopting, rejecting, modifying and misusing products in ways nobody anticipated.

Researchers get a vote by finding effects the organisation did not measure or did not prioritise.

Journalists get a vote by moving information across organisational boundaries.

Employees get a vote when internal information reaches places the formal hierarchy did not route it.

And women have, apparently, been trying to submit the same fucking ticket since 2003.

## 14. 🔧 The Missing Architectural Break

We can now state the concern precisely.

The objection is **not** that Meta has continued doing the same thing since 2003.

It plainly has not.

The technology changed enormously.

The company changed enormously.

The products changed enormously.

Privacy controls changed.

Safety systems changed.

Moderation changed.

Regulation changed.

The social environment changed.

The question is whether twenty-three years of extraordinary technical development have been accompanied by an equally fundamental redesign of the informational relationship between:

1. **the system**;
2. **the person operating the system**; and
3. **the human being represented, observed, inferred about or acted upon by the system.**

That third human is the problem.

The non-user.

The bystander.

The friend in the photograph.

The person being discussed.

The person entering the camera frame.

The person whose behaviour becomes useful to somebody else’s model.

The person who exists in the environment without having accepted a licence agreement to exist there.

So the Web 4 design review needs boring questions.

Beautiful, tedious, civilisation-preserving questions.

### 📋 The Boring Questions

- What information can the device sense?
- What information can the model infer?
- What processing occurs locally?
- What leaves the device?
- What is retained?
- What is associated with an account?
- What can be deleted?
- What can a non-user contest?
- What does the bystander know is happening?
- What happens when capture indicators do not map neatly onto AI processing?
- Which features require cloud processing?
- Which features can function without persistent identity?
- What does the system learn from interactions?
- Can researchers independently test important effects?
- What information can regulators lawfully require?
- What evidence would demonstrate that a known failure class has actually been removed?
- What happens when safety, privacy, engagement and commercial incentives conflict?
- Who has authority to say **no**?

This is not a demand for technological paralysis.

It is a demand for **systems engineering that remembers humans are part of the system**.

Which brings us back to the beginning.

```text
2003
scraped / unauthorisedly repurposed directory photograph
        ↓
person becomes computational object
        ↓
HOT / NOT
        ↓
behavioural signal

2026
vision + voice + surroundings + relationships
+ physical context + AI interaction
        ↓
person / environment becomes computationally legible
        ↓
interpretation / recommendation / assistance / action
        ↓
human behaviour
        ↺
```

The second system is vastly more sophisticated.

That is not reassuring by itself.

**Sophistication increases capability.**

It does not automatically correct premise.

If the underlying relationship remains rickety, more capable sensors and models can simply give the rickety premise a much larger operational surface.

> **It is a much larger sensorium attached to a design premise that requires proof of correction.**

Show the break.

Show the correction.

Show the rights of the observed.

Show the external observability.

Show the regression tests.

Then by all means, continue inventing the future.

## 15. 📋 Exhibit G Finding — The Humans Are Not A Greenfield Deployment

Meta’s progression from social networking towards ambient AI computing represents enormous technological change.

That change may produce genuinely valuable technologies.

The relevant systems question is whether the governance relationship between computational observer and observed human has undergone an equally fundamental transformation.

Facemash matters not because it directly became Meta’s modern products unchanged.

It did not.

Facemash matters because the first corrective feedback arrived at the beginning.

People objected to photographs being obtained and computationally repurposed without their permission for that use.

The problem was visible.

The system received the signal.

Twenty-three years later, the proposed computing environment has considerably more sensors, considerably more inference capability, considerably more context and considerably more potential to accompany human beings through physical space.

Before increasing the observational and intervention surface again, the burden is not merely to demonstrate greater capability.

> **Show the architectural correction.**

Because Web 4 should not be built as a greenfield system.

Humanity is not a greenfield deployment environment.

Existing autonomy is part of the installed base.

Existing social relationships are part of the installed base.

Existing legal rights are part of the installed base.

Existing cultural context is part of the installed base.

Existing human competence is part of the installed base.

And the ability to leave the fucking computer is part of the installed base.

The interface is not the system.

The AI is not the system.

The glasses are not the system.

> **The human information ecology into which you have inserted all of those things is the fucking system.**
