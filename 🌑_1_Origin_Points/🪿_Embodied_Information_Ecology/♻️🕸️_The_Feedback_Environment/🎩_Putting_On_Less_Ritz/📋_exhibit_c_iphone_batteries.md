# 📋 Exhibit C: iPhone Batteries
**First created:** 2026-09-16 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-18  
*The battery was always going to wear out. Why did repair become a governance architecture?*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Before explaining that the next generation of technology requires more hardware, more extraction, more infrastructure and more frequent replacement, we would like to submit another familiar object for the record.

**The smartphone battery.**

Batteries wear out.

This was known before the iPhone.

It was known during the iPhone.

It remains known now.

So the interesting question is not whether a battery eventually degrades.

It is:

**why did replacing the predictably degrading part become so fucking complicated?**

---

## 🔋 Congratulations, We Have Invented A Consumable

A battery is a consumable component inside a much more expensive durable object.

That gives the design problem a fairly obvious shape:

> Battery getting shit?
>
> Replace battery.
>
> Continue using expensive object.

This was not speculative engineering.

Mobile phones already did it.

The battery could be removed because everybody understood that the battery would eventually need to be removed.

Then the smartphone era increasingly gave us:

> Battery getting shit?
>
> Book appointment.
>
> Determine repair route.
>
> Work out whether the device can be serviced locally.
>
> Possibly send device away.
>
> Pay considerably more than the bare component suggests.
>
> Hope the rest of the expensive object remains economically worth servicing.

America.

**Everybody knew the battery would wear out.**

Why is the predictably degrading part buried inside the expensive part?

## 📱 Control Specimen: Nokia 3310

Surprise win for Finland.

The Nokia 3310 had already established a remarkably sophisticated maintenance protocol:

1. take back off;
2. take battery out;
3. put replacement battery in;
4. put back on;
5. continue having telephone.

No heat gun.

No adhesive choreography.

No appointment.

No mailing the telephone to a distant shrine.

No **Battery Replacement Journey™**.

The 3310 was not an iPhone. Modern smartphones involve very different engineering constraints, including thinness, structural design, large displays and water resistance.

Fine.

That makes the correct question:

**what did we gain by making the battery substantially harder for the owner to replace, and was the trade worth it?**

It does not make the question:

**could humans possibly design a telephone with a replaceable battery?**

Yes.

**THE FINNS HAD ALREADY FUCKING DONE IT.**

And it ran Snake.

## 🪛 Excuse Me, Was The Phone Actually Unrepairable?

No.

It was **difficult and increasingly controlled to repair**.

Those are different statements.

People opened iPhones.

People replaced screens.

People replaced batteries.

Independent repair shops did it.

Technically confident owners did it themselves.

The device had screws.

The device could be opened.

Components could be removed and replaced.

The interesting question was therefore never simply:

**“Can this object be repaired?”**

It was:

**“Who is permitted, equipped, documented, supplied and economically enabled to repair it?”**

That distinction matters.

Repairability is not binary.

It includes:

- physical access;
- component availability;
- manuals;
- diagnostics;
- specialist tools;
- software pairing and calibration;
- warranty consequences;
- labour costs;
- local skills;
- whether the repair remains economically rational.

A repairable object can be placed inside an ecosystem that makes repair unnecessarily difficult.

## 💷 I Have Repaired At Least Four Of Your Phones

There is a particularly irritating customer experience hidden inside all this.

A technically competent owner could take apart an iPhone and replace a broken screen.

It was fiddly.

It was annoying.

It required care.

But it was doable.

And there were periods when an owner could source a replacement screen cheaply enough that a DIY repair might cost a small fraction of the official service price.

That produces a fairly obvious question:

> I possess sufficient technical competence to open this device.
>
> I can purchase a replacement component.
>
> I can replace the component.
>
> I can put the device back together.
>
> **It works.**
>
> Why is the institutional version of this transaction so much more elaborate and expensive?

The technician's capability and the repair system's permission are not the same thing.

A person can know how to fix the object while working inside a system that does not permit that repair locally, does not provide the component through that route, requires prescribed diagnostics, or sends the device elsewhere.

From the customer's perspective this can produce the absurd experience of standing in a technology shop, speaking to a technology expert, holding a repairable technology product and nevertheless being told:

**“We'll have to send it away.”**

Mate.

**THE SCREWS ARE RIGHT THERE.**

## 🧰 “Experienced Users”

Modern self-service repair programmes sometimes describe themselves as intended for people with suitable knowledge or experience.

Fine.

We welcome the recognition that technically competent owners exist.

We do, however, have one question.

**WHERE DO YOU THINK THE EXPERIENCED USERS CAME FROM?**

They came from bedrooms.

Kitchen tables.

Market stalls.

Little repair shops.

School technology clubs.

Sheds.

They replaced screens.

They changed batteries.

They built computers.

They stripped screws.

They swore at adhesive.

They discovered which tiny fucking cable should absolutely not be pulled like that.

They became experienced.

**That is how technical cultures reproduce themselves.**

## 🐣 Where Are The Next Experienced Users Supposed To Come From?

A technically competent society requires somewhere for people to become technically competent.

Not all of that happens in classrooms.

Some happens because a child asks:

**“Can I take this apart?”**

and an adult makes the consequential decision:

**“Yeah. It's already broken. Go on then.”**

That broken object has just become educational infrastructure.  

Technical competence often develops through:

**object → components → interfaces → failure modes → diagnosis → tools → repair → confidence**

The crucial transition is not merely learning one repair.

It is learning:

> **I don't know how this works**
>
> can become
>
> **I can probably find out.**

If ordinary people increasingly cannot open, understand, repair or modify the technology around them, society does not merely lose cheap repairs.

It loses some of the informal routes by which tomorrow's technicians, engineers and curious little bastards become technically confident.

Repairability is therefore not merely consumer policy.

**It is skills infrastructure.**

## 🏪 The Repair Shop Was Part Of The Technology

The little phone repair shop is not an embarrassing residue of an inefficient pre-platform economy.

**It is part of the technology industry.**

The complete technical system is not merely:

**device**

It is:

**device + spare parts + diagnostics + documentation + tools + repair skills + local technicians + economically rational servicing**

When design choices, parts policies, software restrictions or commercial structures remove the ability to repair locally, technical capability becomes more centralised.

A town loses more than a place to replace a screen.

It loses a place where people diagnose faults, handle components, learn practical electronics, exchange knowledge, acquire tools and sometimes discover that they are good at this.

Repair capacity is productive capacity.

**Keep some of it near the fucking object.**

## 🗑️ America, Where Do You Think “Away” Is?

There is another problem with designing around replacement.

The old object does not disappear when the customer buys the new one.

The phone remains matter.

The battery remains matter.

The screen remains matter.

The circuit board remains matter.

The metals remain matter.

The packaging remains matter.

And the replacement device requires another physical production chain.

**There is no “away”.**

This matters particularly when a product philosophy developed in a continent-sized market is exported into places with much tighter physical geography.

Britain does not have America's acreage.

We already need land for:

- housing;
- agriculture;
- energy;
- transport;
- logistics;
- industry;
- nature;
- waste;
- data infrastructure;
- everything else an island full of people would quite like to keep doing.

So:

**“just replace the whole thing” is not spatially neutral.**

Neither is:

**“just build another data centre.”**

Neither is:

**“the cloud.”**

The cloud is physical infrastructure.

The discarded phone is physical matter.

Recycling does not mean no resources were consumed.

Upgrade does not dematerialise the previous device.

Sometimes the environmentally, economically and spatially sensible technological innovation is:

**replace the £20 fucking part.**

## 🌍 Products Have Geography

This is not:

**America bad, Europe good.**

Europe produces waste.

Britain produces waste.

European technology companies make stupid design decisions too.

The point is simpler:

**products designed for global markets need to survive contact with different physical geographies.**

Land availability differs.

Energy systems differ.

Housing differs.

Repair markets differ.

Household budgets differ.

Infrastructure differs.

A commercial architecture that feels tolerable in one place may feel absurdly resource-hungry somewhere else.

Same technology.

**Different fucking island.**

---

## 🔧 We Would Like To Keep The Shiny Thing

The argument is not against sophisticated smartphones.

The iPhone is an extraordinary consumer technology product.

The sealed glass-and-metal rectangle is considerably more capable than the Nokia whose back came off when dropped.

We appreciate the shiny thing.

**We would also like to keep the shiny thing.**

Durability without maintainability is incomplete.

A device that lasts physically but becomes economically irrational to repair has not solved the whole lifecycle problem.

A device that can technically be repaired but only through an unnecessarily complicated service architecture has not solved it either.

Sometimes progress should involve making the sophisticated object **more maintainable**, not making maintenance itself sophisticated.
