# 📋 Exhibit E: Printer Ink  
**First created:** 2026-09-16 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-16  
*The printer is the specimen. The actual problem is the unpaid administration, dependency and jurisdictional exposure hidden inside supposedly convenient technology.*

---

## 🖨️ Orientation

There is a particular kind of technological failure that does not look like failure because the device technically works.

The printer works.

The computer works.

The phone works.

The cloud works.

The account works.

The subscription works.

The international data-transfer mechanism works.

The complaints procedure works.

And yet, somehow, the human being has acquired another job.

This exhibit begins with printer ink because printer ink is funny. It ends with transatlantic signals-intelligence redress because the same design problem keeps recurring at larger scales:

> **YOU PUT ADMIN INSIDE THE DEVICE WE BOUGHT TO DO THE ADMIN.**
>
> America.
>
> **THE ROCKEFELLERS WOULD LIKE THEIR BUSINESS MODEL BACK.**

The complaint is not that consumables should be free, that companies should not make money, that international data transfers are inherently unlawful, or that American intelligence agencies automatically receive data processed by American companies.

The complaint is simpler:

**complexity repeatedly leaks out of the system and becomes unpaid work for the user.**

When the complexity also creates proprietary dependency, supply-chain fragility, cross-border data exposure or difficult-to-exercise rights, the problem stops being merely annoying.

It becomes governance.

## 🧾 The Cheap Printer Is Not Necessarily The Cheap Printing System

The familiar printer business model resembles the classic **razor-and-blades** structure:

1.  make the durable device comparatively cheap;
2.  establish an installed base;
3.  recover revenue through recurring consumables;
4.  make the consumable difficult, inconvenient or impossible to
    substitute.

There is nothing inherently illegitimate about charging for toner or ink.

The problem begins when the headline price of the machine becomes a poor guide to the lifetime cost of possessing a functioning printing capability.

The household thinks it bought:

**a printer.**

It may actually have bought:

**printer + cartridge family + authentication regime + firmware relationship + replacement cycle + supply-chain dependency + account + subscription option + future compatibility decisions made by somebody else.**

That is not one object.

It is a small dependency graph with a paper tray.

## 🏷️ Why Am I Doing Your Inventory Management?

The manufacturer created the model range.

The manufacturer created the cartridge families.

The manufacturer created the compatibility matrix.

The manufacturer controls the catalogue.

And yet the consumer is routinely expected to remember whether the black object in the study is a `3720`, `3720e`, `3722`, or some adjacent sequence of numbers whose differences are meaningful primarily to the company that invented them.

> "Which cartridge do you need?"
>
> "The one for my printer."
>
> "Which model?"
>
> "I don't know."
>
> "Please locate the microscopic sticker behind the machine."
>
> **IT IS THE BLACK FUCKING ONE IN THE STUDY.**

This suggests a useful design principle:

> **Complexity should remain with the party that created it.**

Complex engineering underneath a simple interface is excellent
technology.

Complex engineering that requires the customer to learn the manufacturer's internal product taxonomy is **complexity leakage**.

## 🏢 Toner: When The Joke Becomes Procurement

Office printers expose the same problem at organisational scale.

A serious procurement comparison cannot stop at the purchase price of the machine. It needs to consider:

**toner + stated yield + real-world yield + drums + imaging units + waste-toner containers + fusers + maintenance kits + service + energy + downtime + authentication restrictions + expected availability of consumables.**

The useful question is not:

> **How much is the printer?**

It is:

> **What does 100,000 pages cost, and what has to remain available for us to print them?**

A cheap machine attached to expensive or fragile dependencies may be expensive infrastructure.

A more expensive machine using widely available consumables and serviceable parts may be cheaper over its useful life.

**Price the system, not the shiny object.**

## 🚢 The Printer-Shaped Hostage To Logistics

A consumable is not merely a recurring cost.

It is a recurring **dependency**.

If continued operation requires one vendor-specific object, every future page depends on continued access to that object's supply chain.

The printer can be:

-   physically inside the office;
-   owned outright;
-   mechanically functional;
-   fully depreciated;
-   and completely useless.

All that is required is for the required cartridge, chip, drum or maintenance component to become unavailable.

Then the organisation does not possess a functioning printing capability.

It possesses:

> **a printer-shaped hostage to logistics.**

Standardising an entire estate can make the dependency worse.

Efficiency says:

> **one fleet, one cartridge, one supplier.**

Resilience asks:

> **and what happens when the fucking boat doesn't arrive?**

This is the miniature version of a much larger sovereignty question.

Ownership of equipment is not the same as **operational autonomy**.

## 🌍 The Account Is Also A Jurisdiction

The account problem becomes more serious when household and organisational technology routinely transfers personal data across borders.

UK data protection law does not simply cease to apply because information is transferred to the United States. The UK has international-transfer rules, and the **UK Extension to the EU--US Data
Privacy Framework** permits transfers to appropriately certified US organisations without additional transfer safeguards.

But the jurisdictional environment around the data changes.

The Information Commissioner's Office explicitly noted, when reviewing the UK Government's adequacy assessment, that international transfers can place information into a different legal framework. The Commissioner considered the UK Extension adequate overall, but gave a **qualified assurance** and identified four areas requiring monitoring because differences between UK and US law could reduce protection in practice.

Those four areas concerned:

1.  whether certain UK special-category information is actually identified and treated as sensitive after transfer;
2.  protections relating to criminal-offence data, including the absence of an equivalent to some protections under the Rehabilitation of Offenders Act 1974;
3.  the absence of a substantially similar UK-GDPR right concerning significant decisions based solely on automated processing, including human review; and
4.  the absence of substantially similar rights to erasure and unconditional withdrawal of consent.

The ICO additionally recommended ongoing monitoring of US intelligence-community compliance with Executive Order 14086, the effectiveness of oversight and enforcement, and significant changes in
the US legal landscape.

That is important.

**Adequacy does not mean identical law.**

It means the UK Government concluded that the relevant US framework, taken as a whole, provided an adequate level of protection for the transfers within scope.

That is a legal conclusion.

It is not a reason to stop asking what dependency and practical redress look like from the citizen's side.

## 🛰️ Yes, There Is A Signals-Intelligence Redress Mechanism

This is the part British consumers are unlikely to encounter while setting up the printer.

The United States designated the United Kingdom a **qualifying state** for the redress mechanism established under Executive Order 14086.

That matters because a UK individual can use the mechanism if they reasonably believe personal data transferred from the UK to the United States was accessed through US signals-intelligence activities in violation of applicable US law.

The mechanism is not confined to transfers made through the Data Privacy
Framework. The ICO says it can apply to UK personal data transferred to
the US using **any transfer mechanism**, provided the relevant temporal
and other requirements are met.

The existence of this route does **not** establish that US intelligence
routinely accesses British consumer data.

It establishes something narrower and important:

**the possibility of signals-intelligence access to transferred data is
sufficiently material that the transatlantic framework contains specific
safeguards and a specific redress architecture for it.**

---

## 📋 What A British Person Has To Put In The Complaint

The ICO does not itself determine whether the US signals-intelligence
access was lawful.

It checks whether the complaint meets the requirements for consideration
by the relevant US authority: initially the **Civil Liberties Protection
Officer (CLPO) in the Office of the Director of National Intelligence**.

According to the ICO, the complainant must provide information
including:

-   who they are;
-   the specific means by which, or reasons why, they believe their
    personal information was transmitted to the United States;
-   **why they believe that information was subsequently subject to US
    signals-intelligence activities**;
-   when the transfer and suspected access may have occurred, with the
    UK mechanism applying to relevant activity after 12 October 2023;
-   which US Government entities they believe accessed the information,
    if known;
-   whether they believe the access violated the US Constitution or
    other applicable US laws, orders, policies or procedures governing
    signals intelligence and privacy/civil-liberties safeguards;
-   whether they believe the access adversely affected their rights,
    privacy or civil-liberties interests;
-   other attempts made to resolve the matter and any response received;
    and
-   the outcome they seek.

Read that again from the perspective of an ordinary person.

The individual may have to explain **why they reasonably believe a
secret intelligence activity happened to information they may not be
able to observe after it crossed an international boundary**.

This does not prove the mechanism is ineffective.

It does reveal an extraordinary **information asymmetry**.

The intelligence system potentially knows whether collection occurred.

The individual ordinarily does not.

And a person who raises an unsupported suspicion of secret intelligence
collection also faces an obvious social problem:

> **How are you supposed to articulate the suspicion without being
> dismissed as paranoid precisely because you cannot see the secret
> process you are being asked to identify?**

That is not an argument for accepting every suspicion as true.

It is an argument that a complaint architecture concerning secret state
activity cannot be evaluated solely by asking whether a complaint form
technically exists.

## ⚖️ What Happens After The Complaint

If the ICO considers the complaint qualifying, it passes it into the US
redress system.

The first substantive review is conducted by the **ODNI Civil Liberties
Protection Officer**.

If the complainant wishes to challenge that outcome, the matter can
proceed to the **Data Protection Review Court (DPRC)**, established
within the US Department of Justice.

The DPRC can independently review the CLPO's determination and can
require appropriate remediation. The US rules also provide for a Special
Advocate to assist the court in representing the complainant's
interests.

This is stronger than simply telling a foreign complainant to trust the
intelligence agency.

But there is a remarkable limitation on what the complainant learns.

The Department of Justice states that, after review, the individual is
**not told whether they were subject to US signals-intelligence
activities**.

Instead, they receive a standardised outcome stating, in substance, that
the review either found no covered violation **or** that appropriate
remediation was required.

The complainant therefore may receive a legally final answer without
learning which of those two things happened.

That secrecy may serve legitimate intelligence purposes.

It also matters when evaluating how meaningful the remedy feels from
outside the US system.

## 🛡️ Can America Just Say "National Security"?

Not quite.

Executive Order 14086 was specifically designed to impose safeguards on
US signals-intelligence activities, including requirements framed around
legitimate objectives, necessity and proportionality. The redress
mechanism exists to examine whether applicable US law and those
safeguards were followed.

At the DPRC stage, US intelligence agencies can provide views about the
national-security or operational effects of proposed alternative
remediation.

But Department of Justice guidance says that **this does not prevent the
DPRC from ordering that remediation**.

So the mechanism is not accurately described as:

> **America says "national security" and the complaint automatically
> disappears.**

The more precise concern is institutional.

The complainant is asking institutions of the United States to determine
whether institutions of the United States complied with United States
law while conducting secret United States intelligence activity.

There are structural safeguards intended to create independence within
that system.

A British reader is nevertheless entitled to ask:

> **How much confidence should a foreign data subject place in a redress
> architecture whose evidence, law, institutions and national-security
> interests largely sit inside the same foreign state?**

That is a question about **institutional trust**, not proof that the
mechanism is fraudulent.

And institutional trust matters particularly when enormous quantities of
ordinary commercial data routinely enter that jurisdiction.

## 🕵️ The Historical Memory Is Not Invented

There is a reason large-scale American data analysis can trigger
historical unease without requiring a claim about a current secret
programme.

After 11 September 2001, DARPA --- part of the US Department of Defense,
**not the CIA** --- developed the **Total Information Awareness**
programme, later renamed **Terrorism Information Awareness**.

The Department of Defense's 2003 report described it as a
research-and-development programme intended to integrate tools including
data search, pattern recognition, decision support and other analytical
capabilities into an experimental network intended to help identify
terrorist planning.

The programme became intensely controversial over privacy and
surveillance concerns, and Congress withdrew funding for the central
programme in 2003.

That history does **not** prove that present-day commercial telemetry is
secretly feeding an equivalent programme.

It does demonstrate that large-scale integration and analysis of
disparate data for national-security purposes is not a science-fiction
concept that citizens invented after reading too much Reddit.

The appropriate response is not paranoia.

It is governance.

## 🧿 The Epistemic Trap

There is a particularly awkward asymmetry here.

A sensible British person is generally encouraged **not** to infer
secret state activity without evidence.

Good.

But intelligence activity is, by design, difficult for the affected
person to observe.

Also good, in many legitimate intelligence contexts.

Then the redress system asks the person to explain why they reasonably
believe their transferred data was subject to signals intelligence.

This creates an epistemic trap:

> **Do not make unsupported claims about secret intelligence activity.**
>
> Correct.
>
> **To invoke the special remedy, explain why you believe secret
> intelligence activity occurred.**
>
> Also correct.
>
> **How do I obtain enough information to form that belief?**
>
> ...
>
> **AMERICA.**

The answer cannot be to require every British consumer to become a
counter-intelligence analyst.

Rights need to function for people who remain **reasonably
un-paranoid**.

That places the governance burden upstream:

-   minimise unnecessary collection;
-   minimise unnecessary international transfer;
-   make transfer arrangements legible;
-   provide meaningful institutional oversight;
-   audit compliance;
-   preserve domestic capability where dependency is strategically
    material;
-   use local or on-device processing where proportionate and feasible;
-   require interoperability and meaningful exit;
-   and make redress discoverable before somebody has to become an
    amateur specialist in foreign intelligence law.

We bought the device to reduce administration.

Then, naturally:

> **"Good news! AI can help you understand all this complexity."**

Where is the AI processing the conversation?

**AMERICA.**

Could that processing itself create another cross-border data
relationship?

Potentially.

And now we have achieved the perfect technological ouroboros:

> **YOU PUT ADMIN INSIDE THE PRIVACY PROTECTION FOR THE ADMIN YOU PUT
> INSIDE THE DEVICE WE BOUGHT TO REDUCE THE ADMIN.**

This is not superintelligence.

This is **administrative recursion**.

## 🛡️ Whose National Security?

There is another asymmetry hidden inside the phrase **national security**.

Of course the United States is entitled to protect American national security.

The problem begins when **American national security is treated as the point at which analysis stops**, despite the same infrastructure, data flows, dependencies and intelligence relationships also engaging British national security.

This is not an ordinary vendor relationship.

The United Kingdom's 2025 National Security Strategy describes the United States as the UK's **most important defence and security ally** and identifies NATO, intelligence and nuclear sharing, and armed-forces interoperability as structural foundations of the relationship. The same strategy says renewed alliances should be built around **strategic depth, reciprocity and mutual benefit**.

That matters.

Britain shares intelligence with the United States.

Britain integrates defence capabilities with the United States.

Britain accepts substantial technological and commercial dependency on American companies.

British personal and organisational data routinely enters infrastructure controlled by American companies and subject to American jurisdiction.

Those arrangements have historically been easier to tolerate precisely because the United States was not treated as an ordinary foreign power.

**It was the ally.**

So:

> **America:** National security.
>
> **Britain:** Whose?
>
> **America:** Ours.
>
> **Britain:** We noticed.

If Britain's answer to dependency concerns is repeatedly:

> **Trust America; we're allies.**

while America's answer when British interests complicate American ones becomes:

> **National security; we're America.**

then the relationship has stopped functioning as a sufficient answer to the dependency question.

The relevant test is not whether American institutions have legitimate national-security interests.

They plainly do.

The test is whether the **whole arrangement remains reciprocal, proportionate and compatible with the sovereign security interests of both countries**.

## 🔄 Apply The Methodology Symmetrically

The Five Countries themselves recognise sovereignty, critical infrastructure, economic security, supply-chain exploitation, vendors and advanced technology as national-security concerns.

Excellent.

Apply the methodology consistently.

If foreign control of:

- data;
- infrastructure;
- technology providers;
- supply chains;
- critical components;
- remote access;
- and strategically important platforms

can create national-security questions when the foreign country is an adversary, those categories do not become analytically meaningless when the foreign country is an ally.

The **risk assessment can and should produce very different answers**.

The United States is not China.

A Five Eyes partner is not equivalent to a hostile state.

Different laws, institutions, intentions, capabilities, relationships and safeguards matter enormously.

But allied status should affect the **answer** to the risk assessment.

It should not abolish the **question**.

That is particularly important because the Five Country Ministerial communiqué of August 2026 itself describes harmful foreign investment, exploitation of supply chains and vendors, critical infrastructure and advanced technologies as matters affecting national and economic security and sovereign integrity.

Britain therefore does not need to invent a hostile or conspiratorial framework for evaluating American technological dependency.

**The allies have already supplied the framework.**

The British question is simply:

> **Does the framework still work when we point it west?**

## 🤝 Trust Is Part Of The Infrastructure

The deepest UK-US integrations were not priced as ordinary commercial relationships.

They were priced inside an alliance.

That means **trust itself is an infrastructure dependency**.

If Britain accepts greater concentration risk because the supplier is located in its closest security partner, then the reliability, reciprocity and political stability of that partnership form part of the dependency model.

This does not mean every disagreement with Washington requires technological decoupling.

It means that changes in the strategic assumptions underlying a dependency should cause the dependency to be **repriced**.

The UK National Security Strategy already acknowledges that the assumptions underlying alliances are changing, that international politics is becoming more transactional, and that economic and military measures are increasingly used as leverage.

That observation has consequences for technology policy.

A dependency considered acceptable under:

**stable alliance + reciprocal restraint + predictable trade + secure logistics + trusted legal arrangements**

may deserve another look under:

**greater transactionalism + supply-chain disruption + jurisdictional uncertainty + concentrated vendor control**.

Not autarky.

Not panic.

Not **BRITAIN MUST MANUFACTURE EVERY SCREW IN A SHED**.

Just competent resilience planning.

## 🧿 The National-Security Externality

There is therefore a second externality alongside unpaid household administration.

A technology company can optimise for:

**commercial value + product performance + data availability + platform integration**

while leaving the state and citizen to manage:

**jurisdiction + strategic dependency + intelligence exposure + supply-chain concentration + continuity risk + sovereign capability**.

Again, the complexity has leaked outward.

And again, somebody else is doing the admin.

This produces a useful rule:

> **A foreign vendor's national-security environment is part of the lifetime architecture of the product.**

That does not make the vendor malicious.

It means jurisdiction is not metadata.

It is part of the system.

## 🎸 And Then We Ask Where The Beatles Went

There is a cultural version of the same problem.

Britain is periodically asked why it does not seem to produce another Beatles, another Clash, another explosion of strange, globally influential cultural production on command.

Britain has plenty of domestic responsibility here: housing costs, venue closures, arts funding, education policy, insecure work and the destruction of cheap space cannot sensibly be outsourced to America as blame.

But the broader optimisation culture still deserves scrutiny.

**Culture requires slack.**

People need:

- time;
- cheap enough space;
- scenes;
- venues;
- other strange people;
- room to be mediocre before becoming good;
- and cognitive capacity not already allocated to administrative maintenance.

Four musicians spending an enormous amount of time fucking around can look spectacularly inefficient.

Until they are the Beatles.

A society that attempts to optimise every measurable unit of human activity should therefore be cautious about subsequently asking:

> **Where are all the interesting weirdos?**

They may be resetting their passwords.

Or comparing toner numbers.

Or administering subscriptions.

Or completing mandatory training for the optimisation platform.

Or learning enough transatlantic signals-intelligence law to understand the privacy architecture attached to the fucking printer.

Not all socially productive capacity looks productive **before it produces something**.

If technology genuinely returns human time and attention, that surplus can become music, friendship, parenting, community work, hobbies, invention, political participation, rest or simply messing around.

If every efficiency gain arrives with another layer of administrative maintenance, the promised surplus never quite appears.

> **America:** We have optimised the human.
>
> **Britain:** Can the human make *London Calling*?
>
> **America:** The human has a 14:30 compliance webinar.
>
> **Britain:** **PUT IT BACK.**

## 🚢 No, You Do Not Inherit The Submarine

The same distinction matters at the most absurdly high-stakes end of the relationship.

Britain's nuclear deterrent contains deep American technological dependencies. That does not make **dependency**, **ownership** and **operational control** the same thing.

Polaris has an ongoing joke about the United States apparently assuming that, come Doomsday, it somehow gets the British SSBN.

No.

> **America:** But we supplied important parts of the system.
>
> **Britain:** Yes.
>
> **America:** And we're your closest nuclear ally.
>
> **Britain:** Yes.
>
> **America:** So after the apocalypse—
>
> **Britain:** **YOU DO NOT INHERIT THE SUBMARINE.**

The serious governance point is the same one the printer has been trying to teach us for several thousand words:

> **Who supplied it?**
>
> **Who services it?**
>
> **What dependencies remain?**
>
> **Who owns it?**
>
> **Who possesses it?**
>
> **Who has operational authority?**

These are different questions.

A supplier relationship does not silently become command authority.

An alliance does not silently become ownership.

Dependency does not silently become sovereignty.

And at the terminal edge of British nuclear doctrine, uncertainty is not a software bug to be patched by the vendor. The entire purpose of preserving a survivable deterrent and sealed letters of last resort is that there may be circumstances in which nobody outside the submarine knows what instruction governs the final decision.

So, America:

**you do not know what the letter says.**

You do not know where the boat is.

And, continuing a long-running Polaris joke:

**please stop assuming you are getting the subs on Doomsday.**

The toner-cartridge rule has therefore reached nuclear doctrine:

> **Ownership, dependency and operational control must not be conflated.**

## 🧭 The Procurement Rule

The printer gives us the rule for the entire stack:

> **Do not price the object. Price the dependency.**

Ask:

-   What must remain available for this to function?
-   Which company controls those dependencies?
-   Which jurisdiction governs them?
-   What information leaves the user or organisation?
-   Is that collection actually necessary?
-   Can the system operate without continuous vendor contact?
-   Can consumables or components be sourced elsewhere?
-   Can the user repair it?
-   Can the organisation migrate away?
-   What happens during a supply-chain disruption?
-   What happens if the allied relationship changes?
-   What does the user have to remember?
-   What administrative labour has been externalised?
-   What rights exist?
-   Can an ordinary person actually discover and exercise them?
-   What remains operational if the vendor, network, cloud service or
    international transfer becomes unavailable?

That is not technological isolationism.

It is **whole-system due diligence**.  

## 🧿 The Citizen Should Not Have To Become Suspicious Enough To Compensate For Institutional Opacity

There is a further problem here that does not require establishing that any particular foreign intelligence, commercial or administrative system **has** classified a particular person incorrectly.

The problem is that, if it did, the affected person may be extraordinarily poorly placed to discover it.

An incorrect classification, inference, identity match, risk marker or other conclusion can potentially create downstream consequences without giving the person affected a clear view of:

- what the originating record says;
- whether the record exists at all;
- who created it;
- what evidence produced it;
- which organisations received it;
- which organisations subsequently relied upon it;
- whether it has been corrected;
- whether correction propagated downstream;
- or which jurisdiction's law provides an effective route to challenge it.

That creates an obvious evidential asymmetry.

The person may experience something downstream.

The institution holds the information required to establish what happened upstream.

The responsible response cannot therefore be:

> **“Unless you can prove the invisible record exists, stop worrying about it.”**

Nor should it be:

> **“Yes, obviously the invisible record exists.”**

The appropriate response is:

> **We should have institutions capable of answering the fucking question.**

That is what effective governance is for.

A British citizen should not personally have to negotiate with the United States as though they are **a tiny sovereign state with a Gmail account**.

Britain has diplomatic relationships.

Britain has regulators with statutory powers.

Britain has courts.

Britain has intelligence and security institutions with longstanding liaison relationships.

Britain has Parliament.

Britain negotiates the agreements under which data, intelligence and technological dependencies cross jurisdictions.

Those institutions exist partly because an individual citizen cannot realistically reproduce the investigative, legal and diplomatic capabilities of a state.

So when the practical answer to a possible cross-border information problem becomes:

> **“Perhaps the individual can navigate the specialist foreign redress mechanism.”**

there is a prior governance question:

> **Why is so much of the investigative burden sitting with the individual in the first place?**

The administrative sequence can become absurd:

↓

↓

Provide enough evidence to persuade somebody to investigate.

↓

**The evidence required to establish what happened is held inside the system you are asking them to investigate.**

That is the boulder.

And where intelligence activity is potentially involved, the asymmetry becomes particularly difficult because secrecy may itself be legitimate.

The answer cannot reasonably be to require the citizen to become sufficiently suspicious, technically capable, legally knowledgeable and persistent to compensate for that secrecy.

**Rights need to work for people who remain reasonably un-paranoid.**

### 🛡️ The State Is Supposed To Be Bigger Than Sandra

This is also where sovereignty becomes personal.

A state protecting its sovereignty is not merely protecting borders, military installations or abstract constitutional prerogatives.

It is also maintaining enough institutional capacity to protect its citizens when they encounter systems substantially more powerful than they are.

If a British person's information is wrong, misused, improperly transferred or otherwise producing consequences inside an allied foreign system, the British answer cannot always be:

> **Here is the website where you can complain to America.**

Sometimes the relevant question should instead be:

> **What can British institutions establish on this person's behalf?**

That does not require assuming wrongdoing.

It requires refusing to make **institutional opacity somebody else's unpaid administrative problem**.

Because eventually:

**device admin**

becomes

**account admin**

becomes

**data admin**

becomes

**rights admin**

becomes

**foreign-jurisdiction admin**

becomes

**signals-intelligence admin**

becomes

**the individual having to investigate why the individual has been treated strangely by systems the individual cannot see.**

And at some point the citizen is entitled to say:

> **I AM FUCKING TIRED.**
>
> I have done enough administration.
>
> **My country can do some now.**

“But why were you so interested in America?”  

Because I thought you guys were cool.  

Thank you for robustly proving my naïveté.
