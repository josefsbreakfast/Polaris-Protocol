# 📋 Exhibit M — Why Is My Yarn Getting VIP Shipping?
**First created:** 2026-10-13 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-13  
*America, please stop breaking the international creative economy. The woman has money. The woman wants pretty yarn. This should not require a defence procurement programme.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation — The Woman Has Attempted Commerce

America. A question.

Do you want us to buy your stuff or not?

Because I have spent approximately twenty years buying weird, wonderful, highly specific things from American independent sellers. I am not asking you to convince me that America produces interesting things. I know. I have been paying your makers for them. This is, in fact, what the internet was supposed to make possible.

And now I am looking at **two balls of wool** with a checkout total of **£88.17**, wondering whether the yarn has booked a private cabin, diplomatic protection, or a seat in business class with a complimentary glass of champagne.

I don't need Elon Musk getting any richer. I don't need to be bombed with oxytocin. I don't need an AI-generated photograph of the yarn, or a ten-page business plan about how the yarn could revolutionise defence. **I need the fucking yarn.**

And the independent American maker needs people to be able to buy it.

This exhibit is about the economic and cultural network that made those transactions possible: ordinary creators, niche communities, rural postal access, payment systems, marketplaces, international friendships, small suppliers, and the millions of little commercial relationships that do not look particularly impressive in a venture-capital pitch deck. It is also about what happens when those relationships become too expensive to maintain.

**The central question:** what if America is preserving the machinery for monetising culture while neglecting the people, materials and infrastructure that actually produce it?

## 1. 🧶 Exhibit M — Apparently the Yarn Has a Chauffeur

The observed checkout, 13 October 2026:

| Charge | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Two balls of yarn | £52.12 |
| Seller discount | −£2.61 |
| Discounted goods | £49.51 |
| Shipping | £23.96 |
| Tax | £14.70 |
| **Total** | **£88.17** |

The tax is exactly 20% of the goods **plus** shipping (£73.47). That is consistent with UK VAT on the combined taxable value; it is **not evidence of a £14.70 American export tariff**. The postage itself is £23.96; postage plus the VAT attributable to it is approximately **£28.75**. Goods plus their VAT are approximately **£59.41**. The total bill is still £88.17, and the woman still has only two balls of wool.

For a faster delivery option, the quoted additional cost was about £30. Apparently the yarn can arrive this week, provided we charter the fucking Concorde.

From my own shopping experience, Canadian independent sellers have recently offered delivery at roughly **£10–15**. That is a customer observation, not a controlled postal-price comparison. It matters because I remember American and Canadian sellers being much closer in affordability. My question is not whether postage has ever cost money. I have been buying internationally for two decades. My question is **what changed**.

Before we teach future researchers a lie: this screenshot does not establish the seller's carrier, parcel weight, dimensions, postal class, actual postage paid, or margin on shipping. Nor does it prove that tariffs caused the charge. Those are precisely the things to investigate.

## 2. 📮 America, Your Post Office Is Not a Decorative Institution

A small maker in a rural American town is not a multinational logistics company. She may not have convenient access to courier collection, bulk rates, overseas warehouses, or a shipping department called Kevin. She may have a kitchen table, some stock, an internet connection and a post office.

That can be enough to make her an exporter.

USPS's nationwide network is therefore not merely a way to deliver birthday cards. It is part of the physical infrastructure connecting geographically isolated producers to customers around the world. An online marketplace cannot substitute for the actual movement of goods. The checkout button is not a teleportation device, California.

International rates can reflect domestic collection, sorting, transport contracts, fuel, labour, destination-country handling, international postal arrangements, parcel characteristics and commercial pricing decisions. Tariffs may increase some imported inputs or equipment costs directly or indirectly. **But the existence of tariffs does not prove that a particular postal price increase was caused by them.**

That distinction is worth preserving because the mechanism is more interesting than a convenient villain. We need to compare official USPS rate schedules over time, the equivalent Canada Post services, actual service classes, currency conversion, carrier surcharges and marketplace shipping settings. Earlier figures discussed in conversation are **not yet independently verified for publication**; they must not be presented as an audited time series.

What matters for the small seller is the *delivered price*. The maker can price her yarn perfectly sensibly and still become uncompetitive once the buyer reaches the shipping screen.

**The product has passed the demand test. The infrastructure is failing the checkout test.**

## 3. 🌐 The Internet Was Not Built So Three Men Could Own a Spreadsheet

The American technology sector helped build enormously important digital infrastructure. Fine. Congratulations. Gold star. Nobody is disputing that.

But the economic and cultural world that grew through it was **not exclusively American**, nor was it created solely by the companies hosting it.

Over twenty years, people in Britain, Ireland, Canada, the United States, Australia, Europe, Japan, Indonesia and elsewhere exchanged designs, techniques, tutorials, materials, jokes, references, patterns and purchasing recommendations. The cultural product was often cross-border from its inception.

A British person finds an Australian tutorial. An Indonesian maker demonstrates a technique. A Canadian shop stocks an unusual component. An American artist turns it into something nobody realised they needed. Someone in the UK buys it and introduces it to a friend. Indonesia is, somehow, involved again. Indonesia is always involved.

This is not a neat pipeline from American innovation to passive foreign consumption. It is a **distributed feedback environment**: people responding to one another, learning, adapting, recombining and producing new possibilities.

The platform owner may capture the fee. The network generates much of the reason anyone came to the platform in the first place.

## 4. 👾 The NPCs Made Your Cool Shit

Let's discuss the allegedly unimportant people.

The normies. The hobbyists. The girlies. The blokes making model railways. The woman who knows an unreasonable amount about miniature embroidery. The person who has spent six years finding the perfect green dye. The fandom artist. The pattern tester. The local supplier. The person who can explain why your crochet tension is wrong without making you feel like an idiot.

They are not merely an audience for technological innovation. **They are participants in innovation.**

A niche community can identify demand that no major retailer knew existed. Its members can test techniques, develop tools, document failures, teach each other and establish specialist supply chains. A hobby becomes a product; a product becomes a tutorial; a tutorial becomes a new hobby. Some people earn their living this way. Others make no money at all, but still create value for everyone around them.

Then, after enough iterations across enough countries, somebody produces a tiny amigurumi Sith Lord.

**CALIFORNIA, DO YOU WANT THE LITTLE SITH LORDS OR NOT?**

Because the little Sith Lords do not descend fully formed from a server farm. They come from people with materials, skills, spare time, shared culture and the capacity to experiment. You can generate ten thousand images of crocheted Darth Maul. That is not the same thing as maintaining the network of people who can design, make, teach, sell and improve an actual object.

The weird niche stuff was not peripheral to the internet. **Finding the weird niche stuff was one of the internet's central attractions.**

## 5. 🕸️ Girl Internet Was Economic Infrastructure, Actually

The story of the internet is often told through founders, engineers, hardware, venture capital and spectacular valuations. Meanwhile, communities associated with crafts, fandom, fashion, domestic knowledge, personal publishing and mutual teaching have repeatedly been treated as unserious.

Except these communities were developing habits of networked participation that became central to online commerce: recommendations, reviews, collaborative knowledge, trust, identity, small-scale entrepreneurship, peer support and the rapid circulation of specialist information.

This is not a claim that women invented every platform or that all male-coded internet cultures were unproductive. It is a criticism of the cultural sorting mechanism that labels one set of networked practices *innovation* and another set *women being online*.

Bro. **It was the same fucking internet.**

The men playing with computers, games, modding and fan projects were not somehow operating outside the social world of women making patterns, trading knowledge and organising communities. Their practices overlapped, influenced one another and shared infrastructure. The distinction was often one of status and recognition, not whether anyone was creating value.

See also the Polaris node *Girl Internet Explained With Techbros*. Exhibit M supplies the physical fulfilment problem: even when the network successfully creates demand, its participants still need materials, parcels and affordable access to markets.

## 6. 🏭 Copying the Makers, Then Wondering Where the Makers Went

Before today's generative-AI arguments, independent artists had already spent years dealing with copied designs, unauthorised print-on-demand listings, mass-market imitations and cross-border enforcement headaches.

Print-on-demand marketplaces lowered barriers to legitimate independent selling. They also made it possible for other people to upload artwork they did not own. A creator could discover copies of a design across multiple listings, report them, and face the prospect of repeating the exercise tomorrow. Platforms have takedown processes; copyright litigation and enforcement also exist. **The existence of a legal remedy does not mean an individual creator can afford to use it effectively.**

Temu is associated with PDD Holdings; SHEIN is a separate business. Both have faced intellectual-property disputes, but they are not one interchangeable Chinese parent company, and allegations must be separated from established findings. Copying a protected design is also not legally identical to producing a broadly similar product.

Generative AI introduces different technical and legal questions: training inputs, licensing, exceptions, memorisation, outputs, attribution and compensation. It would be wrong to assert that historical failures of marketplace copyright enforcement were *deliberately engineered* to clear the way for AI without evidence.

But the common economic question is unavoidable: **who gets to scale the monetisation of creative work, and who is left paying to produce and defend it?**

When the creator must personally police a thousand copies, the creator is doing unpaid enforcement labour. When a platform earns money from high-volume activity, its incentives may not perfectly align with the creator's. Those conflicts deserve scrutiny, not another speech about democratising creativity.

## 7. 🤖 AI Slop Is Not a Substitute for a Living Culture

There are useful applications of AI. This archive itself is produced through human–AI collaboration. The issue is not whether a machine can assist someone. The issue is whether economic systems reward the reproduction of existing cultural outputs while undermining the conditions in which people create new ones.

If cheap synthetic material floods discovery channels, the scarce resource becomes attention. If artists must compete with copied designs, automated listings and algorithmic volume, some will struggle to earn enough to continue. Whether, where and how much this is happening requires evidence: platform data, creator surveys, longitudinal income studies and careful distinctions between synthetic material, spam, legitimate reuse and infringement.

The same goes for claims about a 'Yandex bubble' or a sudden drop in Russian bot traffic. A change in crawler behaviour, referrals or search indexing is **not automatically** a measured fall in bot activity. That claim needs its own evidence before being used here.

The concern remains serious without pretending every hypothesis has already been proven: **a system can become extremely good at manufacturing more content while becoming worse at supporting the humans who generate cultural novelty.**

What a spectacular achievement. We have invented a machine that can show me a picture of yarn while the actual yarn is being escorted through customs by the Royal Marines.

## 8. 🏘️ Where Did All the Little Shops Go?

I have watched independent American sellers I used to buy from disappear, restrict their international sales, or become prohibitively expensive to order from. That is my experience; it is not a census of American small businesses.

Some sellers will have retired, changed interests, become ill or moved on. Others may have been squeezed by rising materials costs, platform fees, weak demand, copying, time pressures, shipping costs or the need to take more hours in another job. We cannot assign causes to particular people without asking them.

But this is precisely why the economic question matters. The disappearance of a small maker is not always just one lost shop. It can mean the loss of a specialist supplier, a teacher, a product line, a customer relationship, a local income stream and a node in a wider creative network.

For a rural or small-town producer, modest international revenue may be meaningful household income. That income can circulate through local shops, services and suppliers. The size of that effect must be measured rather than inflated with invented multipliers. But dismissing it because each individual transaction is small would be an error.

The economy is not composed solely of companies large enough to have their own lobbying department.

## 9. ♻️ The Sale That Never Happened

Here is the feedback problem.

The customer wants the yarn. The maker has made the yarn. The platform displays the yarn. Then the customer sees the delivered price and closes the basket.

The maker might see a missed order, or nothing at all. The platform might record abandonment. The postal service never sees a parcel. The government sees no export. If the customer stops browsing American sellers entirely, even the abandoned basket disappears.

**Demand has not necessarily vanished. The ability to convert demand into a transaction has deteriorated.**

That is a critical distinction. A policymaker looking at falling small-exporter sales could interpret the result as weak demand, low productivity or insufficient entrepreneurship. But the products might still be desirable. The connection between producer and customer may simply have become too costly.

There is a possible feedback loop: higher fulfilment costs discourage orders; lower order volumes reduce the commercial appeal of international services; some sellers stop offering them; customers learn not to bother; potential demand becomes less visible. This is a **hypothesis**, not a demonstrated USPS causal chain.

The system can lose participants and then misread their absence as evidence that they never mattered.

**America, your feedback environment has a hole in it the size of a postage label.**

## 10. 🌍 America, You Are Losing More Than the £49.51

An international customer who finds a maker she loves may buy from them repeatedly, recommend them, learn from them and introduce other people to their products. The first purchase is also the beginning of a relationship.

If an American seller becomes difficult to buy from and a Canadian seller becomes the practical alternative, the customer may develop new habits. Even if American shipping later becomes cheaper, the relationship may not automatically return.

This matters to cultural influence as well as trade. American-owned platforms helped facilitate international creative exchange, but the exchange was reciprocal. Foreign customers and creators helped make those spaces vibrant. Ordinary transactions generated familiarity and goodwill in ways that no state messaging campaign could fully manufacture.

There is no need to pretend every yarn order is a geopolitical summit. That is rather the point. **A country's global presence is also made of millions of ordinary, voluntary relationships.**

Why would you make those relationships harder to maintain and then spend a fortune wondering how to improve your international appeal?

## 11. 🏛️ A Respectful Request to Reprioritise the Economy, FFS

American policymakers, technology executives, investors and anyone else currently trying to build the future: could you please examine the economic consequences of your decisions **outside the industries whose executives you already know by name**?

The request is practical:

1. **Audit the delivered-price problem.** Compare like-for-like USPS and Canada Post international small-parcel rates, service levels, weights and historical changes. Do not treat a seller's checkout charge as an official postal tariff.
2. **Measure the exports that fail before shipping.** Ask marketplaces and small sellers about abandoned international baskets, destination-specific conversion, suspended international delivery and lost repeat customers, with privacy safeguards.
3. **Assess tariff spillovers.** Identify whether imported materials, equipment, packaging and logistics inputs are raising costs for American makers and carriers. Separate documented effects from conjecture.
4. **Protect practical rural access.** Evaluate international shipping affordability as a small-business and regional-development issue, not merely a line on a postal balance sheet.
5. **Make creator rights usable.** Improve affordable cross-border mechanisms for reporting and contesting infringement, with safeguards against false claims and abusive takedowns.
6. **Study the whole creative ecology.** Account for hobbyists, part-time sellers, teachers, microbusinesses and specialist suppliers, not only large exporters and platform revenue.
7. **Test whether AI and platform incentives sustain creators.** Measure participation, discovery, income and exit, not just how cheaply systems can generate another million items.

These are requests for investigation and policy attention, not claims that one administration, tariff or company caused every price rise and closure. British VAT is British VAT. Postal costs have multiple drivers. Small businesses face different circumstances. Fine. **Then investigate the actual mechanisms and stop treating the cumulative result as somebody else's problem.**

The point is not that defence, advanced computing or industrial strategy never matter. The point is that **ordinary productive infrastructure matters too**. If you only count what looks spectacular, you can miss the systems making ordinary prosperity possible.

And while we're reprioritising: please stop proposing elaborate technological interventions to manage women's alleged grumpiness when some of us are providing extremely clear information about what is irritating us.

I do not require an oxytocin bombardment. The effectiveness and delivery assumptions behind that idea belong in their own scientific critique. Here, the relevant mechanism is much simpler:

**Woman wants yarn → woman can afford delivered yarn → woman buys yarn → American maker gets paid.**

No chemical weapons. No billionaire enrichment scheme. No venture-capital pitch about disrupting crochet. Just commerce.

## 12. 🧶 The Little Sith Lords Are a Leading Indicator of Whether Anyone Is Having Fun

This is not an argument that society should measure its entire prosperity in tiny crocheted Darth Mauls. Although, frankly, it would be an improvement on several dashboards.

It is an argument that the ability to make, exchange and enjoy strange little things tells us something about the health of an economy. People need time, materials, knowledge, tools, customers and enough security to experiment. The product might be frivolous. The underlying capabilities are not.

A flourishing creative network does not only produce things people already knew to ask for. It produces the unexpected: techniques, friendships, skills, specialist businesses, local income, shared culture, and yes, a little Sith Lord somebody in another country suddenly needs very badly.

If you extract the finished designs, flood the market with copies, make the original makers harder to find, and make the materials expensive to obtain, you can preserve an enormous archive of yesterday's creativity while weakening the conditions for tomorrow's.

And then you will have the nerve to announce that the future of culture is a subscription service.

**America. Please stop breaking everything.**

I have money. Your makers have talent. The international network exists. The woman has already found the yarn.

**PLEASE JUST LET ME BUY THE PRETTY YARN.**
