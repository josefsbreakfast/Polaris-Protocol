# 📋 Exhibit D: Fucking Cables
**First created:** 2026-09-16 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-16  
*Interoperability is a consumer-facing property, and the string between the two holes should not require specialist knowledge.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Humanity discovered that electricity could travel through wires.

The consumer technology industry responded by producing:

**a frankly unnecessary number of slightly different fucking ends for the wires.**

This would be irritating enough if the cable were incidental.

It is not.

The miraculous portable computer at the end of the cable has a battery.

The battery requires electricity.

Electricity must reach the battery.

Therefore:

**THE FUCKING CABLE IS PART OF THE SYSTEM.**

And for a remarkably long period, nobody seemed particularly interested in making the boring bit of the system boring.

---

## 🔌 Exhibit D, In One Drawer

USB-A.

USB-B.

Mini-USB.

Micro-USB.

USB-C.

Apple 30-pin.

Lightning.

MagSafe in several incarnations.

Barrel connectors whose principal technical specification appears to be:

**almost, but not quite, the one you need.**

Then USB-C finally arrives looking like standardisation has won.

Excellent.

Except physically identical USB-C cables can support different combinations of charging power, data speed, display capability and other functions.

So:

> **Customer:** I have a USB-C cable.
>
> **Technology industry:** Excellent.
>
> **Customer:** Will it work?
>
> **Technology industry:** What do you want it to do?
>
> **Customer:** **BE A FUCKING CABLE.**

A standard is only as useful as the predictability it gives the person holding the object.

---

## 🧺 The Cable Drawer Is An Archive Of Industrial Failure

Every household contains, or has contained, some version of:

- a drawer;
- a biscuit tin;
- a box;
- a carrier bag;
- a nest behind the television;

containing cables that cannot safely be discarded because nobody can establish whether the mysterious black connector at the bottom still belongs to something important.

This is not necessarily hoarding.

**This is vendor-risk management.**

Somewhere in the archive is a cable belonging to a camera last seen in 2007.

We cannot identify the camera.

We cannot identify the connector.

We nevertheless know with absolute certainty that **three days after throwing it away somebody will need the bastard.**

A successful standard reduces the amount of private archaeology required to live with ordinary technology.

---

## 🪢 The Cable Itself Was Often Shit

There was another problem.

For years, enormous engineering effort went into the object at the end of the cable while the cable itself could feel like an afterthought.

Some soft-jacketed cables developed familiar failure patterns near the connector:

**pristine → grubby → suspicious wrinkle → tiny split → hello, conductor.**

Other cables went in the opposite direction.

The outer jacket felt stiff and reassuring until repeated bending concentrated stress at the same point and the casing cracked.

Pets did not improve matters.

Neither did children.

Neither did bags, desks, sofas, chairs, bedside tables or the radical consumer behaviour known as:

**using the fucking cable.**

Braided jackets and better strain relief eventually became more common.

For a long time, however, **cable that tolerates being a cable** could feel suspiciously like the premium option.

A high-value device attached to a shit cable is a system containing a shit component.

**You do not get to average the engineering excellence upwards.**

---

## 💷 The Cable

There was also a period when a genuine replacement charging lead could be expensive enough that many ordinary users did not casually accumulate spares.

You had:

**The Cable.**

The Cable lived beside the bed.

The Cable went downstairs.

The Cable went to work.

The Cable went on holiday.

The Cable was retrieved from behind furniture.

The Cable developed an alarming structural weakness immediately adjacent to the connector.

The Cable was nevertheless informed that it would be remaining in service.

Because replacing a basic piece of charging infrastructure had somehow become a meaningful purchase.

This matters because redundancy is useful.

A resilient household wants:

**charger in bedroom + charger in bag + charger at work + spare in drawer + reasonable chance your friend has one**

not:

**one proprietary sacred object whose disappearance creates a minor household emergency.**

A household should be able to possess redundant pieces of mundane infrastructure without conducting a capital-allocation meeting.

---

## 🎵 Apple Had Already Understood This

The particularly annoying thing is that Apple had already demonstrated that it understood the value of compatibility inside its own ecosystem.

The 30-pin dock connector travelled across generations of iPods and then into early iPhones and iPads.

For a user who already owned an iPod, a later Apple device could arrive into a household that already possessed:

- a cable;
- a charger;
- perhaps a dock;
- accessories;
- knowledge of how the thing connected.

That was proprietary interoperability.

It still created a real household benefit.

> **New Apple thing?**
>
> Oh good.
>
> **I already have the fucking cable.**

That is valuable.

The spare cable in the drawer is not merely junk.

It is **installed household infrastructure**.

When the next product works with it, the manufacturer has increased the usefulness of the new product without requiring the household to manufacture, purchase, learn or store another fucking thing.

---

## 🔋 We Had Just Learned To Recharge The Fucking Batteries

This transition was especially strange because consumer culture had just spent years learning a perfectly sensible material lesson:

**stop throwing batteries away; recharge them.**

Rechargeable AAs and AAAs created a wonderfully legible household system.

> Batteries flat?
>
> Take them out.
>
> Put them in charger.
>
> Put spare set in device.
>
> Continue using device.

The battery was not married to the object.

It was household infrastructure.

Cameras, toys, radios, game devices and other electronics could share compatible cells.

Then portable digital electronics arrived with an apparently even better proposition:

> **The battery is rechargeable and included.**
>
> Excellent.
>
> **You cannot necessarily take it out.**
>
> What?
>
> **Just charge the device.**
>
> Fine.
>
> **With its cable.**
>
> Where is the cable?
>
> **The special one.**
>
> **FOR FUCK'S SAKE.**

Integrated lithium-ion batteries brought real engineering advantages in energy density, weight, form factor and device design.

The point is not that portable electronics should have remained full of AA cells forever.

The point is that two separate design choices arrived together:

**rechargeable battery — excellent**

and

**rechargeable battery increasingly integrated into a proprietary object — hang on**

With rechargeable AAs:

**battery reaches end of useful life → replace battery**

With a deeply integrated battery:

**battery reaches end of useful life → repair problem**

We had just learned to recharge the fucking batteries.

**Why did the next technological revolution make the rechargeable battery harder to replace?**

---

## 🔌 Please Use The Charger You Already Own

Then came another magnificent phase of the transition.

Manufacturers began removing mains adapters from some device boxes.

There is a perfectly coherent environmental argument for this.

If households already possess compatible chargers, do not manufacture and ship another redundant object.

Excellent.

**But compatibility has to come first.**

The absurd transitional experience is:

> **Manufacturer:** We are no longer supplying the mains adapter because you already own one.
>
> **Customer:** Fine. Here is the mains adapter I already own.
>
> **Manufacturer:** Excellent.
>
> **Customer:** And here is the cable supplied with the new device.
>
> **Manufacturer:** Excellent.
>
> **Customer:** These do not fucking connect.
>
> **Manufacturer:** Ah.

You can sell us less stuff.

In fact:

**please sell us less unnecessary stuff.**

But the stuff we already fucking own has to work.

Dematerialisation without interoperability simply transfers the compatibility problem to the customer.

---

## 🥛 Availability Is A Product Feature

There is a commercial point here that should not have taken decades to become obvious.

**device easy to keep charged → device available when wanted → device gets used → user experiences value → user likes device**

A magnificent portable computer with a dead battery is:

**a small glass rectangle.**

Charging availability therefore increases the value of the product without changing the product.

Every compatible charger at:

- home;
- work;
- a friend's house;
- a hotel;
- a café;
- a train;
- an airport;
- a car;

makes the device easier to use.

This is the clever version of ecosystem design:

**stay because everything works nicely together**

rather than:

**stay because replacing everything you own would be an administrative and financial catastrophe.**

Convenience creates loyalty too.

You do not necessarily need to trap Sandra.

**Make sure Sandra's phone is not fucking dead.**

---

## 📳 The Vibrator Test

We regret to inform Silicon Valley that certain categories of cheap rechargeable consumer electronics have, at various points, achieved a remarkable user-facing technological capability:

**you could identify the fucking charger.**

We are not proposing the global personal-device industry should take all engineering instruction from the bedside drawer.

We are proposing a benchmark.

If Sandra can identify the correct charger for a small rechargeable object in that drawer more reliably than she can determine whether the USB-C cable beside her laptop carries the power, data or display capability required for the task:

**your standardisation programme requires further work.**

The user should not need specialist knowledge to select:

**the string between the two holes.**

If a trillion-dollar technology ecosystem loses the **can an ordinary person identify the correct fucking cable?** benchmark to inexpensive mass-produced silicone dicks:

**do not announce Computer God yet.**

Fix the strings.

---

## 📡 Wireless Charging Is Cool. We Need A Second.

Wireless charging is clever.

It is useful.

Increasing standardisation is welcome.

It also arrived in households that had not finished digesting the previous generations of charging infrastructure.

The real household does not transition like:

**old state → innovation → new state**

It looks like:

**old state + newer state + device from 2008 + Micro-USB + Lightning + USB-C + adapter + power bank + old tablet + work phone + one inexplicably immortal peripheral → Tuesday**

A new standard enters a physical world already containing decades of objects.

The old things do not disappear because the keynote ended.

---

## 💣 WE STILL HAVE RANDOM UXO

America,

Britain occasionally discovers unexploded ordnance from the Second World War and has to close a road.

We mention this because you appear unclear on the concept that:

**infrastructure generations overlap.**

USB-C did not vaporise Micro-USB.

Wireless charging did not remove cables from houses.

A new laptop did not make old peripherals cease to exist.

A new operating system did not make Sandra's printer younger.

A new standard enters a physical world in which time has already happened.

**Transition is part of the engineering problem.**

Backward compatibility is not nostalgia.

It is recognition that civilisation cannot conduct a simultaneous global hardware refresh every time somebody in California has a keynote.

We need a second to catch up.

**WE STILL HAVE RANDOM UXO.**

---

## 🔪 Sometimes A Thing Has Enough Things

The cable also returns us to the wider product philosophy.

Sometimes a thing does not need to do more things.

A charging cable can be:

**a good charging cable.**

A cable should not require an app.

A charger should not require an account.

The object does not become technologically primitive because its function is comprehensible.

We just about tolerate Swiss Army knives having special editions.

That does not mean every household object needs to become one.

The correct product roadmap can sometimes be:

**it works; leave it the fuck alone.**

---

## 🧱 Interoperability Is Infrastructure

A standard is not merely a standards document.

Its value appears in ordinary life.

It means:

**the cable already in the drawer has a reasonable chance of solving today's problem.**

That creates:

- household resilience;
- lower replacement costs;
- less duplication;
- reduced material consumption;
- easier travel;
- easier borrowing;
- simpler support;
- lower cognitive burden;
- less private cable archaeology;
- more useful existing hardware.

Interoperability is therefore not merely:

**two engineers can make these systems communicate under laboratory conditions.**

It is:

**Sandra can connect the fucking thing.**

If everything technically complies with standards but Sandra still needs six cables, three dongles and a forum thread:

**Sandra does not experience interoperability.**

## 🏠 Next Problem: Why Does My Lightbulb Have A Brand Allegiance?

The cable problem does not end at the cable.

It scales.

Smart speakers.

Lights.

Televisions.

Audio.

Security devices.

Home automation.

Accounts.

Apps.

Hubs.

Cloud services.

Vendor ecosystems.

Suddenly:

> **Your whole house can work together!**
>
> Excellent.
>
> **Provided your whole house belongs to the correct ecosystem.**
>
> For fuck's sake.

Interoperability inside one manufacturer's garden can be useful.

It is not the same thing as an interoperable technological environment.

That is Exhibit E's problem.

For now:

**please make the fucking cable work.**

Exhibit A:

**VLOOKUP could not look left.**

Exhibit B:

**Windows eventually made OFF not quite mean OFF.**

**everybody knew the consumable battery would wear out and put it inside anyway.**

**humanity standardised electricity and then spent decades arguing about the shape and capabilities of the fucking string.**

The recurring requirement remains remarkably modest:

**make the ordinary thing boring, durable, predictable and compatible.**

Do not make Sandra understand the product architecture merely to keep the product alive.

And if you intend to remove something from the box because she already owns one:

**MAKE SURE THE ONE SHE OWNS FUCKING FITS.**
