## ✨ Orientation — Hello, Bankers

Today we are going to learn something exciting.

### A little girl is not a digit.

Very good.

A little girl is also not:

- a data point;
- a customer relationship;
- an asset class;
- a counterparty;
- an entry in a compliance database;
- a risk-weighted exposure;
- an inconvenient provenance problem;
- a tiny securitisation opportunity wearing shoes.

She is, and this is where we shall have to concentrate very hard:

**a person.**

🌟 Excellent work, everyone.

Unfortunately, people can produce information.

And sometimes information is extracted from people through circumstances which we technically describe in law as **crime**.

This creates a difficult problem for institutions accustomed to believing that information begins wherever their database says it begins.

It does not.

Let us fetch the crayons.

This node uses a deliberately generic **Exhibit X** to examine what happens when criminal exploitation produces information, that information survives the original exploitation, and successive institutions inherit, copy, analyse, transfer, preserve or derive value from it.

The contemporary Epstein material provides an obvious reason to ask the question. Financial institutions provide an obvious institutional test case because they sit at the intersection of information, assets, transactions, compliance and regulation.

That does **not** establish that any particular institution possesses, exploits or derives value from any particular Epstein-derived dataset.

We will be doing the unfashionable thing and establishing that separately.

Because provenance.

See? We're learning already. 💕

## 💕 This Is A Little Girl

Here is our first diagram.

```text
        👧
        │
        ▼
      PERSON
```

Wonderful.

Now:

```text
        👧  ≠  1
```

Still with us?

Excellent.

A person can have information **about** her.

That does not make the information the person.

```text
PERSON
  │
  └── produces / reveals / is observed through
                    │
                    ▼
               INFORMATION
```

And from there we can have:

```text
person
  ≠
information about person
  ≠
record containing information
  ≠
dataset containing record
  ≠
legal rights in dataset
  ≠
analysis derived from dataset
  ≠
institutional knowledge derived from analysis
  ≠
economic value derived from any of the above
```

These are different objects.

They can have different owners, controllers, custodians, recipients, purposes, legal statuses and jurisdictions.

The person may be protected by one body of law.

Her personal data may be protected by another.

A database containing it may create different legal rights.

Money derived from criminal conduct may engage proceeds-of-crime law.

A copy held by police may be evidence.

An inference derived from the information may become institutional knowledge.

This distinction will become important later when somebody discovers a spreadsheet and begins behaving as though the spreadsheet was discovered naturally occurring in the wild.

## ⚡️ Where Did The Number Come From, Sweetheart?

Suppose somebody has:

`DATA POINT X`

An exciting financial question might be:

> What is X worth?

An Embodied Information Ecology question comes first:

> **Where the fuck did X come from?**

Perhaps X was voluntarily supplied.

Perhaps it was publicly available.

Perhaps it was legitimately purchased.

Perhaps it was lawfully observed.

Perhaps it was reasonably inferred from other lawfully obtained information.

Lovely.

Or perhaps X exists because somebody **exploited a child**.

Oh.

Now we have provenance.

And that changes the informational object we are examining.

Compare:

> X happened.

with:

> X happened, and we know X because somebody obtained that information while committing a serious offence against a child.

The underlying fact may be identical.

The informational history is not.

**Provenance is information too.**

## 💖 Crime Can Produce Information

This is apparently our difficult lesson.

Exploitation does not only produce physical, psychological, sexual, social and economic consequences.

It can also produce **information**.

An exploited person may become an information source inside an abusive information environment.

The resulting information might include:

- names;
- addresses;
- relationships;
- communications;
- photographs;
- recordings;
- medical information;
- sexual information;
- financial information;
- behavioural observations;
- vulnerabilities;
- preferences;
- social-network information;
- information about other people;
- information capable of creating coercive leverage;
- inferred characteristics.

The important cybernetic point is simple:

**ending access to the human being does not necessarily end access to what was learned from the human being.**

The person can leave the room.

The information can remain.

It can then be copied.

Transferred.

Archived.

Combined.

Analysed.

Monetised.

Inherited.

Disclosed.

Preserved as evidence.

Used to produce further information.

Used to make decisions.

Used to inform computational systems.

And so we arrive at:

```text
exploitation
     ↓
information
     ↓
spreadsheet
```

Please observe that:

```text
spreadsheet
     ↑
     │
STILL CAME FROM HERE
     │
exploitation
```

🎉 **Well done.**

Microsoft Excel has not performed baptism.

## 🌙 Provenance Is Information Too

Where information came from can alter what later actors are entitled to do with it.

That means provenance is not merely tedious administrative metadata.

It can be legally, ethically and operationally material.

It may affect questions of:

- lawfulness;
- fairness;
- transparency;
- permissible purpose;
- disclosure;
- evidential handling;
- regulatory duties;
- economic benefit;
- remediation.

UK data-protection law already contains a rather troublesome recognition of this.

Where UK GDPR applies and personal data were not obtained from the person themselves, the provenance of that information can matter. Article 14 creates transparency requirements for indirectly obtained personal data, while Article 15 can entitle a data subject to **available information about the source** of personal data being processed.

So British data law has, in fact, encountered the advanced philosophical proposition:

### Where did you get that?

Nobody panic.

There are exemptions, jurisdictional limitations and circumstances in which information cannot be disclosed.

But the conceptual recognition matters.

The origin of information can itself be information to which legal consequences attach.

## 💫 Data Does Not Become Clean Because You Copied It

Another challenging exercise.

If:

`A = information extracted through exploitation`

and somebody makes:

`A₂ = copy of A`

we have not necessarily performed:

`A₂ = ethically and legally neutral information`

Likewise:

```text
A
↓
analysis
↓
B
↓
model
↓
C
```

does not answer the provenance question.

It makes the provenance question **harder to see**.

And here we reach one of the central propositions of the node:

> **Human provenance can disappear faster than informational utility.**

The information may remain useful precisely because it preserves something learned from the original person.

But successive transformations can make the original human relationship less visible.

For our financial-services learning group:

> **Copying the naughty spreadsheet does not make it a good spreadsheet. 😌**

## ✨ Four Laws For Our Four Little Boxes

Unfortunately, Parliament has not enacted the:

**Little Girls Are Not Data Points Act 2015.**

We must therefore use several existing legal regimes.

### Modern slavery law

Modern-slavery law principally sees **the exploitation of the human being**.

The Modern Slavery Act 2015 defines forms of exploitation relevant to trafficking broadly, including sexual exploitation and circumstances involving force, threats or deception used to induce a person to provide services or enable another person to acquire benefits.

That gives us a route into the **information-producing event**.

But it does not obviously create a permanent legal label reading:

`⚠️ INFORMATION PRODUCED THROUGH EXPLOITATION`

which automatically travels with every copy and derivative forever.

### UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act

Data-protection law principally sees:

**processing of personal data.**

Its concepts include:

- lawfulness;
- fairness;
- transparency;
- purpose limitation;
- data minimisation;
- special-category data;
- source;
- recipients;
- access;
- restriction;
- erasure.

But please put down the tiny Union Jack.

**British citizenship does not make UK GDPR follow somebody's data everywhere on Earth.**

Territorial jurisdiction depends principally upon the relevant processing and establishment/activity tests.

`British person + American database`

does not automatically equal:

`Britain controls American database`.

### Proceeds of Crime Act

POCA asks a different question:

> Is there property or value representing benefit from criminal conduct?

Its concept of property is broad and includes intangible or incorporeal property.

But:

```text
personal information
        ≠ automatically
criminal property
```

Everybody keep your little Bloomberg terminals calm.

The actual asset or economic benefit has to be identified and the statutory tests applied.

### Criminal evidence

Criminal procedure sees yet another object:

**evidence.**

And here British data law already contains a wonderfully useful distinction.

Material may sometimes need to be retained because it is evidence even where other considerations would favour erasure.

That can require **restriction of processing rather than destruction**.

In other words:

> **Preservation is not permission.**

We may need to keep the thing.

That does not mean:

> **WHEEEEEEEEE FREE DATA.**

## 💕 Exhibit X

Let us meet X.

X is a little girl.

X is criminally exploited.

During that exploitation, information concerning X and potentially other people is produced or obtained.

Call the resulting informational corpus:

`Dataset A`

```text
👧 X
 ↓
exploitation
 ↓
Dataset A
```

Eventually, the direct exploitation ends.

### Dataset A does not evaporate.

Oh dear.

Copies exist.

```text
A
├── A₁
├── A₂
└── A₃
```

A₁ remains with its original custodian.

A₂ travels abroad.

A₃ enters Institution B.

Institution B analyses A₃ and produces:

`Analysis C`

C contributes to:

`Inference D`

D influences:

`Decision E`

Now we have a question.

At which arrow does the law stop remembering X?

And at which arrow does the **institution** stop remembering X?

Those may not be the same point.

Our question is therefore no longer merely:

> Who hurt X?

It is also:

> **Where did the information produced through what happened to X go?**

And:

> **What happened to its provenance along the way?**

## ⚡️ A Special Exercise For Banks

Now imagine Institution B is a bank.

Everybody remain calm.

We are **not** asking:

> IS THE BANK DOING SLAVERY?????

We are asking several considerably more boring questions.

Does the bank possess:

- original information?
- copies of it?
- transaction records concerning it?
- estate records?
- compliance information?
- records revealing provenance?
- an economic asset derived from it?
- money potentially representing criminal benefit?
- information capable of triggering regulatory or reporting obligations?

These are not interchangeable propositions.

A bank could know something about an asset without owning it.

It could process information without possessing an economic interest in it.

It could possess transaction records revealing provenance without possessing the underlying dataset.

It could possess information generating suspicion of criminal property without possessing that criminal property itself.

It could also have made confidential reports to regulators or law enforcement which are not publicly observable.

Therefore:

### 💕 Baby's First Due Diligence 💕

```text
What do you have?
        ↓
Where did you get it?
        ↓
Where did they get it?
        ↓
What was done to obtain it?
        ↓
What have you done with it?
        ↓
Who did you give it to?
        ↓
What did you make from it?
        ↓
What did you know?
        ↓
When did you know it?
        ↓
What duties did that trigger?
```

Look!

**Provenance.**

And nobody even had to invent blockchain.

## 💖 Britain Would Also Like To See The Homework

The British state does not have one enormous red button marked:

### `FIX INTERNATIONAL CRIME`

This may disappoint the finance sector.

Different legal systems instead attach to different parts of the problem.

```text
personal-data processing
        ↓
UK GDPR / DPA where jurisdiction attaches

exploitation
        ↓
criminal / modern-slavery law

criminally derived property or value
        ↓
POCA / financial-crime architecture

regulated financial institution
        ↓
financial regulation / reporting

evidence held overseas
        ↓
international evidence mechanisms
```

If relevant evidence is held in America, Britain is not necessarily helpless.

Mutual Legal Assistance provides one established mechanism for obtaining overseas evidence.

For qualifying electronic evidence and providers, additional UK-US mechanisms may sometimes be available.

The correct mechanism depends upon **who actually holds the information, what kind of information it is, what investigation exists and what statutory requirements are satisfied**.

The Atlantic is an enforcement boundary.

It is not necessarily an information black hole.

## 🌙 Oh No. We Found A Gap.

Here the jokes thin out.

Modern-slavery law can remember the exploitation.

Data-protection law can remember qualifying personal data and its processing.

POCA can remember property and value representing criminal benefit.

Criminal procedure can remember evidence.

Financial regulation can remember regulated actors and obligations.

International cooperation can move certain evidence between jurisdictions.

Each regime may work perfectly well according to its own ontology.

And something can still fall between them.

There does not appear to be one general legal mechanism saying:

> **This informational object exists because a human being was criminally exploited. That provenance must remain legible through every subsequent copy, transformation, transfer, derivative and jurisdiction.**

Instead:

```text
human harm
   ↓
information
   ↓
copy
   ↓
transfer
   ↓
analysis
   ↓
derivative
   ↓
institutional knowledge
```

can gradually become:

```text
DATA 😌
```

The human provenance can disappear while the informational advantage survives.

That is not merely a data-protection problem.

It is an **information-ecology problem**.

They allow:

```text
source information
      ↓

      ↓
statistical relationship
      ↓

      ↓

      ↓
```

A person can cease to be individually identifiable at some stage while an informational advantage derived from what happened to them continues to influence later systems.

This produces a particularly nasty possibility:

> **The system can stop legally recognising the person before it stops benefiting informationally from what happened to them.**

That requires considerably more attention than:

`we deleted the row 👍`

## 💫 Putting On Less Ritz

This is why the node lives here.

Complex institutions frequently present information-governance problems as though they are impossibly sophisticated technical questions requiring seventeen consultants, several committees and a man called Sebastian.

Sometimes they are.

Sometimes the difficult technical question is:

> **Where did you get that?**

And when the answer is:

> Database B.

you ask again.

Where did **Database B** get it?

And when the answer is:

> Archive A.

you ask again.

Where did **Archive A** get it?

And you keep going until the system reaches the beginning of the chain.

Sometimes the answer at the beginning is another database.

Sometimes it is a government.

Sometimes it is a company.

Sometimes it is a criminal.

And sometimes, at the beginning of a very impressive institutional information architecture, there is:

`👧`

The trick is continuing to ask the question until the system reaches the **human being**, rather than accepting the first database as the beginning of reality.

CRAKEN follows the information.

Not merely:

```text
offender → victim
```

but:

```text
offender
   ↓
victim
   ↓
information
   ↓
institution
   ↓
database
   ↓
analysis
   ↓
decision
   ↓
feedback environment
   ↓
human beings
```

The original crime can stop while some of its informational consequences continue circulating.

The perpetrator can disappear.

The company can close.

An estate can be distributed.

A custodian can change.

The information can cross an ocean.

The file format can change.

A database can be rebuilt.

An analysis can be derived.

A model can learn.

A decision can be made.

And none of those transformations necessarily tells us whether the original human provenance remains legible.

A system which cannot reconstruct that route cannot reliably determine **what it has inherited**.
