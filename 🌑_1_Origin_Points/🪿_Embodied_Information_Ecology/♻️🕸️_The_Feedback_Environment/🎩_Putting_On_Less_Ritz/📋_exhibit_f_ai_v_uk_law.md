# 📋 Exhibit F: AI v UK Law  
**First created:** 2026-09-16 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-20  
*America sold us artificial intelligence. Britain appears to have neglected to mention that the computer would first have to meet Britain.*  

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Dear America,

We have encountered a slight implementation problem.

You told us artificial intelligence would reduce administrative work.

We therefore pointed some artificial intelligence at the British state.

It found **approximately 7,000 statutory duties to consult**.

This is not, strictly speaking, less admin.

It is, however, an extremely useful discovery.

On 4 September 2026, Conservative MP Mike Wood asked the Cabinet Office a spectacularly boring Parliamentary Question:

> To ask the Minister for the Cabinet Office, pursuant to the Answer of 28 August 2026 to Question 18648 on Government departments:  
> artificial intelligence, how much statutory consultation duties have been identified by the AI tool for which the latest data is available.  

On 14 September, following a correction to the original answer, Cabinet Office minister Mark Ferguson supplied the number:

> Using the AI tool, the Cabinet Office has identified approximately 7000 statutory duties to consult.

That answer does **not** demonstrate that there are 7,000 decisions requiring lawyers to manually override AI systems.

It does something more useful.

It gives us a glimpse of the **legal constraint surface against which automated British government must operate**.

And that raises a rather larger question.

Before everybody spends another enormous pile of Treasury money automating government:

**Do we actually understand what the existing complexity is for?**

---

## 🧮 Seven Thousand Ways To Ask Somebody First

A statutory duty to consult is not simply an annoying piece of paperwork somebody forgot to delete.

Some undoubtedly deserve review. Some may duplicate other requirements. Some may reflect obsolete institutional arrangements. Some may have become unnecessarily cumbersome. Some may be excellent candidates for legislative simplification.

Others exist because Parliament previously decided:

**before exercising this power, somebody must ask somebody else.**

That distinction matters.

An automated workflow encountering an applicable statutory consultation requirement has not merely encountered an inefficient step in a business process.

It has encountered **law**.

The resulting pathway might look something like:

**automated process → potentially applicable statutory duty → applicability assessment → consultation → consideration of responses → authorised decision → evidential record → workflow continues**

AI may be extremely useful throughout that chain.

It may identify the legislation.

It may retrieve relevant material.

It may prepare documents.

It may organise consultation responses.

It may identify inconsistencies.

It may substantially reduce the repetitive administrative labour surrounding the decision.

Wonderful.

**Please sell us that.**

But if the difficult part of the workflow requires somebody to determine whether a statutory duty applies, whether the consultation satisfies the law, what weight may lawfully be given to representations, or who possesses authority to make the resulting decision, the labour-saving device may have reached a boundary where specialist human judgement remains necessary.

The machine has not failed.

Britain has not necessarily failed.

America has not necessarily failed.

**The task was badly specified.**

## 💥 Constraint Collision

This gives us a more useful way of talking about AI implementation than simply asking whether Britain is burdened by too much regulation.

The British state contains different kinds of complexity.

### Accidental Complexity

Legacy systems.  
Duplicate records.  
Re-keying.  
Incompatible databases.  
Obsolete forms.  
Poor information architecture.  
Processes nobody has reviewed since Sandra got her first Nokia.  

**Please destroy this with extreme prejudice.**

### Necessary Complexity

Some things are genuinely complicated.

Tax law governing several kinds of transaction is more complicated than recording who ordered sandwiches.

There is no technological principle requiring reality to become simple because somebody has purchased a dashboard.

### Protective Complexity

Consultation.  
Appeal rights.  
Audit trails.  
Safeguarding requirements.  
Separation of authority.  
Evidential standards.  
Requirements to obtain specialist input.  

These may make a process slower because **sometimes friction is governance**.

### Institutional Sediment

Requirements that once solved a real problem but whose continued usefulness nobody has properly tested.

These are excellent candidates for review.

### Vendor-Created Complexity

The new portal.

The new login.

The new dashboard.

The AI assurance workflow.

The AI transformation programme.

The mandatory training required to operate the labour-saving device.

Sandra is delighted to announce that she has been promoted to **AI Exception Governance Lead (Statutory Consultation Transformation)**.

Sandra would like Thursday back.

---

## 🪤 Do Not Automate Complexity Before Establishing What The Complexity Is For

That is the actual implementation rule.

If the obstacle is obsolete law:

**change the law.**

If the obstacle is terrible data:

**fix the data.**

If the obstacle is incompatible systems:

**fix the interoperability.**

If the obstacle is a statutory safeguard Parliament still considers necessary:

**design the technology around the safeguard.**

If the decision requires accountable specialist judgement:

**leave an accountable specialist human there.**

If the AI saves twenty minutes but operating the assurance, exception and verification architecture takes thirty-five:

**stop.**

This should not be controversial.

It is merely difficult to fit on a keynote slide.

## 🏛️ This Did Not Begin With Starmer

There is an understandable temptation to turn British AI implementation into another party-political argument.

That would miss the more useful question.

The UK Government published a **Generative AI Framework for HMG in January 2024**, under the previous Conservative administration.  

It included contributions from departments and institutions across government, including HM Treasury, the Government Legal Department, Cabinet Office, DSIT, HMRC, the Ministry of Defence and GCHQ.

The subsequent Labour government significantly expanded the adoption programme.

In July 2024, Technology Secretary Peter Kyle commissioned Matt Clifford to lead an **AI Opportunities Action Plan**.  

Its remit explicitly included AI adoption, government use, data, infrastructure, procurement and regulatory reform, drawing on evidence and interviews across industry, academia, government, regulators and civil society.

Published in January 2025, the plan recommended a **Scan → Pilot → Scale** model for government adoption.

It also acknowledged that the AI application layer remained early in its development and that deployments needed to be tailored to their particular settings.

At the same time, it encouraged government to **“move fast and learn things.”**

The Government accepted the adoption recommendations and committed to building a cross-government technical horizon-scanning and market-intelligence capability.

This therefore isn't particularly well described as:

**Labour discovered AI and spent all the money.**

Nor is it:

**the Conservatives would never have done this.**

British government was already developing institutional approaches to generative AI before the change of administration. The subsequent government chose to accelerate and broaden adoption.

The more useful historical question crosses the party boundary:

**How did the British state decide what this novel technology could usefully do?**

## 👁️ Who Was Advising?

Now we reach the interesting bit.

Not:

**WHO DID THIS?**

Not:

**WHICH AMERICAN CONNED WESTMINSTER?**

Not even:

**WHICH MINISTER SIGNED THE PAPER?**

Those questions may occasionally matter.

But the more useful systems question is:

**Who supplied the information from which government decisions about AI were made?**

For a major adoption programme, we should be able to reconstruct something like:

**political objective → advice → technical assessment → legal assessment → operational assessment → alternatives → business case → procurement → implementation → KPI → evaluation**

Who occupied those positions?

What expertise was represented?

What expertise was missing?

What evidence was available at the time?

Which assumptions came from vendors?

Which came from civil servants?

Which came from academics?

Which came from economists?

Which came from lawyers?

Which came from people who actually perform the work being automated?

What implementation dependencies were identified?

Were data remediation and interoperability costs included?

Were legal constraints mapped before the productivity assumptions were calculated?

Were projected savings measured against the cost of assurance, verification, specialist escalation and ongoing infrastructure?

And—critically—

**who inside government possessed sufficient technical understanding to say “yes, this technology works” while retaining sufficient institutional independence to say “and we should not buy it for this problem”?**

## 🎩 The Adviser Problem Is Not An Adviser Scandal

There is already a formal advisory architecture.

Matt Clifford led the AI Opportunities Action Plan and was subsequently appointed the Prime Minister's AI Opportunities Adviser, reporting directly to the Prime Minister and Technology Secretary and working with DSIT's AI Opportunities Unit. His published remit includes advising on adoption and productivity, engaging with leading AI companies and strengthening sovereign UK AI capability.

The Action Plan itself recommended government relationships with AI vendors and startups so that officials could understand forthcoming capabilities and communicate public-sector needs.

The Government also published arrangements concerning Clifford's declared outside interests and conflict management.

None of that establishes improper influence.

Indeed, government **needs people who understand the technology**.

It needs relationships with the companies building it.

It needs academics.

It needs entrepreneurs.

It needs lawyers.

It needs economists.

It needs procurement specialists.

It needs security expertise.

It needs operational civil servants.

And it needs Sandra.

The governance question is whether those perspectives are assembled in a structure capable of distinguishing:

**technological capability**

from

**commercial opportunity**

from

**government adoption**

from

**public value**.

Those things overlap.

They are not synonyms.

## 💰 Treasury Would Like To Know What We Bought

This is where adoption as a policy objective becomes interesting.

If an institution's objective is:

**increase AI adoption**

then increased AI adoption demonstrates progress.

Treasury's question is different:

**Did Britain receive more useful government for the money?**

Those measures can diverge.

A technically successful AI deployment might still be poor value if it requires enormous integration work.

A tool might perform its designated task brilliantly while failing to reduce total labour.

A pilot might save staff time but create expensive assurance requirements elsewhere.

A model might automate document handling while revealing that the underlying information architecture needs several years of remediation.

Conversely, an unglamorous £2 million interoperability project might produce considerably more usable administrative capacity than a £20 million AI programme.

The relevant KPI cannot therefore merely be:

**AI deployed: yes/no.**

It needs to include things like:

**net staff time saved.  
total implementation cost.  
ongoing operating cost.  
specialist escalation rate.  
error and correction rate.  
service quality.  
legal compliance.  
user experience.  
vendor dependency.  
switching cost.  
domestic capability retained.**

And preferably:

**Did Sandra get Thursday back?**

## 🇺🇸 America Has Interests; Britain Has Subscriptions

Dear America,

This is where you come back into the story.

You are allowed to have national interests.

You are allowed to have industrial policy.

You are allowed to want American technology companies to succeed.

You are allowed to sell us things.

Britain is also allowed to ask whether buying them serves **British** interests.

This should not be interpreted as hostility.

It is procurement.

The complication is that the United States is not merely another vendor jurisdiction.

It is one of Britain's central defence, intelligence, diplomatic, financial, scientific and technological relationships.

American political and industrial decisions therefore produce consequences well outside America.

That does not give Britain a vote in American elections.

It does mean Britain has a legitimate interest in the stability and policy choices of a country upon which it has deliberately become substantially dependent across several strategically important systems.

And vice versa.

This is what interdependence means.

---

## 🗽 The Special Relationship Needs A Product Specification

If American technological leadership is supposed to form part of a wider allied strategic advantage, then the relationship cannot operate solely as:

**America develops.  
America scales.  
Britain subscribes.**

Britain contributes research, skilled labour, capital, procurement, defence cooperation, market access, infrastructure, scientific relationships and demand.

American firms contribute extraordinary technology, capital, scale, products and infrastructure.

Fine.

**Price the exchange.**

What value does each side contribute?

What capability remains in Britain?

What becomes dependent upon an overseas supplier?

What happens when prices change?

What happens when the supplier changes direction?

What happens when American domestic politics changes the strategic environment?

What happens when the technology works but British law requires a different workflow?

What information was given to British decision-makers before dependency was created?

These are not anti-American questions.

They are the questions one asks when an alliance becomes materially important.

The Special Relationship cannot mean:

**America has interests and Britain has subscriptions.**

## 💍 Mates' Rates

We are not demanding free GPUs because Churchill knew Roosevelt.

Although, while we have you—

No.

Focus.

The serious requirement is **candour**.

If you are selling technology to an ally whose government may reorganise critical public infrastructure around it, tell us:

**what it does.  
what it does not do.  
what remains experimental.  
what data architecture it requires.  
what integration normally costs.  
what expertise remains necessary.  
what new administrative work it creates.  
what becomes dependent upon the vendor.  
what comparable deployments actually saved.**

Then Britain can make its own decision.

That is considerably more useful than:

**THE FUTURE OF HUMAN INTELLIGENCE HAS ARRIVED.**

Dave does not procure futures of human intelligence.

Dave has a budget.

## 🧠 Perhaps Nobody Lied

There is another possibility worth preserving.

Perhaps nobody had a complete answer.

Generative AI developed extraordinarily quickly.

Governments were simultaneously being told that it represented a major productivity opportunity, a strategic capability, an economic growth sector and an international competitive race.

Companies themselves were discovering capabilities and limitations as models developed.

Governments were learning how the technology interacted with institutions that had never been designed for it.

Britain was not built as a machine-readable administrative state.

Law was not written as an API.

Departments accumulated legislation, guidance, case law, databases, records, delegations, forms, professional practices and institutional memory over decades.

Then somebody introduced a machine capable of processing information at extraordinary scale.

And the machine said:

**I have found approximately 7,000 statutory duties to consult.**

Oh.

That may not be evidence of deception.

It may be evidence that **the implementation problem was much larger than the product problem**.

And if so, saying that clearly is important.

Because the answer determines what Britain should buy next.

## 🪜 What Stopped The System Being Elegant?

This may be the most useful question in the entire exhibit.

Not:

**Why is British government so complicated?**

Ask:

**What stopped this particular system from becoming elegant?**

Was it bad technology?

Bad procurement?

Historical accumulation?

A legal safeguard?

Institutional fragmentation?

Incompatible data?

Underinvestment?

Vendor lock-in?

A deliberate distribution of authority?

A decision nobody has reconsidered for forty years?

Something that should be abolished?

Something that absolutely should not be abolished?

Only after answering that question do we know what kind of intervention is appropriate.

That is the difference between **digital transformation** and putting a computer on top of an unresolved institutional problem.

## 🧰 The Capability Britain Actually Needs

The answer may therefore be less glamorous than another AI initiative.

Britain needs durable, technically literate, multidisciplinary institutional capability sitting close enough to senior decision-making to interrogate major technology propositions **without having adoption itself as the success metric**.

Not an anti-technology office.

Not an AI sales department.

Something closer to a technologically competent institutional memory.

It should be capable of bringing together:

- technical capability;
- administrative and public law;
- information architecture;
- cybersecurity;
- procurement;
- economics and public finance;
- records and provenance;
- operational knowledge;
- domain specialists;
- workforce knowledge;
- implementation evidence.

Its most valuable sentence might occasionally be:

**“Yes, Minister, the technology is extremely impressive. No, Minister, we should not buy it for this.”**

Its second most valuable:

**“You do not have an AI problem. You have three databases that don't talk to each other.”**

And its third:

**“Treasury would like its money back.”**

## 📋 Exhibit F

The Parliamentary Question does not prove that Britain's AI programme is failing.

It does not prove that 7,000 lawyers are required.

It does not prove that American technology companies misrepresented their products.

It does not prove that ministers were badly advised.

It does not establish that British law is excessively complicated.

**That is precisely why it is useful.**

One tiny question has exposed a much larger field of inquiry.

The British state is attempting to apply rapidly developing computational systems to an institutional environment containing thousands of legally significant procedural constraints.

The sensible response is neither:

**DELETE ALL THE LAW**

nor

**THE COMPUTER IS EVIL.**

It is:

**What are these constraints?  
Why do they exist?  
Which should remain?  
Which should change?  
Which can be automated around?  
Which require human authority?  
What does that do to the business case?  
And who is responsible for knowing the difference before we spend the money?**

That is the conversation Britain needs.

And America—

because apparently we need to keep explaining this—

we do actually like your shiny things.

We would simply prefer to know what they cost **after they encounter reality**.
