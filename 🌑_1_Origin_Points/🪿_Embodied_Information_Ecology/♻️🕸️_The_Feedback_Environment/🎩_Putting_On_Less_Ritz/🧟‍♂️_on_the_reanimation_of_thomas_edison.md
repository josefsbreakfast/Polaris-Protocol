**First created:** 2026-10-25 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-25

🇬🇧 “That's lovely. Who built it?”

Well. Here we are again.

This is not a prosecution of technological ambition. I like technological ambition. I like extraordinary machines. I like eccentric people who become unreasonably excited about a technical problem and spend years finding out what happens if they try something that hasn't worked before. I like rockets. I like electricity. I like people who get carried away with a photocopier. I like the fact that somebody can spend six hours making an HTML background tile seamlessly, despite the fact that absolutely nobody asked them to.

What I do not understand is why some corners of Silicon Valley insist that admiring a machine requires me to suspend my interest in how it works, who made it, whether its claims are accurate, what happens when it fails, and whether its owner is allowed to collect an unnecessarily intimate account of my shitty angsty zine.

**WE WERE ALREADY IMPRESSED.**

You did not need to invent a god.

This node begins with Thomas Edison and Nikola Tesla, passes through Elon Musk and the peculiarly modern business of being a solitary genius with several thousand employees, and then takes a fairly serious detour through Leonardo da Vinci, Paul Orfalea, Kinko's, MySpace, PayPal, Mark Zuckerberg, HTML glitter, photocopier art, consent, privacy, Icarus, and the engineering department's sudden reappearance whenever a rocket goes somewhere unfortunate.

The thread is **information**. Who gets credit for producing it? How does it travel? Who can challenge it? What does the user learn? What does the platform learn about the user? And what happens when the prestige of the person at the top becomes more important than the feedback that would help the organisation correct itself?

The argument is not that successful founders are frauds, nor that all American companies operate alike, nor that the past was innocent. It is that **a story about technological achievement can become so devoted to its protagonist that it destroys useful information about the achievement itself**.

This is a survivor-authored analytical and satirical node. Dialogue is fictional, not a transcript. Personal recollections are presented as such. Historical claims should be checked against the sources and verification notes below; jokes about celestial navigation are not accident findings. And when I say *have you considered decolonising, sir? 🥺*, I am asking who has claimed the intellectual territory, not asserting that every act of misallocated engineering credit is literally colonialism.

## ✨ Key Features

- **Actual invention versus the legend:** Edison and Tesla as working technologists inside organisations, markets and technical histories—not comic-book opposites.
- **The unnecessary god:** Musk can be a consequential investor, organiser and technical participant without personally becoming every engineer in the company.
- **The accountability trap:** if the public is told one genius did everything, the public will eventually ask that genius why everything went wrong.
- **The cow placenta:** Leonardo remains extraordinary even when we learn exactly where his anatomical knowledge ran out.
- **The photocopying man:** Orfalea's account of working with his limitations offers something that heroic spectacle often doesn't: a method somebody else can use.
- **The glitter enclosure:** Kinko's, DIY zines and MySpace foreground what users could *make*; standardised social platforms raise different questions about who controls the medium and extracts information.
- **The feedback test:** who can interrupt, inspect, repair, decline, leave and say **HANG ON**?

## 1. 🧟‍♂️ America, What Have You Done With Thomas Edison?

Thomas Edison was a real inventor and industrial organiser. Nikola Tesla was a real engineer. Their work sits within an enormous transformation of electrical technology, industrial production and everyday life. It involved other inventors, technicians, machinists, financiers, patent systems, customers, manufacturers, utilities, and infrastructure. The electrical grid did not materialise because two men had an argument.

Yet popular culture has a peculiar fondness for turning this history into a two-man play: Edison the commercial villain; Tesla the pure misunderstood genius. Or Edison the miraculous inventor; Tesla the eccentric supporting character. Either way, the people who built the apparatus have a habit of disappearing.

Then a company called Tesla became closely associated with Elon Musk. The public story sometimes treats its leader as an Edison-like industrial polymath and a Tesla-like solitary visionary simultaneously.

**AMERICA, YOU HAVE RESURRECTED EDISON AND PUT HIM IN TESLA'S CLOTHES.**

And now we have to have a conversation about what a company actually is.

The funny thing is that the history was already impressive. We did not need to delete the laboratory to admire the laboratory's organiser. We did not need to erase the engineer to admire the person who helped finance the engineering. We did not need to reduce industrial development to a battle between good and evil before anybody could learn something from it.

The inventor is not the entire invention. The machine is not the entire infrastructure. The chief executive is not the entire engineering department. These are not insults. They are descriptions of reality.

## 2. ⚡ Thomas Edison Was Several Thousand People in a Trench Coat

Edison's Menlo Park laboratory, established in the 1870s, and the later West Orange complex matter partly because they show invention being organised. Experiment, fabrication, testing, documentation, patents, finance and commercial development were coordinated rather than treated as the occasional private inspiration of one man. Edison had important technical ideas and a remarkable ability to pursue commercially viable systems. Other people performed important technical work, too.

One of the interesting things about the historical Edison is precisely that he did **not** do everything alone.

He assembled people and facilities. He pursued problems through repeated experiments. His teams developed and improved devices within larger systems of production and distribution. The incandescent lamp is a useful case: a commercially workable electric-lighting system requires rather more than a glowing filament. It requires power generation, distribution, metering, components, maintenance, customers, financing and a way of making the whole business continue tomorrow.

That is an achievement of organisation as well as invention.

It is therefore a little unfortunate that the heroic retelling so often deletes the organisation.

🇬🇧 “Then why did he have a laboratory?”

🇺🇸 “TO INVENT THINGS.”

🇬🇧 **“IS THOMAS EDISON SEVERAL THOUSAND PEOPLE IN A TRENCH COAT?”**

Not literally several thousand in one room, obviously. The joke is about the way industrial work disappears into one surname.

There is an equally unhelpful counter-myth in which Edison invented nothing and merely stole everything. It is attractive because it restores a villain to the story. It is not an adequate history of invention. Patents, priority, technical originality, commercial viability and collective contribution are separate questions; each needs evidence.

The point is not to strip Edison of credit. It is to stop treating **credit** as a scarce substance that must be confiscated from everybody else before one person can possess it.

**Coordination is a contribution. Collaboration is not an embarrassment.**

## 3. ⚡ Nikola Tesla, the Engineer Beneath the Ghost

Tesla's work on polyphase alternating-current systems and induction motors deserves serious technical history. He contributed important designs and ideas to a field in which other engineers, firms and infrastructure builders were also active. He worked with employers and commercial partners. He had technical successes and commercial disappointments. Some of his later proposals were not realised. None of that is a contradiction.

What makes the subsequent legend difficult is the insistence that Tesla must be either a wronged saint or an impossible genius whose every speculative idea was a suppressed truth.

An engineer can be brilliant without being infallible. A patent can be important without being an entire power grid. A commercial setback can reveal something about finance, timing or organisation without proving the inventor was either a fool or a prophet.

🇺🇸 “EDISON WAS THE BUSINESSMAN. TESLA WAS THE GENIUS.”

🇬🇧 “They both worked inside industrial systems.”

🇺🇸 “ONE WAS GOOD. ONE WAS EVIL.”

🇬🇧 “That seems an unnecessarily small cast for the electrification of civilisation.”

🇺🇸 “TESLA UNDERSTOOD ELECTRICITY.”

🇬🇧 “So did quite a lot of other people.”

🇺🇸 “NOT LIKE TESLA.”

🇬🇧 “Are we doing history or casting a superhero film?”

The historical Tesla is interesting enough without requiring supernatural rehabilitation. Indeed, the mythology can be another form of disrespect: it replaces the technical record with an emotional demand to believe in the man.

**YOU CAN LIKE NIKOLA TESLA AND STILL READ THE ENGINEERING.**

---

### 🔧 What the electrical engineering actually involved

The historical Tesla deserves more than a supporting role in somebody else's origin story. A polyphase alternating-current system is not simply a different kind of wire or a clever slogan about electricity. Alternating currents offset in phase can create a rotating magnetic field. That field can drive an induction motor without the commutator arrangement associated with many earlier motor designs. Tesla's late-1880s patents and demonstrations were important contributions to making that approach practicable; they were not, by themselves, the whole process of electrifying a continent.

The distinction between **principle, machine and network** matters. A workable motor needs designs, materials, manufacture, maintenance and loads worth driving. An electrical supply system needs generation, transformers, transmission, distribution, standards, finance, rights of way and people who will keep it operating. Tesla's work belongs inside this history alongside other inventors, engineers and organisations, including Westinghouse's commercial and industrial efforts. Recognising that network does not take the rotating magnetic field away from Tesla. It makes the significance of his contribution easier to understand.

There is a second, quieter insult in the mythology of the solitary, misunderstood Tesla. It invites us to admire his isolation more than his electrical engineering. But his designs become *more* interesting when we ask how they worked, who developed them further, and what had to happen before they could power anything beyond a demonstration.

**YOU NAMED THE COMPANY AFTER AN ENGINEER. PLEASE LET US TALK ABOUT THE ENGINEERING.**

## 4. 🚗 You Named the Company Tesla

Tesla Motors was founded in 2003 by Martin Eberhard and Marc Tarpenning. Musk joined as an early investor and chairman in 2004, and became chief executive in 2008. Its subsequent development involved major contributions from many employees and partners, as well as Musk's leadership and financing. Those are different roles, and there is no good reason to pretend they are identical.

Nor is there a good reason to pretend the leadership role is trivial.

Recognising an opportunity, attracting money, recruiting specialists, accepting risk, making strategic decisions, developing manufacturing capability and sustaining an enterprise can all matter enormously. Someone may also participate meaningfully in technical discussions without being the sole author of the engineering.

But once we start calling the chief executive the inventor of everything, we lose the ability to distinguish what the chief executive actually did from what the organisation did.

And that matters because it affects how we understand success, failure and responsibility.

## 5. 🚀 Elon, You Were Already Doing Something Impressive

There is a perfectly respectable version of this story:

*I love this shit. It's really fucking cool. I have enormous wealth and access to resources. I am going to use them to help make it a reality.*

That is not an embarrassing thing to say. It is not a consolation prize for people who aren't clever enough to design a rocket engine. It describes a real function within technological development.

SpaceX's achievements in launch operations and reusable rocket technology are consequential. They depend on extraordinary work by engineers, technicians, production staff, operations teams, suppliers and many others, alongside leadership, financing, customers and public-sector infrastructure. We can examine Musk's documented decisions and technical involvement without pretending that he personally performed all of that work.

**THAT IS A WHOLE FUCKING JOB, ELON.**

The difficulty begins when the public story demands more: not merely a consequential leader, but a person whose demonstrated success is evidence of exceptional competence in every technical domain.

A person can understand a great deal about rockets and still need specialist advice about materials, medicine, election infrastructure, environmental science, law or the reproductive anatomy of a cow.

## 6. 🛰️ Having Satellites Is Not Having Magic

Starlink satellites and optical intersatellite links are impressive communications infrastructure. They are not evidence of an arbitrary capacity to alter unrelated systems from orbit. If somebody claims a satellite network can produce a specific effect, they need to identify the mechanism, access path, constraints and evidence.

This is the distinction explored elsewhere in Polaris in **🛰️ Guys, He Does Not Have a Death Star for Votes**. Possessing infrastructure, having access to a target, possessing a theoretical capability, and having actually carried out an operation are different propositions.

🇺🇸 “HE HAS SATELLITES.”

🇬🇧 “Yes.”

🇺🇸 “IN SPACE.”

🇬🇧 “That's where they're normally kept.”

🇺🇸 “HE CAN DO ANYTHING.”

🇬🇧 “Can he?”

Technological awe can be exploited by people who wish to imply powers they have not demonstrated; it can also lead critics to attribute fantastical powers to people they distrust. Both errors produce bad analysis.

## 7. 🌕 The Moon, the Maths and the Missing Engineers

And then there is the author's perfectly unreasonable desire to ask how bad one man's maths would have to be to crash into the Moon.

As a joke about the **solitary-genius narrative**, splendid. As a finding about any particular mission, absolutely not. The precise lunar-impact incident, spacecraft operator, cause and responsible personnel would need identification before making a factual attribution. Spacecraft can fail through interacting guidance, propulsion, communications, integration, environmental and operational factors. The chief executive does not become the trajectory calculator simply because the public recognises his name.

But if your public story says one genius did everything, it is not surprising when the public asks that genius to explain everything.

**When you present yourself as the solitary genius, everything becomes your fault.**

Not necessarily legally. Not necessarily technically. *Narratively.*

An organisation needs to distinguish technical causation, organisational responsibility and public attribution. They overlap, but they are not the same thing. The responsible executive may be accountable for oversight without having designed a faulty component. The engineer who made an error may have worked within organisational conditions that encouraged, concealed or failed to detect it.

If we care about preventing recurrence, we need the whole system back in view.

## 8. 🧠 The Halo Effect: When the Reputation Starts Doing the Engineering

A successful organisation can create a reinforcing loop:

```text
Real achievement
      ↓
Reputation for extraordinary ability
      ↓
Attention, capital and access
      ↓
Resources for further development
      ↓
Potential new achievements
      ↓
Stronger reputation
```

This is not automatically a problem. Reputation can help an organisation recruit, finance ambitious projects and sustain difficult work. It can also reflect genuine ability.

The risk appears when a reputation established in one domain is treated as proof of claims in another, or when a founder's personal authority substitutes for technical review. The relevant questions are empirical: who can challenge a decision, what evidence is required, how are dissenting specialists treated, and what happens when promises fail?

The halo effect is a useful explanatory concept, not a diagnosis of any particular person's mind. Audiences, investors, journalists, customers and critics all participate in building the story. A mythology can be bigger than its supposed hero.

**The reputation is not the test report.**

## 9. 🐄 Leonardo da Vinci: We Still Have the Painting, Darling

Britain and Europe do not require scientific heroes to have been right about everything before we can enjoy their work. We queue to see Leonardo's *Mona Lisa*. We examine his notebooks. We study his anatomical drawings. We ask which bodies he had access to, which observations were direct, which analogies he imported, and where his conclusions were mistaken.

His studies of human reproduction drew in part on animal anatomy; some representations of the uterus and associated structures reflect non-human anatomical models. The precise identification of particular bovine structures in particular drawings should be checked against specialist catalogues before this becomes a technical claim about a named folio. The point survives that necessary care.

**THE PATIENT IS NOT A COW.**

A mistake in anatomy does not make Leonardo's paintings vanish. It makes his scientific practice more interesting. Now we can investigate the observational limits, assumptions and comparative methods through which the drawing was produced.

🇺🇸 “HE WAS A GENIUS.”

🇬🇧 “Absolutely.”

🇺🇸 “A VISIONARY.”

🇬🇧 “Certainly.”

🇺🇸 “BEYOND THE UNDERSTANDING OF ORDINARY MEN.”

🇬🇧 “Well, he got some anatomy wrong.”

🇺🇸 “WHAT?”

🇬🇧 “Cow placenta.”

🇺🇸 “...”

🇬🇧 **“WE STILL HAVE THE PAINTING, DARLING.”**

An account of greatness that permits error gives us two useful things: the achievement and the evidence about how knowledge was produced. An account that cannot tolerate error sacrifices the second to protect the first.

**WE CAN ADMIRE LEONARDO MORE INTELLIGENTLY BECAUSE WE CAN EXAMINE WHERE HE WAS WRONG.**

## 10. 🖨️ Paul Orfalea and the Photocopying Man

I read about Paul Orfalea, the founder of Kinko's, when I was a teenager. What stuck with me wasn't a demand to worship the man. It was his frankness about dyslexia, limitations and the value of finding people whose skills complemented his own.

My response was something like: **Oh. I just need to find what my version of Kinko's is.**

That was useful information.

It did not require me to have his money, his circumstances or his particular abilities. It did not tell me I could achieve anything by believing hard enough. It gave me a way of thinking about a problem: find something worth doing; understand the skills and resources required; work with people who can do the things you cannot.

This is not a claim that Orfalea was a flawless employer or that Kinko's had no commercial problems. It is an account of what one person's explanation made available to another person.

The Great Man story says: **BEHOLD AN EXTRAORDINARY MAN.**

The more useful story says: **HERE IS HOW AN IMPERFECT PERSON ACCOMPLISHED SOMETHING.**

One invites admiration. The other can generate a plan.

And it matters that his limitations were part of the explanation. They were not an unfortunate secret to be removed by the publicity department. They explained why certain organisational choices were necessary.

**Being able to recognise, employ and support people who know things you don't is a form of competence.**

Silicon Valley, the photocopying man is giving the rocket man a masterclass in how to tell a useful story about entrepreneurship.

---

### 🧠 What the story actually taught me

This is an information-ecology point, not merely a motivational anecdote. An account of success can change at least two different things in its audience: **what they believe about the person being described**, and **what they understand how to attempt themselves**. Those are different outputs.

The heroic narrative may increase awe, admiration, confidence in a brand, or willingness to follow a leader. None of those necessarily tells the listener what to *do*. Orfalea's account, as I encountered it, carried a usable organisational principle: identify the opportunity, recognise the limits of your own abilities, and work with people whose abilities complement yours. I could adapt that principle without sharing his circumstances. The information survived transport from his life to mine because it described a mechanism rather than demanding imitation of a personality.

That is what made **“Oh, I just need to find what my version of Kinko's is”** possible. It was not a promise that I would become him. It was a change in my model of what building something might require.

A useful account of achievement does not merely tell us that something extraordinary happened. **It preserves enough information about how it happened that somebody else can learn from it.**

🇬🇧 “What did he do when he couldn't do something himself?”

🇺🇸 “HE TRANSCENDED HUMAN LIMITATIONS.”

🇬🇧 “The photocopying man hired someone who could do it.”

🇺🇸 “THAT'S NOT VERY CINEMATIC.”

🇬🇧 **“IT WAS MORE USEFUL TO ME.”**

## 11. 🖨️ Kinko's Was MySpace. I'm Just Saying.

Kinko's helped make reproduction and small-scale publishing equipment available to people who did not own a print shop. This matters for student papers, local newsletters, flyers, independent music scenes, campaign materials, pamphlets, small businesses and, most importantly for the purposes of this discussion, **shitty angsty zines**.

The photocopier was not merely a machine for duplicating an existing page. It was a creative instrument. Enlargement, reduction, contrast, repeated copying, collage, layering, physical movement across the glass: users discovered ways to make new images by playing with the reproductive process.

You did not need to be a publishing house to produce a publication. You did not need to be a professional designer to make something visually interesting. You could make something terrible and give it to six friends. You could make something brilliant and leave it at a gig. You could be a teenager with an opinion and a stapler.

**I COULD MAKE MY SHITTY ANGSTY ZINE IN PEACE.**

Kinko's was not MySpace literally. But both belong in a history of ordinary people gaining access to information-production infrastructure and discovering unexpected expressive uses for it.

The important innovation was not confined to the people who built the machines. The users were innovating too.

The copier operator who discovered a visual effect was experimenting with the apparatus. The teenager who learned a trick from a friend's zine was transmitting technical knowledge. The band that found a cheap way to reproduce artwork was using infrastructure to make cultural participation possible.

**The most revolutionary thing about a technology is not always what its inventor intended it to do. Sometimes it's what ordinary people discover they can do with it.**

## 12. 💻 HTML, Glitter and the Democratisation of Bad Taste

Early personal-web culture gave many people the digital equivalent of being left alone with a photocopier and a very questionable aesthetic instinct.

HTML tables. Background tiles. Animated GIFs. Custom fonts. CSS. Marquees. Entirely unnecessary cursors. Music that started playing when you opened the page. Several things blinking at once, none of them in agreement about the tempo.

Glorious.

There is a genuine technical and cultural connection here. Both photocopier art and personal-page design encouraged **vernacular technical literacy**: people learning by experimentation, imitation, error and play rather than waiting for professional permission.

| Photocopier play | Personal-web play |
|---|---|
| Cut-and-paste collage | HTML layout and embedded images |
| Enlarging and reducing | Resizing page elements |
| Letraset and hand lettering | Fonts and CSS |
| Repeated copying and distortion | GIFs, digital effects and remixing |
| DIY zines | Blogs and personal pages |
| Trading physical copies | Linking, sharing and circulating files |

MySpace, within its own limits, offered an environment where personalisation and amateur design could become part of participation. People made pages that were recognisably theirs. They discovered music. They joined scenes. They made catastrophically ugly things with enormous affection.

Not every MySpace page was accessible, secure or usable. The platform had commercial interests and privacy problems. We do not need to pretend it was paradise to recognise the importance of what users could control.

**The users were doing technological experimentation.**

And sometimes the experiment was a dancing hamster.

## 13. 💸 PayPal Was Also Cool, Actually

PayPal belongs in this story because payments are infrastructure. Helping individuals and small businesses transact online without independently arranging complex merchant-payment facilities made certain kinds of economic participation easier.

There were and are fees, fraud risks, account restrictions, disputes and regulatory obligations. None of those disappear because the core proposition was useful. Nor do they make the proposition useless.

The broad family resemblance to Kinko's is access: a capability once difficult for an individual to arrange becomes more readily available through a shared service.

**You want to receive money for something you've made? Here's a way to do that.**

Wonderful. Now we can discuss how the payment provider handles disputes, information, fraud, access and accountability. Those questions do not mean we hate payments.

This is a theme the industry could stand to remember.

**A technology can expand human agency while still requiring governance.**

---

### 🛒 A payment is a small act of sovereignty

The appealing thing about early online payment services was not that the world had suddenly invented money. It was that an individual selling a second-hand book, a handmade object, a design, a service or something peculiar they'd made on their own computer could take part in a transaction without first becoming a financial institution. That changes the practical meaning of having an audience. You can publish something; somebody can find it; they can pay you; you can post it. The distance between making and participating in an economy gets shorter.

The genealogy matters. PayPal did not emerge fully formed from a single founder's forehead. Confinity developed an early payment service; Musk's X.com and Confinity merged in 2000, and the combined enterprise later took the PayPal name. The corporate and technical history contains multiple founders, teams, commercial decisions and shifts of strategy. Even here, the history of an apparently simple product is a history of organisations learning, negotiating and changing course.

But there is a second lesson. A payment processor must do more than move numbers. It must establish identities and authorisations where necessary, resist fraud, handle disputes, comply with financial obligations and provide meaningful redress. The service acquires the power to deny access as well as enable it. That is not a reason to return to sending cheques in the post. It is a reason to take the rules of access seriously.

There is a difference between **giving someone a way to transact** and **claiming an unlimited entitlement to know and control the person transacting**. A system can legitimately need particular information for a particular transaction. That does not make every further use of that information legitimate.

The useful question is not simply, *did the company innovate?* It is: **what new capability did the user gain, what new dependency did they acquire, and what recourse do they have when the system gets it wrong?**

## 14. 👀 Zuckerberg and the Interview Somebody Should Have Conducted

In 2003 Mark Zuckerberg created Facemash, a Harvard website that presented student photographs for attractiveness comparisons. Contemporary accounts describe photographs being obtained from university online directories without the students' permission. Facemash preceded Facebook; it is not identical to Facebook, and the later history of social networking should not be treated as an inevitable consequence of one undergraduate project.

But the early incident invites a very ordinary question.

**Why did you use other people's photographs for this without asking them?**

That is not an objection to coding. It is not a refusal to recognise technical ability. It is a question about consent and the treatment of people affected by a system.

🇺🇸 “THIS YOUNG MAN IS A TECHNOLOGICAL VISIONARY.”

🇬🇧 “Wonderful. What has he built?”

🇺🇸 “A WEBSITE.”

🇬🇧 “Excellent. What does it do?”

🇺🇸 “RANKS PEOPLE BY ATTRACTIVENESS.”

🇬🇧 “Did they agree to participate?”

🇺🇸 “HE IS A GENIUS.”

🇬🇧 “That wasn't the question.”

🇺🇸 “HE DID IT IN HIS DORM ROOM.”

🇬🇧 “Did they agree?”

🇺🇸 “HE'S GOING TO CHANGE THE WORLD.”

🇬🇧 **“YES, THAT'S WHY WE'RE ASKING NOW.”**

The question is not whether someone should be permanently defined by a youthful act. People can learn, change and improve. The question is whether institutions sufficiently distinguish impressive technical initiative from permission to use other people as material for it.

**Being able to obtain information about somebody does not establish permission to repurpose it.**

Consent did not have to be invented after social media. The technology made existing ethical questions newly scalable.

## 15. ⬜ And Then There Was White Space

Here is a design-history shorthand, not an exact chronology:

**Kinko's:** Make a publication.

**MySpace:** Make a page.

**Facebook:** Populate a profile.

The differences are not absolute. Kinko's had rules, MySpace had platform control, and Facebook users made all kinds of creative things. But the contrast captures a real shift in what the interface invites people to do.

The photocopier foregrounds the user's artefact. The customisable personal page foregrounds the user's arrangement of the medium. The standardised social profile foregrounds fields and relationships defined by the platform.

Standardisation can improve accessibility, consistency, mobile compatibility and usability. A white background is not an act of political oppression. The interesting question is **who gets to decide the structure of participation, and who benefits from that structure**.

🇺🇸 “WE HAVE REVOLUTIONISED SOCIAL NETWORKING.”

🇬🇧 “Can I change the background?”

🇺🇸 “NO.”

🇬🇧 “Can I edit the HTML?”

🇺🇸 “NO.”

🇬🇧 “Can I make the text purple?”

🇺🇸 “THAT WOULD BE INCONSISTENT.”

🇬🇧 “Can I put a dancing hamster in the corner?”

🇺🇸 “WE HAVE OPTIMISED THE USER EXPERIENCE.”

🇬🇧 “What can I do?”

🇺🇸 “UPLOAD YOUR PHOTOGRAPHS, IDENTIFY YOUR FRIENDS, RECORD YOUR INTERESTS AND TELL US WHAT YOU'RE THINKING.”

🇺🇸 “A CLEAN, MINIMALIST INTERFACE.”

🇬🇧 **“YOU'VE TAKEN AWAY MY GLITTER AND GIVEN ME A FORM.”**

The form is useful to the platform. It makes information easier to organise, analyse and commercialise. That does not mean every design decision was made solely for extraction, but it means we should ask what the design makes possible for the operator as well as for the user.

### 🧾 The form is an information architecture

The Facebook/MySpace contrast becomes more interesting when we stop arguing about whether the interface looked nice. A free-form personal page and a structured profile do not merely *look* different. They invite different kinds of data and make different operations easy.

An amateur webpage might contain a blinking poem, a music player, an elaborate in-joke, an incomprehensible CSS arrangement and six friends who know what it all means. A platform can display that page, but turning every element into a comparable, queryable category requires interpretation. By contrast, standardised profile fields and explicitly recorded social connections already supply a degree of structure. That structure can support genuinely useful things: searching for a friend, making a service accessible across devices, filtering abuse, translating an interface, or connecting a community. It can also support profiling, targeting, ranking and measurement at scale.

This is not a claim that MySpace collected no data or that Facebook invented behavioural surveillance. Both platforms were commercial systems; both had incentives and constraints. The claim is narrower and more revealing: **the structure of the interface shapes the structure of the information available to its operator**.

When the user can alter the page but not the platform's rules, we should ask which decisions remain theirs. When the platform can alter ranking, visibility, field definitions, export tools and account access, we should ask who is permitted to contest those decisions. A user's capacity to speak inside an environment is not the same thing as their capacity to change the environment.

And there is a peculiar asymmetry in the resulting relationship. The platform can become increasingly capable of describing the user: their contacts, preferences, transactions, movements through a feed, reactions and likely interests. The user may become less capable of describing the platform: why they saw a particular item, why something vanished, how a recommendation was made, or what will happen to their work when the rules change.

**The platform learns to describe you while you lose some of the ability to describe it.**

That is the information-ecology problem beneath the white space. Not that the internet ought to be purple. Although, for the record, I do miss being allowed to make it purple.

🇺🇸 “WE HAVE REMOVED UNNECESSARY COMPLEXITY.”

🇬🇧 “From whose experience?”

🇺🇸 “THE USER'S.”

🇬🇧 “And made it easier to classify the user?”

🇺🇸 “WE HAVE CREATED A SEAMLESS EXPERIENCE.”

🇬🇧 **“YOU'VE TAKEN AWAY MY GLITTER AND GIVEN YOURSELF A DATABASE.”**

### 🪩 Who gets credit for the glitter?

The photocopier–HTML comparison is not only about nostalgia for creative freedom. It complicates the story of *where invention happens*. Once a technology is available, people experiment with it: they find unexpected effects, work around constraints, invent techniques, teach each other, and establish new cultural conventions. Some of those practices are later copied by companies, formalised into features, or turned into profitable markets.

That is another kind of distributed invention. The people building the infrastructure are not the only people generating knowledge about what it can do. The users are part of the feedback environment. Their improvisations reveal affordances, unmet needs, and entirely new uses that a product team may never have imagined.

Which creates an awkward question for a culture already struggling to remember its engineers: **when users invent a practice that makes the platform valuable, who gets recognised for that contribution?** A platform may provide the means, while a community supplies the aesthetic, technique, vocabulary or social purpose. Those contributions are not identical, and they do not automatically establish legal ownership; but they should not vanish from the history of innovation simply because they were unpaid, informal or gloriously ugly.

🇺🇸 “WE INVENTED A NEW FORM OF ONLINE EXPRESSION.”

🇬🇧 “Did you?”

🇺🇸 “OUR USERS HAVE BEEN INCREDIBLY CREATIVE.”

🇬🇧 “Then perhaps mention them.”

🇺🇸 “WE BUILT THE PLATFORM.”

🇬🇧 **“YES. AND THEY PUT THE HAMSTER ON IT.”**

The feedback is not just a metric telling the platform what people clicked. It can be a public record of people discovering what the technology *could become*.

## 16. 🕸️ From Tools to Environments

**When did the technological visionary stop selling us tools and start selling us environments in which we were expected to live?**

This is a question about degree and governance, not a claim that old tools were neutral or that modern platforms have no benefits.

A tool may let someone accomplish a bounded task. A large digital platform can also define the rules of interaction, the visibility of participants, the routes by which information travels, the conditions for leaving, and the data collected about people who never consciously chose to become its customers.

The questions change accordingly.

Who sets the rules? Who can inspect the system? Who can repair it? Who can take their work elsewhere? Who can refuse the collection of information? Who is represented in the data? Who can contest that representation? What happens to a community when the platform changes its interface, business model or moderation policy?

The operator of a platform may have enormous information about the people using it while those people have very little information about how the platform operates.

That is a feedback asymmetry.

And it is a very different proposition from letting someone borrow a photocopier to make a zine.

## 17. 🔐 The Photocopier Did Not Need to Know Why

There is an obvious qualification: a copy shop could handle confidential documents, record transactions or retain information. A particular historical Kinko's branch should not be assumed to have been perfectly private.

But a photocopier's basic function does not require it to infer the user's personality, classify their political opinions or build a persistent behavioural profile of why they wanted twenty copies of something.

Data minimisation is not nostalgia. It is a design and governance principle: collect and use the personal information appropriate and necessary for a specified purpose, rather than treating every accessible detail of a person's life as potential commercial inventory.

**Not every creative act is an invitation to establish a commercial relationship with its creator's inner life.**

The zine might be embarrassing. It might be brilliant. It might be political, romantic, ugly, silly, experimental or none of your business.

Sometimes the most empowering machine is the one that performs its function competently and **minds its own fucking business**.

### 🪞 Consent, control and correction are not the same thing

We need three distinct concepts here. **Permission or another valid authorisation** concerns whether an action is legitimate *before it happens*. **Ongoing control** concerns what choices, rights and meaningful avenues of objection people retain while a system operates. **Feedback and correction** concern whether new information about consequences can change the system's behaviour. These interact, but they are not substitutes for one another.

A thousand enthusiastic clicks do not retroactively grant a person permission to publish somebody else's photograph. A feedback survey cannot repair the absence of a required lawful basis. And a consent box, where consent is genuinely the relevant basis, is not a licence to disregard later objections, applicable rights or the effects of the system in practice. Nor is consent the lawful basis for every legitimate processing activity: the applicable law and context must be considered specifically.

The Facemash example is useful because the question the author wants asked is so basic that it almost sounds embarrassingly unsophisticated: *did those people agree?* But consent is one of the ways a system discovers that the people it acts upon have their own purposes, boundaries and authority. It is not simply an obstacle placed between an engineer and a clever experiment.

A photograph is not an invitation to every possible use of that photograph. A publicly reachable page is not permission to extract every available relationship. The existence of a technical route to some information does not settle the ethical or legal basis for taking it, recombining it, or making it visible to a different audience.

Nor does this mean every information use requires the same form of consent. Lawful bases, contexts and expectations differ. The point is that the affected person's position must be represented somewhere in the design and governance of the system. If the only question anybody asks is *can we build it?*, the system has lost an entire category of information about whether it should operate as proposed.

A good review would ask: whose information is involved, what did they reasonably expect, what new audience or inference will the system create, how can someone object, what happens when a mistake spreads, and who can stop the deployment? These are engineering questions as well as governance questions because they determine whether the system is fit for its actual environment.

**Consent is not an obsolete technology.**

And the more transformative the capability, the less persuasive it is to reply to these questions by pointing out how clever the inventor is.

## 18. 🎩 The Pitch Deck Is Not the Machine

Now we return to *Putting On Less Ritz*.

A technological company needs to communicate possibility. Investors may be interested in growth, market size, competitive advantage and the chance that something difficult can be made to work. This is a legitimate part of technological development.

A hospital, ministry, utility or public body buying that technology needs something else as well: assurance. Dependencies. Maintenance arrangements. Failure modes. Security. Data governance. Procurement terms. Repairability. Exit routes. Independent testing. A named person who can answer the telephone when something catches fire.

The same organisation may need to answer both sets of questions, but one answer cannot substitute for the other.

🇺🇸 “WE HAVE BUILT THE FUTURE.”

🇬🇧 “Does it take USB-C?”

🇺🇸 “NO.”

🇬🇧 “Can we repair it?”

🇺🇸 “THAT WOULD VOID THE WARRANTY.”

🇬🇧 “Can we inspect it?”

🇺🇸 “PROPRIETARY.”

🇬🇧 “Can we change supplier?”

🇺🇸 “NOT WITHOUT REBUILDING YOUR INFRASTRUCTURE.”

🇬🇧 “And who's responsible if it fails?”

🇺🇸 “PLEASE CONTACT CUSTOMER SUPPORT.”

🇬🇧 **“SO GOD HAS VENDOR LOCK-IN.”**

The dialogue is satire, not a description of the terms offered by any one company. Its question is real: **does the buyer possess enough information and leverage to govern the dependency they are acquiring?**

*I Still Dream of Orgonon* makes the same distinction between technological optimism and the institutional conditions necessary to make extraordinary machines safe to use. It is possible to love the machine and still demand a working stop button.

## 19. 🧯 Who Is Allowed to Tell the Genius He's Wrong?

The heart of this node is a feedback problem.

A technical organisation needs information about whether the machine works. It also needs information about whether the organisation works: whether staff can raise concerns, whether bad news reaches decision-makers, whether customers can report failures, whether independent reviewers can inspect claims, and whether anyone is empowered to say **HANG ON**.

Different loops ask different questions:

| Loop | Question |
|---|---|
| Technical | Does the apparatus behave as specified? |
| Operational | What happens under real conditions? |
| Organisational | Can people report problems without punishment? |
| Commercial | Do claims match demonstrated capability? |
| Regulatory | Can external institutions inspect and intervene? |
| Social | Is correction treated as information or disloyalty? |
| User | Can the person affected understand, refuse or change the system? |

A culture that treats the leader's reputation as the ultimate evidence can make the correction process more difficult. The risk is not that one person knows nothing; it is that other people may be discouraged from saying what they know.

**The organisation may possess the technical information necessary to correct itself while lacking the social conditions necessary for that information to travel.**

*Orgonon* gives us a useful operational sequence:

`NORMAL → MONITOR → HANG ON → INCIDENT → REMEDIATE → RESUME`

Who can trigger HANG ON? What evidence must they provide? Who is allowed to disagree with the founder? Does the organisation distinguish embarrassment from danger? Are the people with authority to halt a system also rewarded for refusing to halt it?

Those are not anti-innovation questions. They are what make continued innovation possible without accumulating preventable harm.

## 20. 👾 Debugging the Men: The Masculinity of Genius

The Great Man story is not just an account of how inventions happen. It is also a social performance.

The inventor who needs no help. The industrialist who never doubts. The cowboy who never falls off the horse. The man who commands every discipline because admitting a limit would make him less of a man.

This is not a description of all men or all founders. It is a particular cultural ideal that can become deeply inconvenient in collaborative technical work.

Because engineering requires people to ask questions, admit uncertainty, borrow expertise, document failures, change plans and accept that the junior specialist may know something the chief executive does not.

A masculine performance of invulnerability can therefore conflict with the very competence it claims to celebrate.

Four questions travel directly into **👾 Debugging The Men**:

1. **Who receives credit?**
2. **Who is allowed to be wrong?**
3. **Who is allowed to interrupt?**
4. **Who lives with the consequences?**

And one more, from the photocopier: **who gets to make something without becoming somebody else's data product?**

A culture that can celebrate technical ambition while admitting dependency and limitation is not weaker. It has more accurate information about what its members can actually do.

---

### 🧠 Competence is knowing where your competence ends

The Orfalea comparison deserves to return here, because it is the antidote to a particular performance of masculine authority. If the heroic founder must always know the answer, the people around him learn that bringing him an answer he dislikes may be socially dangerous. If his limitations are treated as shameful, the organisation acquires a powerful incentive to disguise them. An executive can then be surrounded by specialists and still make decisions in an artificially impoverished information environment.

The more useful account is not *I was incapable, therefore other people did everything*. It is *I had strengths and limitations; I identified the work; I found people with the complementary abilities; we organised the work so it could be done*. That is a description of competence, not a confession of its absence.

We should not pretend this problem belongs exclusively to men, Americans or technology firms. Status can distort feedback in hospitals, universities, militaries, governments, families and any other organisation. The gendered cultural story matters because the invulnerable male genius is one recognisable way of making a bad organisational habit seem admirable.

An institution that prizes heroic certainty may be excellent at making declarations and poor at receiving corrections. It can tell the world that the rocket will fly while making it difficult for a person with an inconvenient test result to say *hang on*. That is not toughness. It is a communications defect.

Four questions therefore recur: **Who receives credit? Who is allowed to be wrong? Who is allowed to interrupt? Who lives with the consequences?**

🇺🇸 “HE DOESN'T NEED ANYBODY.”

🇬🇧 “Then why does he employ anybody?”

🇺🇸 “THEY EXECUTE HIS VISION.”

🇬🇧 “And what if one of them knows something he doesn't?”

🇺🇸 “HE IS A GENIUS.”

🇬🇧 **“WHY HAVE YOU MADE YOURSELF SIT THE ENTIRE ENGINEERING EXAMINATION, SIR? NOBODY ASKED YOU TO.”**

## 21. 🌲 Stop Being Weird, California

Two nearby information-ecology arguments make this even clearer.

In *Old Man Willow Is Just a Fairy*, the problem is not merely identifying a dangerous tree. It is understanding the history, territory and relationships that the visitors have entered. The forest has memory; the visitors have an itinerary.

In *Baiting Metsänviha*, the company can represent a forest as a site for development while residents, animals, water, soil and cultural traditions experience a much larger environment. A construction plan is not the entire place.

The shared mistake is **selective representation**:

- The inventor is not the entire invention.
- The machine is not the entire infrastructure.
- The development site is not the entire forest.

## 22. 🖨️ Why Kinko's Is Still Cooler Than SpaceX — In My Head

This is not a technical ranking of photocopying against orbital launch systems. It is an explanation of what different stories of achievement did for one particular reader.

SpaceX's machines are remarkable. Kinko's machines were useful, accessible and culturally enabling in ways that deserve their own history. But when I remember reading about Orfalea, what I remember is acquiring a method I could apply to my own life.

**Oh. I just need to find what my version of Kinko's is.**

I did not need to become the inventor of the photocopier. I did not need to become a billionaire. I did not need to be the smartest person in every room. I needed to think about what I wanted to make possible, what I could do, what I could not do, and who or what could help.

That is what a useful account of achievement can transmit.

## 23. 🪽 Icarus Would Like His Engineers Back

The Great Man narrative makes for compact storytelling. It is often terrible incident analysis.

When everything goes right, the genius did it. When everything goes wrong, the genius did it. Except now, suddenly, the engineers are back, and everybody would like the public to appreciate the complexities of distributed technical responsibility.

We should appreciate those complexities. We should have appreciated them yesterday, too.

The concentration of credit creates a corresponding concentration of perceived blame. Both obscure the distributed mechanisms that produced the result.

That does not mean leaders escape accountability. A leader can be responsible for decisions, priorities and institutional conditions without personally making every technical choice. Accurate accountability requires knowing which is which.

**If you want the public to understand failure, teach them the organisation that produced success.**

### 🥺 The territory, the credit and the people who live there

The decolonising joke is worth returning to, but with its mechanism intact. It is not a claim that every dispute over invention is a colonial relationship, or that a person's nationality proves their politics. It is an invitation to examine the habit of treating collective work as territory that can be occupied by a single prestigious name.

In histories of empire, questions about whose land, labour, knowledge and institutions were made available to whom are indispensable. In histories of industrial technology, the corresponding questions concern whose experimental results, maintenance, manufacturing skill, public funding, technical standards and accumulated expertise are being compressed into the biography of one celebrated man. These are different historical processes, and the distinction matters. The shared analytical question is **who gets to define the account of what was produced, and whose contribution disappears when that account becomes authoritative?**

There is an especially awkward irony in celebrating Tesla's name as the emblem of technological brilliance while telling the story of a modern vehicle company as though its many engineers and workers were decorative. The best tribute to an engineer is not a mythology in which engineering labour vanishes.

## 24. 🧟‍♂️ We Were Already Impressed

I want the inventions. I want the ridiculous machines. I want the eccentricity. I want the experiments that fail and the experiments that work. I want the person who has been thinking about a problem for twenty years and finally finds the missing piece. I want the teams who build the thing. I want the person who finances the team because they think the thing is really fucking cool. I want the teenager with the photocopier. I want the awful HTML background. I want the person who knows when to stop and ask somebody else.

What I don't want is to be told that asking how the machine works constitutes a moral failure to appreciate the man whose name is on the company.

Edison is more interesting with his laboratories intact. Tesla is more interesting with his engineering and commercial history intact. Leonardo is more interesting when his errors can be examined. Orfalea was useful to me because his account left his limitations visible. A rocket is more interesting when we can understand the people and systems that made it fly.

The Great Man mythology offers a hero and asks us to stop there.

**We would like to keep going.**

The inventor is not the invention. The machine is not the entire infrastructure. The customer is not a data quarry. The forest is not an empty rectangle. The public is not obliged to mistake marketing for evidence.

And if you have built something extraordinary, we would very much like to see the drawings.

**The problem with turning inventors into gods is not that the inventors become too impressive. It is that the inventions become harder to understand.**

**WE WERE ALREADY IMPRESSED. STOP MAKING THE ENGINEERS DISAPPEAR.**

### 🐹 The users were part of the invention

There is one last category of people the Great Man narrative tends to leave out. Not the investors. Not the executives. Not even the engineers. **The people who got their hands on the machine and tried something nobody had asked them to try.**

A laboratory can invent a photocopier. A business can make it accessible. A teenager can discover that moving an image across the glass produces something extraordinary. An engineer can make a webpage possible. A community can decide what a webpage is *for*, and invent a visual language out of animated hamsters and terrible backgrounds. Later companies may learn from, standardise, restrict or monetise those discoveries. But the knowledge did not arrive from the founder's head alone.

So when we ask who built the future, the answer has to be larger than the person whose name is on the building. The workers built parts of it. The users taught it new tricks. The critics found its failures. The people who said **HANG ON** helped keep it accountable. And the people who could explain their methods made it possible for somebody else to begin.

**THE PHOTOCOPIER LET ME PUT MY FACE ON A FISH. THAT WAS RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT, DARLING.**
