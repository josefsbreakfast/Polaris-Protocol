# 🇪🇺 EU Wot, Tony?

**First created:** 2026-10-02 \| **Last updated:** 2026-10-02\
*If Britain is going to reopen the question of Europe, it might first
want a regulator capable of seeing the information environment in which
Britain will answer it.*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🛰️ Orientation

Apparently we are talking about Europe again.

Fine.

Good, even.

There are serious questions to be asked about Britain's relationship
with the European Union, what Brexit has actually produced, what forms
of cooperation are now materially useful, and what constitutional
arrangements Britain might want in future.

Unfortunately, the minute you make that politically salient, the
information environment does not politely wait for Parliament to decide
whether this is officially A Debate Yet.

Nobody needs a referendum date.

Nobody needs a manifesto commitment.

Nobody needs Tony Blair to finish the PowerPoint.

If a future British relationship with the EU becomes a plausible
political question, then it becomes a plausible object of:

-   polling;
-   audience research;
-   behavioural observation;
-   data brokerage;
-   profiling;
-   inference;
-   segmentation;
-   message testing;
-   recommender optimisation;
-   influencer recruitment;
-   paid amplification;
-   fundraising;
-   political consultancy;
-   and every other perfectly modern mechanism for discovering which
    message moves which person, through which channel, at which moment.

This is not an argument for controlling political speech.

It is an argument for recognising that **speech now travels through a
behavioural feedback environment**.

And Britain already knows this.

We had Cambridge Analytica.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. 🎩 Congratulations, Tony Has Discovered Europe

There is something faintly absurd about reopening a constitutional
question of this magnitude while discussing technological governance as
though the central problem were simply **AI**.

AI matters.

AI can make behavioural systems faster, cheaper, more adaptive, more
personalised and more scalable.

But AI did not invent the system.

The underlying machinery is older:

**observe → infer → classify → segment → test → target → measure →
adjust → target again.**

That is a feedback loop.

It can use AI.

It does not require AI.

So an AI regulatory framework which does not adequately address
behavioural influence can end up regulating the newest and most
glamorous component of a much older machine.

This isn't just about AI, Tony.

AI is the amplifier.

**The behavioural modification infrastructure is the thing being
amplified.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2. 🕸️ Political Advertising Is Much Too Small A Box

Calling this merely a problem of *political advertising* risks shrinking
the regulatory object until most of the system disappears.

The relevant system includes:

-   how behavioural data is captured;
-   what can be purchased from data brokers;
-   what can be inferred from apparently unrelated behaviour;
-   how people are sorted into audiences;
-   how political characteristics or susceptibilities are modelled;
-   how messages are experimentally tested;
-   how recommendation systems alter distribution;
-   how campaigns learn from response data;
-   how intermediaries obscure spending or responsibility;
-   how influencers and nominally organic content interact with paid
    political strategy;
-   how different messages can be delivered to different populations
    without meaningful public visibility;
-   and how the resulting response becomes new data for the next
    intervention.

The UK's own Information Commissioner's Office describes microtargeting
as analysing personal data to identify the interests of an audience or
individual in order to influence their actions. It describes tracking
technologies which observe browsing habits, likes and social
interactions to construct profiles.

Its political-campaigning guidance says parties and campaign groups
across the political spectrum have used data analytics to deliver highly
specific messages to small groups or individuals.

The Electoral Commission has described the democratic visibility problem
even more plainly: campaigners can adjust messages rapidly according to
what appears to work, while in some cases only the voter, campaigner and
platform know who received what and why.

That is not merely an advert.

That is a **feedback environment**.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 3. 🧪 We Already Did The Cambridge Analytica Bit

Britain does not need to imagine what happens when behavioural
analytics, political campaigning, wealthy patrons, platform data and
computational targeting meet.

We already had the fucking demonstration.

Cambridge Analytica was not a mysterious American weather event which
drifted across the Atlantic.

Christopher Wylie gave evidence to the House of Commons Digital,
Culture, Media and Sport Committee in March 2018. Parliament published
material concerning SCL Elections, Cambridge Analytica, Global Science
Research, AggregateIQ, data use and voter microtargeting.

Wylie told the Committee that before going public he had already begun
working with the ICO and UK authorities.

In May 2018 he then appeared before the United States Senate Judiciary
Committee at its hearing **Cambridge Analytica and the Future of Data
Privacy**.

The ICO subsequently described its investigation as the largest of its
type undertaken by a data-protection authority. It involved social-media
platforms, data brokers, analytics companies, academic institutions
**and all the major UK political parties**.

There were investigations.

There was enforcement.

There were parliamentary inquiries.

There have been subsequent reforms.

So the serious claim is not that Britain literally did nothing.

It is this:

> **Britain investigated the scandal without constructing a sufficiently
> comprehensive, continuously responsive regulatory architecture for the
> behavioural political system the scandal exposed.**

That distinction matters.

Because otherwise we are waiting for Cambridge Analytica II and
pretending the problem becomes real only after somebody gives evidence
to a select committee.

No.

The capability has already been demonstrated.

The market exists.

The techniques exist.

The regulator itself describes them.

**Treat the capability as present.**

That is not an allegation that a named actor is presently conducting an
unlawful operation.

It is basic regulatory competence.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4. 👁️ The ICO Can See The Problem

The especially interesting thing is that the ICO's own guidance already
contains much of the diagnosis.

It says political profiling can be invisible.

It says people may not understand how profiling affects them.

It says insufficient transparency can undermine trust in the democratic
system.

It says sophisticated profiling has the potential to influence the
voting behaviour of large numbers of people.

It explicitly tells organisations to consider effects on **individuals
and wider society**.

And then we encounter the conceptual pinch point.

UK data-protection law understandably focuses heavily on the processing
of personal data and the rights and effects experienced by identifiable
individuals.

The ICO's current guidance explains that although much political
profiling and microtargeting may constitute solely automated
decision-making, in most cases it is unlikely to produce the kind of
legal or similarly significant individual effect which triggers the
strongest Article 22 restrictions.

That is coherent within the legal instrument.

It is not sufficient as a complete theory of democratic information
ecology.

Because:

> **What happened to this individual's data?**

and:

> **What happened to the population's information environment?**

are not the same question.

A behavioural intervention can be individually modest and collectively
enormous.

That is precisely why the regulatory object has to extend beyond
individual privacy.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 5. 🧠 Assume Capability, Do Not Invent Operations

There is an important evidential rule here.

We do **not** need to announce that Reform, Labour, Tony Blair, Peter
Thiel, Elon Musk, an American campaign network, a British consultancy or
three men in a trench coat are definitely running a secret behavioural
operation about Europe right now.

That would require evidence.

It is also unnecessary.

The evidential threshold for alleging a particular operation is not the
same as the evidential threshold for recognising a regulatory risk.

Britain already has evidence that:

-   political microtargeting exists;
-   behavioural data can support profiling;
-   inferred data can be produced from observed behaviour;
-   political organisations use data analytics;
-   platforms provide audience-targeting infrastructure;
-   messages can be adjusted rapidly in response to observed
    performance;
-   sophisticated profiling can create wider societal risks;
-   and these systems can operate with limited visibility to the wider
    electorate.

Therefore:

> **The appropriate regulatory assumption is that technically available,
> economically valuable and previously demonstrated capabilities may be
> used. Democratic infrastructure should be designed accordingly.**

We do not need another whistleblower before beginning.

We have had the whistleblower.

His name was Chris.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 6. 🪞 Unfortunately, The Customers Also Write The Rules

There is another structural difficulty.

Political organisations are not detached observers looking down upon an
alien behavioural-advertising industry.

They campaign.

They profile.

They target.

They advertise.

They buy services.

They want to know which voters might listen to them and which messages
work.

Some of that is completely ordinary democratic campaigning.

Some of it can be useful.

Some of the people around the relevant tables will sincerely think that
they should behave responsibly and that better regulation is necessary.

That is not the point.

**Good intentions are not regulatory architecture.**

Nor is:

> *Well, our side only uses the machine responsibly.*

A democratic rule has to survive the actor you distrust.

And when the institutions making the rules can themselves benefit from
the regulated capability, there is an obvious collective-action problem.

Nobody needs to prove that this explains every legislative failure.

It is enough to recognise the structural conflict:

> **Who regulates the tool when the people writing the regulation are
> also potential customers of the tool?**

This is why self-restraint cannot carry the system.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 7. ⚖️ Please Locate Regulator

Britain does have regulators touching different parts of this
environment.

The ICO regulates personal-data processing.

The Electoral Commission regulates electoral finance and campaigning
rules.

Ofcom regulates substantial parts of the online-services environment
under the Online Safety Act.

That does not automatically create a regulator of the **whole
behavioural feedback system**.

The machine does not care which institutional box an intervention
belongs in.

Data can be acquired in one jurisdiction.

Processed by another company.

Modelled through another service.

Delivered through an American platform.

Purchased through a British campaign intermediary.

Optimised through an algorithm nobody outside the platform can inspect.

Paid for through political expenditure governed by another statutory
regime.

Experienced by a British voter.

And consequential for a British democratic decision.

Respectfully:

**please locate regulator.**

The regulatory architecture needs either a new institution or
sufficiently explicit coordination and expanded powers across existing
institutions so that the system cannot disappear into the cracks between
them.

That architecture needs to be capable of:

-   rapid investigation;
-   compulsory information gathering;
-   preservation of relevant campaign and targeting records;
-   meaningful access to targeting and delivery information;
-   audit of behavioural and recommendation systems where democratic
    risk is engaged;
-   scrutiny of political-data supply chains;
-   visibility over relevant intermediaries;
-   tracing expenditure through the behavioural influence chain;
-   protected researcher access;
-   cross-border regulatory cooperation;
-   enforceable technical standards which can change faster than primary
    legislation;
-   meaningful sanctions;
-   judicial challenge and appeal;
-   parliamentary scrutiny;
-   and transparent publication of enforcement activity.

Most importantly, it needs to be **responsive**.

A regulator which establishes what happened three years after the
democratic event is an archive.

Archives matter.

But an archive is not a brake.

## 8. 🗣️ No, This Does Not Require A Ministry Of Correct Opinions

This distinction needs to be brutally clear because otherwise the
easiest response is to pretend that regulating behavioural
infrastructure means regulating political thought.

It does not.

Nigel Farage is allowed to argue against European integration.

Tony Blair is allowed to argue for it.

Labour can make its case.

Reform can make its case.

Conservatives can make theirs.

Greens can make theirs.

A person with fourteen followers and an inexplicable commitment to
posting about customs unions at 3.17am can make theirs.

**The regulator does not need to decide which argument is true enough to
be spoken.**

The regulator needs to be able to see the machine around the speech.

Who paid?

What data was used?

What was inferred?

Which populations were constructed?

What targeting criteria were applied?

Was sensitive information involved?

What intermediaries participated?

How was the message distributed?

Was it experimentally optimised?

What did the recommender system do?

What records exist?

Can an auditor inspect them?

Can the public discover that different groups were being told materially
different things?

Can the regulator intervene when the law is breached before the entire
democratic event has finished?

That is infrastructure regulation.

Not thought policing.

Indeed, the democratic safeguards around the regulator are part of the
design precisely because **the British state should not acquire
arbitrary authority over political speech either**.

Independence.

Clear statutory purposes.

Published rules.

Due process.

Appeal.

Judicial oversight.

Parliamentary scrutiny.

Transparent enforcement.

The answer to private behavioural power is not unlimited state
behavioural power.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 9. 🤖 AI Is The Amplifier, Tony

AI obviously changes the threat model.

Generative systems can make message production cheaper.

Machine learning can improve classification.

Models can assist experimentation.

Synthetic media can increase the volume and variety of persuasive
material.

Automated systems can potentially shorten the distance between:

**observe response → infer preference → modify message → deliver
again.**

But if regulation begins and ends at *AI*, it misses the system into
which AI has been inserted.

The feedback loop existed before large language models.

Cambridge Analytica existed before ChatGPT.

Political microtargeting existed before the current generative-AI boom.

Data brokers existed.

Tracking pixels existed.

Behavioural advertising existed.

Recommendation systems existed.

Political consultancies existed.

Experimental message optimisation existed.

**AI did not invent behavioural governance.**

It increases the capabilities available to it.

So if Tony Blair's answer to technologically mediated democracy is
principally an AI-governance conversation, somebody needs to widen the
fucking diagram.

## 10. 🇺🇸 Sovereignty Means More Than Where The Server Lives

This becomes particularly important for Britain because much of the
relevant technological infrastructure is transnational.

A company may be American.

Its investors may be elsewhere.

Its models may operate across distributed infrastructure.

Its data supply chain may involve multiple jurisdictions.

Its political customer may be British.

Its users may be British.

And the constitutional question being influenced may belong entirely to
Britain.

That does not mean American people or American companies collectively
want one particular outcome on Europe.

They do not constitute one political mind.

Nor should the node pretend that every wealthy technology investor has
the same interests.

The structural problem is simpler.

**A British democratic choice should not become dependent on Britain's
inability to supervise foreign-owned behavioural infrastructure
operating inside the British information environment.**

That is a sovereignty problem whether the eventual result is Leave,
Remain, Rejoin, Rejoin-A-Bit, Norway-But-Not-Norway,
Customs-Union-With-A-Hat, or whatever constitutional beast emerges next.

If Britain cannot observe and regulate the systems through which
political influence is bought and optimised, then sovereignty has been
defined rather strangely.

## 11. 🇨🇳 China Is Not The Model, But It Does Ruin One Excuse

China needs handling carefully here.

It would be speculative to assert that the Chinese government declines
to raise a particular international proposal *because* it benefits from
behavioural control domestically unless there is evidence establishing
that motive.

We do not need that claim.

The more useful observation is that China plainly does not behave as
though algorithmic recommendation and behavioural information
infrastructure are inherently beyond regulation.

Chinese rules have addressed algorithmic recommendation, user profiling,
information distribution and services with public-opinion or
social-mobilisation capacity.

So:

**the technology is governable.**

The important democratic question is **how**.

China is not the regulatory model proposed here.

A British democratic regulator should not determine acceptable political
doctrine, mandate state-approved viewpoints, or turn behavioural
oversight into ideological control.

The useful contrast is almost the opposite.

Britain needs to demonstrate that a democratic state can constrain
behavioural power **without acquiring authoritarian power over political
thought**.

That means regulating systems, incentives, transparency, data use,
targeting and accountability while preserving plural political speech.

Harder?

Yes.

Welcome to democracy.

## 12. 🇪🇺 And Now You Have Mentioned Europe

This is why talking seriously about a future British relationship with
the EU creates an immediate governance question.

Not because somebody has secretly scheduled a referendum.

Not because we know a particular campaign is already committing a
particular abuse.

Because **political salience is itself enough to make behavioural
research valuable**.

If an issue may matter later, actors can begin learning now.

Which populations care?

Which are movable?

Which messages produce anger?

Which produce hope?

Which produce disengagement?

Which identity frames work?

Which economic fears work?

Which cultural associations work?

Which messenger works?

Which channel works?

Which sequence works?

What happens after exposure?

What should be tried next?

That learning does not have to begin during a regulated referendum
period.

The feedback environment can be learning long before the ballot paper
exists.

So if Britain's political class genuinely intends to reopen the European
question, then **regulatory readiness belongs upstream of the
campaign**.

Not after it.

Not after the scandal.

Not after the inquiry.

Not after Chris Wylie II arrives wearing an alarming amount of pink and
asks whether Parliament has five hours.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 13. 🗳️ The Democratic Principle Is Actually Boringly Reasonable

This node can be satirical because the institutional position is
ridiculous.

The principle underneath it is not radical.

If Britain decides to remain outside the European Union, that should be
a British democratic decision.

If Britain decides to move closer to the European Union, that should be
a British democratic decision.

If Britain eventually decides to rejoin, that should be a British
democratic decision.

That does not mean voters must be protected from persuasion.

**Politics contains persuasion.**

It means the infrastructure through which persuasion is purchased,
targeted, hidden, measured and recursively optimised must itself be
subject to democratic law.

A sovereign democratic choice requires sovereign democratic
infrastructure.

Otherwise the state can spend years arguing about whether Britain has
*taken back control* while remaining unable to inspect a substantial
part of the machinery attempting to control Britons.

Which is, admittedly, very funny.

But perhaps not ideal.

## 14. 🎩 Administrative Note For Mr Blair

Tony has discovered Europe.

Excellent.

Tony has also spent quite a lot of time explaining that technology moves
very quickly.

Also excellent.

Unfortunately, everyone else's technology moves quickly too.

Cambridge Analytica was investigated in Britain.

Chris Wylie testified in Britain.

Chris Wylie testified in America.

The ICO investigated platforms, data brokers, analytics firms, academic
institutions and every major UK political party.

The ICO currently describes political profiling as potentially
invisible, socially consequential and capable of influencing voting
behaviour at scale.

The Electoral Commission has already explained the transparency problem
created when campaigners and platforms can know which voter received
which optimised message while the wider electorate cannot see the same
system.

We have therefore progressed beyond:

> *Gosh. Do you think behavioural political targeting might become a
> thing?*

It became a thing.

It has a regulatory history.

It has an institutional history.

It has a British history.

AI now gives parts of the machine more horsepower.

So, before everybody congratulates themselves for having a sophisticated
conversation about AI and Europe:

**please regulate the fucking feedback environment.**

Not the opinion.

Not the voter.

Not the existence of political disagreement.

**The machinery which observes the voter, models the voter, experiments
upon the information environment around the voter, learns from the
response, and feeds the result back into the next intervention.**

That is the cybernetic object.

That is the regulatory object.

And now that you have mentioned Europe:

**please locate regulator.**
