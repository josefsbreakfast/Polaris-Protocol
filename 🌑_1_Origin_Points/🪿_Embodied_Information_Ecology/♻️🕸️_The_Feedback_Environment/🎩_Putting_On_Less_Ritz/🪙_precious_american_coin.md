# 🪙 Precious American Coin

**First created:** 2026-09-24 \| **Last updated:** 2026-09-24\
*On American Approved™️, accidental proxies, self-fulfilling signals,
useful liabilities, legitimacy, and the ancient British desire to know
whether the fucking decimal point is going in the right direction.*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🧭 Orientation

There are many conversations one can have about the United States.

There are conversations about American culture, American power, American
security, American intervention, American technology, American capital,
American trauma, American paranoia, American optimism, American
institutional confidence, American institutional overconfidence, and the
occasional American tendency to walk into a room carrying seventeen
extremely powerful systems and then look faintly surprised when
everybody notices the systems.

This is not an attempt to settle all of those conversations.

This is about a smaller and stranger informational object:

> 🪙 **American Approved™️**

The coin is not real.

There is no Department of Precious American Coin. There is no official
mint. There is no little velvet pouch distributed at Dulles Airport to
approved Atlanticists. There is, regrettably, no published specification
explaining what the coin buys.

The coin is a metaphor for the **informational value that other people
may assign to visible proximity to American power**.

A politician, former politician, adviser, think tank, company, investor,
security figure or international intermediary appears repeatedly inside
American political, commercial or security networks. They have access.
They are invited places. Senior Americans speak to them. American
institutions work with them. They remain legible as respectable or
useful inside American elite environments.

A third party therefore has to ask:

> **Does this person have the coin?**

And then the much harder question:

> **What, exactly, does possession of the coin tell me?**

Because these things are not interchangeable:

``` text
FORMALLY SPEAKING FOR AMERICA
≠
INFORMALLY AWARE OF AMERICAN THINKING
≠
NETWORKED WITH AMERICAN POWER
≠
IDEOLOGICALLY ALIGNED WITH SOME AMERICAN ACTORS
≠
COMMERCIALLY ALIGNED WITH SOME AMERICAN ACTORS
≠
TOLERATED BY AMERICAN POWER
≠
PERCEIVED BY SOMEBODY ELSE AS AMERICAN-ALIGNED
```

The entire problem lives in the gaps.

This is a node about what happens when those gaps themselves begin
producing information.

## 🪙 1. My Precious

The coin is doing at least three jobs.

### 💍 The possessed object

First: *my precious*.

The phrase is unavoidably Ring-coded.

That does not mean America is Mordor. Calm down.

The useful part of the metaphor is that a valuable object can begin
reorganising behaviour around its continued possession. The Ring in *The
Lord of the Rings* is not interesting merely because it is powerful. It
interacts with desire, fear, possession, rationalisation, status and the
terror of losing what one has acquired.

American approval can carry real material value.

It can mean:

-   security relationships;
-   diplomatic access;
-   investment;
-   technology;
-   contracts;
-   market access;
-   introductions;
-   institutional prestige;
-   access to elite networks;
-   perceived international credibility;
-   and, sometimes, the expectation that somebody very large will
    continue answering the telephone.

There is nothing mysterious about actors wanting those things.

The interesting question begins when retaining access becomes important
enough that people start **anticipating what the owner of the valuable
relationship might want before the owner has actually asked**.

The object is useful.

Then the object becomes precious.

Then everybody starts behaving around the object.

### 🗡️ The Braavosi coin

The second cultural object is the iron coin Jaqen H'ghar gives Arya
Stark in *A Song of Ice and Fire* / *Game of Thrones*.

The useful feature here is different.

The coin acts as **portable evidence of an invisible relationship**.

Arya does not need to carry an organisational chart explaining the
Faceless Men. The token itself communicates enough to alter how another
person interprets her.

That is very close to what 🪙 American Approved™️ does in this node.

The observer does not necessarily know the precise relationship.

They know enough to wonder whether the relationship matters.

### 💰 And sometimes coin is just coin

There is also the considerably less mystical fact that people like
money.

Tony Blair, for example, has built a large post-premiership ecosystem of
speaking, advising, institutional and policy work. It is perfectly
possible for money, ideology, status, personal conviction, access and
institutional mission to coexist without one reducing cleanly to
another.

This matters because:

> **transactional does not mean controlled.**

Someone may repeatedly cooperate because cooperation gets them money,
access, status, influence, protection, introductions or opportunity.

That does not necessarily make them a disciplined agent.

Indeed, somebody whose governing motivation is **I would personally like
more of the thing I like** may be unusually easy to understand while
remaining extremely difficult to own.

## 🔮 2. Who Put Another Fucking Coin In Tony?

Tony Blair is useful here because he is almost comically overdetermined
as an informational object.

A proposition goes into the Tony Blair Zoltar Machine.

Tony comes out.

Tony says something polished.

And different receiving environments hear completely different things.

``` text
🇺🇸:
Experienced former allied prime minister has an opinion.

🇬🇧:
Oh for FUCK'S sake.
Who put another coin in it?

🌍:
Interesting.
Does this contain information about Washington?
```

The joke works because Blair carries historical metadata.

The Iraq War matters enormously to that metadata. The Iraq Inquiry later
found that intelligence and assessments were used in support of
government statements in ways that conveyed certainty without adequately
acknowledging the limitations of the intelligence. Earlier parliamentary
scrutiny likewise identified missing context and insufficiently
communicated uncertainty around Iraqi WMD claims.

That history does **not** establish the proposition that every statement
Blair made was knowingly false. Nor does it make every later Blair
position wrong.

It does mean the messenger arrives carrying a large amount of prior
information.

And this creates a particularly British interpretive trap for outsiders.

> **"I think Tony Blair is a fucking liar" is not a sufficiently precise
> foreign-policy questionnaire.**

A British person can have an intensely negative Blair response while
supporting NATO.

They can support nuclear deterrence.

They can believe military intervention is justified in some
circumstances.

They can oppose a particular occupation while supporting the initial use
of force.

They can believe Iraq was catastrophically mishandled while holding
quite interventionist views elsewhere.

They can believe Blair's presentation of the case for war destroyed
trust without adopting pacifism as a general doctrine.

So the Blair response is often:

**high-information about Blair and institutional trust**\
but\
**low-information about the respondent's complete military or
foreign-policy model**.

That distinction matters if Blair is then used internationally as a
proxy for what British or Atlanticist opinion supposedly thinks.

The Zoltar Machine does not merely output propositions.

It outputs propositions with metadata attached.

## 📡 3. Does Zoltar Contain Washington?

Suppose Blair says X.

What has actually happened?

Possibilities include:

``` text
A. Washington asked Blair to say X.

B. Blair knows that relevant people in Washington want X.

C. Blair believes relevant people in Washington probably want X.

D. Blair and relevant people in Washington independently want X
   because they inhabit overlapping strategic environments.

E. Blair wants X for reasons that have little to do with Washington.

F. Blair's institutional, commercial or network incentives favour X.

G. Blair is testing an idea.

H. Blair is just fucking talking.

I. Several of the above are simultaneously true.
```

The third country cannot necessarily distinguish them.

That is the important part.

The analytical question is therefore not:

> **Is Tony Blair secretly an American asset?**

There is no need to assert that.

The useful question is:

> **How much information about American preferences should another actor
> infer from an American-adjacent figure saying X?**

The answer may be **none**.

But the receiving actor does not get to know that merely because
Washington knows it.

This is where a public relationship can generate diplomatic effects even
if the relationship is being misread.

If America repeatedly tolerates, platforms, consults, contracts with,
listens to, hosts or visibly associates with a person, another state may
infer that the person's views are at least somewhere inside an American
**acceptable-policy envelope**.

That inference can be wrong.

Wrong information still changes systems.

## 🕸️ 4. You Do Not Need To Send A Signal To Generate One

Power communicates accidentally.

This is not peculiar to America. It is a general problem of observable
power networks.

People infer meaning from:

-   access;
-   invitations;
-   appointments;
-   contracts;
-   investment;
-   proximity;
-   repeated meetings;
-   institutional tolerance;
-   prestigious platforms;
-   who gets listened to;
-   who gets excluded;
-   who remains respectable;
-   who appears beside whom;
-   who publicly disagrees;
-   and who conspicuously does not.

The powerful actor may regard these as separate relationships.

The receiving system may experience them as one informational
environment.

That creates the **perceived-proxy problem**:

> **A person does not have to be a proxy to acquire some of the
> signalling characteristics of one.**

This matters particularly for a superpower.

If another government believes proximity to Washington contains
information about Washington, then American proximity itself becomes
communicative.

That does **not** imply America should control everybody around it more
tightly.

That would reproduce the very problem under discussion.

Sometimes the better intervention is simply **legible independence**.

For example:

> This person is speaking in a private capacity.\
> This proposal is not US policy.\
> We agree with them on X and disagree on Y.\
> We have taken no position on Z.

That is not weakness.

It improves the information environment.

Because otherwise an unofficial figure can accidentally spend American
credibility on a proposition America never authorised.

Country B hears X.

Country B believes X came with 🪙.

Country B incurs costs accommodating X.

Washington later says:

> What? We don't support X.

Country B has not merely learned that Zoltar was wrong.

It may learn:

> **American signalling is unreliable.**

Even though America never intended to signal anything.

That is an extraordinary diplomatic liability.

## 🔁 5. Everyone Seemed Very Keen

Now put the coin into a feedback loop.

``` text
ZOLTAR SAYS X
      ↓
COUNTRY B
infers that Washington may want X
      ↓
B SHADES ITS BEHAVIOUR TOWARDS X
      ↓
WASHINGTON
observes B apparently supporting X
      ↓
WASHINGTON UPDATES
"Interesting. B seems keen on X."
      ↓
WASHINGTON BECOMES MORE RECEPTIVE TO X
      ↓
B OBSERVES INCREASED AMERICAN RECEPTIVITY
      ↓
"Ah. We interpreted the signal correctly."
```

Except there may originally have been **no American signal**.

This is the self-fulfilling-prophecy problem.

The feedback is not necessarily fraudulent.

Nobody needs to be lying.

Nobody needs to be coordinating.

Nobody even needs to understand the complete loop.

Everyone can sincerely report:

> **Well, everyone else seemed very keen.**

That is the problem.

Apparent consensus can be partly **endogenous to the signalling system
producing it**.

The behaviour produced by the inference becomes evidence for the
inference.

The evidence changes the environment.

The changed environment makes the original interpretation increasingly
accurate.

A phantom preference can acquire material reality.

## 🙋 6. Mate, I Watched You All Look Around The Table

There is a very good slapstick representation of this in *The Death of
Stalin*.

The committee members watch one another.

Hands move.

Other hands respond.

Everyone is attempting to infer the consequences of everybody else's
behaviour while simultaneously producing the behaviour everybody else is
interpreting.

The analogy has limits.

Stalinist political coercion was vastly more violent, explicit and
immediate than normal allied diplomacy.

The transferable mechanism is narrower:

> **The vote itself becomes information about how one should vote.**

Therefore a unanimous outcome does not necessarily prove that every
participant independently arrived at the same preference.

Sometimes:

> Mate, I watched you all look around the table before putting your
> hands up.

This is why preference discovery matters.

If everybody is responding to everybody else's inferred preference, the
final consensus may tell us considerably less about independent
preference than the final vote count suggests.

## 🌍 7. The Coin Does Not Have A Universal Exchange Rate

Information does not possess a fixed meaning independent of the system
processing it.

The same American-adjacent figure can be interpreted differently because
different receiving environments contain different:

-   histories;
-   memories;
-   alliances;
-   colonial relationships;
-   interventions;
-   economic dependencies;
-   military dependencies;
-   intelligence relationships;
-   media environments;
-   domestic political cultures;
-   and prior experiences of American power.

🪙 does not have a universal exchange rate.

A Washington audience may see an experienced international statesman.

A British audience may see Tony fucking Blair again.

An Iranian receiving environment contains, among many other things, the
documented history of the 1953 overthrow of Mohammad Mosaddegh.
Declassified US records describe an operation approved by the President,
Secretary of State and Director of Central Intelligence targeting
Mosaddegh's government and seeking its replacement with a pro-Western
government under the Shah.

That does **not** prove that a later ambiguous political event is
secretly American-directed.

It does mean:

> **"Could Washington be involved?" enters an information environment in
> which Washington has, historically, sometimes been covertly
> involved.**

That changes the prior.

Past covert action therefore has a long afterlife.

Even when the present action is benign, the historical receiving system
remains.

This is one reason covert intervention can damage future diplomacy long
after the original operation has ended.

The state may sincerely say:

> We are not doing that now.

The receiving system remembers:

> You did not announce it while you were doing it then either.

The problem cannot be repaired simply by demanding greater trust.

## 🇬🇧 8. Cousin, They Think We Contain You

Britain has its own version of the perceived-proxy problem.

In some international environments, a British official, adviser or
intermediary may reasonably be interpreted as carrying information about
American preferences.

Not because Britain simply obeys America.

It does not.

Suez alone should kill the cartoon version of that claim: Britain and
France acted against Egypt in 1956 while the Eisenhower administration
opposed the intervention and exerted pressure against it.

But the longer historical sequence matters too.

Britain remains deeply connected to the United States through defence,
intelligence, diplomacy, finance, technology and the political mythology
of the "special relationship".

So when Britain arrives somewhere, another actor may ask:

> **Have they already talked to Washington?**

They may behave as though Britain contains America even where Britain
has not been sent by America at all.

And that can contaminate the information Britain receives.

If the other actor thinks Britain is carrying Washington in its handbag,
they may stop telling Britain what they actually think and start telling
Britain what they believe Washington wants to hear.

Britain then returns home with bad information.

A more useful diplomatic register is sometimes:

``` text
Washington thinks X.

We also think X, but for different reasons.

We disagree with Washington about Y.

We have not asked Washington about Z.

Now please tell us what YOU think,
rather than what you think we want you to say.
```

That is not anti-Americanism.

It is **information hygiene**.

Independence has to be legible inside an alliance if the alliance wants
accurate information from outside itself.

## 🗝️ 9. Controllable Is Not The Same Thing As Safe

The coin also intersects with a much older security problem: leverage.

A person with a vulnerability may appear useful because the
vulnerability creates control.

Possible vulnerabilities include:

-   money;
-   criminal exposure;
-   status;
-   access;
-   ego;
-   ideology;
-   fear;
-   reputational risk;
-   dependence on protection;
-   or some private desire that another actor can facilitate.

From an intelligence perspective, that can look attractive.

If losing your relationship with an institution would cost you money,
status, liberty, protection or access, the institution may reasonably
expect the relationship to influence your behaviour.

But:

> **controllable and safe are not synonyms.**

Someone susceptible to **your** leverage may be susceptible to somebody
else's leverage.

Someone motivated by money may accept somebody else's money.

Someone motivated by access wants whoever can provide access.

Someone who conceals things for you may conceal things from you.

Someone whose conduct would be disastrous if exposed may eventually
become disastrous precisely because exposure becomes harder to tolerate.

So:

``` text
LIABILITY
   ↓
LEVERAGE
   ↓
USEFULNESS
   ↓
TOLERANCE
   ↓
INSTITUTIONAL INVESTMENT
```

At first the institution has leverage over the person.

That arrow does not necessarily stay one-way.

## 🪤 10. Oh Fuck. Now We Have To Protect Him.

Suppose Person A has committed serious wrongdoing.

Institution B finds A useful.

B tolerates A because the relationship produces intelligence, access,
influence, information or some other benefit.

Later, exposure of A would also expose B's previous tolerance.

B now has a new incentive.

It is no longer protecting A only because A is useful.

It may be protecting A because **A has become evidence about B**.

``` text
USEFUL COMPROMISED INTERMEDIARY
        ↓
INSTITUTION TOLERATES LIABILITY
        ↓
EXPOSURE WOULD REVEAL THE TOLERANCE
        ↓
INSTITUTION ACQUIRES REASON TO PREVENT EXPOSURE
        ↓
PROTECTION INCREASES ENTANGLEMENT
        ↓
EVENTUAL EXPOSURE BECOMES MORE COSTLY
        ↓
GREATER INCENTIVE TO PROTECT
```

At the beginning:

> **We have leverage over him.**

Later:

> **Oh fuck. Now we have to protect him.**

This is why the internet's favourite question about Jeffrey Epstein ---
*whose intelligence asset was he?* --- can obscure a more generally
useful systems question.

Public evidence has generated extensive speculation about intelligence
relationships, but it has not established the broad claim that Epstein
was an intelligence asset for any particular service.

We do not need to solve that unknown to see the structural risk.

A person who is primarily out for himself, who possesses access to
powerful people, and whose desires or conduct create exploitable
vulnerabilities may be attractive to many other actors.

But if an institution knowingly becomes dependent upon somebody whose
conduct would be catastrophic to defend publicly, the institution has
created a future problem for itself.

The asset can become the liability.

The leverage can become reciprocal.

The protection can become the evidence.

## ⚖️ 11. This Is Not A PR Problem

Calling that a **PR problem** is too small.

For a government, intelligence service, military, police force,
regulator or other coercive institution, it can become a **legitimacy
problem**.

The damaging revelation is not merely:

> You knew a horrible person.

It may become:

> **You knew this person presented a serious liability, but tolerated or
> protected them because they were useful to you.**

That changes the public question.

A security institution often asks people to accept exceptional powers
because those powers are supposedly exercised within legitimate
constraints.

Sometimes the institution must also ask the public or foreign partners
to accept:

> We cannot show you all the evidence, but there is a real security
> reason for this decision.

That request depends partly on accumulated credibility.

If "national security" has previously appeared to conceal embarrassment,
misconduct, privileged treatment or institutional self-protection, later
legitimate secrecy becomes harder to believe.

So:

``` text
SECURITY REQUIRES SOME SECRECY
        ↓
SECRECY CAN CONCEAL LIABILITY
        ↓
LIABILITY MAKES DISCLOSURE DANGEROUS
        ↓
DANGER CREATES INCENTIVE FOR MORE SECRECY
        ↓
EXPOSURE DAMAGES LEGITIMACY
        ↓
FUTURE LEGITIMATE SECRECY BECOMES LESS CREDIBLE
```

That is not communications damage.

That is **security capacity eating its own legitimacy substrate**.

> **Legitimacy is not the decorative layer applied to security power
> after the machinery has finished working. Legitimacy is one of the
> things that makes security power work.**

A state with enormous international power has particularly high stakes
here.

People who cannot vote in American elections still have to decide
whether to believe American security assessments, share intelligence
with American institutions, host American forces, buy American
infrastructure, accept American technology, enter American partnerships
or trust American representations.

Accumulated credibility is therefore part of the security architecture.

Burn enough of it protecting useful liabilities and eventually you make
yourself less secure.

## 🪞 12. Your Enemy Is Also Part Of Your Training Data

There is another mechanism hiding underneath the coin.

Institutions adapt to adversaries.

That is obvious.

What is less often discussed is that **an adversary can therefore become
part of the environment selecting the institution's own form**.

American law enforcement provides a useful organised-crime example.

The Mafia relied on secrecy, trusted relationships, controlled access,
compartmentalisation and *omertà*. After the 1957 Apalachin meeting
helped expose the national scale of organised crime, federal attention
intensified. By the 1970s, the FBI was using tools including
court-authorised wiretaps, high-level informants and long-term
undercover operations to penetrate organised-crime networks.

Look at the functional resemblance:

  Organised crime               Institution attempting to penetrate it
  ----------------------------- ----------------------------------------
  secrecy                       secrecy
  compartmentalisation          compartmentalisation
  trusted intermediaries        confidential sources
  vetting                       vetting
  coded identity                cover identity
  network intelligence          network intelligence
  loyalty                       institutional loyalty
  information about opponents   information about targets

This is **not moral equivalence**.

It is adaptive resemblance.

If an adversary communicates secretly, defeating it requires some
capacity to discover secret communications.

If it vets entrants, infiltration requires credible identities.

If it uses distributed networks, investigators need network
intelligence.

If *omertà* prevents ordinary witnesses speaking, informants become
unusually valuable.

Some mirroring is simply functional.

The problem is what happens next.

``` text
ADVERSARY HAS FEATURE X
        ↓
X MAKES ADVERSARY EFFECTIVE
        ↓
INSTITUTION DEVELOPS COUNTER-X
        ↓
COUNTER-X REQUIRES SOME FUNCTIONAL ANALOGUE OF X
        ↓
ANALOGUE BECOMES INSTITUTIONALISED
        ↓
ORIGINAL THREAT CHANGES
        ↓
INSTITUTION RETAINS X BECAUSE X IS NOW USEFUL
```

And then comes the question:

> **When you defeat an adversary, how much of them have you accidentally
> kept?**

This is much wider than organised crime.

Terrorists partly shape counterterrorism.

Spies partly shape counterintelligence.

Hackers partly shape cybersecurity.

Opposing militaries partly shape militaries.

Repeated interaction produces co-evolution.

The important governance problem is therefore not:

> **Did our institution ever resemble the thing it fought?**

Of course some functional resemblance will occur.

The question is:

> **Which adaptations remain necessary, which require constraints, and
> which have outlived the environment that selected them?**

## 🧯 13. The Threat Changed. The Machinery Stayed.

This brings us to over-securitisation.

A threat appears.

A state builds capacity.

The capacity detects threats.

Greater detection increases the amount of potentially significant
information.

More information creates demand for more analysis, more infrastructure,
more vendors, more monitoring and more institutional capacity.

The capacity itself becomes part of the security environment.

``` text
THREAT
  ↓
SECURITY / SURVEILLANCE CAPACITY
  ↓
MORE THREATS AND ANOMALIES BECOME OBSERVABLE
  ↓
MORE POTENTIALLY SIGNIFICANT INFORMATION
  ↓
MORE ANALYSIS / INFRASTRUCTURE / PERSONNEL
  ↓
MORE INSTITUTIONAL DEPENDENCE ON THE CAPABILITY
  ↓
REDUCING THE CAPABILITY BECOMES LEGIBLE AS A SECURITY RISK
```

Some of this machinery is necessary.

Some of it solved real problems.

Some of it may still be essential.

Some of it created new problems.

Some of it may have become commercially valuable.

Some of it may have become institutionally self-preserving.

Those categories need separating.

The opposite of over-securitisation is not **under-securitisation**.

The question is whether a particular adaptation still improves security
under present conditions.

## 🇺🇸 14. America Was Frightened

This conversation has to pass through 9/11 carefully.

Nearly 3,000 people were killed in the attacks. The fear, grief and
security response did not emerge from nowhere.

And America was not alone.

NATO's Article 5 was invoked after the attacks --- the first and, as of
September 2026, only invocation in the Alliance's history.

At the 25th anniversary commemoration in September 2026, NATO Deputy
Secretary General Radmila Shekerinska put the point plainly:

> **"America was not alone."**

That matters.

America's allies understood that something enormous had happened.

They showed up.

So the useful conversation twenty-five years later is not:

> You should never have been frightened.

It is:

> **We know why you were frightened. We came when you called.
> Twenty-five years later, we are allowed to ask which pieces of the
> machinery built in response are still helping you.**

That is not surrender.

It is maintenance.

## 👁️ 15. America Has Overplucked Its Eyebrows

Unfortunately the technical term for the next stage is:

> **Why aren't my eyebrows growing back? 🥺**

America has spent decades building systems designed to increase
observability, predictability and control.

Reducing some of those systems can therefore feel immediately like
becoming less secure.

You can see less.

You can compel less.

A partner can say no.

A foreign government can choose a different supplier.

A local institution retains more control.

A system becomes removable.

Information becomes less centralised.

There is an uncomfortable patchy-eyebrow period in which critics can
correctly point out:

> **YOU HAVE LESS CONTROL THAN YOU DID BEFORE.**

Yes.

That may be the point.

The relevant question is not whether the central actor can observe every
variable.

It is whether the overall ecology has become more resilient.

The alternative to omniscience is not blindness.

The alternative to total control is not chaos.

And sometimes the attempt to eliminate every vulnerability creates
vulnerabilities of its own.

The eyebrows may grow back.

Everybody has to tolerate looking a bit weird first.

## ☘️ 16. Britain Has Seen This Movie From Inside The Cinema

The Polaris nodes on republicanism, militancy, the British state and
Northern Ireland are not finished.

This section is not their substitute.

It is a bridge.

Part of what can look like an unusually instinctive British or Irish
familiarity with agents, handlers, informants, covert relationships,
security legitimacy and blowback is not innate cultural wisdom.

There is a lot of political history sitting underneath it.

During the Troubles, the British state and republican armed
organisations both had real security problems.

Both developed systems for dealing with those problems.

Both experienced gains and losses of legitimacy over time.

Those changes were not perfectly zero-sum. They were not symmetrical.
The actors did not possess equivalent powers, responsibilities or legal
status.

But legitimacy moved.

Actions changed how communities interpreted actors.

Those interpretations changed the operating environment.

Bloody Sunday is a particularly clear feedback example because the
Saville Inquiry's conclusion, later quoted in Parliament by Prime
Minister David Cameron, was that what happened strengthened the
Provisional IRA, increased nationalist resentment and hostility towards
the Army, and exacerbated the later violent conflict.

That is cybernetically horrible.

``` text
REAL SECURITY THREAT
      ↓
STATE RESPONSE
      ↓
STATE CONDUCT DAMAGES LEGITIMACY
      ↓
DAMAGED LEGITIMACY CHANGES THE INSURGENT OPERATING ENVIRONMENT
      ↓
INSURGENCY MAY BECOME STRONGER
      ↓
GREATER PERCEIVED SECURITY THREAT
```

Operation Kenova gives us another part of the picture.

Its final report examined alleged criminal conduct involving the agent
known as STEAK KNIFE and alleged handlers. The UK government's 2025
response described the alleged agent's conduct as deeply disturbing and
pointed to later statutory regulation and independent oversight of agent
handling. The same response also addressed findings concerning collusion
by individual security-force members with the loyalist Glennane Gang.

Again:

> **The existence of a real security threat does not make every response
> to the threat security-producing.**

And this cuts in more than one direction.

Republican armed organisations faced real infiltration.

Internal-security systems intended to combat infiltration could
themselves produce fear, coercion, killings, distrust and legitimacy
loss.

The state faced real paramilitary violence.

Penetrating those organisations could produce valuable intelligence.

Agent handling could also generate profound legal, moral and legitimacy
problems.

So the inherited lesson is not:

> **Security services bad; insurgents legitimate.**

Nor:

> **Insurgents bad; therefore every state response legitimate.**

It is:

> **Legitimacy is dynamic information inside conflict. What you do
> changes not only how people evaluate you, but how they evaluate
> everybody else.**

This is why a British response to American security concerns can
sincerely be:

> **Yes. We know there was a threat. That's why we're talking about what
> happens next.**

## 🏢 17. Then Silicon Valley Ate The Coin

The old problem has acquired new infrastructure.

It is important not to collapse all of the following into one imaginary
machine:

``` text
US GOVERNMENT
CIA
NSA
PENTAGON
DEFENCE CONTRACTORS
CLOUD PROVIDERS
SOCIAL PLATFORMS
VENTURE CAPITAL
AI LABS
```

They are not one organisation.

They do not have identical interests.

They frequently disagree.

Their legal powers differ.

Their commercial incentives differ.

Their institutional cultures differ.

But from outside the United States, the boundaries can become difficult
to perceive.

People move between sectors.

Companies hold government contracts.

Governments rely on private infrastructure.

Private companies depend on public policy.

Cloud providers host state systems.

Technology firms operate across borders.

American law and jurisdiction matter.

Investment networks overlap.

The same company can be simultaneously a consumer brand, infrastructure
provider, government contractor and major political-economic actor.

The receiving system therefore sees not one machine, but an increasingly
dense **American power ecology**.

And 🪙 American Approved™️ begins attaching itself not merely to
diplomats and former prime ministers, but to infrastructure.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## ☁️ 18. The Palantir Problem Is Bigger Than Palantir

Palantir is an easy object onto which public anxiety can attach because
its work visibly intersects data, government, defence, intelligence and
large-scale institutional systems.

But the mechanism is not fundamentally about Palantir.

The same structural questions can attach to Microsoft.

Or AWS.

Or Google.

Or whichever companies become essential to future AI infrastructure.

The questions are boring because the questions are important:

> Where does the data live?

> Under whose jurisdiction?

> Who can access it?

> Who controls the infrastructure?

> What happens during political disagreement?

> What are the contractual limits?

> Is there independent oversight?

> Can we migrate?

> What does exit cost?

> Can the institution continue functioning if the supplier relationship
> ends?

The concern therefore moves beyond:

> **Can America observe us?**

towards:

> **How much of our ability to act independently depends upon
> American-owned systems?**

That is a sovereignty and dependency question before it is a
surveillance question.

And if the answer to distrust is:

> **Become more deeply dependent on us,**

the receiving system may interpret the proposed reassurance as
confirmation of the original concern.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🤖 19. AI Makes The Coin Fucking Enormous

AI concentrates several already-important resources:

``` text
COMPUTE
DATA
CAPITAL
SCIENTIFIC TALENT
BEHAVIOURAL INFORMATION
CULTURAL PRODUCTION
INFRASTRUCTURE
MODEL CAPABILITY
```

A great deal of the value generated by those systems is also
international.

Models are used across borders.

Research moves across borders.

Capital moves across borders.

Culture moves across borders.

Data and behavioural information cross jurisdictions.

States may increasingly depend on privately developed systems for
administrative, scientific, security and economic capacity.

So the global question becomes:

> **What exactly is the bargain?**

Who captures the value?

Who assumes the risk?

Who controls the infrastructure?

Whose law applies?

What happens when governments disagree?

How much local capability remains?

Can participants leave?

Can institutions function after exit?

The interesting answer cannot simply be:

> **Trust us.**

The system is too consequential for that.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🚪 20. What Happens If We Say No?

Formal sovereignty does not mean every state possesses equivalent
alternatives.

America can often choose among many strategic relationships.

Most countries cannot choose another America.

Britain cannot rapidly replace its entire defence, intelligence,
nuclear, technological, financial and diplomatic relationship with the
United States as though changing broadband provider.

Smaller states may have even less room.

This does not mean:

> **America will secretly destroy us if we say no.**

That claim would require evidence.

The subtler problem is:

> **What exactly happens if we say no?**

What happens to intelligence cooperation?

Defence procurement?

Market access?

Investment?

Cloud infrastructure?

Diplomatic support?

Technology?

The answer may genuinely be:

> Nothing punitive. We disagree and continue being allies.

Excellent.

Make that legible.

Because uncertainty itself changes behaviour.

If the smaller actor believes refusal may carry large unknown costs, it
may accommodate the powerful actor before the powerful actor has asked
for accommodation.

And there is our coin again.

``` text
PERCEIVED AMERICAN PREFERENCE
      ↓
ANTICIPATORY ACCOMMODATION
      ↓
APPARENT VOLUNTARY ALIGNMENT
      ↓
AMERICAN OBSERVATION OF ALIGNMENT
      ↓
REINFORCEMENT OF THE RELATIONSHIP
      ↓
🪙 BECOMES MORE PRECIOUS
```

The system can produce obedience without an instruction.

That is precisely why agency needs to be real rather than ceremonial.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🧱 21. Trust Is Not The Product

The answer is not:

> **Please trust America harder.**

It is also not:

> **America must control every American-adjacent person so nobody can
> misunderstand anything ever again.**

That is both impossible and undesirable.

The better architecture reduces the amount of discretionary trust
required.

Things such as:

-   clear authority;
-   clear limits;
-   visible disagreement;
-   independent oversight;
-   interoperability;
-   data boundaries;
-   jurisdictional clarity;
-   migration paths;
-   exit rights;
-   distributed capacity;
-   auditable contracts;
-   predictable consequences;
-   and explicit distinctions between public policy and private
    advocacy.

The receiving system may not be asking for reassurance.

It may be asking for:

> **legible constraints on power.**

That is different.

A trustworthy system is nice.

A system that remains bounded when trust fails is considerably more
useful.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🌱 22. Bounded Vulnerability

No security architecture eliminates vulnerability.

A state can attempt to see everything.

It still cannot know everything.

It can attempt to control every intermediary.

The intermediaries acquire their own incentives.

It can attempt to protect every useful asset.

The asset can become a liability.

It can attempt to centralise every dependency.

Centralisation becomes a dependency.

It can attempt to eliminate disagreement.

Then it loses access to information generated by genuine disagreement.

So the opposite of over-securitisation is not under-securitisation.

The alternative to omniscience is not blindness.

The alternative to American dominance does not have to be another
superpower reproducing the same structure.

A more useful target is:

> **bounded vulnerability inside systems where other people genuinely
> have agency.**

That means accepting that allies can disagree.

Suppliers can be replaced.

Local institutions retain capacity.

Foreign governments can say no.

Information sometimes remains outside the centre.

Not every variable can be observed.

Not every future can be guaranteed.

That can feel frightening.

It can also make the system harder to catastrophically break.

And there is a useful historical reminder here.

When America suffered the attacks of 11 September 2001, distributed
responsibility did not mean abandonment.

Its allies came.

America was attacked.

Its friends showed up.
