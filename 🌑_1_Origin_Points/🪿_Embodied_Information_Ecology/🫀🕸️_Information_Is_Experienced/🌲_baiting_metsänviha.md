# 🌲 Baiting Metsänviha
**First created:** 2026-10-23 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-23  
*The forest has entered the planning consultation. The wolves have followed. The thunder god would like to see the engineering drawings.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation — Google, What the Fuck?

In October 2026, photographs emerged of the ground prepared for Google's proposed data-centre developments in northern Finland. At **Muhos**, an estimated **250,000 trees** had been felled across approximately **330 hectares**. The pictures show something more than missing tree trunks: exposed ground, machinery tracks, disturbed drainage, and the removal of topsoil. At **Otanmäki**, near Kajaani, further extensive logging was reported. These are **different sites**, and the evidence concerning each must remain separate. [1–3]

Finland's Licensing and Supervision Authority (LVV) instructed Google's representative, **Tuike Finland Oy**, to suspend environmentally significant preparatory work pending completion of environmental impact assessment procedures. Finnish police were also reported to be examining suspected illegal logging. A regulatory intervention and a police investigation are not a final criminal conviction; equally, Google's assertion that it believed it was acting in good faith is not a substitute for completed environmental assessment. Google says it has halted relevant preparatory work, will cooperate with the authority, and has proposed tree planting at Muhos. [2–4]

This is a story about a technology company planning to construct extremely sophisticated computing infrastructure while the physical preparation for that infrastructure has already removed an enormous area of living forest.

**Why the fuck did it need this much destruction, exactly?**

Not why computing exists. Not whether Finland can attract investment. Not whether AI has applications. **Why this forest, why this footprint, why this sequence of works, and why these ecological costs?**

And, because this is Finland, a further question presents itself.

**Has anyone notified the forest?**

## 🏗️ Show Us the Engineering — Not the Marketing

A data centre is not a mysterious box that must occupy whatever acreage its developer happens to purchase. It comprises server halls, electrical equipment, cooling systems, fibre connections, backup and resilience systems, service roads, security arrangements and space for maintenance. Some of these functions require separation or particular geometries. Others can be configured in more than one way.

The fact that a site is designated for a data centre **does not demonstrate that all of its cleared land is required for the first operational phase**.

### What, specifically, occupies the land?

An adequate public explanation should distinguish the gross landholding from the initial building footprint, substations, cooling plant, access roads, construction compounds, stormwater facilities, setbacks, habitat buffers and land reserved for expansion. It should identify which clearances are permanent and which were made for temporary works. It should show what was retained and what was removed, including soil and drainage features.

If an expansion area will not be needed for years, **why clear it now?** If the design is intended to be built in phases, why not phase the ecological disturbance as well?

The question becomes particularly pointed when environmental assessment is still incomplete. Assessment is supposed to inform decisions, not merely describe what remains after the landscape has been transformed.

### Why so horizontal?

Multistorey data centres are technically possible. Higher-density racks, liquid-cooling systems, different equipment layouts, more compact electrical plant and alternative building forms can change land requirements. None is universally suitable. A multistorey design may increase structural loads, construction cost, vertical power and coolant distribution complexity, fire-safety requirements and maintenance challenges. High-density systems may demand greater cooling and power concentration. But **a trade-off is not the same thing as an impossibility**.

The appropriate engineering question is not whether a hypothetical tower could contain every server. It is whether a smaller-footprint design was compared with the chosen one, against a clearly stated set of requirements.

| Option to test | What it might save | What must be demonstrated |
|---|---|---|
| Taller server halls | Horizontal building footprint | Structural, cooling, power and operational feasibility |
| Higher-density deployment | Space per unit of compute | Heat removal, electrical supply, serviceability and upgrade path |
| Liquid cooling where appropriate | Some air-handling and equipment-space demands | Whole-system energy, water, maintenance and safety implications |
| Brownfield or former industrial land | Existing woodland and soil | Availability of grid, fibre, access, sufficient space and permissions |
| Phased development | Immediate forest clearance | Actual demand, phase boundaries, habitat protection between phases |
| Reconfigured campus | Roads, duplicated plant and fragmentation | Fire access, electrical resilience, construction practicality |
| Multiple sites or reuse of existing capacity | Single-site land pressure | Network latency, resilience, energy and aggregate impacts |

Google's existing **Hamina** data centre, developed from a former paper mill, is an especially useful comparison: the company already knows how to reuse industrial land. That does **not** prove an equivalent brownfield site was available for Muhos or Otanmäki. It does mean brownfield reuse is not an alien engineering concept. [5]

### Efficiency for whom?

A design can be efficient by one metric and appalling by another.

- **Cost per deployed megawatt** may favour a sprawling campus.
- **Speed to construction** may favour extensive early clearance.
- **Future optionality** may favour buying and preparing more land than is immediately required.
- **Compute per hectare of damaged habitat** may favour a completely different design.
- **Lifetime ecological harm per useful computational outcome** may favour another again.

These objectives are not interchangeable. The design brief determines which losses are treated as constraints and which are treated as somebody else's problem.

**If the instruction had been “provide the necessary computing capacity while preserving the maximum viable amount of existing forest, soil and habitat connectivity”, would the engineers have produced the same plan?**

We cannot answer that without the project alternatives analysis. Which is precisely why it should be disclosed to the extent possible.

### How long does the bargain last?

Servers and accelerators can be replaced several times during the lifetime of a data-centre building. Cooling and electrical plant may have longer service lives. Buildings may last decades. An established forest's soils, habitat relationships and ecological continuity may take decades or much longer to recover, and some losses may not be recoverable in their previous form.

A computer becoming obsolete is not the same event as a building becoming useless. Good infrastructure can be upgraded. But that is another reason to ask about **adaptability**: will future generations of computing equipment fit within the infrastructure already constructed, or will every technological leap arrive with a request for another piece of the landscape?

**Why should a forest absorb a long-lived loss to support a short-cycle technological product unless the full bargain has been demonstrated?**

### What is this capacity actually for?

Cloud hosting, enterprise workloads, search, storage, AI training and inference are different uses. The project belongs to a broader announced investment in Finnish AI and digital infrastructure, but a headline investment figure is not a public demonstration of the demand, utilisation or social benefit of each proposed site. [1–3]

If demand is committed, say what can be said. If expansion is speculative, say that. If the argument is that the site supports strategic resilience, identify the accountable institution and the requirement without inventing a defence connection.

**“More compute” is an output. It is not, on its own, a justification for destroying a particular forest.**

## 🐺 The Wolves Have Entered the Village

At **Otanmäki**, resident **Anni Ranta** told *The Guardian* that wolves had come into the village after forest clearance, which she associated with wildlife fleeing the disturbed area. She also described construction noise reverberating through hard rock. Her father, a former wilderness guide, reported brown water in a stream after the forest was removed. [1]

These are important **reported observations and interpretations**, not a completed causal study. Wolf movements can have multiple explanations; a properly designed investigation would examine sightings before and after works, prey distribution, habitat use, construction activity, seasonal patterns and alternative causes. The Muhos runoff concerns must not be silently conflated with Otanmäki's testimony.

But consider the experience.

A developer defines a parcel. Machinery arrives. Trees disappear. The soundscape changes. Residents describe animals entering a village. The development boundary, so crisp in the planning documentation, turns out to be considerably less authoritative to wolves, water, noise and the people who live nearby.

**The wolves did not read the development plan.**

This is not merely an ecological issue. It is a lesson in what information *is*. The corporate record may describe a successful clearance operation. A resident experiences an altered landscape and unexpected encounters. A wolf encounters changes in cover, movement, disturbance or prey. Each body meets a different consequence of the same intervention.

And in a culture with a long history of understanding the forest as inhabited by powerful beings, wolves appearing in the settlement after the forest is disturbed is **omen-level shit**.

That is a literary and cultural reading, not evidence of supernatural intervention. It is also a striking reason to investigate the material ecology properly.

> **Google:** The site is now clear.  
> **Metsänväki:** The wolves have entered the village.  
> **Google:** We did not authorise that.  
> **Metsänväki:** THE WOLVES DID NOT REQUIRE YOUR AUTHORISATION.

## 🌲 Metsänväki — The Forest Was Not Empty

In Finnish folklore, **metsänväki** can denote the forest's supernatural inhabitants and the power associated with the forest. *Väki* cannot always be flattened into an English list of discrete “spirits”: the term also carries the sense of force or potency. The forest may be encountered as a domain with inhabitants, agencies, rules, obligations and dangers. [6–7]

That matters because the usual development-language move is to make the land legible by making its existing relationships secondary. A site is described in hectares, titles, permits, grid connections and construction schedules. Those are useful descriptions. They are not exhaustive descriptions.

Finnish forest traditions are **not one uniform, universally codified pantheon**. Regional oral traditions, charms, poems, local spirit beliefs, later literary synthesis and the *Kalevala* do not always tell precisely the same story. The following is therefore a **map of overlapping traditions**, followed by a deliberately fictional organisational chart. Please do not cite the meeting minutes as a primary source on Finnish religion.

### 🌳 Tapio — The Forest's Ruler

Tapio appears in Finnish tradition as a principal ruler or master of the forest. The animals are sometimes imagined as his herds; hunters seek his favour, and he is associated with woodland life and the renewal of the forest. [6]

A developer might say it has purchased the land. Tapio's folkloric jurisdiction asks an older question: **who, exactly, has been living there?**

### 🌿 Mielikki — Forest Mistress, Care and Healing

Mielikki, often described as Tapio's wife, is associated with the forest, hunting fortunes, its beauty and healing knowledge. The forest is not simply a dangerous place that punishes trespass. It can nourish, protect and restore. [6]

The distinction matters. When a forest is removed, the loss is not limited to standing timber. It may include opportunities for recovery, familiarity, sensory regulation, foraging, recreation and relations with other living things.

### 🐿️ Tuulikki, Tyytikki and the Squirrel Question

Names and family relationships vary across accounts of Tapio's household. Tuulikki and Tyytikki appear in overlapping traditions; **we should not mechanically treat every variant name as a separate, universally attested daughter**. Some accounts associate Tyytikki with squirrels. The particular wording and regional attestation deserve checking against original Finnish material before being presented as settled genealogy.

Nevertheless, the narrative significance is irresistible: **the squirrels have representation at the highest levels of forest government**.

This is also a reminder that small and easily overlooked animals are not optional scenery. Earlier reporting about protected flying-squirrel habitat affected by Google's Finnish operations should be checked and dated separately before being used as evidence of any repeated pattern. One verified incident is not permission to invent another.

### 🌾 Tellervo — Forest Maiden

Tellervo is associated in literary and folk traditions with Tapio's household and woodland animals. Her role offers a route into animal care and the forest as a place already occupied by creatures with their own requirements.

### 🏹 Nyyrikki — Hunting, Movement and the Forest

Nyyrikki is another forest-associated figure in traditions involving Tapio's household, hunting and the fortunes of those moving through woodland. Specific roles vary by source. His usefulness here is not that he owns the modern planning department; it is that human entry into forest space has historically been understood as a **relationship**, not a unilateral entitlement.

### 🌳 Metsänhaltijat — Local Guardians

The named household is not the whole forest. *Haltijat* and other forest beings may be attached to places, trees or distinctive features. Accounts of forest power and inhabitants refer to locations such as hollows, roots, stumps, rocks, anthills and unusual trees. [7]

Think about the difference between removing a tree and removing the topsoil. Within the folklore, it is almost as though somebody has cleared the houses, the roads, the foundations and the municipal archives.

**Google has not merely removed the offices. Google appears to have excavated the supernatural housing stock.**

### 🐻 The Bear Is Not a Background Asset

The bear occupies an important place in Finnish and wider northern traditions, including respectful names, hunting rituals and careful treatment of a powerful animal. It would be a mistake to file the bear as just another employee of Tapio Inc. Its significance lies partly in the terms on which humans may speak about, approach and kill another creature.

Even vocabulary can express restraint. The way we name an animal is part of how we organise our relationship to it.

## 🦠 Metsänviha — When the Forest Takes Offence

**Metsänviha**, literally the forest's anger or wrath, belongs to Finnish folk accounts of affliction and dangerous encounters with forest power. Disrespect, fright or transgression could be understood as provoking harmful consequences. Such beliefs belong to historical explanatory and ritual systems; they are **not modern biomedical accounts of infectious disease**. [7]

What interests us is the structure of the relationship.

A human acts within an environment. The environment is not inert. Consequences may arrive through pathways the human cannot see, understand or control. The body experiences them nevertheless.

Modern ecology offers a different, testable account of invisible relationships: fungal associations, soil microorganisms, hydrology, nutrient cycles, root systems, food webs, chemical processes and habitat dependencies. These are not *proof* of metsänviha, and we should not pretend Finnish folklore secretly discovered microbiology. They are a useful **conceptual comparison**: the invisible is not the same as the inconsequential.

Removing trees can change light, temperature, evaporation, wind exposure, soil moisture and habitat. Stripping topsoil can remove seed banks, organic matter and biological communities. Altering drainage can affect water and sediment transport beyond the boundary of a site. The scale and persistence of these effects depend on local conditions and require measurement.

The company may have a spreadsheet showing what was removed. The ecosystem contains dependencies the spreadsheet may not fully represent.

**THE FOREST ALSO HAS A NETWORK, YOU ABSOLUTE WALNUTS.**

And unlike a server cluster, its recovery cannot necessarily be purchased on a quarterly procurement cycle.

> **Google:** We have identified no immediate operational risk.  
> **Metsänviha:** That is a very interesting definition of *operational*.

## ⚡ Ukko Has Joined the Call

Ukko is a major figure associated with thunder, weather and the sky in Finnish tradition. He is **not** a subsidiary of Tapio's forest household. His arrival expands the discussion to the broader systems through which forest, soil, rainfall, fertility and human life are connected. [6]

And now, regrettably for the applicant, we must discuss **Thor**.

Technology culture, especially its nerdier corners, is perfectly capable of being intensely enthusiastic about northern European thunder-god imagery: Thor, hammers, lightning, Vikings, epic powers, fantasy and superhero mythologies. These are not all Finnish traditions; **Ukko is not Thor**, and Finnish mythology must not be treated as a footnote to the Marvel Cinematic Universe.

But the juxtaposition is difficult to resist.

**You cannot be in love with the aesthetics of northern thunder gods and then be astonished that people in northern Europe have culturally deep relationships with actual forests, water and weather.**

That is not a claim that every engineer loves Thor, or that enjoying a film makes anyone responsible for environmental damage. It is a satire of the gap between consuming mythological imagery and respecting the places in which related cultural traditions remain meaningful.

> **Engineer:** Thor is my favourite Avenger.  
> **Finnish folklorist:** Lovely. Have you met Ukko?  
> **Engineer:** Is he in the MCU?  
> **Finnish folklorist:** No.  
> **Engineer:** What's his thing?  
> **Finnish folklorist:** Thunder. Weather. Rain. Fertility.  
> **Engineer:** Cool.  
> **Finnish folklorist:** You have removed a forest and disturbed its drainage.  
> **Engineer:** Oh.  
> **Finnish folklorist:** Yes. Oh.

### 🌊 Vellamo — Water Has Its Own Questions

Vellamo is associated with water in Finnish mythological tradition. She does not need to be recast as the literal cause of runoff to have a place in this discussion. Residents and conservationists have raised concerns about disturbed water and sediment at the Finnish sites. [1]

**Where does the water go when the trees and soil are removed?**

The answer should come from hydrological surveys, site design and monitoring, not a corporate assurance that somebody has considered drainage.

### 🌍 Earth-Mother Traditions — And the Soil?

Finnish traditions concerning earth, fertility and maternal figures vary; **Akka, Maaemä and related names should not be compressed into one uncontested modern goddess biography**. The narrative function here is plain: a forest stands in soil, not in a PowerPoint deck.

The removal of topsoil is not a cosmetic matter. It is a physical intervention into the living and chemical substrate of the landscape.

### 🪨 Kivutar — Pain Has a Department Too

Kivutar is associated in Finnish runic and healing traditions with pain and suffering. She is not, so far as this node establishes, a historically documented regulator of data-centre construction. Her appearance in our fictional hearing draws attention to the fact that harm is **felt**: in bodies, households and communities, not only in environmental impact matrices.

The wider cast belongs in the satire precisely because the landscape's consequences do not stay inside one administrative silo.

## 🫀🕸️ Information Is Experienced — The Node's Actual Mechanism

A corporation can encounter a forest as a land parcel, a schedule, a site model, an energy requirement and a set of regulatory constraints. A resident encounters it as a place lived in. An animal encounters it as territory, cover, movement, food, sound and risk. A hydrologist encounters it through flows, soils and catchments. A folklorist may encounter it through the inherited language of relationships and obligations.

**None of these is simply the same information in a different font.**

They are different ways of selecting, processing and experiencing a shared material world. They illuminate different dependencies. They also distribute consequences unequally.

A company may experience the clearance as progress against a construction milestone. A villager may experience the loss of a familiar horizon, the arrival of noise or the fear of encountering a wolf. An organism in the removed soil experiences a change in its conditions of existence. These are not all morally or biologically equivalent experiences; they are nevertheless **not cancelled by the company's representation**.

A map is not a walk. An environmental inventory is not a lifetime spent inhabiting a place. Nor is lived experience a replacement for technical ecological assessment. The serious task is to integrate forms of knowledge without pretending that the most powerful institution's account is the only one that counts.

This is why the node belongs in **`🫀🕸️_Information_Is_Experienced`**, rather than being merely a piece about folklore or merely an engineering complaint.

**The question is who gets to turn their description of the environment into an irreversible decision about everyone else's experience of it.**

## 🇺🇸 Why Is the Finnish Reaction the Interesting Part?

The reporting itself deserves examination as an information environment.

Compare two possible constructions:

1. **A company has cleared an enormous area of forest; residents are furious.**
2. **Residents are furious about a technology company's expansion.**

Both can describe elements of the same event. They do not assign significance in the same way. The first makes the destruction the thing requiring explanation. The second can make local anger the deviation from an assumed norm of development.

This is an **analytic framing test**, not a finding that all American reporting takes one line or all European reporting takes another. In fact, contemporary US technology and business coverage also reports the extent of the tree felling and the regulatory halt. Compare actual headlines, leads, photographs and sourcing rather than substituting nationality for close reading. [1, 5, 8]

The author's European perspective is not a claim of innocence. Europe has cut down forests, industrialised rivers, drained wetlands, mined landscapes and exported ecological harm. It has plenty of its own shit to fix.

But that history does not make this degree of destruction ordinary or acceptable.

**Why is Finnish anger surprising? Why isn't the sheer scale of the clearance surprising?**

There is a difference between recognising that infrastructure imposes costs and accepting a development culture in which very large ecological losses are treated as routine preparatory works.

Nor is this an argument that Americans lack ways of understanding inhabited land. Indigenous nations, environmental movements and affected communities throughout the United States have long contested extractive development. The target is **an institutional logic**, not a national incapacity for feeling attached to a forest.

## 🕹️ Are We All Supposed to Live in the Metaverse Now?

The digital future is being advertised in terms of intelligence, connection, virtual environments, productivity and possibility. Meanwhile its physical support systems demand electricity, land, materials, water, construction and maintenance.

There is a peculiar mismatch when the benefits of digital expansion are described in rich human terms, but the environments sacrificed to build it are described as *sites*.

People still inhabit places. They have bodies. They know the smell of rain and the sound of animals. They remember what stood beyond the road. They live with dust, traffic, water and noise. **They do not cease to care because somebody elsewhere has produced a compelling model of a computational future.**

And the forest does not become virtual just because its destruction is administered digitally.

**You cannot upload the lost soil into the cloud.**

## 🗳️ Consultation Is Not the Same as Power

Major developers can hire planning consultants, ecologists, lawyers, engineers and public-relations teams. A resident may be expected to decipher technical documents, respond to consultations, organise neighbours, obtain independent advice and challenge decisions while getting on with the rest of their life.

Formal opportunities to comment are important. They are not automatically evidence that the public could shape the decisive choices.

The questions are: could residents challenge the location, the footprint, the timing of clearance, the design alternatives and the cumulative effects? Could they access the relevant drawings and assessments before irreversible works? Could they obtain independent technical scrutiny? Could their participation materially change the plan, rather than merely negotiate mitigation?

For Muhos and Otanmäki, these questions require investigation of the actual Finnish procedures, municipal decisions and chronology. We should not assert that every consultation failed without examining the record. Nor should the existence of a formal process be used to assume that its outcomes were fair.

**Were the people who live with the consequences asked what the design should optimise for — or merely informed what the developer had optimised?**

## ☢️ The Button Nobody Has to Push

Now enlarge the frame.

Suppose rapid compute expansion is increasingly justified by strategic competition: if the United States does not build more, China will; if China builds more, others must accelerate; if competitors accelerate, delay becomes a security risk. This is a familiar **security-dilemma structure**, even where the underlying technologies and purposes differ.

The danger is not that a data centre is a nuclear weapon. It isn't. The danger is an escalation process in which each actor can regard its own expansion as prudent while the collective consequences become damaging.

More accelerators. More megawatts. More campuses. More land. More grid capacity. More construction. More material throughput. The scorecard becomes **capacity installed**, rather than useful outcomes delivered or security actually gained.

The environmental damage need not wait for a war. It can accumulate during ordinary procurement, construction and operation. The comparison with a Cold War crisis concerns the logic of escalation and the absence of a stopping rule, **not an evidence-based claim that an ecological cataclysm is already inevitable or that the world is literally minutes from nuclear launch**.

Computing can also improve science, resilience and environmental management. Data centres differ enormously in their impacts, and one forest clearance cannot establish the global ecological balance of AI. The legitimate question is whether marginal benefits are being tested against full marginal costs, including land, power, water, materials and cumulative effects.

**An arms race that destroys the conditions necessary for its participants to survive is not producing security. It is consuming it.**

And there is the central absurdity: the competitors may disagree about almost everything, but they do not possess separate planets. Shared climate systems, biodiversity losses, supply chains and material dependencies are not neatly divided by geopolitical allegiance.

### 🛡️ NATO, If This Is the Rationale, Explain the Requirement

There is **no evidence established here that NATO ordered, financed or required Google's particular Finnish forest clearance**. Commercial data-centre expansion and defence infrastructure cannot be treated as synonyms.

But where governments or defence organisations do invoke strategic necessity for compute expansion, that claim should create a higher standard of systems-level justification, not a lower one. What capability is required? How much compute? How much redundancy? What can be obtained by improving utilisation, software efficiency, distributed architectures or existing facilities? What are the environmental trade-offs? Who is accountable to affected communities?

Some defence details may be classified. **The existence of classification does not make every land-use and environmental decision beyond public explanation.**

National security is not merely the capacity to deploy more computation. It also concerns the physical, ecological and social systems that make a population secure.

The planet is not on either side of the arms race.

> **Ukko:** Superiority over whom?  
> **Applicant:** Our strategic competitors.  
> **Ukko:** On which planet?  
> **Tuulikki:** Because the squirrels only have the one.

## 🌲 The Forest Was Never Empty

Return to the photographs. The mud. The exposed ground. The machines. The absent trees. The distinction between a forest and land prepared for development.

The central problem is not that data centres are intrinsically illegitimate, nor that every engineering alternative is feasible, nor that folklore can replace environmental science. It is that a very powerful institution can render a living environment legible as a development opportunity, act upon that representation, and distribute the consequences to people and organisms who never agreed that the representation was complete.

**The forest was not empty before the project arrived.**

It contained living relationships, familiar places, ecological functions, animals, histories and meanings. Some are measurable; some are experienced; some are culturally expressed. All deserve better than being silently demoted beneath a construction schedule.

If this infrastructure is necessary, demonstrate the necessity. If this footprint is necessary, show the alternatives. If this timing is necessary, explain why assessment could not come first. If the development serves a strategic interest, identify the accountable public rationale. If a smaller and less destructive design was rejected because it cost more, **say so**.

And if we are going to build ever more sophisticated intelligence, we might reasonably expect its builders to show a little more intelligence about the world they are building it in.

*Google has prepared the site.*

*The residents have lost their forest.*

*The wolves have entered the village.*

*And somewhere in the Finnish supernatural administrative structure, a very substantial complaint has been filed.*

**🌲 Metsänviha has entered the chat.**

## 🔎 Evidence Boundaries and Questions Still Open

**Established in reporting as of 23 October 2026:** substantial clearance at Muhos and Otanmäki; removal of trees and topsoil; LVV's intervention before completed environmental impact assessment; Google's acknowledgement of shortcomings and stated cooperation; residents' descriptions of disturbance. [1–4]

**Reported but not independently established as causal findings here:** the cause of wolves entering Otanmäki; the full origin and downstream effect of discoloured water; exact ecological losses at every part of each site; the legal outcome of investigations.

**Not established here:** that a smaller design would definitely have met Google's requirements; that the entire site is needed for immediate computing capacity; that NATO required either development; that all US coverage normalises the destruction; that Finnish mythological beings have one fixed hierarchy.

**Documents worth obtaining next:** full environmental impact assessment materials and alternatives; site masterplans and phase drawings; area-by-use tables; habitat and large-carnivore surveys; hydrology and soil reports; municipal consultation records; grid and capacity assumptions; regulatory correspondence; Google response and any later decisions; comparative layouts for brownfield and compact alternatives.
