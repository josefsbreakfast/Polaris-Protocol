# 🫂 Free Mo Chara
**First created:** 2026-10-04 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-04  
*An intervention does not merely respond to information. It becomes information, and institutions do not control how that information will be experienced.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation – Free My Friend

There are three members of Kneecap.

There is **Móglaí Bap**, whose stage name grew out of a Mowgli-ish childhood nickname: essentially, *look at the bowl cut on that child*.

There is **DJ Próvaí**, whose name quite deliberately plays with *Provo*: the Provisional IRA.

And then there is **Mo Chara**.

**Mo chara** means **my friend** in Irish.

So when Liam Óg Ó hAnnaidh – Mo Chara – was charged with a terrorism offence in 2025, the British state found itself facing an exceptionally low-friction solidarity slogan:

> **FREE MO CHARA.**

Which also says:

> **FREE MY FRIEND.**

For God's sake.

This is funny. It is also an unusually compact specimen for **Embodied Information Ecology**.

The same visible string can carry different information to different receivers. One person sees the stage name of a rapper. Another reads ordinary Irish. Another recognises the grammar inside it. Another sees a Belfast Irish-language act. Another sees a British counter-terrorism intervention involving an Irish republican band. Another sees a history of British state action in Ireland sitting behind the contemporary encounter.

Nobody has to consciously hold all of those things in mind at once.

That is rather the point.

**Information is experienced.**

And when a state intervenes, the state does not remain outside the information environment observing it.

**The state enters the signal.**

---

## 1. 🫀 The Same String Is Not The Same Information

Information does not have a fixed social meaning independent of the system processing it.

The information available to a receiver depends on things including:

-   language;
-   cultural knowledge;
-   relationships;
-   location;
-   bodily state;
-   previous encounters;
-   institutional memory;
-   historical knowledge;
-   political context;
-   expectations;
-   and what happens immediately before and after the signal arrives.

`MO CHARA` can therefore be several things simultaneously:

-   a stage name;
-   a member of Kneecap;
-   an Irish phrase;
-   *my friend*;
-   an affectionate form of address;
-   Irish-language cultural expression;
-   a Belfast cultural marker;
-   a political signifier;
-   a search term somebody only learns because a prosecution makes it
news.

The wrong question is therefore only:

> **What does "Mo Chara" mean?**

The more useful question is:

> **What information becomes available from "Mo Chara" to this receiver,
> in this environment, at this moment?**

That distinction matters to communication.

It also matters to security.

## 2. 🗣️ Gaeilge Is Not A Nice Cultural Bolt-On

First: **Irish is Gaeilge**. Scottish Gaelic is **Gàidhlig**. Manx is **Gaelg**. They are related Goidelic languages, but their histories should not be collapsed into one interchangeable blob called *Gaelic*.

This node is principally concerned with **Gaeilge**, because Kneecap are a Belfast Irish-language act and the relevant contemporary jurisdiction is Northern Ireland.

Irish did not simply wander out of ordinary life through some politically neutral process of linguistic fashion. Its long decline and displacement occurred inside centuries of English and British political, legal, educational and economic power. Later revival, preservation and reclamation therefore also carry historical information.

Nor is Irish merely a heritage language belonging to the Republic of Ireland.

It is spoken, taught, transmitted and politically protected in Northern Ireland.

The **Identity and Language (Northern Ireland) Act 2022** provides official recognition of the status of Irish in Northern Ireland, provides for an Irish Language Commissioner, provides for standards of best practice concerning the use of Irish by public authorities, and requires relevant public authorities to have due regard to those standards.

The same legislation repealed the **Administration of Justice (Language) Act (Ireland) 1737**, which had required court proceedings to be conducted in English. Remarkably, that repeal came into force only on **26 February 2025**.

A few months later, Mo Chara was charged.

That chronology does not establish anything about the motives behind his prosecution.

It does establish something much simpler:

> **Irish is not an obscure foreign-language complication that
> unexpectedly wandered into British administration.**

It is a living language used by people over whom the contemporary United Kingdom exercises governmental responsibility, and whose status the United Kingdom has itself formally recognised.

Understanding that environment is not foreign-policy expertise.

**It is domestic governing competence.**

## 3. 🔴 Even The Letters Are Relational

There is a particularly delightful little information problem hiding inside the word **chara**.

The base noun is:

> **cara --- friend**

After **mo --- my**, the initial consonant is lenited. In Irish this grammatical process is called **séimhiú**.

So:

``` text
cara
friend

mo + cara
my + friend

séimhiú

mo chara
my friend
```

In older Gaelic typography, lenition could be represented using the **ponc séimhithe**, a dot above the consonant. In the now-familiar Roman-type orthography, lenition is generally represented by placing `h` after the consonant.

The grammatical information survives even though its graphical encoding can change.

That history should not be flattened into a tidy story in which typewriters arrived one morning and personally murdered the dot. The `h` convention predates modern typewriters, and the history of Gaelic and Roman type is longer and messier than that.

What we can securely say is that the independent Irish state itself later standardised spelling and, in **1963**, announced the gradual introduction of Roman type into school texts and examination papers.

And we can also say something analytically important:

> We cannot know what Irish typography would look like in a
> counterfactual Ireland untouched by English and British colonisation.
> We also cannot sensibly treat the technological, educational and
> administrative environment in which Irish writing developed as though
> centuries of English and British power were irrelevant to it.

People learning, recovering or reading Irish can still encounter these histories in the writing system itself.

They may have to learn why one text contains a dot where another contains an `h`.

The history is not always at the forefront of the receiver's mind.

It does not have to be.

The receiver has learned the decoder.

And so the stage name **Mo Chara** is an almost absurdly good archaeological specimen for this node:

``` text
cara
  ↓
friend
  ↓
mo + cara
  ↓
séimhiú
  ↓
mo chara
  ↓
stage name
  ↓
British counter-terrorism prosecution
  ↓
FREE MO CHARA
  ↓
FREE MY FRIEND
```

Even before the police, courts, newspapers, placards or historical memories arrive, **the written form of "friend" has already changed because it entered a relationship with "my".**

Information was relational before we even got started.

## 4. 📣 Congratulations, You Have Improved The Slogan

None of this proves that an individual police officer, prosecutor or official failed to know what **Mo Chara** meant.

That would require evidence we do not have.

There are at least three institutional possibilities:

``` text
NOBODY RELEVANT KNEW
        ↓
contextual knowledge missing

SOMEBODY KNEW
BUT IT DID NOT REACH THE DECISION
        ↓
routing failure

THE INFORMATION REACHED THE DECISION
BUT DID NOT MATERIALLY ALTER IT
        ↓
weighting / decision failure
```

The observable output is the same:

> **FREE MO CHARA**

The translation is therefore not proof that some particular official was stupid.

It is a **diagnostic prompt**.

If a consequential intervention involving a Belfast Irish-language act does not appear to have adequately modelled something as elementary as the ordinary meaning of one member's stage name, what else about the receiving environment might not have become causally effective at the decision point?

Irish-language reclamation?

Northern Irish identity?

British institutional memory?

Republican political culture?

The history of counter-terrorism intervention?

The possibility of prisoner or martyr framing?

Palestinian solidarity?

Diaspora reception?

The question is not whether all of those variables should have vetoed an intervention.

The question is whether the system knew enough to **model what its intervention would itself communicate**.

## 5. 🕸️ The State Enters The Signal

The state does not merely encounter information.

The state can alter its salience, meaning, emotional charge, distribution and audience.

``` text
MO CHARA
    ↓
STATE INTERVENTION
    ↓
FREE MO CHARA
    ↓
FREE MY FRIEND
    ↓
PLACARDS / CHANTS / REPORTING
    ↓
NEW RECEIVERS ASK WHAT "MO CHARA" MEANS
    ↓
TRANSLATION
    ↓
MORE RECEIVERS CAN DECODE THE SIGNAL
```

This is more specific than saying *Streisand effect* and going home.

Intervention can supply information with things it previously lacked:

-   salience;
-   translation;
-   context;
-   legitimacy;
-   emotional charge;
-   a protagonist;
-   an antagonist;
-   a news hook;
-   a photograph;
-   a court date;
-   a solidarity ritual;
-   distribution.

**Suppression can become part of the transmission mechanism.**

Sometimes the institution does not write the propaganda.

It merely supplies everything required for everybody else to print it.

## 6. ☘️ Unfortunately Britain Is Already Information

There is an easy analytical mistake here:

> *The state did not create the history through which its action would
> be interpreted.*

Mate.

Britain did not invent Gaeilge.

Britain did not invent Irish identity.

Britain did not choose Mo Chara's stage name.

Britain did not create every political belief held by Kneecap or their audience.

But English and British states, imperial structures, security institutions and legal systems played substantial roles in producing parts of the historical archive through which later British state action in Ireland and Northern Ireland can be interpreted.

So:

``` text
PAST STATE ACTION
      ↓
LIVED EXPERIENCE
      ↓
COMMUNITY MEMORY
      ↓
LANGUAGE / SONG / STORY / POLITICAL CULTURE
      ↓
PRESENT-DAY RECEIVER PRIORS
      ↓
NEW STATE ACTION
      ↓
INTERPRETATION THROUGH THOSE PRIORS
      ↓
NEW INFORMATION / MEMORY
```

**Institutions can become part of the prior information through which their own future actions are interpreted.**

Britain is already information in the system.

This creates a particular problem for institutional ignorance.

From inside an institution, there can be an enormous moral difference between:

> **We deliberately did this because of your identity.**

and:

> **We did not realise this would carry that meaning for you.**

From outside the institution, the receiver does not get privileged access to internal intention.

They observe behaviour.

Where an institution has historically acted coercively towards a community, **contemporary institutional ignorance can therefore be experienced as continuity**.

Ignorance and hostility are not morally identical.

But repeated:

> **We didn't realise.**

can eventually communicate:

> **We did not consider you important enough to find out.**

That is an information problem.

And, eventually, a trust problem.

## 7. 🩹 Historical Trauma Is An Operational Variable

This is not an argument that the prosecution of Mo Chara was equivalent to Bloody Sunday.

It plainly was not.

Events can differ enormously in violence, consequence and moral magnitude while still revealing related information mechanisms.

Northern Ireland contains historical memories of:

-   British military and security intervention;
-   internment;
-   Bloody Sunday;
-   imprisonment;
-   the H-Blocks;
-   hunger strikes;
-   republican prisoner symbolism;
-   contested policing;
-   contested legitimacy;
-   language suppression and reclamation.

Those memories do not dictate the correct interpretation of every subsequent state action.

They do alter the informational environment into which subsequent state action arrives.

The receiver is not a blank machine.

Neither is the institution.

A competent security system therefore asks not merely:

> **What are we doing?**

but:

> **What historical information will become available when *we* are the
> ones doing it?**

This is why the Bloody Sunday material should be cross-linked rather than used as rhetorical escalation.

The analytical object is the feedback mechanism:

``` text
STATE ACTION
   ↓
COMMUNITY EXPERIENCE
   ↓
MEMORY
   ↓
FUTURE INTERPRETATION
   ↓
NEW STATE ACTION
   ↓
MEMORY ACTIVATED / MODIFIED
```

The past does not remain politely in the past merely because the current intervention is less severe.

## 8. 🪢 Governing Authority Inherits Governing Responsibility

The contemporary United Kingdom is not simply interchangeable with the British Empire.

But the contemporary **United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland** exercises governmental authority in Northern Ireland.

That means responsibility for the safety, dignity, rights and security of people there does not disappear when their politics become inconvenient.

Unionist.

Nationalist.

Republican.

Neither.

Something else entirely.

Everyone.

Historical responsibility does not require pretending that today's officials personally committed yesterday's harms.

It means recognising that contemporary institutions govern inside an environment partly shaped by their predecessors.

Where trust has historically been damaged, government may inherit something resembling a **trust debt**.

That does not mean:

> give every community whatever it demands.

It means that fairness, restraint, transparency, procedural reliability and demonstrated contextual competence may have to do more work before trust becomes rational.

**Trust is not something the population owes the state.**

It is something the state continually has to make itself worthy of.

## 9. 🚪 The State Comes To Your Street

Cable Street is useful here precisely because we can temporarily strip away preferred political programmes and run it as an emergency security-planning exercise.

On **4 October 1936**, Oswald Mosley's British Union of Fascists planned to march through London's East End. Anti-fascist demonstrators mobilised in enormous numbers. The National Archives notes an important complication in popular memory: much of the direct confrontation was between counter-demonstrators and police attempting to keep the groups separated.

Now forget, for ten minutes, everybody's preferred constitution.

There are frightened people.

There are angry people.

There are police.

There are competing groups.

There is a residential neighbourhood.

There is a potential confrontation.

**You have ten minutes. What security arrangement produces the least harm?**

Questions suddenly become operational:

-   Do police need to advance?
-   Can distance be created?
-   Can the contentious group be routed elsewhere?
-   Can residents leave safely?
-   Who can communicate with whom?
-   Who already has community trust?
-   What are officers actually being asked to accomplish?
-   Are those objectives compatible?
-   How long can officers remain in position before exhaustion degrades
judgement?
-   What happens when somebody panics?
-   What happens when a frightened person moves *towards* the perceived
threat instead of away from it?
-   Does achieving the nominal mission require behaviour likely to
escalate hundreds of nervous systems at once?

That is not fluffy community engagement.

That is security planning.

## 10. 🫀 Distance Is Information

A residential street is not legally the same thing as somebody's living room.

Human beings do not necessarily experience the pavement outside their home as equivalent to an anonymous patch of ground fifty miles away.

**Proximity communicates.**

So does:

-   uniform;
-   formation;
-   numbers;
-   posture;
-   tone;
-   movement;
-   horses;
-   weapons;
-   vehicles;
-   blocked exits;
-   whether somebody approaches;
-   whether somebody backs away;
-   whether somebody stands over you;
-   whether somebody leaves you physical space.

Somebody moving too close to your body can produce an automatic response:

> **back off**

or:

> **square up**

before a beautifully reasoned constitutional analysis has had time to load.

So the embodied security question becomes:

> **What does state proximity communicate to the nervous system
> receiving it?**

This is one reason the home and its surrounding environment matter.

State power entering the space in which somebody ordinarily expects safety can itself change the information available to the body.

## 11. 🧠 Frightened Mammals Remain Mammals

High arousal does not have one behavioural presentation.

A frightened person can:

-   cry;
-   become silent;
-   freeze;
-   retreat;
-   comply immediately;
-   become confused;
-   shout;
-   pace;
-   appear defiant;
-   become aggressive;
-   square up.

Aggression does not automatically tell us what generated it.

And professional policing asks something extremely difficult of officers:

> **remain more regulated than the frightened person in front of you.**

The College of Policing's conflict-management guidance explicitly identifies emotional awareness and self-regulation, situational awareness, active listening, communication and negotiation as important non-physical skills. It advises officers to create safe distance where possible, give people space and time, remain calm, understand possible reasons for behaviour and recognise their own stress.

This is difficult work.

It is also work institutions can either make easier or harder.

The officer's nervous system is part of the information ecology too.

A uniform does not remove:

-   fear;
-   adrenaline;
-   fatigue;
-   cognitive overload;
-   anger;
-   tunnel vision;
-   stress.

Professional responsibility means officers are expected to regulate those states particularly well.

Institutional responsibility means giving them a realistic chance of doing so.

## 12. ⏱️ De-Escalation Needs Bandwidth

The point is not that time pressure mechanically produces violent policing in every encounter.

The evidence supports a more careful mechanism.

The College of Policing reports that complex demand and resource pressures were perceived by officers and staff as contributing to escalation by creating a more stressful environment. Accumulated jobs and pressure to resolve incidents quickly made decision-making more pressured; stress could lower tolerance, make officers react or use force sooner, and inhibit listening and creative problem-solving.

Its conflict-management guidance simultaneously tells officers to:

-   create time and space;
-   remain calm;
-   listen;
-   negotiate;
-   check understanding;
-   explain what is happening;
-   recognise their own stress;
-   understand the motivations of others;
-   avoid unnecessary proximity;
-   use communication to reduce conflict.

Those two things belong in the same policy conversation.

> **You cannot demand emotionally regulated policing while
> systematically removing the conditions required for emotional
> regulation.**

This matters when discussing police budgets.

It is entirely possible that some functions currently handled by police would be safer and more effective if resourced elsewhere:

-   mental-health care;
-   youth services;
-   housing;
-   domestic-abuse provision;
-   drug treatment;
-   community services;
-   preventive support.

But where police remain responsible for a security encounter, removing the things that make good encounters possible is not reform.

Training matters.

Supervision matters.

Staffing matters.

Recovery matters.

Communication skill matters.

Tactical time matters.

**Bandwidth matters.**

## 14. 👮 Policing By Consent Is An Information Relationship

Police possess coercive legal powers.

Routine effective policing nevertheless depends heavily on public cooperation, legitimacy and trust.

The College of Policing's ethical guidance tells officers to consider the potential effects of decisions, use powers proportionately, use communication to de-escalate where possible, consider the impact of police powers on trust and confidence across communities, and consider whether decisions, actions or omissions might cause the public to lose trust.

That makes trust operational.

It affects things including:

-   willingness to report;
-   willingness to provide evidence;
-   voluntary compliance;
-   emergency communication;
-   information sharing;
-   perceived legitimacy;
-   whether an officer's explanation is believed.

A particularly nasty feedback loop can therefore emerge:

``` text
LOW TRUST
   ↓
LESS VOLUNTARY COOPERATION
   ↓
SECURITY OBJECTIVE BECOMES HARDER
   ↓
GREATER PRESSURE FOR COERCIVE INTERVENTION
   ↓
POORLY HANDLED COERCION
   ↓
LOWER TRUST
```

Security policy can manufacture some of the conditions that make future security policy harder.

Which is why **trust belongs inside the security architecture**, not in a communications appendix added after the operation has finished.

## 15. 👜 Daloy Politsey --- Please Do Not Distribute The Decoder

A different but related information failure appeared in Glasgow in 2021.

The Pink Peacock, a queer Jewish anarchist café and cultural project, displayed a tote bag saying:

> **FUCK THE POLICE**

Police attended following complaints, confiscated the bag and charged one of the owners with breach of the peace.

The English-language bag was then replaced with a Yiddish one:

> **DALOY POLITSEY**

The attending officers reportedly did not respond to it in the same way.

This is funny.

Again, unfortunately, it is also theory.

`Daloy Politsey` is not merely *fuck the police but in Yiddish*.

It belongs to a historical Yiddish political archive associated with anti-Tsarist revolutionary culture and later reuse in other movements resisting coercive and authoritarian power.

That means the two signals have different decoder distributions.

``` text
ENGLISH SLOGAN
      ↓
broad immediate legibility

YIDDISH SLOGAN
      ↓
selective immediate legibility
      +
additional historical information
for receivers possessing the decoder
```

And then institutional intervention can do something magnificently counterproductive:

``` text
SELECTIVELY LEGIBLE SIGNAL
       ↓
INSTITUTIONAL CONTROVERSY
       ↓
NEWS EVENT
       ↓
TRANSLATION
       ↓
HISTORICAL EXPLANATION
       ↓
MORE PEOPLE ACQUIRE DECODER
```

Congratulations.

**You have distributed the decoder ring.**

## 16. 🩹 Cultural Memory Is Not A Loyalty Test

Recognising `Daloy Politsey` does not establish that somebody subscribes to every political programme associated with every historical person who has sung it.

Speaking Irish does not establish one compulsory constitutional position.

Recognising republican cultural material does not by itself establish support for contemporary violence.

Historical cultural objects can carry:

-   heritage;
-   mourning;
-   family memory;
-   humour;
-   political identification;
-   trauma;
-   reclamation;
-   cultural restoration;
-   solidarity;
-   historical warning;
-   aesthetic pleasure;
-   contemporary activism.

Different people can inhabit the same cultural archive while holding different present-day politics.

This is particularly important where languages and cultural practices have themselves experienced suppression.

Recovering information damaged or marginalised by historical violence can be part of cultural repair.

It does not automatically amount to reenacting the politics of the historical moment in which that information was produced.

> **Historical recognition is not itself evidence of contemporary
> political intent.**

Again:

**information is experienced.**

## 🛑 Hang On A Fucking Second

There is one question that needs permission to interrupt the entire chain.

> **If we actually do this, what happens next?**

Not:

> Is this lawful?

That has already been asked.

Not:

> Do we have evidence?

Ask that too.

Not:

> Does this fall within our remit?

Fine.

The **Golden Rule Question** is the systems question:

> **What will this intervention look like to the people receiving it — particularly the communities to whom this person, organisation or symbol already means something — and what are we likely to produce by acting?**

Before a consequential intervention leaves the building, somebody needs permission to say:

> **Hang on a fucking second.**

And then look at the whole pitch.

Ask:

- Who already identifies with this person?
- Who is frightened of us?
- Who distrusts us, and why?
- What historical memories does our involvement activate?
- What does this person, organisation or symbol already mean to the communities receiving our intervention?
- Are we about to transform an obscure person into a much larger political symbol?
- Are we about to turn a musician into a political prisoner in the eyes of their audience?
- Are we giving an existing movement a martyr, slogan, photograph, court date or organising ritual?
- Will intervention increase the audience for information we are trying to contain?
- Will we accidentally translate, contextualise or distribute something that previously had a much smaller decoder population?
- What happens if the case subsequently collapses, changes substantially, or never reaches substantive adjudication?
- What happens to trust if the public sees the intervention but never sees the evidential case they assume must sit behind it?
- **Is there another lawful way to achieve the actual security objective without producing those secondary effects?**

None of those questions means:

> **Don't act.**

Sometimes the answer will still be:

> Yes. We have considered all of that. The risk is sufficiently serious that intervention remains necessary.

Fine.

**Now the institution knows what it is buying.**

This is the bit that can disappear when everybody in the chain is doing their own job perfectly competently.

```text
LEGAL:
Is there an offence?

EVIDENCE:
Can we evidence it?

PROCEDURAL:
Can we do this?

SECURITY:
Does this fall inside our remit?

COMMS:
What do we say afterwards?

HANG ON A FUCKING SECOND:
What happens if we actually do it?
```

The institutional failure does not necessarily require everybody involved to be incompetent.

That is almost the more frustrating possibility.

An institution can contain excellent lawyers, investigators, intelligence analysts, civil servants, community teams, language expertise, historical knowledge and people with decades of professional experience — and still produce an output that makes all of them look like tits.

Because **local competence does not automatically produce system-level competence**.

Everybody can competently maintain their own square metre of the pitch while nobody's job description includes checking whether there is a fucking goalkeeper.

The failsafe therefore needs to exist **between the boxes**.

Before a high-consequence intervention escapes the institution, somebody needs both the responsibility and the institutional permission to ask:

> **Have we looked at this from outside our own process?**

> **How will this look from where they are standing?**

> **What are we about to create?**

That is not public relations.

It is not asking whether Twitter will be cross.

It is not allowing anticipated criticism to override law or security.

It is **second-order threat modelling**.

The intervention itself is about to enter the information environment.

Model it accordingly.

### 🗃️ What Did You Know At The Time?

There is also an important limit to what the public can know while events are unfolding.

Police, prosecutors, government and security institutions may possess information that the public does not.

There can be legitimate reasons why intelligence, investigative hypotheses, sources, methods or operational assessments cannot be disclosed while an investigation is active.

The public record can therefore sometimes make a historical decision appear less comprehensible than it appeared to the people making it at the time.

That possibility matters.

But secrecy creates an information problem of its own.

If the state undertakes a highly visible and consequential intervention while being unable to explain much of the information behind it, the public is temporarily being asked to assess the intervention using an incomplete information environment.

Sometimes that is unavoidable.

It does not follow that the explanatory obligation disappears forever.

Where circumstances later permit, an institution may need to be capable of saying:

> **Here is what we were responding to.**

> **Here is what we believed at the time.**

> **Here is what we could not tell you then.**

> **Here is what subsequently changed.**

> **Here is what we can now disclose.**

> **Here is what we got right.**

> **Here is what we got wrong.**

> **Here is what we have changed as a result.**

That does not require publishing information that would expose sources, methods, individuals or continuing operations.

It means recognising a basic legitimacy problem:

> **Operational secrecy can defer an explanation. It cannot necessarily erase the eventual need for one.**

Otherwise the information available to the public can look like this:

```text
SERIOUS STATE INTERVENTION
        ↓
PUBLIC ALARM
        ↓
WE CANNOT EXPLAIN WHY
        ↓
TIME PASSES
        ↓
EXPECTED OUTCOME DOES NOT MATERIALISE
        ↓
NO SUBSTANTIVE EXPLANATION
        ↓
???
```

And then the institution discovers that people have constructed their own explanations.

Of course they fucking have.

**Information vacuums are information environments too.**

The state does not necessarily get to choose between:

> **our secret explanation**

and:

> **no explanation**

Over time, it may instead be choosing between:

> **the best explanation we can responsibly provide**

and:

> **the explanations everybody else has constructed in its absence.**

That matters particularly where the receiving communities already possess historical reasons to pay close attention to coercive state power.

People carrying memories of persecution, discriminatory policing, political violence, language suppression or institutional abandonment do not encounter contemporary state action without priors.

Nor are those people necessarily hostile to the security institutions involved.

They may also be among the people who most urgently need those institutions to work.

That gives us another important security proposition:

> **The people most frightened by a state intervention may simultaneously be people the state has a duty to protect.**

Their alarm is therefore not automatically an adversarial signal.

Sometimes:

> **What the fuck are you doing?**

is useful feedback from somebody who very much wants the institution to remain trustworthy.

And if retrospective examination eventually produces the answer:

> **We done fucked up.**

there needs to be an institutional route for saying so.

Because these are not the same failure state:

```text
WE KNEW SOMETHING THE PUBLIC COULD NOT KNOW

CIRCUMSTANCES CHANGED

A REASONABLE DECISION PRODUCED AN ADVERSE OUTCOME

THE PROCEDURE FAILED

RELEVANT CONTEXT NEVER REACHED THE DECISION

WE SIMPLY GOT THIS WRONG
```

A functioning institution should be able to distinguish between them.

It should be able to learn differently from each of them.

And it should be able, where possible, to explain that difference to the people whose trust it requires.

Because:

> **The state was correct because the state did it**

is not a feedback mechanism.

It is how you disable one.

So before the intervention:

> ## **If we actually do this, what happens next?**

And afterwards:

> ## **Did what we thought would happen actually happen?**

If not:

**hang on a fucking second.**

--- 

## 17. 🥅 Put A Goalkeeper In The Fucking Net

None of this means that states should never intervene because somebody might dislike the intervention.

Security institutions exist because there are situations in which intervention is necessary.

The point is to model **first-order objectives and second-order informational effects at the same time**.

Before consequential intervention, ask:

-   What is the actual objective?
-   What lawful mechanisms exist?
-   Which mechanism is necessary?
-   Which is proportionate?
-   What alternatives exist?
-   What does each mechanism communicate?
-   Which populations will receive the intervention?
-   What languages matter?
-   What historical priors are likely to become active?
-   What will physical police presence communicate?
-   What does intervention make more visible?
-   Could it create a prisoner, martyr or solidarity frame?
-   Could it provide a better slogan?
-   Could it increase dissemination?
-   Could a less escalatory mechanism achieve the same legitimate
objective?
-   What happens to trust afterwards?
-   How will we know whether our prediction was wrong?

This is not surrendering security policy to vibes.

It is threat modelling.

> **For God's sake put a goalkeeper in the net.**

---

## 18. ⚖️ The Prosecution That Never Reached The Allegation

Precision matters here.

Mo Chara was **not acquitted**.

The alleged offence concerned conduct at a performance in November 2024. The CPS made its charging decision on **20 May 2025**. A written charge was issued on **21 May**. The Attorney General gave the necessary permission on **22 May**, by which point the relevant six-month period had expired.

The High Court later dismissed the Director of Public Prosecutions' appeal.

The court was unusually explicit about the consequence:

Mo Chara had not been tried for the alleged conduct.

He had not been convicted.

He had not been acquitted.

He would not be tried for it.

The underlying allegation therefore did not receive substantive adjudication at trial.

That produces an unusual informational sequence:

``` text
ALLEGED CONDUCT
      ↓
COUNTER-TERRORISM INVESTIGATION
      ↓
TERRORISM CHARGE
      ↓
PUBLIC CAMPAIGN
      ↓
FREE MO CHARA
      ↓
POLITICAL / HISTORICAL INTERPRETATION
      ↓
PROCEEDINGS INVALID
      ↓
NO TRIAL OF THE SUBSTANTIVE ALLEGATION
```

The state incurred much of the secondary informational cost of a terrorism prosecution without obtaining the intended adjudicative endpoint.

That does not tell us whether intervention should originally have occurred.

It gives us a very obvious post-event question:

> **Was this outcome inside the risk model?**

And, if not:

> **What changes now?**

## 19. 🌍 Shared Intelligence Is Not Shared History

There is no evidential basis in this node for claiming that the United States ordered or caused the prosecution of Mo Chara.

Do not smuggle that into the chronology.

The transatlantic problem is structural and more interesting.

An ally can possess security information Britain does not possess.

Britain can possess contextual information an ally does not possess.

Both things can be true simultaneously.

So:

> **Shared intelligence ≠ shared history.**

> **Shared concern ≠ identical legal mechanism.**

> **Shared adversary ≠ identical receiving population.**

An effective alliance must leave room for one government to say:

> We understand the concern.

and:

> That intervention will communicate something different here.

The existence of Irish and Jewish diasporas across the Atlantic makes receiver populations even less territorially tidy.

Information environments do not stop at customs.

Neither does historical memory.

---

## 20. 🕸️ Contextual Competence Is Operational Competence

No, every police officer in Britain does not need to learn Irish tomorrow.

Although, to be completely fair, a sudden nationwide police Gaeilge Duolingo emergency would be extremely funny.

The institutional requirement is more serious:

> **Does the system know when it does not know enough?**

And:

> **Can it route the question to somebody who does before acting?**

Security decisions routinely use several kinds of information:

``` text
LEGAL
What are we permitted to do?

THREAT
What might happen?

TACTICAL
What is physically happening?

RECEIVING-ENVIRONMENT
What will our intervention mean here?
```

That fourth category is not decoration.

If misunderstanding changes behaviour, and behaviour changes risk, then cultural and contextual competence can become part of operational risk assessment.

This applies regardless of why a community is being policed.

Police may be present because somebody is threatened.

Police may be investigating somebody.

Police may be separating opposing groups.

Police may need witnesses.

Police may be protecting a demonstration.

Police may be restricting one.

Police may have been called into a home because something terrible has happened.

The politics does not remove the human problem.

**An agent of state coercive authority has entered somebody's information environment.**

Uniform communicates.

Distance communicates.

Language communicates.

Pronouncing a name correctly communicates.

Understanding what somebody is saying communicates.

Failing to recognise their language as meaningful communicates.

Knowing why somebody might be frightened communicates.

And the officer's benign reason for being there does not automatically determine what their presence communicates to the receiver.

---

## 21. 🧠 Institutions Can Know Something Without Being Able To Use It

An institution can contain:

-   Irish speakers;
-   Northern Ireland specialists;
-   historians;
-   community liaison officers;
-   counter-terrorism specialists;
-   diplomats;
-   lawyers;
-   psychologists;
-   communications staff;
-   people with lived experience of the relevant community.

And still produce a decision that behaves as though none of that information exists.

This is the difference between **institutional ignorance** and **institutional disconnection**.

Institutional knowledge is not simply the sum of facts possessed by individual employees.

Information becomes operational only when it can:

1.  reach the decision point; 2.  arrive before the decision is irreversible; 3.  be understood; 4.  be weighted appropriately; 5.  possess enough institutional authority to alter the output.

A system may therefore contain the information necessary to predict its own mistake while lacking the pathways required to make that information **causally effective**.

That is not merely a personnel problem.

That is cybernetics.

And it gives us a harder question than:

> **How could they be so stupid?**

The better question is:

> **What information architecture allows an institution containing
> knowledgeable people to repeatedly behave as though it does not know
> things that it demonstrably contains?**

---

## 22. 🔄 Shit. That Didn't Work.

A functioning security institution needs to be capable of saying:

> **Shit. That didn't work as expected.**

That is not institutional humiliation.

That is a feedback loop.

``` text
OBJECTIVE
   ↓
INTERVENTION
   ↓
OBSERVED EFFECT
   ↓
COMPARE WITH EXPECTED EFFECT
   ↓
DISCREPANCY
   ↓
LEARN
   ↓
MODIFY
```

The alternatives are worse.

A bad outcome does not prove everyone involved was malicious.

Good intentions do not prove nothing needs changing.

The useful questions are:

> **What did we predict?**

> **What happened?**

> **Why were they different?**

Immediate response may require reducing danger, communicating clearly and creating physical space.

Short-term review may require identifying missing context, correcting misinformation, examining decisions and supporting affected communities.

Medium-term repair may involve training, staffing, intelligence routing, prosecution interfaces, community relationships and international coordination.

Long-term repair may require rebuilding trust and measuring whether institutional reforms actually changed outcomes.

A system that cannot metabolise error has to keep rediscovering the same lesson through somebody else's body.

---

## 23. 📊 Trust Belongs On The Security Dashboard

Trust is not an atmospheric feeling to be measured only when somebody in communications has a spare afternoon.

It affects security outcomes.

Possible indicators include:

-   community confidence;
-   willingness to report;
-   willingness to provide evidence;
-   complaints;
-   use of force;
-   voluntary compliance;
-   perceived procedural fairness;
-   community contact;
-   mobilisation following intervention;
-   changes in the reach of political messages before and after
intervention.

None of those measures proves simple causation by itself.

A fall in confidence after a particular intervention does not automatically mean the intervention caused all of it.

But neither should institutions respond to declining trust by classifying public mistrust itself as merely another security threat.

First ask:

> **What did the system just communicate?**

## 24. 🫂 Free My Friend

After all of this, return to the smallest unit.

**My friend.**

That is relational information.

It says:

> this person belongs to somebody's social world.

> somebody cares about them.

> somebody has positioned themselves beside them.

People can say **FREE MO CHARA** while carrying very different histories, intentions and politics.

The relational layer remains available.

And the state does not control which layer the receiver experiences.

There is something almost comically perfect about the grammar.

`cara` becomes `chara` because it enters a grammatical relationship with `mo`.

The written information changes because the relationship changes.

Then a state intervenes.

The social information changes again.

Then supporters respond.

The information changes again.

Then newspapers translate it.

Again.

Then somebody learns why there is an `h`.

Again.

Then somebody learns about the dot.

Again.

Then somebody learns about the history of Irish typography.

Again.

The signal keeps acquiring information because it keeps entering new relationships.

**Free my friend, indeed.**

## 25. 🧭 Be Interested In The People You Govern

The policy demand produced by all this is remarkably modest.

Not omniscience.

Not perfect prediction.

Not capitulation.

Not never using force.

Not requiring every constable to possess a postgraduate degree in the cultural history of everybody they might encounter.

It is:

> **reasonable contextual competence before consequential
> intervention.**

Know the population.

Know enough of the history to recognise when history matters.

Know which languages materially affect the encounter.

Know when you need somebody else's expertise.

Create routes through which specialist and community knowledge can reach operational decisions.

Give frontline personnel the training, supervision, time and cognitive space required to use that knowledge.

Reduce unnecessary encounters.

Where intervention is necessary, use the least escalatory effective mechanism available.

Protect dignity even when coercion is required.

Observe what happens.

Correct course.

And where the state itself has contributed to historical trauma, do not treat mistrust as an inexplicable character defect in the population.

Northern Ireland cannot simultaneously be treated as sufficiently part of the United Kingdom for Westminster to exercise sovereignty, security powers and criminal law there, but sufficiently *other* that understanding its languages, histories and communities remains an optional specialist hobby.

If we say these are our fellow citizens, we have to be fucking curious about them.

Because:

> **You cannot police by consent people you cannot be bothered to
> understand.**

## 27. 🥅 The Actual Lesson

The state is not outside the information ecology.

Security is experienced by bodies.

Those bodies arrive carrying languages, relationships, memories and histories.

Institutions carry histories too.

Sometimes the state has legitimate reasons to intervene.

Sometimes people will dislike a perfectly lawful and necessary intervention.

Sometimes no available option is clean.

None of that removes the requirement to understand the environment well enough to avoid creating unnecessary danger by accident.

Historical context is not an inconvenient distortion surrounding the *real* security problem.

**Historical context is part of the security environment.**

Britain therefore needs systems that allow it to know better **before** the intervention rather than discovering the context afterwards.

Very often the knowledge already exists.

The problem is getting the right information to the right decision-maker, at the right time, in a form they understand, with enough institutional permission for it to change what happens next.

And, lads.

His fucking name means **my friend**.

**Please put a goalkeeper in the net.**
