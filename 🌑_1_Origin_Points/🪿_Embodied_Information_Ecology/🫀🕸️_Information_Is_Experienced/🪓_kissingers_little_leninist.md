# 🪓 Kissinger’s Little Leninist
**First created:** 2026-08-15 | **Last updated:** 2026-08-15  
*How revolutionary aesthetics, selective historical memory and American exceptionalism can allow reactionary power to imagine itself as counterculture — and what happens when the historical analogy turns back on the person making it.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

In 2016, Ronald Radosh reported that Steve Bannon had described himself several years earlier as a **“Leninist”**, explaining the comparison through Lenin's supposed desire to destroy the state and his own desire to bring the contemporary establishment “crashing down.”

Bannon later said that he did not remember the meeting or conversation. The quotation therefore belongs here as a **reported retrospective account**, not recorded speech.

But the usefulness of the remark extends beyond whether one American political strategist said precisely those words.

The more interesting question is:

> **Why Lenin?**

Why does a revolutionary communist associated with the destruction of an existing political order become available as an exciting model of rupture within parts of an American reactionary culture that simultaneously treats comparatively moderate left-wing politics as evidence of dangerous communism?

And why can that transgression remain playful while comparison with **Hitler, Nazism, or particular fascist practices** often produces a dramatically different reaction?

One possibility is that these political memories operate differently.

> **One historical enemy can be feared principally through its vocabulary; another can be feared through resemblance to its actions.**

That difference tells us something about historical memory, national mythology and the political position from which the comparison is being made.

This node therefore does **not** attempt to describe “the American mind.”

Its narrower object is a recognisable political subject within American reactionary culture: disproportionately **white, male, cis-passing, socially dominant or elite-adjacent**, and sufficiently comfortable within the historical distribution of American power that the coercive state can sometimes be imagined principally as an intellectual object to **capture, dismantle, redirect or destroy**.

For Americans differently positioned in relation to race, gender, sexuality, disability, poverty, immigration, religion, Indigenous sovereignty, policing or other structures of exclusion, the state may already be something considerably less theoretical.

Bannon is an opening case.

He is not the whole subject.

## 🪓 1. “I’m A Leninist”

### The reported remark

Evidence pass:

- Ronald Radosh's account of the 2013 conversation;
- publication in 2016;
- the reported “I'm a Leninist” statement;
- its explanation through destruction of the state / establishment;
- Bannon's later statement that he did not remember the meeting or conversation;
- evidentiary label: **reported retrospective quotation, not recorded speech**.

The first analytical mistake would be to treat “Leninist” as self-explanatory.

It is not.

If the identification is ideological, it demands specificity.

If it is strategic, it demands specificity.

If it is theatrical, the choice of theatrical reference itself demands explanation.

## ❓ 2. Why Lenin? Why Here?

“Lenin wanted to destroy the state” does not by itself explain why **Lenin** should be the chosen historical analogue.

Europe alone supplies an enormous repertoire of:

- revolutions;
- insurrections;
- coups;
- revolutionary nationalisms;
- fascist movements;
- communist movements;
- anarchism;
- syndicalism;
- Jacobinism;
- Bonapartism;
- military dictatorship;
- collapsing monarchies;
- civil wars;
- resistance movements;
- failed republics;
- successful and unsuccessful attempts to reconstruct political order.

A European political actor saying simply:

> I want to destroy the existing political settlement.

does not obviously arrive at Lenin first.

That produces a useful fork.

### Possibility A — Lenin is specific

If the speaker really means **Lenin**, identify the Leninist mechanism:

- vanguard organisation?
- revolutionary rupture?
- destruction rather than capture of existing state machinery?
- disciplined party organisation?
- dual power?
- exploitation of state crisis?
- revolutionary coercion?
- dictatorship of the proletariat?
- treatment of political opponents?
- some particular theory of historical transformation?

If so, the comparison can be tested.

### Possibility B — Lenin is symbolic

If none of those distinctions matter and Lenin means only:

> extremely radical man who smashed the establishment,

then Lenin's function may be principally **cultural**.

For an American conservative audience shaped by generations of anti-communism, Lenin is not simply a historical political thinker.

He is a **forbidden object**.

Invoking him therefore generates its own rebellious energy:

> You were taught to fear this man.  
> I am willing to learn from him.  
> Your taboo does not constrain me.

In that case, the Americanness of the quotation becomes analytically important.

The reference may tell us less about Lenin than about **what Lenin means inside American conservative mythology**.

## 🩸 3. Okay Steve, How Leninist?

If Lenin is being invoked as more than decoration, theory and praxis both belong in the comparison.

### Theory

Potential areas:

- destruction rather than straightforward inheritance of the bourgeois state;
- revolutionary seizure of political power;
- class conflict;
- dictatorship of the proletariat;
- coercion against class enemies;
- transitional state power;
- eventual “withering away” of the state;
- revolutionary organisation and discipline.

The theoretical question is not merely:

> Does Lenin want radical change?

It is:

> **Who receives power, through which institutions, with what coercive authority, against whom, and toward what proposed endpoint?**

### Praxis

Potential areas requiring careful historical treatment:

- Cheka;
- Red Terror;
- political repression;
- hostage-taking;
- executions;
- concentration camps;
- suppression of rival political organisations;
- restrictions upon press and political activity;
- Civil War conditions;
- revolutionary emergency government;
- the relationship between ideological permission and contingent escalation.

### Avoid two convenient myths

Do not reduce the history to:

> Lenin inevitably equals Stalin.

But equally:

> Leninist theory was lovely and then history inexplicably became authoritarian.

does not resolve the relationship between theory and practice.

The useful historical question concerns **political theory, institutional design, emergency conditions, coercive permission and actual outcomes simultaneously**.

## 🚨 4. Scared Of The Words; Scared Of The Actions

A central asymmetry now appears.

Within some forms of American reactionary politics, the language associated with the revolutionary left can itself trigger the extremism category:

- socialist;
- Marxist;
- communist;
- collectivist;
- radical left;
- far left.

The behaviour required to activate those labels can be remarkably ordinary.

Progressive taxation, public healthcare, labour protections, welfare provision or other policies entirely compatible with liberal democracy can become rhetorically adjacent to revolutionary communism.

Yet the historical archive contains abundant examples of **actual extremist and revolutionary-left violence**.

If the analytical concern genuinely is far-left extremism, there is no shortage of comparison material.

That produces another diagnostic question:

> **If you mean far-left extremism, why aren't you comparing the thing with examples of far-left extremism?**

If moderate policy is repeatedly placed in the same category as revolutionary dictatorship, the category eventually stops describing political behaviour.

It begins describing **distance from the speaker's preferred political settlement**.

### Semantic inflation

If:

> public healthcare = communism,

what vocabulary remains for a government actually undertaking revolutionary collectivisation, political terror or mass repression in the name of communist transformation?

The word has been made frightening by making it enormous.

Its analytical usefulness has been destroyed in the process.

## 🔥 5. Mythological And Situated Anti-Communism

Not all American anti-communism operates this way.

There is an important distinction between:

### Mythological anti-communism

Communism functions principally as:

> the great foreign ideological enemy.

Its symbolic meaning can remain enormous even where detailed knowledge of individual regimes, mechanisms and victims is comparatively weak.

### Historically situated anti-communism

For communities shaped by:

- revolutionary government;
- political imprisonment;
- confiscation;
- exile;
- displacement;
- authoritarian repression;
- family memory;

“communism” may refer to a much more specific political experience.

Cuban-American political traditions provide one obvious American case worth examining.

Marco Rubio may therefore be useful as a **counter-case**, not because his politics must be accepted, but because his anti-communism can be examined against a concrete Cuban and exile political history rather than treated simply as generic Cold War inheritance.

The distinction matters.

One position fears **an abstract ideological category**.

Another can point toward **a particular state, particular institutions, particular actions and particular inherited memories**.

The latter can still produce exaggeration, mythology or political opportunism.

But at least the historical referent is visible.

## 🕯️ 6. The Ideology Remembered More Clearly Than Its Victims

This produces a peculiar possibility:

> **The ideology can be remembered more intensely than the people harmed in its name.**

Communism becomes terrifying as an abstraction.

But:

- which government?
- which period?
- which institution?
- which policy?
- which victims?
- which mechanism of repression?
- which internal political dispute?
- which historical conditions?

can become strangely indistinct.

Lenin can consequently occupy two positions simultaneously:

**terrifying communist icon**  
and  
**interesting revolutionary technician**.

That is considerably harder to sustain once Lenin ceases to be a poster.

The moment the historical figure becomes:

> a particular political leader governing particular institutions whose decisions had consequences for particular human beings,

the rebellious abstraction begins turning back into **government**.

## 🎸 7. Punk Without The Politics That Made Punk

The Lenin remark has a recognisable countercultural structure.

The respectable conservative is supposed to reject Lenin.

Calling oneself Leninist therefore performs transgression.

But punk was never merely:

> say something respectable people dislike.

Different punk movements and scenes intersected variously with:

- class;
- unemployment;
- anti-authoritarianism;
- DIY production;
- independent cultural infrastructure;
- anti-racism;
- feminism;
- anarchism;
- squatting;
- hostility toward commercial and cultural gatekeepers.

Punk was contradictory.

Its politics cannot be reduced to a single programme.

But much of its disruptive force emerged from a material relationship to **power and exclusion**.

### Breaking illusions

Part of counterculture's power lies in breaking an illusion that still exists.

The shocking gesture works because something remains available to shock.

Once the illusion has already been punctured, reproducing the original gesture becomes:

- reenactment;
- nostalgia;
- fashion;
- branding;
- merchandise.

The safety pin eventually becomes something you can buy.

### The reactionary inversion

The contemporary political version can therefore become:

> **people with substantial access to institutional, financial or political power borrowing the cultural vocabulary developed by people excluded from it.**

The language survives:

- disruption;
- rebellion;
- dissidence;
- forbidden knowledge;
- outsiders;
- dangerous ideas;
- “they don't want you to say this”;
- destruction of establishment legitimacy.

But the politics can remain:

- hierarchical;
- highly concentrated;
- compatible with extraordinary private wealth;
- comfortable with coercive state power;
- interested less in abolishing hierarchy than changing **who controls it**.

### The better test

The useful question is not:

> Does this look transgressive?

It is:

> **What illusion about contemporary power does this actually break?**

If the answer is none, the object may possess **countercultural aesthetics without countercultural function**.

Working formulation:

> **Punk semiotics applied to competition between elites.**

## 🕯️ 8. Kissinger’s Little Leninist

This produces the archetype at the centre of the node.

Perhaps Lenin describes the desired **historical drama** while Kissinger describes the desired **personal role**.

### Lenin

The fantasy of:

- rupture;
- revolutionary transformation;
- historical discontinuity;
- recognising institutional weakness;
- destroying an apparently permanent order.

### Kissinger

The fantasy of:

- strategic intellectual importance;
- proximity to sovereign power;
- geopolitical sophistication;
- becoming indispensable to decision-makers;
- influencing events beyond formal electoral authority;
- being remembered as someone who understood history while everybody else was living inside it.

### The hybrid

> **Kissinger's little Leninist wants the historical importance of the elite strategist and the cultural glamour of the revolutionary outsider.**

“I want to be Kissinger” admits proximity to power.

“I am a Leninist” says:

> Fuck your system.

The second sounds considerably more punk.

Even where the underlying aspiration contains an extraordinary amount of:

> **I would actually quite like your system's power.**

## ☠️ 9. Why Nazi Is Different

Now reverse the historical analogy.

Direct Nazi or Hitler comparison frequently behaves differently from provocative appropriation of Lenin.

One possible distinction is:

### Lenin

> foreign enemy whose revolutionary technique can be provocatively appropriated.

### Hitler / Nazism

> historical category into which the contemporary actor himself may now be placed.

That is not merely another naughty historical reference.

It threatens the subject's **position inside the national story**.

### Hero category and enemy category

A powerful American World War II narrative places the United States among:

> **the people who defeated the Nazis.**

A specific Nazi comparison can therefore perform a particularly destabilising move:

> You are not being compared with the heroic force in this story.

> **You are being moved into the enemy category.**

That may explain why the comparison can threaten political identity differently from “extremist,” “authoritarian,” or even sometimes “fascist.”

### Guardrail

This does **not** license indiscriminate Nazi analogy.

Comparison should specify:

- mechanism;
- institution;
- rhetoric;
- legal technique;
- target population;
- political function;
- similarities;
- material differences.

The interesting evidence lies partly in what happens **when a historically specific comparison lands**.

## 🇺🇸 10. Why Must The Villain Be Foreign?

The Nazi comparison raises another problem.

If Americans require historical material for understanding:

- racial hierarchy;
- racialised citizenship;
- political exclusion;
- state-tolerated racial terror;
- eugenics;
- disenfranchisement;
- coercive policing;
- authoritarian local government;

why must the analogy cross the Atlantic?

The American archive already contains:

- slavery;
- Indigenous dispossession;
- Reconstruction and its violent overthrow;
- Jim Crow;
- lynching;
- racial terror;
- segregation;
- eugenics;
- exclusionary immigration systems;
- political surveillance;
- racialised policing and incarceration.

These systems are **not identical to Nazi Germany or the Holocaust**.

Comparison is not identity.

But the domestic archive destroys the comforting proposition that racial authoritarianism and systematic political violence are phenomena Americans can recognise only by looking overseas.

### Comparative adjacency

It is legitimate to examine historically meaningful relationships among:

- white supremacy;
- racial classification;
- citizenship;
- eugenic thought;
- racial terror;
- segregation;
- state and para-state violence;

without claiming that distinct historical systems were interchangeable.

The methodological requirement is precision.

The political consequence is more uncomfortable:

> **racial authoritarianism does not require somebody else's uniform.**

## 🧿 11. The Exceptionalism Escape Hatch

American exceptionalism need not take the crude form:

> America has never done anything bad.

A more resilient form is:

> **whatever America does, America remains fundamentally the kind of country in which that cannot happen.**

When the country violates its proclaimed principles, the contradiction can therefore be resolved through classification:

> America did not produce this.

Instead:

> **bad Americans temporarily failed to be American.**

The ideal becomes definitionally innocent.

Evidence against the national mythology is reclassified as deviation from the mythology.

That makes the proposition unusually difficult to falsify.

### The alternative proposition

A less comforting historical position is:

> **No constitutional culture possesses immunity from political degeneration.**

Institutions can fail.

Populations can accommodate authoritarianism.

Courts can legitimate terrible rules.

Police can enforce them.

Educated people can become propagandists.

Neighbours can participate.

The question is never whether a country possesses the correct national essence.

It is whether its institutions and populations can **prevent particular mechanisms from developing under particular conditions**.

## 💣 12. Geography Changes Political Memory

The United States suffered enormous military losses during the twentieth century.

But the continental homeland did not experience its major twentieth-century wars across its cities and civilian landscape in the manner experienced across much of Europe and Asia.

Important qualifications include:

- Pearl Harbor;
- attacks on US territories;
- 9/11;
- earlier continental warfare;
- Indigenous experiences of conquest and state violence;
- differential domestic exposure to political violence.

The broad geographical distinction nevertheless matters.

Vietnam happened overwhelmingly in Vietnam.

Korea happened overwhelmingly in Korea.

World War II did not leave continental American cities resembling Warsaw, Coventry, Rotterdam, Stalingrad, Manila or Hiroshima.

### War can remain geographically external

A society can therefore experience war as:

> **politically central and personally costly while geographically elsewhere.**

That affects political imagination.

### Pearl Harbor as evidence

Pearl Harbor need not be treated simply as the exception that defeats this argument.

Its extraordinary cultural significance may partly demonstrate **how exceptional direct territorial attack felt**.

The event becomes foundational precisely because it violates an expectation of geographical distance.

## 🪫 13. Ruins, Exhaustion And The Limits Of Myth

Physical destruction is only part of the difference.

War also produces:

- civilian death;
- disability;
- displacement;
- destroyed housing;
- damaged infrastructure;
- food scarcity;
- rationing;
- occupation;
- reconstruction;
- sovereign debt;
- economic exhaustion;
- missing generations;
- altered borders;
- family memories that remain physically local.

Britain provides a useful intermediate case.

British political culture contains plenty of:

- imperial exceptionalism;
- national self-congratulation;
- heroic war mythology;
- selective historical memory.

But it also contains inherited memories of:

- bombing;
- threatened invasion;
- enormous military loss;
- rationing;
- postwar austerity;
- economic exhaustion;
- visible urban destruction.

There are limits imposed by the ruins.

Continental European societies may possess even less comfortable inheritances:

> sometimes your country was invaded.

> sometimes your government collaborated.

> sometimes your neighbours participated.

> sometimes the resistance story became considerably prettier after the war than it had been during it.

Europe does not thereby become historically wise.

It simply possesses a **crowded graveyard of political certainties**.

Liberal democracy failed.

Empires committed atrocities.

Fascists governed.

Communists governed.

Nationalists produced catastrophe.

Democratic governments also produced violence.

There are rather a lot of dead theories of national immunity lying around.

## 👁️ 14. Whose America?

This is where the demographic qualification becomes indispensable.

The state looks different depending upon where a person encounters it.

For the socially dominant subject, the state can remain something to:

- reform;
- capture;
- disrupt;
- shrink;
- enlarge;
- smash;
- redesign.

It is an object of political imagination.

For someone encountering the state through:

- racial exclusion;
- misogyny;
- homophobia or transphobia;
- disability;
- poverty;
- immigration enforcement;
- Indigenous dispossession;
- religious marginalisation;
- policing;
- incarceration;
- political surveillance;

the state may already be something that:

> **does things to you.**

That difference matters enormously to revolutionary aesthetics.

“Destroy the state” sounds different when state coercion is principally a theoretical concern than when the institutions under discussion already structure the boundaries of one's everyday life.

## 📚 15. Baldwin And The View From Outside

James Baldwin provides a particularly useful route into this problem because his writing repeatedly shifts between:

- being Black in America;
- being American in Europe;
- racialisation across national contexts;
- Europe looking at America;
- America imagining itself universal.

The important analytical movement is not simply:

> marginalised people understand oppression better.

That would turn social position into mystical wisdom.

The mechanism is more concrete.

### Marginalisation as information production

A person repeatedly positioned against the boundary of a social category receives information about that boundary.

Someone continually told:

> you are not quite the default citizen

has reason to notice what **default citizen** means.

Someone comfortably occupying the category may never need to examine it.

Marginalisation can therefore force repeated encounters with structures that dominant subjects are able to experience as neutral.

### Baldwin's comparative inversion

Europe could place Baldwin in a peculiar position.

He remained Black.

But he was also unmistakably **American**.

That allows “American” itself to become visible as a situated identity rather than the invisible standpoint from which the rest of the world is interpreted.

The American abroad can encounter something uncomfortable:

> the assumption that one's own categories are universal can itself look, from outside, like a form of social presumption.

That creates a powerful inversion with Baldwin's experience of white American normativity at home.

The person accustomed to occupying the unmarked national category discovers that **America is also a category other people can look at**.

## 🕳️ 16. “That Couldn’t Happen Here” — To Whom?

Once different American social positions are introduced, the exceptionalist proposition fragments.

One person says:

> **That could never happen here.**

Another can reasonably ask:

> **Which part?**

And then:

> **To whom?**

Black American history does not require Nazi Germany to discover:

- racial terror;
- exclusion from citizenship;
- discriminatory policing;
- political disenfranchisement;
- state-supported or state-tolerated violence.

Indigenous history does not require Europe to discover:

- dispossession;
- forced movement;
- state coercion;
- cultural destruction.

Other marginalised histories supply different encounters with the boundaries of American liberalism.

These histories are not interchangeable.

But together they undermine the idea that **American coercion becomes historically meaningful only when it resembles something foreign**.

Two Americans can therefore describe apparently different countries while occupying the same one.

One says:

> America could never become a state that does this.

Another replies:

> **Parts of the American state already did something structurally relevant. You simply weren't the population experiencing it.**

## 🕸️ 17. Historical Memory As Political Attack Surface

Selective historical memory does more than generate bad analogies.

It creates political vulnerabilities.

Suppose a movement understands itself as:

- rebellious;
- transgressive;
- anti-establishment;
- historically literate;
- dangerous to complacent elites;
- willing to violate liberal taboos;
- courageous enough to use the enemy's techniques.

Calling that movement:

> extreme

may strengthen the desired identity.

Calling it:

> dangerous

may strengthen the desired identity.

Calling it:

> politically incorrect

almost certainly strengthens the desired identity.

But another kind of comparison does something different.

It asks:

> **Are you actually occupying the historical role you think you are?**

The movement says:

> We are the punks fighting the establishment.

The historical comparison replies:

> **Perhaps you are the people the punks eventually have to organise against.**

That is not merely an accusation of extremity.

It attacks the **countercultural self-conception itself**.

## 🪩 18. When The Rebel Fantasy Breaks

This is where the different threads converge.

### Communism

The radical-left enemy can remain sufficiently abstract that its vocabulary becomes frightening while selected revolutionary figures remain available for provocative appropriation.

### Nazism

The comparison becomes more dangerous when it relocates the contemporary actor from America's preferred historical hero category into the category of the defeated enemy.

### Domestic racial history

The foreignness of authoritarianism becomes harder to maintain once the comparison turns toward America's own history of racial hierarchy and political violence.

### Marginalised American perspectives

The proposition that state coercion is foreign or hypothetical becomes harder to sustain once Americans who have experienced coercive institutions are allowed to define the historical dataset.

### Punk

The rebellious aesthetic becomes harder to maintain once the question changes from:

> Are you shocking respectable people?

to:

> **What relationship do you actually have to power?**

Together, these create a vulnerability:

> **the revolutionary reactionary needs to remain the outsider in his own story.**

If historical comparison successfully relocates him as:

- incumbent;
- hierarchy;
- coercive authority;
- establishment beneficiary;
- elite faction;
- enforcer rather than dissident;

then the aesthetic machinery begins to fail.

The problem is no longer:

> You are too radical.

It becomes:

> **You may not be the rebel at all.**

## 🥸 19. The Moustache Problem

A brief but constitutionally essential section.

Historical revolutionary leadership appears disturbingly well supplied with facial hair.

Potential dataset:

- Lenin;
- Stalin;
- Trotsky;
- Marx;
- Engels;
- Attlee;
- Macmillan;
- assorted European statesmen requiring subsequent classification.

> Facial hair has historically participated in the visual grammar through which masculinity, maturity, military authority, intellectual seriousness and political leadership were communicated.

## ⚱️ 20. The Founder Eventually Becomes Furniture

Lenin also raises the problem of what happens **after revolutionary success**.

### Founder cult

Potential evidence:

- Lenin's death;
- preservation and display of his body;
- Lenin Mausoleum;
- monumentalisation;
- naming of places and institutions;
- construction of revolutionary legitimacy through the dead founder.

### Stalin and inherited legitimacy

The successor does not initially need to erase the founder.

He can position himself as:

> the founder's legitimate interpreter.

The founder becomes transferable political authority.

Possible sequence:

**founder → sacred ancestor → authorised interpreter → successor accumulates independent authority → history is rewritten through whoever controls the institutions**

### The succession problem

Destroying institutional constraints creates another question:

> **What prevents the person who survives you from deciding what your revolution meant?**

Trotsky provides an obvious route into:

- revolutionary coalition;
- factional struggle;
- expulsion;
- exile;
- assassination;
- retrospective construction of the traitor;
- control of historical memory.

The aspiring revolutionary generally imagines himself as the person who controls succession.

History contains rather a lot of people who made the same assumption.

### 🇺🇸 American adaptation

Lenin received a mausoleum on Red Square.

The United States may require a more culturally appropriate form.

**Provisional model:** enormous Bannon float in the Reflecting Pool, direction maintained by a small remote-control submarine.

At least he gets remembered.

## ⚖️ 22. Evidentiary Guardrails

Distinguish throughout between:

**Documented fact**  
What a person demonstrably said, wrote, supported or did.

**Reported statement**  
Material dependent upon another person's recollection or account.

**Historical comparison**  
Specific similarities and differences among mechanisms, institutions or political conditions.

**Genealogy**  
Evidence of transmission, influence or historical relationship.

**Adjacency**  
Relevant structural or ideological proximity without a claim of identity or direct descent.

**Interpretation**  
What rhetoric or behaviour may reveal about political self-conception or historical memory.

**Hypothesis**  
A proposed explanation requiring comparative evidence.

Avoid:

- treating all Americans as possessing one historical consciousness;
- allowing “American” to default silently to a white male dominant subject;
- treating all European societies as historically sophisticated;
- treating marginalisation as automatic political wisdom;
- collapsing communism into Stalinism;
- collapsing revolutionary socialism into liberal social democracy;
- collapsing fascism into Nazism;
- treating Jim Crow and Nazi Germany as identical systems;
- treating comparison as proof of genealogy;
- assuming provocative historical identification constitutes complete ideological agreement;
- diagnosing political actors through vibes where documentary evidence exists;
- mistaking aesthetic resemblance for institutional equivalence;
- treating war geography as a deterministic explanation for political culture.

## 🧭 23. Working Thesis

The useful question is not whether an American reactionary who calls himself “Leninist” is **secretly a communist**.

The more interesting possibility is that selective historical memory allows Lenin to function as an **aesthetic of forbidden revolutionary competence** while leaving much of Leninism's political theory, institutions, victims and historical violence conveniently outside the frame.

That possibility becomes particularly revealing when contrasted with political reactions to Nazi comparison.

> **One historical enemy is available as a costume. Another remains an accusation.**

The asymmetry may arise partly because American reactionary anti-communism has often invested enormous emotional power in the **vocabulary** of communism, while Nazism remains attached to a historical repertoire of **actions** from which American political actors urgently wish to distinguish themselves.

The distinction is intensified by American national mythology.

The United States can remember:

> communism as the enemy it contained;

and:

> Nazism as the enemy it defeated.

More difficult is the history demonstrating that racial hierarchy, political violence and coercive state institutions are not inherently foreign phenomena.

More difficult still is recognising that different Americans have never encountered the state from the same position.

For a socially dominant political subject, the state can remain something to smash.

For someone repeatedly encountering its coercive boundaries, the state is already something that acts.

That difference changes the meaning of revolutionary fantasy.

It also changes the meaning of counterculture.

Counterculture does not consist simply in violating yesterday's taboos. Its political significance depends upon **what illusion it breaks and what relationship it bears to contemporary power**.

The resulting reactionary archetype wants to be simultaneously:

> **inside power and outside respectability;  
> elite strategist and insurgent;  
> historically consequential and countercultural;  
> Kissinger and Lenin;  
> establishment beneficiary and punk.**

That self-conception creates its own vulnerability.

The most disruptive historical comparison may therefore not be the one that demonstrates that the actor is **extreme**.

Extremity can be incorporated into the performance.

The more dangerous comparison is the one that asks:

> **What if you are not the rebel in this story?**

Hence:

**Kissinger's little Leninist.**
