## 🛰️ Orientation — Apparently, We Have to Talk About Logan Paul

Somehow, a conversation about an AfD politician trying to insert himself into a memorial at Halle synagogue has brought us to a forest at the foot of Mount Fuji.

I would like to stress that this was not the original itinerary.

The original question concerned an extraordinarily basic social skill: **at what point does another person's humanity become sufficiently real to interrupt what you want to do?** A memorial isn't an empty stage waiting for your political performance. The people whose histories and losses it holds are not decorative scenery. Their objections aren't merely inconvenient feedback from the audience.

The particular Halle incident that prompted this discussion needs a properly sourced reconstruction before publication: who attended or attempted to attend, what organisers or community representatives said, when, and what actually happened. It would be dishonest to fill in those particulars from a half-remembered news item. The comparison here is about a *question*, not an assertion that the politician and Logan Paul committed identical wrongs.

And then we remembered that video.

At the end of 2017, American YouTuber Logan Paul published footage filmed in Japan's Aokigahara forest after encountering a deceased person. The incident became an international scandal. There were apologies, platform sanctions, public debate about suicide reporting, and a great deal of attention devoted to the man who had uploaded the footage.

What received rather less international attention was the forest.

Not the forest as a horror destination. Not the forest as a search term. Not the forest as an atmospheric backdrop for somebody else's terrible decision. The actual forest: its geology, ecology, cultural relationships, local communities, visitors, people experiencing distress, and the people working to protect life there.

And that omission is the beginning of our problem.

This node belongs to **Embodied Information Ecology** because the same place can generate very different information for different participants. A forest is not merely a photograph, a rumour, a ghost story, a government policy, a tourist itinerary or a YouTube thumbnail. Those are different *representations* of a place that exists independently of the representation.

Some representations can help people understand it. Others make a small, saleable fragment stand in for the whole thing.

Our working proposition is that the Aokigahara scandal illustrates several interacting failures: a failure to recognise another person's dignity; an attention economy that can reward transgression; a cultural information environment that privileges the sensational; and models of masculinity and heritage that teach some men to collect the *accessories* of meaning while neglecting the relationships that make meaning possible.

This is not an argument that men are uniquely bad, that Japanese people are uniquely good, that every ghost story is exploitative, or that all historical cultural loss was caused by one political regime. Those would be remarkably convenient simplifications for a node specifically objecting to convenient simplifications.

It is an invitation to look properly.

**Recognising that another person, place or living system has relationships independent of you ought to change how you behave towards it.**

## 🌋 1. Mount Fuji Has Been Doing Landscaping

Aokigahara, 青木ヶ原, is also known as *Jukai*, 樹海: the **Sea of Trees**. It lies on the north-western flank of Mount Fuji, principally in Yamanashi Prefecture. The forest developed over lava flows associated with the mountain's major **864 CE Jōgan eruption**. Before it acquired its international reputation, it was already the outcome of geological time, ecological succession and an enormous quantity of biological labour.

A volcano erupts. Lava cools. Rock fractures. Organisms establish themselves. Organic matter accumulates. Roots work into available spaces. Plants, fungi, microorganisms and animals participate in the gradual making of a forest. That process is not quick and it is not arranged for the convenience of a visiting human with a camera.

The lava substrate contributes to distinctive topography, vegetation and caves. It is tempting to describe the forest as a supernatural acoustic chamber in which compasses stop working and visitors are helplessly swallowed by the trees. Those claims have circulated widely, but the more sensational versions need independent verification rather than repetition. Volcanic rock is a geological fact; the internet's insistence that every compass becomes possessed is another matter.

Mount Fuji is also a mountain with a long and complex religious history, involving pilgrimage, mountain worship and overlapping Shinto and Buddhist traditions. That wider sacred geography matters, but it does not authorise us to declare every square metre of Aokigahara the site of a particular named *kami* or ritual. We need local evidence for local claims.

This is the first rule of the expedition: **the forest existed before our preferred explanation of it.**

*Mount Fuji has been doing landscaping for considerably longer than YouTube has been offering monetisation.*

### The ecological research we actually need

A published version should draw on Japanese natural-history and conservation material to establish documented species, rather than borrowing generic woodland fauna because it improves the prose. The forest supports trees, mosses, ferns, fungi, birds, insects and other organisms whose identities and relationships deserve specificity. Its volcanic terrain shapes those relationships. Ecological processes continue regardless of which international film has most recently used the place as a backdrop.

I would very much like to tell the boys that there are flying squirrels. But we are going to check which species occur locally before recruiting one into the argument.

The point is not that the forest is a pristine paradise without danger, death or disturbance. Death is part of ecology. So are decomposition, competition, predation and change. The point is that **a living forest cannot be reduced to the human deaths with which it has become internationally associated**.

## 🌳 2. There Are Butterflies Here, Bro

An animal moving through a forest does not experience the place as a tourist brochure. A bird may respond to canopy structure, sound and food sources. An insect encounters plants through sensory capacities radically unlike ours. Fungi interact with organic material and other organisms through biochemical processes that do not require a human spectator. The forest's inhabitants are not waiting for us to confer meaning on their lives.

This is an ordinary ecological observation with unusually useful philosophical consequences.

**Information is experienced by a system with particular capacities, histories and relationships.** The same physical environment affords different possibilities to different organisms. Even among humans, the forest is not the same informational environment for a local guide, a conservation worker, a child on a school trip, a religious practitioner, a distressed visitor, a bereaved relative or a YouTuber searching for a striking video.

Those perspectives are not all interchangeable. A person can misrepresent a place. A platform can amplify that misrepresentation. An audience can learn to recognise the distortion faster than the reality.

We should therefore introduce the forest through living processes and return to them throughout the node: trees growing, moss retaining moisture, birds moving through the canopy, insects going about their own business. The reader should repeatedly be reminded that an enormous amount of life has continued while the international internet argues about the forest's reputation.

*Bro. There is an entire ecosystem here. Please develop an interest in something other than the thumbnail.*

## 👻 3. Unfortunately, There Are Also Ghosts

Japanese folklore includes **yūrei** (幽霊), commonly understood as spirits of deceased humans; **yōkai** (妖怪), a broad and varied grouping of supernatural beings and phenomena; and **kami** (神), sacred presences or powers associated with Shinto traditions, which do not map neatly onto the English words *ghost*, *monster* or *god*. These are not three interchangeable species in an international supernatural zoo.

Aokigahara is associated in popular storytelling with spirits of the dead, and accounts of **ubasute**—the abandonment of elderly people—are sometimes attached to the forest. The existence of a story is not proof that the events described occurred there. We must not turn an evocative legend into a historical assertion simply because it would make the opening more dramatic.

Nor should we treat every Japanese ghost story as evidence of one uniform Japanese religious worldview. Folklore changes across regions, periods, tellers and media. Stories can entertain, frighten, mourn, warn, remember and provoke. They can also be commodified. A person telling a ghost story in a local cultural context is not automatically doing the same thing as an international production company using a Japanese forest as an exotic horror location.

Most importantly, **stories about the dead may preserve relationships with the dead**. Grief, remembrance, obligation, anxiety and respect can all be involved. A ghost is not necessarily an invitation to go looking for a fright. It may be part of a much more complicated cultural conversation about what the living owe to people who have died.

That does not mean we can romanticise death in Aokigahara. The people who died there were not born to become a legend. They had lives, relationships and histories that cannot be recovered from a horror premise.

*Even the ghosts were people before somebody decided they were content.*

That line is an ethical provocation, not a technical definition of every Japanese supernatural category.

## ⛩️ 4. Excuse Me, Where Are the Kami?

Here is the question that made this node considerably more interesting than a straightforward critique of Logan Paul.

In the English-language material we encountered, *yūrei* appeared readily. Ghosts, hauntings, restless dead, the allegedly cursed forest. Yet the wider religious geography of Mount Fuji, the variety of Japanese relationships with sacred places, and the vocabulary of *kami* were comparatively difficult to find.

**Does that tell us which spiritual traditions matter locally? Or does it tell us which Japanese supernatural concepts Anglophone media knows how to sell?**

A ghost is legible to an English-speaking horror audience without much explanation. *Kami* are harder to compress into the same genre conventions. They are not simply benevolent woodland fairies, and Shinto is not an environmental self-help programme. But they open up questions about place, sacred presence, ritual, obligation and relationships between human and non-human worlds that the haunted-forest template can leave out.

This is a hypothesis about **selective representation**, not a demonstrated finding that Anglophone media systematically erased particular Aokigahara *kami*. To test it we would need a defined comparison of Japanese-language and English-language sources, and locally grounded research into shrines, pilgrimage routes, religious sites and practices. The sacred geography of Mount Fuji cannot be substituted for evidence of a specific forest tradition.

There is a further complication: Japanese horror and ghost-story industries exist in their own right. It would be absurd to pretend sensationalism was imported wholesale from the West. The question is about *selection and amplification*: what travels internationally, how it is framed, and what gets left behind.

The difference matters because a place presented exclusively as haunted invites a particular kind of visitor. A place presented through its ecological, religious and community relationships may invite different expectations and different behaviour.

Not necessarily better behaviour in every individual case. But the information environment changes what a visitor thinks they are arriving at.

**The absence of Totoro doesn't tell us what's missing from the forest. It tells us something about what we're looking for.**

## 🌱 5. Bro, Where Is Totoro?

This is our translation layer for the nerdy boys.

They have probably encountered at least some of these ideas already, without having to study a textbook on Japanese religion. They may simply have met them in a Studio Ghibli film.

### My Neighbor Totoro — the forest has neighbours

*My Neighbor Totoro* imagines a forest inhabited by extraordinary beings whose existence is not reducible to a human child's immediate purposes. Totoro is not an Aokigahara-specific spirit, and we should not present him as one. But the film offers a useful imaginative possibility: a forest may be wonderful because other beings exist there, not frightening merely because they do.

You don't need to claim that Totoro is a documentary account of Shinto to understand the relational point. The children encounter a world that is already inhabited.

### Princess Mononoke — the forest has interests

*Princess Mononoke* is more openly concerned with competing human needs, industrial activity, ecological damage, violence and powerful non-human beings. The forest's gods and spirits are not necessarily safe, agreeable or available to perform a comforting lesson for a visitor.

Respect does not mean expecting the world to be nice to you. It means recognising that you are not its only participant.

### Nausicaä — the environment was not waiting for your explanation

*Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind* presents an ecosystem that people misunderstand, fear and attempt to control. Its creatures and ecological processes should not be flattened into Shinto *kami*. The connection is epistemic: the environment makes more sense when someone investigates the relationships within it rather than accepting the most convenient frightening story.

**The system wasn't necessarily the problem. Your model of the system was incomplete.**

Hello, Embodied Information Ecology.

### Spirited Away — the spirits have an entire society

*Spirited Away* gives us labour, hierarchy, pollution, commerce, identity and reciprocity in a fantastical spirit world. The river-spirit sequence is especially useful: what appears to be an unpleasant being is revealed differently once the accumulated pollution is removed.

The film isn't a straightforward representation of ordinary urban Japanese religious practice. It is a story that allows viewers to think about recognition, care and the damage produced by treating beings and environments as disposable.

The important thing is not to claim that these four films express one uniform doctrine. They don't. They offer different imaginative routes into a shared question: **what happens when humans stop treating the world as though it exists exclusively for their consumption?**

And now we have a small problem for the boys.

*You've watched Princess Mononoke. You already know better than to turn up in somebody else's forest acting like the main character.*

## 🧶 6. The Things Your Mam Always Did

The next connection came from thinking about inherited family practices and regional traditions closer to home.

There are ways of relating to animals, plants, particular places, seasons, death and memory that can feel completely ordinary when you grow up with them. They may become conspicuous only when somebody else notices that you do things differently.

Britain is not culturally uniform. Neither is Europe. Regional, family and community traditions vary; they overlap, change and sometimes contradict each other. Nor are British folk practices simply Shinto under another name. Their histories, institutions and religious contexts are different.

But the comparison helps us understand something important: **a relationship with a place can be culturally inherited without the person maintaining it describing themselves as religious**.

A person might be thoroughly secular, scientifically literate and perfectly capable of explaining that a particular superstition is unlikely to alter the laws of physics. They may nevertheless repeat the practice.

Why?

*Because that's what my mam did.*

Perhaps it is a custom concerning a plant, a bird, a household threshold, a seasonal date, a death, a wedding or a particular stretch of countryside. Perhaps nobody in the family remembers the original explanation. Perhaps the explanation was always different from what later generations assumed. Perhaps the custom is only two generations old and has acquired an entirely fictitious ancient pedigree.

All of these possibilities are interesting.

Cultural information can survive as habit, gesture, etiquette, timing, language, song or story even when its original justification is forgotten or rejected. The body and the household become sites of transmission. Knowledge doesn't always arrive labelled **KNOWLEDGE**.

This is not an invitation to assume that every family superstition encodes ancient ecological wisdom. Some do preserve useful observations; some preserve social relationships; some are harmless conventions; some are mistaken; some can be harmful. Each deserves investigation on its own terms.

But the apparently ordinary things people do are often part of the cultural archive.

And that archive can be extremely easy to overlook when you have been taught that important history is mostly what kings and warriors did.

## 🪻 7. The Women Who Remember Things

Here is where patriarchy becomes relevant in a less theatrical and much more consequential way.

Many societies have assigned particular forms of knowledge to women: household practices, caregiving, kinship memory, seasonal customs, oral storytelling, foodways, rites of passage, and the informal maintenance of relationships across generations. The pattern varies considerably across cultures and historical periods. Men have also maintained rich oral traditions through farming, trades, music, religion, migration, military service and community storytelling.

The claim is not that women are naturally mystical or that men have no memory. It is that **the social distribution of knowledge and the social recognition of knowledge are different things**.

Who keeps a practice alive?

Who gets described as an authority?

Who gets written into the history book?

Who is dismissed as merely having funny little habits?

An older woman may know a remarkable amount about the people, places, names, disputes, customs and changes that shaped a community. Yet someone seeking a grand ancestral identity may overlook her entirely, because her knowledge does not resemble the kind of history he has learned to admire.

The warrior ancestor is heroic. The grandmother is quaint.

Except the grandmother may actually know where the family lived, what language somebody spoke, which neighbour helped during the war, why a surname changed, what happened to a local custom, and why nobody talks about a particular year.

**A society can preserve information while teaching some of its members not to recognise its carriers as knowledgeable.**

This is a gendered information problem. Patriarchal status hierarchies affect not just who speaks but what counts as worth listening to.

And there is a loss for boys and men here too. They may be deprived of access to relationships, memories and skills that would make their own lives richer. The answer is not to appoint women as unpaid heritage librarians for men who have never bothered to ask a question. It is to value the people who hold knowledge, learn how to listen, and contribute to its preservation.

*Unfortunately, the radical masculine intervention may involve allowing your auntie to finish a sentence.*

## 🪵 8. You Bought the Runes. Where's the Skeleton?

Some contemporary far-right movements appropriate Norse and Germanic symbols, historical warrior imagery and imagined ancestral identities. A rune becomes a badge. A myth becomes an assertion about blood. A complicated historical culture becomes a set of marketable accessories.

We must be precise: **Norse symbols and interest in pre-Christian European traditions are not inherently extremist**. People study, practise and creatively engage with these histories for many reasons. Specific extremist uses, organisations and meanings need evidence and context. Nor were historical pre-Christian European societies universally harmonious with nature. Their relationships with land, animals, property, sacred places, warfare and power were varied and often contradictory.

That complexity is precisely the point.

A symbol is not the totality of a worldview. Owning it does not mean understanding how the people associated with it actually lived.

You can buy the helmet.

You can buy the runes.

You can buy the historically questionable wolf pendant.

You can announce that you have discovered your ancestral warrior spirit.

**But where's the bloody skeleton, bro?**

Where is the knowledge? The reciprocity? The practical skill? The understanding of community? The relationships with actual landscapes and actual people? Where is the willingness to discover that your ancestors weren't all warriors, weren't all admirable, and didn't all believe the same things?

The accessories are easy to sell because they are visible and portable. The skeleton takes time to develop. It consists of obligations, practices, memories, capacities and relationships. It is what keeps the thing upright.

There is an especially nasty contradiction when authoritarian movements claim to restore cultural heritage while borrowing from political traditions that have persecuted the people who carry cultural knowledge. But to understand that properly, we need to stop talking about one regime and look at the larger historical landscape.

## 🧶 9. Have You Asked Your Nan, Bro?

Suppose what you actually want is continuity, belonging and an interesting connection to the past.

Excellent. Those are reasonable things to want.

Before purchasing an elaborate reconstruction of your alleged ancestral identity from a man with a suspiciously specific interpretation of Viking masculinity, consider investigating the people and places already connected to your life.

Ask an older relative what their parents or grandparents did. Ask about songs, local sayings, family names, places people visited or avoided, customs surrounding births and deaths, and the things people did every year without really explaining why. Ask what changed when the family moved. Ask who taught whom. Ask which stories have different versions.

And listen to the answer even when it isn't spectacular.

If family contact is unavailable, unsafe or impossible, the expedition does not end. Local history societies, libraries, oral-history archives, museums, community groups, regional folklore collections, environmental organisations, walking groups and public records all offer other routes. You needn't possess a tidy ancestry or a happy family to be curious about the history of a place.

There is a useful distinction between **inheritance** and **ownership**. You can learn about a tradition without claiming it exclusively. You can respect a place without declaring yourself its authentic representative. You can investigate a family history without pretending the findings will all be flattering.

And the research will almost certainly involve discovering that people moved, mixed, borrowed, adapted and argued. Living traditions are not sealed containers of ethnic purity. They are relationships maintained through time.

*Your nan may know considerably more about the local supernatural administration than the bloke selling runes online.*

But sometimes she won't know. And that matters too.

## 🕯️ 10. What Happened to Everybody's Grandparents?

This is the part where the joke becomes a little sadder.

What if the heritage somebody is looking for is not absent because their ancestors lacked culture, or because a conveniently identifiable enemy stole it last Tuesday? What if parts of it were lost because the people who could have transmitted it lived through decades in which survival took precedence over continuity?

The early and middle twentieth century brought world wars, imperial violence, genocide, forced population movements, authoritarian repression, economic depression, mass bereavement, changing borders and extensive transformations in work and domestic life. These events did not affect everybody equally, and they did not begin or end neatly in 1914 or 1945. In many places colonial violence predated the world wars; in others, decolonisation, partition, dictatorship and conflict extended the disruption well beyond them.

Yet the cumulative pressure on cultural transmission was enormous.

Stories require tellers and listeners. Languages require communities of use. Customs require occasions on which they can be practised. Relationships with places require some degree of access to those places. Family knowledge requires people who survive long enough, live close enough, feel safe enough, and have enough time to pass it on.

War, persecution, poverty, displacement and exhaustion can interrupt every one of those conditions.

### 🔥 Deliberate destruction

Some regimes and institutions deliberately targeted cultures: through genocide, religious persecution, forced assimilation, language suppression, child removal, destruction of institutions and restrictions on communal life. Nazi genocide and persecution are central European examples, but this history cannot be reduced to Nazism or Europe. Colonial states, imperial projects and different authoritarian systems across the world also inflicted severe cultural destruction, using different ideologies, instruments and objectives.

Specific histories matter. We should not flatten the Holocaust, colonial child removal, Soviet repression, wartime expulsions and other forms of persecution into one interchangeable category. The common question is how coercive power attacks the people and institutions through which culture survives.

### 🧳 Migration and protective assimilation

Sometimes people moved because they wanted opportunity. Sometimes they fled danger. Sometimes they were expelled. Sometimes the categories overlapped.

And sometimes they concealed or abandoned visible markers of heritage because doing so improved their chances of survival.

A surname changed. A language stopped being spoken at home. A religious practice became private. A child was told to fit in. A story became too dangerous, embarrassing or painful to repeat.

**Sometimes what looks like an absence of heritage is the inheritance of somebody else's survival strategy.**

This should not be turned into a romantic claim that every forgotten custom conceals a persecution story. But protective concealment is a real mechanism of cultural change, and descendants may not know it occurred.

### 🪫 The world was running on fumes

Even families not deliberately targeted for cultural erasure could experience devastating disruption. War deaths, injuries, grief, economic insecurity, rationing, overwork, separation and rebuilding consumed attention and resources. Some communities experienced revival and reinvention amid these upheavals; others lost the conditions required to sustain particular practices.

The author's image is of much of the world running on fumes for forty or fifty years. That is an image of cumulative exhaustion, not a claim that every population experienced the same timeline or severity.

A grandparent loses the elder who knew the stories. A parent moves for work. A child learns a different language. A family decides that survival and stability matter more than preserving a custom nobody has the energy to explain. A descendant looks backwards and sees an empty space.

Not because nothing was ever there.

Because **traditions get lost**.

### 🪵 “But I'm white.” And?

Whiteness is not a coherent ancestral culture, and it tells you remarkably little about what particular people in your family experienced. Being socially classified as white may have conferred advantages and protection in one context without making a family immune to war, displacement, poverty, bereavement or cultural change.

These experiences are not equivalent to racial persecution or genocide. We don't need to pretend they are in order to acknowledge loss.

A family on a victorious side of a war could still have lost relatives, homes, communities and ways of life. A person in a relatively safe country could still inherit silences produced by migration, class mobility, religious conflict or trauma. The question isn't whether everybody suffered in the same way. They didn't.

The question is **what actually happened to the people before you**.

That is a better question than allowing a political movement to tell you that your cultural disconnection proves some other group has stolen your rightful place.

### 🧶 What survived anyway?

Perhaps a phrase. A recipe. A local name. A superstition. A tune. A habit somebody performs every year without remembering why. Perhaps nothing identifiable. Perhaps the family created new traditions in response to loss.

Not every fragment is ancient. Not every reconstruction is possible. Nobody owes the past a flawless restoration.

But a small survival can be meaningful, and an absence can have a history.

The point is not to demand that everybody recover an authentic lost culture. The point is to make curiosity possible without offering false certainty as a substitute.

## 🔥 11. Who Burned the Family Stories?

There is a further historical irony that we should examine without turning it into a universal law.

Political movements that promise to defend ancestral heritage have sometimes participated in the destruction, persecution or silencing of the people who actually maintained cultural traditions. They may also selectively appropriate folklore, reconstruct a usable mythic past, and elevate symbols that support their political programme while suppressing traditions that complicate it.

Two processes can occur together:

**Destruction of living cultural relationships.** People are killed, displaced, assimilated, frightened into silence or separated from institutions and places.

**Manufacture of an authorised cultural identity.** Selected symbols and stories are promoted as the authentic national inheritance, while inconvenient complexity is erased.

The Nazis' appropriation and ideological reconstruction of elements of Germanic history and folklore is one well-known example of the second process; their persecution and destruction of Jewish cultural life, among many other targeted lives and institutions, is a devastating example of the first. Other states and movements have followed different paths to related outcomes. We need specific historical cases rather than one catch-all allegation.

The contradiction is sharp: a modern extremist can be sold a simplified ancestral identity by an ideological tradition whose predecessors helped damage the networks through which actual cultural identities were transmitted.

Not every disconnected young man has that family history. Not every far-right movement has the same genealogy. But **the longing for belonging can be real while the explanation being sold for that longing is dishonest**.

This is why the question isn't merely *which symbols do you wear?* It is *which people do you recognise as belonging to the history?*

Do the women count? The migrants? The religious minorities? The neighbours? The people whose language changed? The people who married across boundaries? The people who were persecuted? The people who resisted persecution? The people whose lives don't fit the heroic nationalist picture?

If your model of heritage requires you to erase most of the people who made it, you may not be preserving very much heritage at all.

*You've bought the runes. Have you considered investigating who survived to tell you what they meant?*

## 📚 12. The Forest Acquires a Literary Career

Back to Japan, where the forest has unfortunately acquired an international reputation that sometimes seems to have swallowed everything else about it.

Seichō Matsumoto's 1961 novel *Nami no Tō* (*Tower of Waves*) is frequently cited in accounts of the modern association between Aokigahara and suicide. We should investigate the novel itself, its reception, and the evidence for its influence. We should not claim that one novel invented the association, nor treat later English-language repetitions of that claim as independent confirmation.

The larger question is how literature and media shape expectations of a real place. A story can draw on an existing reputation, amplify it, transform it and send readers looking for the landscape described. Later storytellers then encounter a place whose reputation has already been shaped by earlier representations.

A forest acquires a literary career. The career develops a life of its own. The forest continues to be a forest.

This distinction is central: **a representation can become socially consequential without becoming a complete description of its subject**.

## 🎬 13. Everybody Has Something to Say About the Forest

Aokigahara appears in international films, music, poetry, theatre and other creative work. *The Sea of Trees* (2015), *The Forest* (2016), Jhené Aiko's *Jukai*, the bilingual poetry anthology *Sea of Trees: Poetic Gateways to Aokigahara* (2019), and other works offer very different engagements with the place.

These should not be lumped together as exploitation. A work about grief may approach human suffering carefully; a horror film may draw on particular conventions; a poetry project may seek to open a landscape to multiple voices and meanings. We need to examine the actual work, its creators, its audience and its relationship to Japanese sources.

The bilingual poetry project is especially promising because it offers an opportunity to encounter more than a sensationalised Anglophone shorthand. Research should prioritise the poets' own words and the conditions under which the work was made.

We should also look at Japanese representations rather than making an English-language list stand in for the forest's cultural history.

The relevant questions are not *may art depict death?* or *may outsiders depict Japan?* Those are too crude.

Better questions: Whose perspectives are included? What is left out? What relationship does the work invite the audience to have with the place? What does it do with the dignity of people who died? Does it encourage understanding, fear, curiosity, imitation, contempt or care? And how do we know?

## 👻 14. Who Gets to Haunt the English-Language Forest?

We can now formulate the cultural-information hypothesis more rigorously.

**The international image of Aokigahara may be shaped by a selection process that disproportionately rewards familiar horror and suicide narratives over ecological, local, historical and religious complexity.**

To test that claim, compare a defined set of Japanese-language and English-language sources across similar time periods and media types. Code for mentions of suicide, *yūrei*, *kami*, mountain worship, local folklore, ecology, conservation, ordinary tourism, prevention, residents and sensational framing. Distinguish Japanese media's own horror traditions from Anglophone reinterpretations. Check whether translation choices collapse distinct concepts into *ghosts* or *spirits*.

We should also examine search engines, travel writing, film marketing, social-media recommendation and platform incentives. An audience looking for a haunted forest is likely to click on a haunted-forest story. Publishers anticipate demand. Creators produce more of the same. The repeated framing becomes familiar, and familiarity can begin to masquerade as completeness.

```text
A complex landscape with many relationships
                    ↓
Selective attention and translation
                    ↓
Recognisable, marketable narrative
                    ↓
Audience expectation and search demand
                    ↓
More content built around that expectation
                    ↓
A narrower international reputation
                    ↺
```

This is a plausible feedback loop, not yet a measured causal model for Aokigahara. It needs evidence.

It also connects with colonial and extractive ways of seeing, without requiring us to call every tourist, horror story or commercial transaction colonial. In extractive systems, the value of a place is often defined by what can be taken from it: timber, land, labour, spectacle, imagery, attention. Cultural commodification can work similarly when a few portable symbols are detached from the relationships that give them meaning.

But cultural exchange is not inherently extraction. Tourism can support conservation and local livelihoods. Artistic engagement can be respectful. Japanese people themselves create horror stories. The issue is the structure of attention and the obligations that are lost when an external audience's preferred story becomes dominant.

**We might be a significant part of the problem.**

Not because Anglophones are uniquely incapable of understanding Japanese traditions. Because powerful English-language information markets can repeatedly centre the elements that are easiest to recognise, package and sell.

And then people arrive expecting the thing they've been sold.

## 🪻 15. Some People Arrive Carrying More Than a Backpack

Aokigahara has become associated with suicide. That fact cannot responsibly be omitted. But it can be discussed without converting human suffering into an aesthetic.

People experiencing suicidal distress are not characters in a ghost story. Their lives are not reducible to a location. The circumstances of a crisis are often complex and cannot be inferred from where somebody was found or what an outsider imagines the landscape meant to them.

Responsible discussion avoids graphic details, romanticised descriptions of a location as an ideal place to die, speculation about individual motives and sensational numerical claims. Research on suicide-related media effects, including the **Werther effect** and **Papageno effect**, provides reasons to take representation seriously, while the evidence should be explained accurately rather than used as a magical incantation proving that any one article caused any one event.

We should also distinguish different data categories. Deaths recorded in a prefecture are not automatically deaths in the forest. A recovered body is not interchangeable with a confirmed suicide. An intervention is not a death. Historical statistics are not necessarily comparable with contemporary ones.

The person matters more than the number.

*Somebody's worst day is not the most important thing there ever was about them.*

That is the ethical starting point for everything that follows.

## 🕯️ 16. The People Who Never Came Home

There are people who died in Aokigahara. We do not know their stories simply because we know where they died.

They had relationships, histories, preferences, ordinary days, moments of kindness, arguments, hopes, disappointments, and people who may still miss them. Their identities are not a mystery prize for an audience to uncover. Their deaths are not a visual asset that becomes acceptable to publish merely because a face has been blurred.

This is where the node's central question becomes unavoidable.

**What does recognising another person's humanity require when that person cannot object?**

It requires more than avoiding a technically identifiable face. Dignity can be violated through context, spectacle, editing, framing and the conversion of another person's death into a performance. The inability to consent is not an opportunity. It is a reason for restraint.

And restraint is not a particularly exotic moral technology. It is something we expect people to exercise in ordinary relationships every day.

---

## 🗾 17. The Forest Is Somebody's Local Landscape

For people in and around Yamanashi, Aokigahara is not exclusively an international horror setting. It is a real landscape used and understood through ordinary life, tourism, environmental education, conservation and public services.

Local guides, residents, school groups, researchers, hikers and officials have relationships with the place that a foreign visitor may never encounter through an English-language search result. Some may dislike its reputation; others may have different concerns or more complicated views. We should not invent a unified local voice. We should find and attribute actual local perspectives.

Nor should we assume that reducing all tourism would automatically benefit the forest. Responsible visitation may support local livelihoods, environmental education and conservation. The questions are about conduct, governance, impacts and local priorities.

The forest is not waiting for outsiders to decide what it means.

**It is somebody's local landscape.**

---

## 🚨 18. The People Who Keep Looking

The forest's association with suicide has consequences for people who work in prevention, outreach, emergency response and recovery. Local institutions have developed initiatives addressing distress, visitor safety and the forest's reputation. The precise division of responsibilities and current operational practices must be established from Japanese official and local sources.

We should investigate Yamanashi's suicide-prevention networks, gatekeeper initiatives, walking and ecotourism programmes, public communications, and any documented use of technology such as thermal imaging or drones. A press release about a pilot or demonstration is not evidence that a programme has been deployed permanently or proven effective.

Responders and prevention workers should not be reduced to background heroes in an outsider's story either. Their actual work, training, institutional constraints and perspectives matter. We should not invent personal trauma or heroic speeches for people who have not supplied them.

The important point is straightforward: **some people are doing difficult, practical work to protect human lives while international media repeatedly sells the forest as a place of death**.

Those are radically different relationships with the same landscape.

## 🌱 19. This Forest Is Full of Life and Light

Yamanashi Prefecture's tourism material describes the forest in striking terms:

> この森は「命」と「光」に満ちている。

**This forest is full of life and light.**

The statement is not a denial that people have died there. It is a refusal to allow those deaths to become the whole identity of the place.

The prefecture has published materials concerning tourism, nature interpretation, conservation and suicide prevention. Some initiatives seek to change how the forest is represented and experienced, alongside direct efforts to protect people in distress. Their effectiveness requires separate evaluation: policy intent is not the same thing as measured outcome.

Still, the framing matters. The forest is not merely a problem to be hidden from visitors. It is a place with life, ecological value and cultural meaning that deserves more than its most sensational international association.

There is an information-environment intervention here. Change the representation, and you may change what visitors expect, what they seek, and how they behave. Whether particular interventions achieve that is a research question. But the mechanism is worth examining.

The forest is full of life and light.

And now, unfortunately, we must discuss the American YouTuber.

## 🎥 20. The Tokyo Adventures

Logan Paul travelled to Japan in late 2017 as an established online personality whose content relied heavily on performance, reaction and attention. Reporting about the wider trip documented other disruptive conduct in public spaces. The exact incidents, footage and dates should be reconstructed from contemporaneous sources rather than embellished through hindsight.

The wider context matters because it raises a question about treating unfamiliar people and places as raw material for a creator's performance. A street, a market, a stranger, a cultural practice: all can be converted into content if the creator assumes that filming is sufficient justification for the interaction.

But we should not pretend to know Paul's private motives or the thoughts of every companion. We can analyse the visible conduct, the decisions documented and the social incentives surrounding them.

Nor does being American explain the behaviour. That would be an impressively lazy cultural diagnosis. The relevant questions concern celebrity, platform economics, socialisation, boundaries and accountability.

The forest was not a stage. Japan was not a theme park. Other people were not hired extras.

## 🫀 21. Bro. That Was Somebody.

On **31 December 2017**, Paul published a video filmed in Aokigahara in which he and companions had encountered a deceased person. Reporting at the time described the encounter as unexpected; we should not claim the group set out specifically to find a body without evidence.

But the distinction between an unexpected encounter and a published video is crucial.

There was an encounter. There was filming. There was material retained. There was editing. There was publication. Those are not all the same event, and they did not all happen in the same instant.

The video showed the deceased person with the face obscured. That did not resolve the dignity problem. The person had not consented to become part of an entertainment product, and the circumstances of death did not make them public property.

Some members of the group reportedly expressed discomfort. The exact exchanges should be checked against reliable reporting rather than quoted from memory. But the larger point does not depend on a perfect reconstruction of every reaction: there were opportunities to recognise that the planned activity should stop or change.

This is the moment when moral recognition has to become behaviour.

Not: *I feel bad, therefore I am a good person.*

Not: *I blurred the face, therefore the footage is ethical.*

Not: *My audience expects a video, therefore the world owes me one.*

**Bro. That was somebody.**

Somebody with a life that did not begin when a camera found them. Somebody whose death was not a surprise plot development for a channel. Somebody whose dignity mattered even when they could not demand it.

The basic test is whether that recognition is allowed to interrupt the production.

## 📈 22. Six Million Views and a Severe Lapse in Judgement

The video attracted millions of views rapidly; contemporary accounts reported approximately **6.3 million views within its first day**, a figure to verify against the original reporting before final publication. Public criticism followed. Paul apologised in early January 2018. YouTube subsequently imposed commercial and programming consequences, including changes to preferred advertising arrangements and planned projects. Paul also publicly pledged support for suicide prevention.

These events should be set out chronologically and with precision. A pledge is not proof of disbursement. A platform sanction is not proof of reform. An apology is not the same thing as repair. A return to content production is not evidence that all consequences have ended.

There are at least four distinct questions:

**What happened?** The documented conduct and decisions.

**Who was harmed?** The dignity interests of the deceased and their loved ones, potential impacts on vulnerable audiences, and the broader cultural and community consequences of the representation.

**What accountability followed?** Apologies, platform action, professional consequences and any independently verified remedial steps.

**What would prevent recurrence?** Changes in individual judgement, production practice, platform incentives and social expectations.

It is tempting to make the apology the climax. But that would place the creator at the centre of the story again. The more important issue is why somebody could pass through multiple opportunities to stop and still publish the footage.

And what kind of media environment made that seem like a plausible thing to do.

## 🧠 23. How Do You Raise a Boy Who Thinks This Is Normal?

We are not going to diagnose a person from a video, nor claim that all men have the same socialisation. But we can ask what kinds of social environments reward particular behaviour.

Some models of masculinity attach status to dominance, risk, humiliation, boundary-crossing, emotional detachment and an appearance of invulnerability. In a peer group, the person who goes further may receive more attention. Online, the same performance may receive more views. Commercial reward and social reward can become mutually reinforcing.

A boy can learn that being the centre of attention matters more than noticing how others experience his behaviour. He can learn to treat discomfort as evidence that a joke is working. He can learn that restraint is weakness and that admitting a mistake is a threat to status.

These patterns are not inevitable, universal or biologically predetermined. Many boys and men reject them; many women participate in attention economies and boundary-crossing behaviour. The point is to examine a particular **socially rewarded performance** rather than assign moral essence to a sex.

And notice what it displaces.

Attention. Care. Competence. Reciprocity. Curiosity. The ability to accept correction. The ability to recognise that somebody else's experience matters even when it interferes with yours.

These are not decorative virtues. They are part of the skeleton that holds relationships upright.

---

## 🕸️ 24. The Algorithm Is Not Your Mum

Platforms organise attention. Creators respond to incentives. Audiences reward what they click, watch, share and discuss. Outrage can itself become distribution. A creator who discovers that increasingly extreme behaviour generates attention may face pressure to repeat or escalate it.

```text
Provocation / boundary crossing
             ↓
Attention and engagement
             ↓
Status, revenue or visibility
             ↓
Reinforcement of the behaviour
             ↓
More provocation
             ↺
```

That is a general mechanism, not a complete causal account of Paul's decisions. Platform recommendation systems are complex, and public outrage can also impose costs. We would need evidence to establish the role of particular algorithms in the video's reach.

But an attention economy does not have to intend harm to reward behaviour that causes it.

Nor does a platform incentive remove individual responsibility. YouTube did not force a camera to keep filming. An audience did not force an editor to publish. The system shapes what becomes attractive, while people still make choices within it.

**The algorithm is not your mum.** It is not there to teach you how to behave around another human being. If you outsource your moral education to the engagement graph, you may find the curriculum somewhat lacking.

---

## 🫀 25. Empathy Is Not the Same as Respect

We should separate several capacities that are too often bundled together under the word *empathy*.

**Emotional recognition:** I can tell that somebody is distressed.

**Perspective-taking:** I can imagine something about their experience.

**Moral recognition:** Their interests and dignity matter independently of what I want.

**Behavioural restraint:** I alter what I am doing because their interests and dignity matter.

A person can identify distress and still exploit it. A person can feel sympathy and still fail to act. A person can lack an immediate emotional connection yet behave with scrupulous respect.

That is why a simplistic demand for more empathy is inadequate. We need norms, habits and institutions that make recognition consequential.

**Recognising somebody's humanity has to be allowed to inconvenience you.**

This is the connection to the memorial at Halle. A living community may articulate a boundary and expect it to be respected. A deceased person cannot object, which makes restraint even more important. The situations are materially different; the shared question concerns whether other people's experiences impose limits on one's performance.

And the same logic applies, with appropriate differences, to environments and cultural traditions. A forest is not a human being with human rights. But it is a living system embedded in relationships, and treating it as a blank stage can harm actual people and ecosystems.

Respect is not merely an emotion. It is a way of behaving in a world where other things exist.

---

## 🪞 26. This Doesn't Look Particularly Happy for the Men Either

Restrictive masculine expectations can also harm the people expected to perform them. Emotional suppression, isolation, shame, status anxiety, fear of vulnerability and the constant need to demonstrate independence can make relationships harder to sustain.

We should use good research rather than assuming a single model explains all male distress. In particular, suicidal distress must not be conflated with exploitative conduct: they are distinct phenomena, and people experiencing suicidality must not be made responsible for someone else's media scandal.

The relevant observation is narrower. If a culture rewards boys for not listening, not asking for help, not acknowledging uncertainty and not taking care, it may also deprive them of capacities that make life meaningful.

This is where the family-history thread returns.

A boy may be taught to admire an imaginary warrior while overlooking the grandmother who knows the family story. He may be offered status through domination when he actually wants belonging. He may be sold a symbol of heritage when what he needs is a relationship with people and places.

That is sad. It is also changeable.

Humanising the men does not mean pretending the harm is harmless. It means recognising that preventing harm requires examining the conditions that produce it, not merely enjoying the spectacle of a public apology.

## 🥾 27. Have You Considered Going for a Walk?

Here is the constructive alternative.

You say you want adventure. Excellent. The world is full of it.

Go outside. Learn the names of the trees. Find out which birds live near you. Ask why the river has that name. Read about the people who worked the land. Investigate the old footpaths. Visit the local archive. Ask somebody older what the place used to look like. Learn which plants are native, which were introduced, and what conservation work is happening now.

If you want folklore, investigate the actual stories people tell. If you want spiritual history, study the traditions on their own terms. If you want family history, speak to people who remember things and check the records. If you want to visit another country, learn enough to understand that the place already belongs to a network of lives and obligations.

And if your family history is full of silences, that is not evidence that you have failed to inherit a proper identity. It may reflect migration, grief, assimilation, economic change or ordinary forgetting. **Traditions get lost.** You can investigate what remains without inventing a pure ancestral past.

You don't have to believe in forest spirits. You don't have to become Shinto. You don't need a girlfriend who owns crystals. You don't have to perform a Viking identity. You do not have to make every interesting thing into content.

You can just be curious.

**Exploration is not the same as extraction.**

Curiosity can become attention. Attention can become knowledge. Knowledge can become care. Care can become a relationship with a place and its people.

That is a much more substantial adventure than collecting an exciting visual and leaving everyone else to deal with the consequences.

*And, as a bonus, it is considerably less likely to end with you publishing footage of a deceased person.*

---

## 🌱 28. Apparently, We Have to Fix the Men

No, not by issuing every boy a moss identification kit and demanding he develop a spiritual relationship with a fern. Although I have heard worse educational proposals.

The intervention has to operate at several levels.

Families and communities can value cultural knowledge that has traditionally been dismissed as domestic or trivial. Schools can teach historical inquiry, ecological literacy, consent, boundaries and the difference between attention and authority. Peer groups can stop rewarding humiliation as entertainment. Platforms can examine how their commercial systems reward escalating transgression. Cultural institutions can make real, complicated histories more accessible than the polished myths offered by extremist entrepreneurs.

And men can learn to listen, ask questions, accept correction, participate in communities and develop forms of competence that are not built around domination.

We should not promise that these interventions will eliminate cruelty, extremism or exploitation. Human behaviour has multiple causes, and structural reform requires evidence, resources and accountability. But we can at least stop pretending that the only available response is to wait for another scandal and then assess the quality of the apology.

**The goal is not men who apologise more convincingly. It is fewer people being harmed in the first place.**

And part of that work involves refusing to sell boys a counterfeit heritage in place of actual relationships.

## 🪿 29. Information Is Experienced — The Other People Were There All Along

Return to the forest. Not the thumbnail. The forest.

For a geologist, the volcanic substrate matters. For a botanist, succession and plant communities matter. For a local resident, familiar paths and memories may matter. For a guide, visitor behaviour matters. For a religious practitioner, particular relationships with sacred places may matter. For a prevention worker, the safety of a person in distress matters. For somebody bereaved, the landscape may carry grief. For an international horror audience, it may have become an image assembled from films and ghost stories.

These experiences are not equally authoritative about every question. The visitor does not become an expert on local religious history because they felt frightened. The content creator does not acquire the right to publish a person's death because they happened to encounter it. A commercial representation does not replace the landscape's ecological or social reality.

**Information is experienced, but experience does not confer unlimited ownership over what is experienced.**

The same applies to heritage.

A family custom may be experienced as an ordinary habit by the person practising it, as a superstition by an outsider, as a clue by a historian, or as an embarrassment by a descendant taught to admire more theatrical forms of ancestry. Political movements can amplify one interpretation while suppressing others. Violence and migration can interrupt transmission altogether. The absence of a story in the present does not prove that nobody ever knew it.

And the same applies to people.

A person can become a prop in somebody else's performance when their own experience is not recognised as imposing any obligation. That is the failure linking the memorial question, the forest video, the undervaluation of women's knowledge, and the appropriation of cultural symbols.

Not all of these are equivalent harms. They are different cases illuminating a shared structural concern: **whose relationships become visible, whose knowledge counts, and who is allowed to define what something means?**

The people were there all along.

The problem was what we had learned to look for.

## 🦋 30. The Forest Is Still Alive

We started with a memorial in Germany, wandered into a Japanese forest, met some ghosts, asked where Totoro had gone, discussed Shinto, British regional customs, grandmothers, pseudo-Viking merchandise, world wars, migration, protective assimilation, the loss of family traditions, platform economics and the extraordinary possibility that men might benefit from listening to other people.

An entirely normal afternoon in Polaris.

But the forest was never merely a metaphor for the men.

It is a real place. The trees are real. The ecology is real. The people who live around it are real. The people who have visited in distress are real. The people who died were real. The people working to prevent further deaths are real. The stories and religious traditions associated with the wider region have histories that deserve to be studied, not simply harvested for atmosphere.

And somewhere, presumably, there is an insect getting on with its day, utterly untroubled by whether an American YouTuber has learned anything.

Yamanashi's description deserves the final return:

> この森は「命」と「光」に満ちている。

*This forest is full of life and light.*

You don't have to believe in ghosts. You don't have to believe in *kami*. You don't have to share somebody else's inherited customs. You don't even have to understand every story associated with a place.

But you do have to recognise that **your relationship with it is not the only relationship that exists**.

There were people here before the cameras arrived.

There were stories here before the internet.

There were people trying to protect lives before an international scandal briefly made suicide prevention a fashionable subject for a creator's apology.

And there is still an entire forest here, full of things that have nothing whatsoever to do with him.

*Including, apparently, butterflies.*

**The world is already full of things worth discovering, bro. You don't have to be a dick to any of them.** 🌳

### Editorial evidence labels

**Documented:** Supported by a dated, attributable source.  
**Attributed:** A person's or institution's account, not independently established as universal fact.  
**Folkloric:** A story or belief whose cultural existence is documented, not proof of its literal historical events.  
**Interpretive:** An analytical inference, openly presented as such.  
**Authorial:** A situated reflection, joke or philosophical claim.  
**Open:** A question requiring further investigation.
