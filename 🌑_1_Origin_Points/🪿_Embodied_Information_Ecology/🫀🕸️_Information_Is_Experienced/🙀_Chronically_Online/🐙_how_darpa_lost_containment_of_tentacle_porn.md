## You are allowed not to look

- The internet repeatedly informs users that another rabbit hole exists.
- Awareness of a rabbit hole does not create an obligation to enter it.
- Curiosity is not a summons.
- Cultural literacy does not require direct consumption of every cultural object.
- People can legitimately decide:
  - “I already know enough for my purposes.”
  - “My brain does not need that additional imagery.”
  - “The likely informational gain is not worth the cost.”
  - “I may satisfy my curiosity and then resent losing an hour of my life.”
- Choosing not to look is not necessarily:
  - prudishness;
  - fear;
  - incuriosity;
  - ignorance;
  - missing out.
- It can be informed self-regulation.
- Long-form internet users often learn this through experience.
- Newer or less experienced users may still feel that every reference must be resolved.
- Platforms encourage that feeling because unresolved curiosity produces:
  - clicks;
  - watch time;
  - searches;
  - scrolling;
  - further recommendations;
  - additional data collection.
- Mature internet literacy therefore includes **attention sovereignty**:
  - recognising that something exists;
  - deciding whether it is useful;
  - refusing unnecessary exposure;
  - closing the tab without feeling that something remains unfinished.
- Possible central line:

> The internet will repeatedly inform you that a rabbit hole exists. It does not follow that you must enter it.

- Possible framing:

> You may notice a tentacle curling around the edge of the internet without following it into the abyss.

## Tentacle porn as a policy-literacy test

- Internet-safety policymakers should be able to encounter an awkward cultural object without:
  - panicking;
  - moralising;
  - becoming prurient;
  - pretending it does not exist;
  - treating recognition as endorsement;
  - treating fiction as literal intention.
- A useful test question might be:

> “I have heard about this tentacle-porn thing. Is that actually a thing online?”

- A competent response should be able to:
  - laugh or acknowledge the awkwardness;
  - ask what the questioner already knows;
  - recognise the object or honestly admit unfamiliarity;
  - place it broadly within pornography, anime, manga, fandom and meme culture;
  - explain that direct consumption is unnecessary;
  - distinguish cultural literacy from sexual preference;
  - identify where specialist knowledge is needed;
  - avoid making policy from disgust or embarrassment.
- Not knowing this particular trope does not automatically prove:
  - dishonesty;
  - incompetence;
  - lack of technical knowledge.
- It does reveal the boundaries of somebody’s internet exposure.
- The serious warning sign is:
  - claiming comprehensive internet expertise;
  - refusing to admit cultural blind spots;
  - treating unfamiliar culture as insignificant or depraved;
  - failing to consult people who understand the relevant communities.
- No individual policymaker must possess encyclopaedic internet lore.
- Institutions responsible for internet safety do need sufficient epistemic breadth.
- A team unable to recognise widely circulating sexual and subcultural references may misunderstand:
  - user behaviour;
  - coded language;
  - moderation disputes;
  - community boundaries;
  - the difference between harm and strangeness.
- The test is not merely “do you know the trivia?”

## Cross-cultural metabolism

- Cultural material does not simply move unchanged from one country to another.
- It is:
  - translated;
  - recontextualised;
  - misunderstood;
  - sexualised;
  - parodied;
  - commercialised;
  - detached from provenance;
  - incorporated into new subcultures.
- Tentacle porn can be used to distinguish:
  - **circulation** — people encounter and share cultural material;
  - **translation** — another community creates meaning through its own cultural needs;
  - **adaptation** — the material changes form in a new setting;
  - **assimilation** — the origin disappears and the imported object is treated as naturally belonging to the dominant culture;
  - **appropriation** — elements are adopted under unequal power relations;
  - **extraction** — attention and money leave the originating ecosystem without value or control returning;
  - **suppression** — the originating community is harassed, exposed or regulated until its capacity to create is damaged.
- Not all borrowing is theft.
- Not all circulation is benign.
- The important question is what movement does to the living cultural ecology:
  - Does it enlarge the ecology?
  - Does it sustain creators?
  - Does it preserve provenance?
  - Does it permit reciprocal transformation?
  - Does it extract value?
  - Does it make the originating community less safe?
  - Can that community continue creating?

## Internet infrastructure and military origins

- The internet’s institutional genealogy includes military and research infrastructure.
- Communities later used that infrastructure for purposes its architects did not necessarily anticipate.
- A network does not retain the intentions of its original institutions once populations inhabit it.
- Users feed culture into the system.
- Communities respond to one another.
- Moderation changes the available forms.
- Users adapt to moderation.
- Those adaptations become new culture.
- This is a cybernetic feedback process.
- Avoid a reductive argument that:
  - DARPA directly caused contemporary internet culture;
  - the CIA consciously authorised particular pornography;
  - state institutions maintained unified control over the network’s cultural development.
- The better contrast is between:
  - the institutional genealogy of infrastructure;
  - commercial incentives for growth;
  - emergent community use;
  - later state and corporate attempts to govern the resulting environment.

  - military, state and commercial systems supplied parts of the infrastructure;

## Short thesis bundle

- You are allowed not to look.
- Curiosity is not consent to consume.
- Recognition is not endorsement.
- Fantasy is not a literal behavioural confession.
- Cultural influence is not the same as infrastructural power.
- Borrowing is not always extraction, but circulation is not automatically benign.
- “General internet” often means an unmarked dominant internet.
- Weirdness travels because platforms reward memorability and reaction.
- AI did not invent the internet’s sexual and abusive incentive structures; it scaled them.
- Online-safety competence includes the ability to discuss strange cultural objects without panic.
- Communities make richer culture when outside attention does not arrive as punishment.
- The internet did not have to become this harmful.
- Its incentives can be changed.
