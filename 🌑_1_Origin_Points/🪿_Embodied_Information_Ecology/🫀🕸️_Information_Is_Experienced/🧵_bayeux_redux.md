# 🧵 Bayeux Redux
**First created:** 2026-10-05 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-05  
*What happens when political information stops being a statement, becomes twenty-seven metres of pseudo-medieval cloth, and makes the audience walk through the story.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

At the 2026 Conservative Party Conference in Birmingham, the Conservative Party installed an enormous pseudo-medieval political chronicle in the exhibition hall.

It was called **The Birmingham Tapestry**.

The name immediately tells the audience what sort of object it is pretending to be: a sister to the Bayeux Tapestry, except instead of the Norman conquest it narrates recent British political history through caricature, satire, recognisable public figures, political mishaps, symbolic scenes and deliberately medieval visual grammar.

It is also very, very long.

**Twenty-seven metres. Approximately eighty-eight feet.**

This is not incidental decoration.

The information has been given a body.

A person cannot apprehend twenty-seven metres of narrative simultaneously. To receive the whole thing, the viewer has to move. Their eyes travel across scenes. Their body travels along the installation. Events acquire neighbours. Characters recur. Jokes become landmarks. Other people stop, point, photograph, explain and laugh.

The political message is therefore not simply contained **in** the object.

It is produced through the relationship between:

- object;
- scale;
- body;
- movement;
- memory;
- cultural prior knowledge;
- place;
- other viewers;
- humour;
- institutional context;
- and subsequent digital circulation.

This is an unusually cheerful little case study for a basic principle of **Embodied Information Ecology**:

> **Information is experienced.**

And, inconveniently for anyone hoping to explain the interesting part by saying “AI”, somebody had the idea.

We should probably find out who.

---

## 🧶 1. What The Object Is

The Birmingham Tapestry was displayed at Conservative Party Conference in Birmingham in October 2026.

Contemporary reporting establishes several useful pieces of provenance:

- Conservative conference chairman John Belsey publicly described it before conference as the **Birmingham Tapestry**, a **27-metre-long** display telling what the party framed as the story of the Labour government.
- *The Times* reported that it had been **secretly commissioned by the Conservative Research Department**.
- *The Independent* described the conference as being illustrated by the **party's publicity team** through its reworking of the Bayeux Tapestry.
- *The Sun* reported that the tableau was **cloth-printed** and produced using a combination of **human and AI design**.
- Reuters photographed both the object and **people viewing it** in the conference exhibition space on 4 October 2026.

This matters because the word *tapestry* can otherwise obscure the production method.

The Birmingham Tapestry is not, on the evidence currently available, a twenty-seven-metre piece of hand embroidery.

It is a contemporary printed installation adopting the representational form of a much older textile artefact.

That distinction does not make the experiment less interesting.

It arguably makes it more interesting.

The project takes:

```text
MEDIEVAL NARRATIVE TECHNOLOGY
        +
CONTEMPORARY POLITICAL SATIRE
        +
DIGITAL / AI-ASSISTED IMAGE PRODUCTION
        +
LARGE-FORMAT PHYSICAL PRINT
        +
PARTY-CONFERENCE EXHIBITION
        +
CAMERA PHONES
        +
ONLINE RECIRCULATION
```

and turns them into one information object.

🧵 Extremely normal behaviour for a tapestry.

---

## 🫀 2. The Body Is Part Of The Reading Mechanism

A conventional webpage can be scrolled.

A book can be paged.

A video supplies its own temporal sequence.

A twenty-seven-metre installation does something slightly different.

**The viewer supplies the movement.**

The body becomes part of the interface.

To move from one end of the Birmingham Tapestry to the other is to move through its chronology. The object's scale prevents the complete narrative from being comfortably consumed as one visual unit from ordinary viewing distance.

So:

```text
VIEWER MOVES
      ↓
NEW PANEL ENTERS PERCEPTION
      ↓
PREVIOUS PANEL BECOMES MEMORY
      ↓
RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN EVENTS FORMS
      ↓
NARRATIVE ACCUMULATES
```

The audience is not merely *looking at an image*.

The audience is navigating an information environment.

This is one reason scale cannot be treated as an ornamental property added after the information has already been created.

**Scale changes the information-processing task.**

A twenty-seven-centimetre version and a twenty-seven-metre version could contain the same pictures and words while producing substantially different experiences.

The content may be reproduced.

The information ecology is not.

---

## 📏 3. Scale Is Information

Twenty-seven metres says something before the viewer has successfully interpreted a single panel.

It says:

> **There is a lot of history here.**

It says:

> **This deserves a chronicle.**

It says:

> **You are going to be here for a minute.**

And, because the object is knowingly ridiculous, it also says:

> **Yes, we really did make the bloody thing this long.**

Scale produces significance.

Museums, monuments, cathedrals, billboards, tiny devotional objects, pocket books and phone screens do not become informationally interchangeable merely because somebody manages to place the same sentence on all of them.

The physical proportions of an information object alter:

- attention;
- duration;
- bodily orientation;
- perceived importance;
- social visibility;
- memory;
- photographability;
- and the kinds of interaction the object invites.

The Birmingham Tapestry does not merely *describe* accumulated political events.

Its length performs accumulation.

---

## 🏺 4. Bayeux Arrives Before The Explanation

The object also exploits something extremely useful:

**the audience does not encounter it empty-headed.**

The Bayeux Tapestry already exists within British cultural memory.

A viewer does not need detailed expertise in eleventh-century embroidery to recognise some combination of:

- medieval chronicle;
- kings;
- armies;
- succession;
- conquest;
- little figures doing consequential things;
- horses;
- banners;
- labels;
- violence;
- history unfolding sideways.

That prior knowledge becomes part of the new object's usable information infrastructure.

The Conservative Party does not need to invent an entirely new visual language and then teach conference attendees how to decode it.

It can borrow one.

```text
EXISTING CULTURAL MODEL
        ↓
BAYEUX TAPESTRY
        ↓
RECOGNISABLE FORM
        ↓
CONTEMPORARY CHARACTERS INSERTED
        ↓
AUDIENCE RAPIDLY INFERS THE GAME
```

This is informational compression through shared culture.

The reference does enormous amounts of work before any particular political claim is evaluated.

It tells the viewer:

> *Read this as history.*

But also:

> *Read this as a joke about history.*

And:

> *Recognise these contemporary people as though some extremely committed medieval embroiderer has been forced to document British politics.*

Excellent.

## 🪡 5. The Same Observation Does Not Produce The Same Information

The Birmingham Tapestry is also a useful reminder that the information produced by an object depends partly on the observer.

Someone with detailed knowledge of the original Bayeux Tapestry may notice compositional jokes that another viewer misses.

Someone who has followed British politics obsessively may immediately recognise a depicted incident.

Someone who vaguely remembers the headline may understand the broad joke.

Someone unfamiliar with the political event may simply perceive:

> little medieval Angela Rayner appears to be vaping.

These are not identical informational states.

The object is the same.

The observers are not.

Each viewer brings:

- prior political knowledge;
- familiarity with public figures;
- knowledge of the Bayeux Tapestry;
- ideological commitments;
- memories of particular news events;
- humour preferences;
- knowledge of British cultural references;
- and information acquired from the people standing beside them.

Therefore:

```text
OBJECT
  +
OBSERVER
  +
CONTEXT
  =
EXPERIENCED INFORMATION
```

Not:

```text
OBJECT
  =
ONE FIXED INFORMATIONAL MEANING
```

The tapestry does not deposit a uniform packet into every passing brain.

It interacts with the models already there.

## 😂 6. Humour Changes The Cost Of Receiving Information

Political chronology is often informationally expensive.

To understand two years of political events conventionally, a person might need to process:

- hundreds of articles;
- speeches;
- interviews;
- polling;
- scandals;
- policy announcements;
- parliamentary events;
- resignations;
- leadership changes;
- and the endless grey paste of British political television.

Or:

**funny medieval bloke.**

Humour does not preserve every detail.

That is not its job.

It can instead operate as a compression and retrieval mechanism.

A caricature can bind:

```text
PERSON
+
EVENT
+
JUDGEMENT
+
EMOTION
+
VISUAL GAG
```

into a single memorable scene.

That compression is politically interested, obviously. Satire selects what to include, what to omit, who becomes ridiculous, what causal story is implied and which interpretation is made emotionally easy.

But that is precisely why the mechanism matters.

Humour changes the energetic and attentional cost of receiving the message.

A person who would not voluntarily read another 4,000 words of political retrospective may quite happily walk along twenty-seven metres of tiny medieval political calamity.

The information has changed shape.

So has the willingness to receive it.

---

## 🏛️ 7. Place Is Part Of The Message

The Birmingham Tapestry was not initially encountered in a neutral gallery.

It was installed at **Conservative Party Conference**.

That location supplies another layer of information.

The surrounding environment contains:

- party members;
- politicians;
- journalists;
- exhibitors;
- political branding;
- speeches;
- conversations;
- existing group identities;
- shared memories;
- disagreement;
- cameras;
- social rituals;
- and an explicit institutional purpose.

The identical cloth displayed somewhere else would not produce an identical event.

Put it in:

- Conservative conference;
- the British Museum;
- a university seminar room;
- a Labour conference;
- a shopping centre;
- a pub;
- or somebody's extremely committed hallway,

and the informational ecology changes.

The physical artefact may remain stable.

Its relationship to the surrounding system does not.

At conference, it is simultaneously:

- satire;
- institutional communication;
- decoration;
- social object;
- political argument;
- entertainment;
- photo opportunity;
- conversation starter;
- and an assertion about the story the party wants its members to inhabit.

**Location is not metadata sitting harmlessly outside the information.**

Location participates in interpretation.

## 👥 8. Other People Are Part Of The Interface

Reuters photographs are particularly useful here because they document something obvious but easy to lose when analysing the image online:

**people stand in front of it.**

They look.

They walk.

They gather.

This creates social information around the designed information.

One person laughs.

Another notices what they laughed at.

Someone points at a panel.

Someone explains the reference.

Someone photographs it.

Someone poses beside it.

Someone sends the photograph to a friend who was never in Birmingham.

The information ecology therefore expands:

```text
TAPESTRY
   ↓
VIEWER
   ↔
OTHER VIEWERS
   ↓
PHOTOGRAPH
   ↓
MESSAGE / POST / ARTICLE
   ↓
REMOTE VIEWERS
   ↓
COMMENTARY
   ↓
NEW CONTEXT
```

The designed artefact is only the beginning of the informational life of the object.

## 📲 9. Twenty-Seven Metres Becomes A Thousand Crops

And then the physical object enters the computational information environment.

This is where the experiment becomes particularly lovely.

The whole joke depends partly on **continuous physical scale**.

But phones and news websites immediately begin breaking that continuity apart.

A person photographs one panel.

A journalist publishes three.

A social-media account crops a character.

A screenshot detaches a scene from its neighbours.

An article chooses the funniest image.

A search engine indexes the resulting fragments.

The twenty-seven-metre object becomes:

```text
ONE PHYSICAL NARRATIVE
        ↓
MANY PHOTOGRAPHS
        ↓
MANY CROPS
        ↓
MANY CAPTIONS
        ↓
MANY PLATFORMS
        ↓
MANY NEW CONTEXTS
```

This is not merely duplication.

Every crop changes the available context.

A panel that originally had:

```text
BEFORE ← [PANEL] → AFTER
```

may circulate online as:

```text
[PANEL]
```

The physical viewer can walk left or right to recover neighbouring information.

The digital viewer may not know that neighbouring information exists.

The same artefact therefore behaves differently across physical and computational environments.

**Media transition changes informational affordances.**

The cloth can be walked.

The photograph can be forwarded.

The crop can become a meme.

The article can explain it.

The search result can detach it.

The archive can later reconnect it.

## 🤖 10. “Human And AI Design” Is Not A Provenance Record

Reporting describes the Birmingham Tapestry as having been produced through **human and AI design**.

Fine.

That tells us something about the production system.

It does not tell us enough about authorship.

At the time of writing, publicly accessible reporting examined for this node identifies:

- the **Conservative Research Department** as commissioner;
- the **Conservative Party / party publicity team** as the institutional creative context;
- and the use of **human and AI design** in producing the printed tableau.

It does **not yet identify the individual human creator or creators responsible for the concept and design**.

That distinction matters.

“AI was used” does not answer:

- Who proposed making a Bayeux-style chronicle?
- Who selected the events?
- Who wrote or devised the jokes?
- Who decided on the narrative sequence?
- Who art-directed the visual system?
- Who prompted, drew, composited, edited or corrected the imagery?
- Who decided what the AI output got wrong?
- Who rejected bad generations?
- Who assembled the final twenty-seven-metre composition?
- Who prepared it for print?
- Which company physically fabricated the cloth installation?
- Who looked at the finished plan and said, **yes, twenty-seven metres, commit to the bit**?

Those are different forms of labour.

Some may have been performed by the same person.

Some may have been collaborative.

Some may have been performed by an external supplier.

**We currently do not know.**

That is not a reason to replace the missing provenance with a machine.

## 🏷️ 11. Credit Is Information

This produces an unexpectedly useful lesson.

**Attribution is not decorative politeness attached to an information object after the interesting work is finished.**

Credit carries information.

A creator credit can tell us:

- where an idea originated;
- whether expertise was internal or commissioned;
- how creative practices move through institutions;
- whether a work was individual or collaborative;
- which production skills were required;
- how human and computational labour were divided;
- which organisations supplied specialist capability;
- and where similar ideas may appear again.

Remove the credit and those relationships become harder to see.

We currently have an odd provenance gradient:

```text
COMMISSIONER
Conservative Research Department
        ↓
INSTITUTIONAL CREATIVE CONTEXT
Conservative Party / publicity team
        ↓
PRODUCTION METHOD
human + AI design / cloth print
        ↓
HUMAN CREATOR(S)
???
        ↓
PHYSICAL FABRICATOR
???
        ↓
PUBLIC EXHIBITION
Conservative Party Conference
        ↓
DOCUMENTATION
named journalists + named photographers
```

The people documenting the object can be easier to identify than the people who designed it.

That is itself an information-ecology problem.

The missing name is information.

## 🧠 12. AI Can Make Human Labour Less Legible

This is not an argument that AI participation makes the work illegitimate.

It is almost the opposite.

AI can be one component in a human-machine creative system while the most interesting decisions remain recognisably human.

The conceptual leap:

> **What if we make a twenty-seven-metre Tory Bayeux Tapestry?**

is not usefully explained by saying:

> **AI.**

Even where generative systems materially contributed to the images, a useful provenance record should distinguish between:

```text
CONCEPT
ART DIRECTION
SOURCE SELECTION
PROMPTING
GENERATION
ILLUSTRATION
EDITING
COMPOSITING
COPY
NARRATIVE SEQUENCING
PRINT PREPARATION
FABRICATION
INSTALLATION
```

Flatten all of that into:

```text
AI-GENERATED
```

and we lose information about the system that actually produced the object.

The problem is not merely that humans may fail to receive deserved praise.

The analytical problem is that **the causal model becomes worse**.

We understand less about how the artefact came into existence.

## 🪞 13. The Medium Is Also Making An Argument About History

There is another layer.

A chronology is never simply a container for events.

To make a chronology, somebody has to decide:

- where the story begins;
- what counts as an event;
- which events deserve space;
- which people recur;
- what causes appear connected;
- what gets omitted;
- where the jokes land;
- and what ending the sequence moves towards.

The horizontal historical form makes those selections feel sequential.

One thing appears after another.

Characters acquire trajectories.

Events become chapters in a larger story.

This is not unique to the Birmingham Tapestry.

Timelines do it.

Museums do it.

Documentaries do it.

News retrospectives do it.

Archives do it.

**Polaris does it.**

The useful lesson is therefore not:

> *Look at those people turning politics into narrative.*

Everyone turns information into navigable structures.

The useful question is:

> **What does this structure make easier to perceive, remember and believe?**

And then:

> **What becomes harder to perceive because of the same structure?**

The tapestry is unusually visible about the fact that history has been arranged.

You can literally see the arrangement stretching down the wall.

## 🧿 14. Information Is Experienced

The Birmingham Tapestry works as a compact demonstration of the larger principle because none of its major informational effects can be adequately described by extracting only its propositions.

Imagine reducing the entire thing to:

```yaml
format: political satire
commissioner: Conservative Research Department
length_metres: 27
subjects: recent British political events
style_reference: Bayeux Tapestry
production: human_and_ai_design
substrate: printed_cloth
location: Conservative Party Conference 2026
```

Useful.

Machine-readable.

Also catastrophically incomplete as an account of **what the object does**.

The object is experienced through:

```text
BODY
+
SCALE
+
MOVEMENT
+
SPACE
+
CULTURAL MEMORY
+
HUMOUR
+
SOCIAL INTERACTION
+
INSTITUTIONAL SETTING
+
MATERIAL FORM
+
DIGITAL RECIRCULATION
+
OBSERVER-SPECIFIC PRIOR KNOWLEDGE
```

The propositional content is real.

It is simply not the whole information event.

This is the distinction.

> **Information is not only what can be extracted from an object.**

> **Information also emerges through the relationship between the object, the observer and the environment in which the encounter occurs.**

Twenty-seven metres of ridiculous pseudo-medieval British politics makes the point rather efficiently.

## 🧵 15. Bayeux Redux

The original Bayeux Tapestry survived because humans made history materially persistent.

The Birmingham Tapestry is doing something stranger.

It begins with a historical material form.

It passes through contemporary digital production.

It becomes a huge physical object.

Humans encounter it bodily.

Phones turn it back into digital fragments.

Journalists caption those fragments.

Platforms redistribute them.

Search engines index them.

Remote observers reconstruct the whole from pieces.

And somewhere inside that loop, the names of the people who actually designed the thing have — at least for the moment — become less retrievable than the fact that AI was involved.

So:

```text
HISTORY
  ↓
TEXTILE CULTURAL MEMORY
  ↓
DIGITAL / AI-ASSISTED DESIGN
  ↓
PRINTED CLOTH
  ↓
EMBODIED VIEWING
  ↓
PHOTOGRAPHY
  ↓
DIGITAL FRAGMENTATION
  ↓
NETWORKED RECIRCULATION
  ↓
ARCHIVAL RECONSTRUCTION
```

Bayeux redux.

Not because the new object is equivalent to the old one.

Because the informational form has been taken apart, recombined and sent through a completely different ecology.

And somebody deserves a credit for thinking of it.

## 🔎 16. Open Provenance Question

**Status: unresolved as of 2026-10-05.**

### Established

- **Object:** *The Birmingham Tapestry*
- **Display:** Conservative Party Conference, Birmingham, October 2026
- **Length:** approximately 27 metres / 88 feet
- **Commissioning attribution:** Conservative Research Department
- **Institutional creative attribution in reporting:** Conservative Party / party publicity team
- **Production description:** cloth-printed tableau using human and AI design

### Not yet established

- named concept originator;
- named designer / illustrator;
- named art director;
- named AI operator or creative technologist, if separately applicable;
- named production studio or agency;
- named large-format textile / exhibition fabricator.

### Research rule

Do not convert:

```text
NAME NOT YET FOUND
```

into:

```text
NO HUMAN CREATOR
```

or:

```text
AI CREATED IT
```

The correct state is:

```yaml
human_creator_credit:
  status: unresolved
  evidence: "Public reporting reviewed as of 2026-10-05 does not provide a named individual creative credit."
```

If the humans emerge:

**give them the bloody credit.**
