# 🌹 100 Days of Treading Water
**First created:** 2026-10-21 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-21  
*We changed the Prime Minister. Which of the problems that made the change necessary have we actually fixed?*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

One hundred days.

A new Prime Minister. The same questions about appointments, advice, political access, procurement, the handling of intelligence, and the peculiar British tradition of announcing things to journalists before remembering that Parliament exists.

Meanwhile, we are discussing reorganising the Cabinet Office.

Wonderful. Have we tried moving the furniture?

The complaint here is not that *nobody* has done anything. Individuals, parliamentarians, journalists, lawyers and groups have continued doing the slow, irritating, necessary work of asking questions and trying to obtain records. Some progress has come from those efforts. The complaint is about the visible response of the institutions that were supposed to learn from the political crisis: **what has government itself demonstrated that it has corrected?**

This is an author's hundred-day accountability audit, not a criminal verdict. Its premise is the post-Starmer leadership transition associated in political debate with the Epstein disclosures and the controversy over Peter Mandelson's appointment as UK ambassador to the United States. The precise succession chronology and the day-100 endpoint require a sourced chronology before publication as a verified historical account. The same is true of the status of individual proceedings and departmental reforms. The point of writing the questions down is not to pretend we already have all the answers.

The point is that we ought to be able to ask them.

---

## ✨ Key Features

- **Political replacement is not institutional repair.** Removing the officeholder does not resolve the decisions, networks or failures that preceded the change.
- **Disclosure is not explanation.** A document released under parliamentary pressure may leave the original decision-making chain unexplained.
- **An unanswered question is not an answered question.** Nor is a response necessarily a substantive answer.
- **Progress belongs to whoever made it.** Work by MPs, journalists, campaigners or litigants must not automatically be credited to the Cabinet Office.
- **An organisational chart is not an accountability mechanism.** Restructuring must be tested against identified failures, preserved records and measurable correction.
- **A hundred days is a review interval, not a magical deadline for criminal proceedings.** We are asking what can actually be shown to have changed.

## 🌹 The Presenting Complaint: Why Did We Change Prime Minister?

The immediate political issue identified for this retrospective is the decision to appoint Peter Mandelson as ambassador to Washington, against the background of his association with Jeffrey Epstein and subsequent disclosures. This raises questions about what information was available at appointment, who provided advice, who reviewed conflicts and risks, who made the final decision, and what was done when further information emerged.

Those questions concern more than the personal judgment of one Prime Minister. Appointments of this kind pass through processes, offices, people and records. If an appointment is later treated as a serious failure, the public is entitled to ask **which parts of the appointment machinery were examined and changed**.

Nor did the controversy arrive in an otherwise untroubled political ecology. Questions about Labour Together, political influence, appointments and access had already supplied a wider context for scrutiny. The connections between those subjects must be evidenced, not assumed. But the existence of a change in leader is no reason to stop asking about them.

**The resignation is an event. The correction is a separate task.**

### What the chronology must establish

1. The Mandelson appointment, relevant warnings and official vetting or advice.
2. The disclosures that intensified the controversy, and the documented political consequences.
3. The actual date and circumstances of Starmer's departure and the successor's arrival.
4. Which investigations, disclosures and accountability questions were already open at the transition.
5. What, if anything, was formally promised by the incoming administration to correct the underlying processes.

Until those dates and records are checked, this node's hundred-day frame is an **authorial assessment period**, not a verified calculation supplied by this draft.

## 🏛️ The Humble Address: Parliament Asked for the Paperwork

The Humble Address disclosure process is central because it changes the question from *what did the government say happened?* to *what do the records show, and what still needs explaining?*

A document release is useful. It is not self-interpreting. It does not automatically establish who read a warning, how advice was weighed, whether a concern was escalated, or why a particular course was selected.

For each material issue, the audit should preserve a chain:

**Document disclosed → question asked in Parliament → answer given → factual gap remaining → subsequent action.**

Where the answer does not address the question, say so precisely. Where information is withheld for an identified legal reason, record the reason. Where a response is partial, identify which part remains open. Where a parliamentary question has subsequently been answered, update the register rather than preserving a stale screenshot as if it were today's status.

The underlying problem is not that ministers have failed to produce an appropriately solemn facial expression while standing at the despatch box. It is that **the information must eventually permit a decision to be reconstructed and responsibility to be understood**.

## 🗃️ Project Cannon and the Labour Together/APCO Dossier

Project Cannon is not a decorative subplot. It is one of the principal reasons this node cannot be reduced to an essay about Mandelson.

Questions concerning Project Cannon and the Labour Together/APCO dossier engage the movement of information through institutions, the roles of government departments, and the relationship between political networks and official decision-making. They deserve their own documented chronology. Nothing here establishes that Project Cannon and the Mandelson appointment were parts of a single scheme; their shared relevance is the demand for transparent, reconstructable government decisions.

### A concrete parliamentary question

A screenshot retained for this node records **Commons written question 20754**, tabled by **Mike Wood MP** to the **Cabinet Office**, under the subject **ThinkLabour**, on **28 August 2026**, with an answer due **2 September 2026**. The question reads:

> To ask the Minister for the Cabinet Office, to which division of the Cabinet Office did the NCSC pass the Labour Together/APCO dossier to, and whether the Joint Intelligence Organisation received the dossier.

The screenshot displays **“Awaiting answer.”** That is evidence of the status shown when the screenshot was captured, **not independent confirmation of its status on 21 October**. The question's wording also contains a premise about an NCSC transfer; the wording alone does not prove the transfer occurred.

But consider what Parliament is trying to establish. Not an abstract feeling that something was peculiar. A **route**: from an identified body, into a particular division of the Cabinet Office, and potentially onward to the Joint Intelligence Organisation.

This is an information-ecology question in its most literal form. **Where did the information go? Who received it? What happened next?**

The documentary work should compare the parliamentary record, the relevant NCSC and Cabinet Office statements, and any MoD correspondence about Project Cannon. The record previously identified for this work includes an MoD response concerning formal Project Cannon correspondence; the exact wording and limits of that response must be quoted from the source before publication.

If the question remains unanswered, record the elapsed time. If it has been answered, assess the actual answer. Either way, the issue is whether the decision and information pathways have become intelligible.

## 🏛️ FCDO, Senior MoD and the Problem of Distributed Responsibility

The Cabinet Office is not the only institution under examination. The author has also identified unresolved transparency and decision-handling questions involving the **Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office** and **senior Ministry of Defence** structures.

The FCDO questions include the handling of diplomatic appointments, advice, warnings, disclosures and departmental responsibility. The MoD questions concern senior-level authorisation, communication, the handling of relevant information and parliamentary responses, particularly where Project Cannon is concerned.

These should not be mashed into one allegation. Civilian ministers, senior officials and military commanders do not all hold identical powers. Nor does a question about one department automatically establish wrongdoing in another.

But distributed decisions create a recognisable accountability risk. One office can say it advised. Another can say it approved. Another can say it was informed. Another can say the question belongs elsewhere. At the end of the tour, the person asking may still be unable to reconstruct the decision.

**If everybody handled a little piece of the decision, who is responsible for the whole thing being explainable?**

That is not an unreasonable demand. It is rather close to the point of having a government.

## ⚖️ Mandelson, Andrew and the Separate Criminal Justice Clock

The author has raised concerns about the publicly visible procedural position of investigations involving **Peter Mandelson** and **Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor**, including arrest-related searches, warrants, bail arrangements and the absence of a publicly demonstrated resolution at the hundred-day mark.

The two matters must be documented separately. The record should establish what police have actually confirmed about arrests, search powers, release status, pre-charge bail or release under investigation, conditions, charging decisions and continuing inquiries. Search warrants and bail conditions are not interchangeable, and neither can be assumed unchanged merely because the public has not seen updated documents.

The absence of a public charging decision is not proof that no investigative work has occurred. Equally, an ongoing investigation does not answer questions about the political appointment process or departmental judgment.

**Two clocks are running.** The criminal law must operate on its own evidential requirements. The government still has to account for decisions that fall within political and administrative responsibility.

## 🇬🇧🇺🇸 Across the Atlantic: What Assistance Is Being Given?

The American proceedings identified for this research include **_Phang v. Blanche_**, described by the author as outstanding. Its case number, court, claims, procedural status and precise relevance to the Epstein disclosures must be verified from the docket before making any claim about what the litigation establishes.

The other question is straightforward: **what assistance have British authorities actually confirmed they are giving their American counterparts?**

That calls for a careful distinction between requests made, assistance provided, channels used, information that can lawfully be disclosed, and information that remains unknown. Mutual legal assistance, police-to-police cooperation and political statements are not synonyms.

A lack of public confirmation is not proof of refusal, obstruction or inactivity. But it leaves a legitimate transparency question, especially when the political crisis itself has an international dimension.

## 🦠 The Covid Inquiry: Institutional Memory Is Supposed to Be Useful

The UK Covid-19 Inquiry offers a separate, evidence-rich means of examining the Cabinet Office's record: coordination, information management, the treatment of warnings, decision-making structures, record-keeping and accountability under pressure.

Specific Inquiry findings must be cited individually; witness criticism is not automatically an adopted Inquiry finding. Equally, a recommendation is not the same thing as an implemented correction.

For every relevant criticism or recommendation, ask:

- What failure or weakness was actually identified?
- What did the Cabinet Office accept or dispute?
- What corrective measure followed?
- Who owns it now?
- How will anyone know it worked?

It is possible to reorganise a department while still failing to answer those questions. Which brings us to the furniture.

## 🗂️ Cabinet Office: Have We Tried Moving the Furniture?

The successor administration's discussion of Cabinet Office restructuring needs to be compared with the problems it says it is solving. Which functions move? Which responsibilities change? Which controls improve? What happens to historical records and unresolved parliamentary questions? Who remains accountable for decisions made under the previous structure?

Reorganisation can be genuine reform. It can also become a way to describe movement without showing correction. The difference is evidence.

*Good news, chaps. We've discovered an organisational chart.*

*Splendid. Can it tell us where the dossier went?*

A new reporting line does not answer an old parliamentary question. A renamed unit does not retrospectively explain an appointment. A new governance framework does not, by existing, prove that a previous failure has been understood.

**If you cannot name the error, how do you know the restructuring fixes it?**

This is not a demand that the Cabinet Office stop reforming itself. It is a demand that institutional reform be connected to the actual institutional problems under scrutiny.

## 🛢️ Jackdaw: The House Is Not a Press Office

The **Jackdaw North Sea oil and gas development** is included as a contemporary test of whether criticised communications practices continue after the change of Prime Minister.

The author identifies a concern that a significant Jackdaw announcement was communicated to the media rather than first to the House of Commons. The precise announcement, its timing, any prior parliamentary notification, subsequent statements and objections must be established from the public record before describing a breach of convention.

There are two separate questions: **what did government decide about oil and gas?** And **how did government account for that decision to Parliament?**

One does not erase the other. Energy security, climate policy, licensing and environmental obligations warrant substantive scrutiny. Parliamentary scrutiny is not an optional decorative flourish attached afterwards.

The comparison with the previous administration is especially relevant because this would concern **behaviour during the new premiership**, not merely inherited paperwork.

If the personnel changed and the parliamentary habits did not, that tells us something worth examining about institutional learning.

## 🌹 Labour Together, TBI and the Policy Shop That Survived

A change of Prime Minister need not change the surrounding political and policy ecosystem. That is not intrinsically sinister: continuity can preserve expertise and stable policy. But continuity is also precisely why the ecosystem remains open to scrutiny.

**Labour Together** belongs in the account of party networks, funding, access, appointments and influence. **The Tony Blair Institute for Global Change** belongs in the account of external policy development, relationships with government and the survival of policy programmes across the leadership change.

They are not the same organisation. A similar policy proposal does not prove that one organisation directed a government decision. Personal acquaintance does not prove an improper appointment. The task is to establish documented meetings, roles, proposals, decision pathways and declarations of interest.

The question is whether the incoming administration has independently examined the practices it inherited, especially those already attracting legitimate scrutiny.

**The Prime Minister changed. Did the policy-development and influence pathways change? And if not, were they at least examined?**

## 💼 Global Counsel: Where Are the Contracts?

The author also identifies an unresolved transparency problem involving **Global Counsel**, the public-affairs and advisory business historically associated with Mandelson, and the question of which relevant contracts were tendered.

This requires a procurement trail, not a collage of insinuations. Search award notices, tender notices, departmental contract registers, framework agreements, declared interests, lobbying disclosures and official meeting records. Distinguish **contracts awarded to Global Counsel** from **contracts on which Global Counsel may have advised clients**. Do not assume either category exists in a particular case without records.

For each relevant contract or decision, establish the public body, value, procurement route, award date, variation history, participants and any declared conflict. Where the answer is not available, say **not established from the reviewed record**, rather than inventing a contract or concluding none exists.

*Not a pip about what Global Counsel was up to or which contracts were tendered* is a sharp description of the author's frustration. The evidential version is a question the government ought to be capable of answering where public procurement and disclosure rules apply.

**Who advised? Who tendered? Who awarded? Who disclosed the relationship?**

This is what tracing influence looks like when it has to survive contact with a spreadsheet.

## 🪿 The Whole Ecology: Changing the Face Is Not Changing the Feedback Loop

Here is the systems problem.

Government is not a single person with a large desk. It is a network of people, departments, procedures, information systems, informal relationships, statutory duties, conventions, budgets and incentives. A Prime Minister matters enormously within that network. But replacing the Prime Minister is not equivalent to rebuilding the network.

If warnings were ignored, the question is how warnings are received and escalated now. If appointments were inadequately scrutinised, the question is what appointment checks changed. If Parliament could not obtain an answer, the question is whether the answer pathway has improved. If records crossed institutional boundaries and became difficult to trace, the question is whether records can now be followed across those boundaries.

The necessary feedback sequence is:

**Problem observed → evidence preserved → responsibility established → correction implemented → outcome tested.**

An announcement is not a correction. A consultation is not an outcome. A restructuring is not an explanation. A new Prime Minister is not a completed investigation.

Nor should the work of people outside government be quietly reclassified as evidence that government has fixed itself. A parliamentary question exists because somebody asked it. Litigation advances because somebody pursues it. Records surface because somebody finds, requests or discloses them. **The burden of producing accountability matters.**

The central hypothesis of this node is that the change at the top has not yet produced a publicly demonstrable correction of the underlying institutional problems identified by the author. That hypothesis must be tested issue by issue, not protected from inconvenient evidence.

## 🌹 A Hundred Days Is Long Enough to Ask What Changed

Nobody serious should expect every criminal inquiry, court case or institutional review to finish on the hundredth day of a premiership. That is not the standard being proposed here.

The standard is considerably more modest: can the government identify the problems it inherited, account for what it knows, say who is responsible for addressing them, show what it has done, and explain what still remains to be done?

Where individuals and groups have obtained small pieces of progress, credit them. Where a department has answered a question, record the answer. Where a legal restriction prevents disclosure, identify the restriction. Where a reform genuinely corrects a weakness, acknowledge it.

But do not hand out an institutional gold star for announcing a plan to reorganise the institution.

One hundred days.

A new Prime Minister.

A long list of questions about appointments, dossiers, Parliament, departments, contracts and the people who had access to power.

And the author is still asking the question that ought to have been at the heart of the transition:

**What have you actually fixed?**

*Treading water is an activity. It is not the same thing as getting somewhere.*
