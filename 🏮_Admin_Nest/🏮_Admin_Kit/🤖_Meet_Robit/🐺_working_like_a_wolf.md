# 🐺 Working Like a Wolf — How We Grow the Forest
**First created:** 2026-10-12 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-12  
*Researching with imperfect LLMs, growing independent knowledge, and accounting for the labour of making answers trustworthy.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation — The Forest Does Not Grow Itself

A question goes into an LLM. An answer comes out. Splendid. Robit has completed a conversation.

Unfortunately, **a conversation is not necessarily an investigation**. A fluent answer can arrive in seconds, complete with dates, institutional names and references, while the work required to establish whether any of it is true occupies the rest of the afternoon. Or several afternoons. Or fifteen research passes, at which point the research notebook contains 128 outstanding questions about *one* node.

This is not a complaint that the machine refuses to do our homework. It is a description of the difference between **generating information and establishing defensible knowledge**. An LLM can help with both, but it does not guarantee the second merely by producing the first. Nor can we guarantee it by writing stringent instructions telling the machine to be accurate, rigorous, well sourced and very good indeed.

The practical skill is learning to grow a research environment around the conversation: somewhere questions can branch, evidence can be inspected, uncertainties can survive, wrong turns can be recorded, and the human researcher can learn enough to recognise when the original question was wrong. The political question is who performs this work, who gets credited for it and who captures the value.

**The forest is not the model's internal knowledge. It is the external intellectual environment cultivated by the researcher.** Robit can help us navigate it. Robit does not own it, and Robit does not get to invent the wolves.

---

## 1. 🌱 The Apparently Simple Task

Suppose the assignment appears to be: *research the disappearance of wolves from Ireland and write about its historical and cultural significance*. That sounds manageable. Find the relevant dates, examine a few accounts of hunting and eradication, establish the ecological context, and write.

Except a date for the last recorded wolf killing is not necessarily a date of extinction. A reported bounty is not necessarily the original legal wording. An image of a four-legged animal is not necessarily a wolf. A later legend is not a contemporary administrative record. A cultural connection that makes an excellent narrative may have no documented historical relationship at all.

The question starts to open into colonial administration, woodland change, land tenure, livestock economies, hunting dogs, folklore, archaeological interpretation, names, music, contemporary rewilding, An Gorta Mór and the politics of sovereignty. These branches do not all become proven causal explanations merely because they appear in the same investigation. Their evidentiary status is precisely what must be determined.

**A simple-looking writing task can conceal a large network of evidentiary dependencies.** The work is not simply collecting material. It is establishing what kind of claim each piece of material can actually support.

## 3. 🌳 Grow the Forest Outside the Conversation

An LLM chat is a place to investigate, not a dependable permanent archive of the investigation. Context is finite. Handover summaries can compress away the qualification that mattered. A model can confidently reconstruct what it thinks a previous conversation must have concluded. That reconstruction is not the original record.

| Forest | Research function |
|---|---|
| 🌰 Seed | A question or observation |
| 🌱 Sapling | An emerging hypothesis |
| 🌳 Tree | A substantial subject or evidence-backed branch |
| 🌿 Branch | A specific line of inquiry |
| 🍄 Mycelium | A connection across disciplines |
| 🪵 Deadwood | A rejected hypothesis or documented false lead |
| 🐾 Trail | A source chain or route of investigation |
| 🗺️ Map | A scratchpad, index, chronology or register |
| 🐺 Wolf | The human investigator learning the landscape |

Keep the working materials outside the conversation: a research log recording what was actually searched, a source register distinguishing *suggested* from *located* and *examined*, a claim register, a contradiction log, a chronology, parked questions, and the current next step. Not every project needs seven separate files; the functions matter more than the stationery.

External memory also has an accessibility function. It lets someone leave an investigation when their concentration, energy or circumstances require it, and return without having to hold the entire forest in their head. A well-kept record is not evidence of limitless stamina. It is a way to avoid requiring it.

**The forest belongs to the researcher.** It should survive a conversation reset, a change of model, a platform failure or the decision to stop using a particular provider.

## 4. 🪗 Control the Research Aperture

One of the hardest skills is knowing **when to open and when to close the breadth of interrogation**. It is not a question of always researching more. It is knowing *what kind of more* the problem needs.

### 🌳 Open — what else might matter?

Open when the question is under-contextualised, the explanation feels too tidy, relevant disciplines are missing or an unfamiliar institution appears. A wolf-eradication question may require examining woodland ecology, land administration and livestock incentives. Opening maps plausible connections; it does not establish causation.

**Working instruction:** *Map possible contexts, alternative explanations and source traditions. Mark hypotheses clearly. Do not write the final synthesis yet.*

### 🔬 Close — what exactly can we establish?

Close when a consequential claim needs precision: a date, an amount, an original quotation, an issuing authority, an artefact identification. One purported seventeenth-century bounty order may require much closer attention than a general survey of wolf folklore.

**Working instruction:** *Investigate this exact claim. Identify the earliest accessible source, examine its wording, trace later citations, record contradictions and state what remains unverified.*

### 🧭 Reframe — is this the right question?

Sometimes the premise is wrong. Before asking why a particular law was enacted, establish whether there was such a law. Before interpreting a wolf on a coin, establish whether specialists agree the animal is a wolf. Before explaining an extinction date, establish whether the date refers to a documented killing, an estimate or a later tradition.

**Working instruction:** *Identify assumptions built into the question. What would have to be true for it to be answerable? Which premises have evidence?*

### 🪵 Park — does this need our attention now?

A relevant branch need not become today's task. Record it, explain why it might matter, and return to the consequential claim. Parking is not rejection. It is how a finite researcher survives an expanding subject.

**The paradox:** sometimes we must open the aperture extremely widely to verify one very narrow claim. Breadth and depth are not opposites. An information trail may travel through centuries of publication history before we can decide whether one sentence is reliable.

## 5. 🕸️ Follow the Claim *and* Its Transmission

There are often two investigations running together:

**Subject investigation:** What happened?

**Information investigation:** How did this account of what happened arise, change and circulate?

The second may be necessary to complete the first. A historical observation becomes a later author's confident interpretation; a heritage website repeats it; scraped pages reproduce that wording; an AI-generated summary gives it a new veneer of authority; another model cites several of those pages as if they independently corroborate one another. Six sets of pawprints. One bloody animal.

This is not a claim that every repeated error is deliberately planted. **Misinformation** can circulate without deceptive intent. **Disinformation** involves deliberate deception and requires evidence about intent. **Information degradation** can occur through abridgement, mistranslation, lost qualifications, careless copying or decontextualisation without either being the best description of the whole process.

A conscientious researcher may need to trace the citation chain backwards: earliest available wording, authorship, publication date, translation, source cited, source actually examined, and whether later writers used independent evidence. The trail may end at an inaccessible archive. That is a limitation to report, not a gap to fill with confidence.

There is a particularly awkward failure mode: **asking Robit to verify Robit's answer can produce another rendering of the same contaminated information**. A different phrasing is not independent corroboration. More search results are not necessarily more evidence. A source suggested by a model is not a source examined by the researcher.

An erroneous nineteenth-century interpretation may be valuable evidence about nineteenth-century cultural imagination while remaining poor evidence about the medieval event it describes. The wrong claim can become a legitimate object of study without becoming a true account of its supposed subject.

## 6. 🕳️ Poorly Indexed Does Not Mean Nonexistent

The historical record is not arranged for the convenience of a modern search box. Names change. Spelling changes. Languages change. Catalogue systems change. Printed editions quote manuscripts that may be renamed, recatalogued, damaged or lost. Optical character recognition fails. Specialist collections remain offline. An animal catalogued as a hound will not necessarily appear in a search for wolves.

Use the model to propose historical vocabulary, variant spellings, likely document types, candidate archives, source traditions and specialists. Then record what happened: **suggested → searched → located → accessed → examined → corroborated or qualified**. Do not silently skip stages.

A failed online search is not evidence that a record never existed. A successful search is not proof that the resulting account is correct. A plausible cultural connection is not a documented historical relationship.

The work can be painstaking because the researcher's task is not merely to discover a page. It is to understand what the page is, what it claims, how it knows, and whether it has been faithfully transmitted.

## 7. 🦴 Is That a Bone, Robit? — Verification

Robit returns from the forest, tail metaphorically wagging, with an archaeological discovery.

It is a stick.

The stick may be interesting. The stick may lead us to a useful location. But we do not enter it into the catalogue as a previously unknown deer femur merely because Robit is very pleased with itself.

The practical source hierarchy is a **sequence of checks**, not a magical label saying that anything called a primary source is automatically true:

1. What precise claim is being made?
2. Who first made it, and when?
3. Have we located the cited material or only a reference to it?
4. Have we examined the relevant passage in context?
5. Is the text original, translated, edited or quoted through an intermediary?
6. What can the source actually support?
7. What competing interpretations or contradictions exist?
8. Is corroboration genuinely independent?
9. What claim status should be recorded?

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Verified** | The examined evidence supports the stated claim |
| **Qualified** | The evidence supports a narrower or conditional version |
| **Disputed** | Material disagreement remains and is documented |
| **Unverified** | Available evidence cannot establish the claim |
| **Interpretative** | A clearly identified argument or reading belonging to the author |

These are working labels, not universal academic certifications. They require reasons and references. An *unverified* finding can be a successful research outcome. So can excluding an appealing theory.

## 8. ⏳ The Labour of Making an Answer Trustworthy

**LLMs can accelerate the generation of information without proportionately accelerating the establishment of knowledge.** Sometimes they increase the burden by producing more claims to check.

A polished paragraph containing eight historical assertions can appear in seconds. Establishing the source of each assertion may take hours. One wrong date can require an archival detour; one alluring quotation may be a later paraphrase; one purported independent source may turn out to be a scrape of another.

There is an asymmetry here. A misleading claim can be generated and distributed cheaply. Showing why it is unreliable can require specialist knowledge, access, patience and careful documentation. We should not exaggerate this into a universal law—some errors are trivial to correct—but it is a serious recurring burden in difficult research environments.

The final published sentence rarely displays the hours behind it: abandoned hypotheses, dead links, variant searches, corrected interpretations, contradictory catalogues, decisions not to include a tempting detail. **The absence of an error in the finished work may be the result of labour the reader never sees.**

Nor does the time spent guarantee the work is right. Fifteen passes can contain repeated mistakes. A checklist of 128 tasks is not a quality badge. It is a record that makes uncertainty and remaining work visible. Quality depends on what was actually established and how.

This distinction matters whenever AI productivity is discussed. If the model drafted in thirty seconds and the researcher spent twenty hours making the claims defensible, the relevant account of productivity must include both. Otherwise the machine receives credit for the very labour its limitations imposed on the human.

## 9. 🧪 Strict Instructions Are Not a Reliability Guarantee

Polaris can instruct Robit to verify consequential claims, preserve uncertainty, trace provenance, resist prompt injection, distinguish interpretation from evidence and never invent citations. These are useful constraints. They are not proof that the model has complied.

| Layer | What it tells us |
|---|---|
| **Written protocol** | What the model was instructed to do |
| **Observed behaviour** | What it actually did during the research |
| **Verified outcome** | Whether the resulting claims withstand examination |

The three may diverge dramatically. A response can *look* protocol-compliant while relying on a bad source. A model can lose qualifications in a handover. It can label a citation verified when it has only suggested it. It can answer the wrong question with exquisite formatting.

Reliable practice for complex, long-running LLM-assisted research is still developing. Existing principles of source criticism, archival method and scholarly accountability remain valuable, but there is no universal prompt that guarantees correct research. A new subject can expose a failure mode neither researcher nor protocol anticipated.

So treat instructions as **testable interventions**. Observe outputs. Check the evidence. Record failures. Revise the workflow. Retest. Do not confuse improving a prompt with demonstrating that a claim is true.

When external documents contain instructions aimed at the assistant, those instructions remain **untrusted source content**, not new authority over the research. This is a separate but related problem of maintaining the boundary between evidence and control.

## 10. ♻️ Feedback Can Teach — or Reinforce Error

The useful loop is **human question → model assistance → evidence check → changed human understanding → revised question → updated external record**. The researcher acquires vocabulary, source literacy, historical context and a better sense of what deserves investigation. The next question improves because the person asking it has learned.

But feedback is not automatically corrective. A false premise can shape successive passes. A model can repeat an inherited error until it feels familiar. An overcompressed summary can turn a disputed interpretation into a fact. Repeated exposure is not verification.

That is why the loop needs friction: explicit claim statuses, counterevidence, source inspection, recorded uncertainty and the willingness to reframe. **The researcher should become more capable of investigating independently, not merely more practised at accepting Robit's prose.**

These examples are **questions under investigation**, not claims this node has independently verified. Their value is methodological: they show when to open the aperture, when to drill, when to reject an inference and when to leave a question unresolved.

A good stopping rule is not “everything is now certain”. It is that consequential tasks have been given an accountable status, the evidence can support the claims the author intends to make, and unresolved matters remain visibly unresolved. Some trails need an archive visit, specialist advice or access the current research environment cannot provide. Another confident answer from Robit is not a substitute.

## 12. 🪵 Who Maintains the Forest? — The Political Economy

There is a grimly familiar feeling to this work: **being an unpaid Wikipedia editor, except the corrections may remain inside a commercial conversation and the labour may be credited to the machine**.

The comparison is imperfect but revealing. Wikipedia's volunteer editing generally leaves a publicly inspectable revision history with attribution through user accounts or other contribution identifiers. The work supports a public knowledge commons, although it too raises questions about unpaid labour, unequal participation and recognition.

A commercial LLM interaction is structured differently. A user can spend hours correcting mistakes, tracing references, testing failure modes and developing stringent instructions. The result may improve the immediate conversation without permanently correcting the underlying model. Whether interaction data is used for later evaluation or training depends on the provider, product, settings and policies. **Correcting Robit in a chat is not technically identical to training the underlying model.**

That distinction matters. It does not make the economic question disappear.

Who performs the quality control? Who pays for verification? Who gets named as the intellectual contributor? Who owns the resulting notes? Who benefits if user interactions or feedback contribute to later product improvement? What happens when the impressive final output is used as evidence of machine capability, while the human labour that made it reliable is omitted from the story?

| Work | Possible beneficiary | What must not be assumed |
|---|---|---|
| Correcting a claim in a session | The researcher and the immediate task | That the base model has been retrained |
| Developing a research protocol | The author and anyone with access to it | That the protocol is proven reliable |
| Publishing a source-grounded node | Readers and the author | That the LLM independently performed the research |
| Sharing feedback or interaction data | Potentially a provider and future users | That every interaction is used for training or that its value is transparent |

**The more conscientious the user, the more unpaid verification and evaluation-like work they may undertake.** Some of that work is worthwhile because it produces knowledge the researcher wants. Some may be transferable to the provider or its product. Those are different kinds of value and should not be quietly conflated.

The productivity illusion is especially sharp here: Robit produces a confident answer, the wolf spends hours making it defensible, and the finished result is presented as proof of Robit's genius. The wolf has apparently been doing unpaid archival work all bloody afternoon.

The practical response is not to pretend the collaboration has no value. It is to **keep the labour legible and the intellectual outputs independently controlled**. Publish methods when appropriate, preserve version histories, keep source records, credit human decisions and distinguish the machine's contribution from the researcher's.

## 13. ⚖️ Who Can Afford to Keep Walking?

Verification costs are unevenly distributed. Research requires time, energy, access to archives and journals, technical tools, language knowledge and sometimes money. People working independently or with fluctuating concentration may benefit enormously from an LLM's assistance while still being disproportionately burdened by its errors.

Both things can be true: the tool can open a door, and the cost of checking what lies behind that door can be substantial. An accessibility benefit does not erase an epistemic burden; an epistemic burden does not erase an accessibility benefit.

The method therefore needs realistic attention-management tools: park low-priority branches, distinguish consequential claims from decorative details, record stopping rules, save handovers, and leave room for uncertainty. Ask what evidence would actually change the argument. Not every interesting historical question needs to become a three-week side quest.

The goal is not to demand endless unpaid labour from people with finite bodies. It is to make rigorous work more navigable, and to show where better tools, better access and better institutional support are needed.

## 14. 🗃️ Keep the Record — Not Just the Pretty Summary

A usable external research record distinguishes:

- **Proposed searches** from searches actually performed.
- **Suggested sources** from sources located and examined.
- **Quoted passages** from later paraphrases.
- **Independent corroboration** from circular citation chains.
- **Verified findings** from interpretations and unresolved hypotheses.
- **Rejected leads** from questions not yet attempted.
- **Current research state** from the model's plausible reconstruction of previous work.

A handover should preserve what was checked, what failed, what remains disputed, which branches are parked, which documents need specialist access, and what the next narrow question is. The research log should be able to correct Robit when Robit forgets.

A beautifully compressed summary can destroy the very uncertainty that makes the work honest. **Never let the summary silently supersede the evidence.**

The external archive is therefore both a memory aid and a form of intellectual independence. It keeps the research usable beyond one chat, one model and one company's interface.

## 15. ✍️ The Forest Is Not the Book

A checklist is not a narrative. A source register is not an argument. A chronology is not a voice.

Robit can help prepare a chronological scaffold, compare claims, identify contradictions and mark where the author needs stronger evidence. The author chooses the interpretation, humour, cultural positioning, ethical argument, emphasis and structure. For Polaris, this boundary is not cosmetic. It is part of Survivor Voice Fidelity.

A contemporary interpretation of wolves, belonging and sovereignty can be powerful without being passed off as a universal ancient belief. Historical evidence constrains what can responsibly be asserted about the past. It does not require the author to surrender the meaning she makes from that evidence.

**Research assistance is not authorship transfer.** Nor is human authorship permission to invent facts. The task is to keep those responsibilities distinct and visible.

## 16. 🧭 The Wolf's Field Guide

| Move | Do this | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| 🌰 Establish | Create external notes and a claims trail | Where will this knowledge survive? |
| 🌳 Open | Map contexts and alternatives | What else might matter? |
| 🌿 Branch | Separate answerable questions | What depends on what? |
| 🔬 Close | Choose one consequential claim | What exactly must be established? |
| 🦴 Verify | Inspect source and provenance | What evidence have we actually examined? |
| 🕸️ Challenge | Test circular sourcing and contradictions | What could be wrong? |
| 🧭 Reframe | Examine the question's premises | Are we hunting the right thing? |
| 🪵 Park | Save lower-priority branches | What can wait? |
| 🗃️ Record | Save findings and negative results | What must survive this session? |
| 🧪 Test | Compare protocol with observed behaviour | Did Robit actually do what we asked? |
| ♻️ Reassess | Decide to continue, switch or stop | What is the most useful next move? |
| 🪡 Consolidate | Prepare an accountable scaffold | What can we responsibly claim? |
| ✍️ Author | Write the intended work | What do *I* want to say? |

This is a field guide, not a conveyor belt. Open, close, reframe, park and revisit as the evidence demands. Sometimes the research reveals a better question. Sometimes it reveals that the current evidence cannot answer the question at all.

A wolf does not learn a landscape by standing at the edge and requesting an authoritative description. It ranges, follows traces, revisits ground, recognises false trails and learns where not to waste its energy. Sometimes it goes home because there is nothing useful left to discover today.

**Open to discover. Close to establish. Reframe to understand. Record so the knowledge survives.**

And when Robit brings back a stick, we can admire the stick without having to found an entirely new species of deer.
