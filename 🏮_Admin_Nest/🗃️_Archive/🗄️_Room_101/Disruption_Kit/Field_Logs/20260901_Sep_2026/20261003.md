explain something that I feel I explain a lot, but worth reiterating.  

I have no idea what caused the tech problems, but from the pattern experienced, and from historical experience in cases where my state will not openly answer reasonable lawful requests for information, it would be reasonable for me to assume it would come from a party/parties directly threatened by one specific policy.  

but it might not be.  

allow me to show how this would be a useful tactic of war for the IRGC, etc:  

- first: why would the IRGC, or another Iranian-aligned actor, theoretically care about something as hilariously granular as votes at a British political-party conference?

- probably not because they desperately need chloe birney's individual green party vote.

- zoom out.

- iran has strategic interests in:
  - the course of the israel–palestine conflict;
  - western military and diplomatic support for israel;
  - domestic political pressure on governments supporting israel;
  - the cohesion of israel's western allies;
  - the credibility of those states and their democratic institutions;
  - and the information environment through which western populations understand the conflict.

- iranian cyber and influence operations have previously been assessed as pursuing several overlapping objectives:
  - retaliation;
  - intimidation;
  - destabilisation;
  - increasing political polarisation;
  - undermining confidence in opposing governments;
  - and weakening international support for israel.

- so a british political conference discussing palestine, israel, sanctions, arms, foreign policy, proscription, military relationships, or adjacent questions exists inside a much larger strategic information environment.

- that does **not** mean iran interfered with this conference.

- it means iran is one example of an actor for whom disruption surrounding politically sensitive democratic activity could, in principle, produce useful effects.

- and importantly, those effects do not require successfully changing the substantive policy outcome.

- imagine instead that your strategic objective is to increase friction inside a country whose foreign policy you want to change.

- making pro-palestinian participants believe the british state is suppressing them could be useful.

- making british institutions believe pro-palestinian political activity is being manipulated by iran could also be useful.

- making everybody argue about which of those things happened could be more useful still.

- because now several political fractures can be widened simultaneously:
  - activist ↔ state;
  - citizen ↔ democratic institution;
  - party member ↔ party infrastructure;
  - pro-palestinian movement ↔ security establishment;
  - different factions within the pro-palestinian movement;
  - and potentially british government ↔ iran.

- this is where the strategy becomes considerably more interesting than simply:

  **BAD GUY HACK VOTE.**

- changing a vote is one possible objective.

- degrading confidence in the environment in which voting occurs is another.

- forcing the target state to spend resources investigating ambiguous incidents is another.

- testing technical access and defensive responses is another.

- observing what happens when particular systems fail is another.

- creating opportunities for later influence operations is another.

- and producing events which can subsequently be narrated differently to different audiences is another.

- the political objective and the technical objective therefore do not have to be identical.

- the technical event can be tiny.

- the information effect can be much larger.

- and there is another particularly nasty possibility:

- an adversary can exploit **real political grievances** without having created them.

- it does not need to invent british distrust around palestine.

- it does not need to invent concerns about surveillance.

- it does not need to invent disagreements about israel.

- it does not need to invent institutional opacity.

- those things already exist.

- from an adversarial perspective, existing fractures are considerably cheaper than manufacturing new ones.

- you find the crack.

- you put pressure on the crack.

- and ideally you arrange matters so everybody disagrees about who applied the pressure.

- which brings us back to my stupid little TLS handshake.

allow me to show how this would be a useful tactic of war for the IRGC, etc:

- you do not actually need to change the vote.

- you do not even necessarily need to prevent me from voting.

- you need to create enough selective, badly timed, technically ambiguous interference that I cannot tell whether:
  - the platform is broken;
  - my browser is broken;
  - my connection is broken;
  - someone is deliberately interfering;
  - or somebody who appears to have an obvious interest in the outcome is deliberately interfering.

- crucially, the last possibility does not have to be true for it to have effects.

- if I already have good historical reasons not to trust my own state to answer reasonable questions about interference, surveillance, or access to information, then ambiguity itself becomes useful terrain.

- an external actor does not need to convincingly impersonate the British state forever.

- it only needs to produce an event which is sufficiently consistent with my previous experiences that I have to consider state interference as one plausible explanation.

- now I have an attribution problem.

- and, more importantly, so does everybody else.

- if I publicly blame the state and I am wrong, trust is damaged.

- if I dismiss the incident as random technical failure and it was deliberate, the interference worked without attribution.

- if I spend hours trying to establish which one happened, my attention has been diverted from the thing I was originally trying to do.

- if enough people experience this around politically sensitive activity, they may begin to anticipate failure before it happens.

- that creates friction.

- friction changes behaviour even where it does not change formal outcomes.

- people retry.

- people give up.

- people arrive late.

- people miss voting windows.

- people become suspicious of platforms.

- people become suspicious of institutions.

- people become suspicious of each other.

- and people who already have reasons to distrust the state acquire another apparently compatible data point.

- this is why "did somebody successfully alter the final vote?" is much too narrow a question.

- the information effect can exist even where the democratic procedure ultimately produces exactly the same numerical result.

- indeed, from the perspective of an external adversary, ambiguity may be considerably more useful than obvious sabotage.

- obvious sabotage gives everybody a shared problem.

- ambiguous sabotage gives everybody different explanations.

- now imagine you are the IRGC.

- you know there is already domestic conflict over Palestine.

- you know there are already allegations and documented histories of state surveillance, covert policing, political monitoring, cyber operations, platform failures and institutional opacity.

- you do not have to invent that information environment.

- Britain has very kindly constructed it for you.

- you merely have to introduce additional uncertainty into it.

- ideally at a moment where several plausible domestic actors appear to have motive.

- congratulations.

- nobody knows whether they are looking at:
  - hostile-state activity;
  - domestic-state activity;
  - political interference;
  - ordinary infrastructure failure;
  - platform failure;
  - ISP failure;
  - or several boring technical failures occurring at an extraordinarily annoying time.

- and because responsible attribution requires evidence, I cannot honestly collapse those possibilities into one answer.

- **that epistemic restraint is necessary.**

- but an adversary can exploit the period before attribution precisely because responsible people *will* exercise it.

- this is one reason resilient democracy cannot depend solely upon eventually identifying who attacked what.

- it also requires reducing the background conditions which make hostile attribution games effective.

- transparency matters.

- trustworthy complaint mechanisms matter.

- auditable voting systems matter.

- technical logs matter.

- institutional willingness to answer lawful questions matters.

- and not generating enormous reservoirs of justified public suspicion through your own behaviour is, as it turns out, also quite useful.

- because if somebody hostile wants me to think *you* did it, one extremely effective defensive measure is for you to have spent the preceding decade behaving in ways which make that explanation less plausible.

- you cannot prevent an adversary from lying about you.

- you can make the lie harder to believe.
