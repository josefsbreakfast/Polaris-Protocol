Whilst destroying Iran’s entire energy system would not automatically be labelled a war crime merely because hospitals use electricity, it would be extraordinarily difficult to reconcile with international humanitarian law. Individual attacks, or the campaign as a whole, could amount to war crimes depending on the intended targets, purpose, expected consequences and decision-makers’ knowledge.

The central rules are:

- **Energy infrastructure is normally civilian infrastructure.** A specific power station, substation, refinery or fuel facility may become a military objective only where it makes an effective contribution to military action and its destruction offers a definite military advantage at that time. The whole national grid cannot simply be declared “dual use” or military infrastructure as a category.

- **Economic coercion is not enough.** Energy infrastructure cannot lawfully be attacked merely to weaken the opponent’s economy, force its government to negotiate, influence the population’s will or intimidate its political leadership. This matters directly where the reported purpose of the attacks is to create coercive leverage.

- **Foreseeable knock-on deaths count.** The legal assessment is not confined to people killed in the initial explosion. Military planners must consider reasonably foreseeable reverberating effects, including:
  - hospitals losing electricity;
  - ventilators, oxygen systems and operating theatres failing;
  - water and sewage pumps stopping;
  - medicine and food cold chains collapsing;
  - communications and emergency services being disabled;
  - food, fuel and medicine distribution breaking down.

- **Hospitals have additional protection.** Hospitals and medical units may not be attacked unless, under narrow conditions, they are being used for acts harmful to the enemy outside their humanitarian function. Cutting electricity deliberately to hospitals can therefore be legally relevant even where the hospital building itself is not bombed.

- **Military value does not end the inquiry.** Even where a particular energy facility is a genuine military objective, an attack remains unlawful if the expected civilian harm would be excessive compared with the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated.

- **Attackers must take feasible precautions.** They must assess civilian dependence on the target, consider less harmful alternatives, issue warnings where circumstances permit, and cancel or suspend an attack if the expected civilian consequences make it unlawful.

Formulations about the **essential conditions for life** are therefore substantially correct. Electricity is not always described in treaty language in exactly the same way as food, crops or drinking-water installations, but modern hospitals, water systems, sanitation, food storage and communications all depend upon it. Destroying the electricity system can therefore destroy the infrastructure necessary for civilian survival without directly bombing every protected facility.

The legally dangerous formulation would be something like:

> We will destroy Iran’s energy infrastructure to impose pain on the economy and population until the government changes course.

That is not a lawful substitute for identifying **specific military objectives**. If the intended or knowingly accepted consequence were nationwide hospital failures, loss of drinking water, sewage collapse and mass civilian deprivation, there would be serious grounds to investigate:

- attacks on civilian objects;
- indiscriminate attacks;
- disproportionate attacks;
- failure to take feasible precautions;
- unlawful attacks affecting medical services;
- and potentially the deliberate use of civilian deprivation as a method of warfare.

A systematic campaign intended to “take out the entire energy infrastructure” would be extremely difficult to characterise as lawful precision targeting.
