## How this is now useful to you  

The most useful way to read Ed’s work is not as commentary about a strange American technology market happening somewhere else.  

Read it as a set of tools for finding where confidence, debt, infrastructure and public exposure have become entangled.  

The method is simple:  

> Read the argument.  
> Find the underlying number.  
> Identify the assumption.  
> Locate the public exposure.  
> Ask who carries the downside.  

### 1. Separate capacity from demand  

A company building data centres, buying chips or securing power does not prove that sufficient paying demand exists.  

Infrastructure can be real while the business case remains fragile.  

Useful questions include:  

- What independently verified demand forecast supports this investment?  
- How much demand is contracted, rather than projected?  
- What level of utilisation is required for the project to remain viable?  
- What happens under lower-demand scenarios?  
- Who has tested those scenarios independently?  
- What would count as evidence that the demand case had failed?  

The useful political translation is:  

> Who pays when the promised demand does not arrive?  

### 2. Follow the risk through the structure  

Special-purpose vehicles, subsidiaries, contractors and financing structures do not make risk disappear.  

They can make it harder to see.  

Ask:  

- Who owns the relevant company or SPV?  
- Who holds the debt?  
- Who owns the land, buildings, chips and other underlying assets?  
- Who holds the power and water contracts?  
- What guarantees have been offered?  
- Is any public body providing subsidy, tax relief, land, infrastructure, underwriting or preferential access?  
- What happens if the project is restructured, sold or abandoned?  
- Who is left with stranded costs?  

The existence of paperwork is not proof that risk has been controlled.  

Sometimes it is evidence that risk has been distributed until nobody feels responsible for the whole of it.  

### 3. Ask what problem is actually being solved  

“AI adoption” is not itself a public outcome.  

Before supporting a system, ask:  

- What measurable public problem is this solving?  
- What is the current baseline?  
- What evidence shows that an LLM is better than a cheaper existing system?  
- What are the expected benefits?  
- How will those benefits be measured?  
- What error rate is acceptable?  
- Who is harmed when the system fails?  
- What human capability is being removed?  
- What would it cost to restore that capability later?  

A fluent demonstration is not the same as a public-interest case.  

A successful pilot is not the same as safe, affordable or resilient deployment at scale.  

### 4. Examine essential-service dependency  

The highest-risk cases are not necessarily the most dramatic.  

They are the systems that become difficult to remove because ordinary services have been reorganised around them.  

Ask:  

- Does this system support a service we cannot afford to lose?  
- Is there a credible offline or non-AI fallback?  
- Can the public body continue operating if the supplier fails?  
- Can it change supplier without losing access to data, records or core functions?  
- Are staff still able to perform the underlying task?  
- Is the organisation retaining enough internal expertise to supervise the vendor?  
- What happens during an outage, cyber incident, legal dispute or sudden price increase?  
- Is the exit plan funded, tested and contractually enforceable?  

“We never want to lose access to this” should trigger more scrutiny, not less.  

Dependence is not resilience.  

### 5. Look for circular demand  

AI companies can spend money with one another in ways that make the sector appear healthier than the underlying demand may justify.  

A cloud provider invests in an AI company.  

The AI company spends heavily on the cloud provider.  

A chip company backs customers that then buy its chips.  

Infrastructure expansion is presented as evidence of future demand, while future demand is used to justify further infrastructure expansion.  

Ask:  

- How much revenue comes from genuinely independent customers?  
- Are major investors also major suppliers or customers?  
- Is spending being recycled within the same group of firms?  
- Are credits, discounts or strategic investments being counted as evidence of ordinary commercial demand?  
- What happens when promotional pricing or investor support ends?  
- Would the customer still buy at the full economic cost?  

The key distinction is between activity and durable demand.  

Large amounts of money moving do not automatically prove that value has been created.  

### 6. Ask where the workers and consumers have gone  

The employment case cannot be separated from the consumer-demand case.  

The same people described as labour costs are also:  

- customers;  
- taxpayers;  
- borrowers;  
- renters;  
- mortgage holders;  
- service users;  
- and members of local economies.  

If the projected efficiency depends on reducing employment, ask:  

- What happens to household purchasing power?  
- What happens to tax receipts?  
- What happens to local businesses?  
- What happens to demand for the goods and services produced by the firms making these cuts?  
- Which workers are expected to absorb the transition?  
- What retraining is actually available?  
- Who pays for it?  
- Are equivalent jobs expected to exist?  
- What happens during the gap?  

A company can reduce its own wage bill.  

An economy cannot indefinitely remove wages while assuming that consumer demand will continue expanding as before.  

### 7. Protect the human capability being treated as disposable  

Not all labour is interchangeable with generated output.  

Some forms of work contain:  

- judgement;  
- tacit knowledge;  
- social trust;  
- taste;  
- cultural interpretation;  
- institutional memory;  
- relationship management;  
- local knowledge;  
- and the ability to recognise when the data is misleading.  

Ask:  

- What knowledge disappears when this role is removed?  
- Who currently notices errors before they become crises?  
- Who understands the local context?  
- Who carries the relationship with the public?  
- Who provides social permission, interpretation or trust?  
- Can that capability be rebuilt later?  
- How long would rebuilding take?  
- What would failure look like during the gap?  

Replacing content production is not necessarily the same as replacing cultural judgement.  

Replacing administrative output is not necessarily the same as replacing institutional knowledge.  

Replacing visible labour may simply hide the remaining work elsewhere.  

### 8. Use this in parliamentary and committee work  

Each economic claim can be converted into a governance question.  

For example:  

**Claim:** AI infrastructure investment demonstrates strong future demand.  

**Question:**  

> What proportion of the projected demand is supported by binding contracts, and what sensitivity analysis has been conducted for lower-than-expected utilisation?  

**Claim:** A special-purpose vehicle limits risk.  

**Question:**  

> Which liabilities have been transferred, to whom, and what exposure remains with public bodies, pension funds, insurers, local authorities or essential-service providers?  

**Claim:** The system will improve productivity.  

**Question:**  

> What is the baseline, what is the measured productivity gain, what additional supervision and correction work is required, and over what period will the claimed savings be realised?  

**Claim:** The system will reduce staffing costs.  

**Question:**  

> What assessment has been made of the effect on service quality, institutional memory, tax receipts, household income and local consumer demand?  

**Claim:** The technology is too strategically important to delay.  

**Question:**  

> What evidence would justify slowing, stopping or reversing the programme, and who has the authority to make that decision?  

### 9. Use this in procurement  

Procurement is one of the most important points of leverage because vague promises eventually have to become contractual claims.  

Ask for:  

- the defined public need;  
- the available alternatives;  
- independent evaluation;  
- error rates;  
- full lifecycle costs;  
- energy and infrastructure requirements;  
- staffing and supervision costs;  
- data-access terms;  
- vendor lock-in risks;  
- subcontractors;  
- conflicts of interest;  
- liability arrangements;  
- audit rights;  
- termination clauses;  
- rollback plans;  
- service-continuity plans;  
- and the cost of leaving.  

Do not allow “innovation” to substitute for ordinary due diligence.  

Do not allow urgency to remove the requirement to identify the downside.  

Do not allow the supplier to define both the problem and the evidence that its product has solved it.  

### 10. Use this in local government  

Local authorities may encounter AI infrastructure through:  

- planning;  
- land use;  
- water demand;  
- grid connections;  
- business-rate arrangements;  
- local subsidies;  
- transport and construction pressure;  
- employment claims;  
- public-service contracts;  
- and regional-development promises.  

Useful questions include:  

- How many long-term local jobs will actually be created?  
- How many are temporary construction jobs?  
- What is the effect on local power and water systems?  
- Who pays for infrastructure upgrades?  
- What happens if the operator leaves?  
- What land has been committed?  
- What public support has been offered?  
- What assumptions support the projected local benefit?  
- What protections exist for residents if demand forecasts fail?  

The headline investment figure is not the same as the local public benefit.  

### 11. Use this in health, education and public services  

In public services, the political question should not be whether a tool appears impressive.  

It should be whether it is safe, necessary, accountable and better than the available alternatives.  

Ask:  

- What task is being delegated?  
- Is the output advisory or determinative?  
- Who remains professionally responsible?  
- Can staff challenge or override the system?  
- Are errors recorded and reviewed?  
- Are affected people told when AI has been used?  
- Is there a meaningful route of appeal?  
- Does the system reproduce bias from existing records?  
- What additional administrative burden does it create?  
- Does it save staff time in practice, or merely move the work?  
- What happens when the supplier changes the model?  
- Can the service function without it?  

A system that cannot survive ordinary scrutiny is not a miracle.  

It is governance debt.  

### 12. Use this in media work  

The strongest public lines are often very simple:  

> Who pays when the promised demand does not arrive?  

> Why are we cutting people while subsidising infrastructure whose demand case has not been demonstrated?  

> Investment is not proof of value.  

> Capacity is not demand.  

> An SPV does not make risk disappear.  

> A fluent output is not the same as judgement.  

> Dependence is not resilience.  

> Public money should not underwrite private hype without public control.  

> The question is not whether the technology looks impressive. The question is whether the public case survives scrutiny.  

These lines work because they move the discussion away from abstract claims about the future and back towards responsibility in the present.

Use the analysis.  

Check the source material.  

Follow the numbers.  

Do not replace one technology priesthood with another.
