The immediate meaning is not that Russia should continue the war in order to preserve national unity. Putin instead says that the war will eventually end and asks how the collective purpose and voluntary mobilisation created around it can be retained afterward.

The significant feature is the **Russian-language register**. Putin does not frame the ending conditionally—there is no **если** (“if”)—nor does he immediately qualify it by saying that the war can end only after Russia has achieved all its objectives. He uses the perfective future **закончится**, placing the war’s completion into an expected sequence of future events.

The conversational opening **«Вот у нас закончится СВО…»** is also important. It is closer in effect to “So, when our operation ends…” than to an abstract observation that all conflicts eventually terminate. Putin briefly positions his domestic audience on the far side of the war and turns immediately to the social question of what comes next.

This is not a peace announcement, a timetable or evidence that Russian policy has decisively changed. The phrase **«рано или поздно»**—“sooner or later”—allows unlimited temporal flexibility. Nevertheless, it represents a notable shift in the permitted horizon of the message. The Russian public is being invited to imagine that the war has an aftermath.

Put simply:

> **The explicit message is that the war will end, but wartime unity should survive it. The deeper political significance is that Putin is now discussing the management of post-war social mobilisation in Russian, rather than speaking only about victory, endurance or the conditions under which peace might become possible.**
