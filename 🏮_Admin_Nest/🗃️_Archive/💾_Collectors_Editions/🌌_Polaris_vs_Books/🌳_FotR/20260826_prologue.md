## 🌳 20260826 — Prologue

### 🧭 Reading boundary

- This node covers the **Prologue** only.

- Working boundary for the reading archive:

- **Prologue:** Hobbit history, culture, geography, institutions, customs, language, calendars, pipe-weed, literacy, Shire organisation, and the prefatory account of the Ring / *The Hobbit*.
- **Book I, Chapter 1 — “A Long-expected Party”:** the present-day narrative beginning with Bilbo’s forthcoming eleventy-first birthday and the local gossip / preparations around it.

- This distinction matters because the Prologue establishes **what a Hobbit society is**, while Chapter 1 immediately starts showing **particular Hobbits living inside it**.

---

### 🧱 Class consciousness / Orwell adjacency

- Immediate emerging question:

> **Tolkien appears much more class-conscious than expected.**

- This does not presently mean:

- Tolkien was a Marxist;
- Tolkien was writing Marxist allegory;
- Tolkien demonstrably borrowed from Orwell.

- It does mean that the text appears alert to:

- class-coded speech;
- material comfort and deprivation;
- ownership;
- local status;
- labour;
- social deference;
- who is assumed to matter;
- who gets to remain ordinary;
- who is treated as morally serious despite low prestige.

- The Hobbits are especially important here.

- Their world is not socially flat. There are distinctions of:

- family;
- property;
- locality;
- manners;
- status;
- occupation.

- Tolkien nevertheless appears willing to give enormous moral weight to people who are not the grandest people in the room.

- Working question:

> **Does Tolkien repeatedly make hierarchy visible and then redistribute moral authority away from social prestige?**

- Possible developing pattern:

- aristocratic / heroic / learned figures possess status;
- ordinary people possess forms of judgement, loyalty, restraint, practical intelligence and moral clarity that status does not guarantee.

- Important:

- class position ≠ moral worth;
- intelligence ≠ moral worth;
- grandeur ≠ moral worth;
- power ≠ moral worth.

- This fits the larger developing pattern that Tolkien may be suspicious of systems that confuse **rank with goodness**.

### 📚 Orwell / *The Road to Wigan Pier*

- Immediate reaction:

> **sir, you read Orwell. This is very Marxy.**

- Stronger version to investigate:

> **Did Tolkien read *The Road to Wigan Pier*?**

- Current status in these notes:

- no direct evidence yet located that establishes Tolkien read it;
- do **not** state as fact.

- But the social/literary proximity is much closer than expected:

- George Orwell’s wife, **Eileen O’Shaughnessy**, had been Tolkien’s student at Oxford.

- This does not prove direct literary transmission.

- It does make “Orwell was culturally remote from Tolkien” a poor assumption.

### Chronology worth retaining

- **1936**
  - Orwell travels through northern English industrial communities researching unemployment, housing, mining and working-class life.
  - Orwell marries Eileen O’Shaughnessy.
- **1937**
  - *The Road to Wigan Pier* is published.
  - *The Hobbit* is published.
  - Tolkien begins developing the material that becomes *The Lord of the Rings*.

- Therefore Tolkien and Orwell are producing very different literary responses inside the **same interwar British argument about**:

- class;
- Englishness;
- industrialisation;
- ordinary life;
- locality;
- social hierarchy;
- what the country is actually for.

### 🏭 Wigan Pier as possible comparative bookshelf

- Even if Tolkien never read *Wigan Pier*, the comparison may still be useful as **shared cultural substrate**.

- Orwell’s recurring concerns include:

- the dignity of ordinary people;
- material conditions beneath abstract politics;
- elite misunderstanding of working-class life;
- discomfort with ideological abstraction detached from lived reality;
- class performance and class disgust;
- industrial systems reshaping bodies, landscapes and communities.

- Tolkien is obviously doing something very different.

- But emerging Tolkien concerns may include:

- ordinary domestic life as morally valuable;
- industrial destruction as more than aesthetic ugliness;
- suspicion of abstraction that overrides particular people;
- attention to land, home, craft, food and locality;
- moral seriousness existing outside elite status;
- power structures becoming corrupt when detached from ordinary human scale.

- Possible overlap:

> **both are interested in what grand systems look like from below.**

- Possible difference:

- Orwell approaches this through reportage / political prose / social criticism.
- Tolkien approaches it through myth, fantasy, locality and moral inversion.

---

### 🪓 Anti-totalisation: ordinary life is not raw material

- The class question potentially joins the existing anti-totalisation hypothesis.

- Totalising systems tend to:

- flatten difference;
- instrumentalise ordinary people;
- value populations according to usefulness;
- centralise decision-making;
- convert human beings into categories, labour, troops, subjects or resources.

- Tolkien repeatedly appears interested in restoring moral significance to:

- particular people;
- particular homes;
- local social worlds;
- ordinary pleasures;
- people with no ambition to become historically important.

- Possible formulation:

> **The political danger is not only domination from above; it is the loss of ordinary life as something valuable in itself.**

- This may become important when comparing:

- Sauronic centralisation;
- industrialisation;
- fascist mobilisation;
- class hierarchy;
- elite intellectual systems;
- Hobbit-scale locality.

### 🍺 Ordinary life is not background scenery

- Increasingly worth watching whether Tolkien treats:

- pubs;
- food;
- gardening;
- work;
- homes;
- local gossip;
- craft;
- social routine

- as the **thing civilisation is supposed to protect**, rather than merely comic relief between serious events.

- If so:

- the Shire is not politically irrelevant because it is small;
- its smallness may be the point.

- Possible moral hierarchy:

- world-historical power is less important than whether people can:

- eat;
- garden;
- sit with friends;
- remain at home;
- have lives not wholly absorbed by somebody else’s Great Project.

- Orwell adjacency:

- politics becomes morally meaningless when it ceases to connect to actual human life.

- Tolkien adjacency:

- heroism becomes morally meaningless if the world it saves has no room for ordinary life afterwards.

### 🌿 The Shire is being the Shire

- A stronger working proposition emerged while reading:

> **The Shire is not “performing”. The Shire is being the Shire.**

- This matters because a modern institutional / optimisation lens can easily treat ordinary life as:

- unrealised productivity;
- undeployed capability;
- insufficient ambition;
- low engagement;
- a population awaiting development.

- But Hobbit life suggests another objective function entirely.

- A Hobbit can be ambitious about **Hobbit shit**:

- learn everything about an obscure subject;
- build an absurdly good garden;
- know the local history;
- become terrifyingly competent at a craft;
- maintain relationships for decades;
- read fifteen thousand books;
- construct an incredibly defensible archive concerning Edwardian occultism;
- drink cider in the grass.

- None of this is failed transformation into something else.

> **Potential does not create an obligation to actualise itself in the form preferred by Elves.**

- The garden is not a waiting room before history begins.

- The pub is not where people sit until somebody gives them a strategic purpose.

- The books are not preparation for becoming useful.

- Sometimes the objective is:

> **books, friends, hobbies, potatoes.**

### 🧺 Hobbits are plural

- Important corrective:

> **Not every Hobbit is the same.**

- The Prologue describes a people and a social ecology, not a standardised behavioural profile.

- Within that ecology there is room for:

- homebodies;
- travellers;
- gardeners;
- readers;
- gossips;
- officials;
- wealthy eccentrics;
- craftspeople;
- people who know family history;
- people who think the person reading all those books is a bit weird.

- Bilbo’s unusualness only works because Tolkien has already supplied a Hobbit baseline against which Bilbo can be unusual.

- So the claim is not:

> **British people are all Bilbo Baggins.**

- It is closer to:

> **the social ecology in which Bilbo Baggins makes sense requires remarkably little imaginative translation from the British Isles.**

### 🗺️ Beating the bounds: the territory is remembered by walking it

- The mention of **beating the bounds** is wonderfully specific.

- It is not merely quaint decoration or a cadastral line with a vicar attached.

- The practice carries a model of locality in which:

- boundaries are physically encountered;
- communal memory is embodied;
- territory is periodically re-known;
- governance is tied to people moving through the place;
- the landscape can remain simultaneously administrative, social, sacred and folkloric.

- Useful formulation:

> **The official does not simply possess a map. The community knows where the boundary is because people periodically fucking walk it.**

- This is part of the Shire’s boundedness.

- The line matters.

- The fact that there is a world beyond the line does not mean the line is hostile.

- It means:

> **this is here; that is there; please stop assuming every boundary is merely an invitation to expand.**

- There is also a useful comparative intuition with Indigenous and other land-relational traditions in which orientation, ceremony, protection, memory and relationship with place are not neatly separable into modern administrative categories.

- The traditions are not equivalent.

- The useful comparison is conceptual:

> **land can be relationship, memory and obligation rather than merely surface area.**

### 🌾 Pastoral and seasonal substrate

- The Prologue’s Hobbit world is already strongly coded through seasonal and pastoral practices.

- Things to retain:

- spring / May traditions;
- harvest rhythms;
- local offices;
- boundary customs;
- food;
- land;
- family;
- recurring community ritual.

- These practices may now be described as Christian, civic or customary while retaining much older folkloric and seasonal texture.

- Useful working idea:

> explanations can sometimes function as **permission structures** for practices whose cultural life is older and messier than the explanation.

- This is relevant to Tolkien because his pastoral Englishness is not simply “church + village + nice fields”.

- It is a **layered folk landscape**.

### ✉️ Correspondence is infrastructure

- The amount of letter-writing and correspondence is itself culturally informative.

- Hobbit sociality can be:

- highly connected;
- information-rich;
- relationally dense;
- physically bounded.

- Those things are not contradictory.

- The wider world can enter through:

- letters;
- books;
- stories;
- visitors;
- gossip;
- correspondence.

- It does not therefore require the Hobbit to desire constant physical access, travel or integration.

- Very Hobbit communication principle:

> **I care about you enormously. Here are four pages about the garden. Please do not interpret this as permission to appear unexpectedly at my house.**

- Boundedness ≠ isolation.

- Curiosity ≠ aspiration to leave.

### 🔤 Tolkien likes playing with English

- The Prologue immediately establishes Tolkien’s pleasure in treating language as something people **use, bend, inherit and play with**, not merely something grammarians police.

### “Tweens”

- The Hobbit twenties as **tweens** are funny because the word treats the period between childhood and coming-of-age as a legitimate social category.

- The Shire’s answer to premature adulthood appears to be:

> **stop fucking rushing everybody.**

### “Eleventy-one”

- `eleventy-one` works by taking a perfectly intelligible English numerical pattern and extending it beyond the point where convention permits it.

- seventy-one;
- eighty-one;
- ninety-one;
- therefore:
- **eleventy-one.**

- The humour is systematic rather than random.

- The form is “wrong”, but the rule is immediately legible.

### “Drownded”

- `drownded` belongs to a different but related register:

- historical / dialectal;
- vernacular;
- child-adjacent;
- rural;
- expressive;
- completely intelligible.

- The important point is not “Hobbit says word incorrectly”.

- It is:

> **Hobbits have their own English texture.**

### Carroll adjacency

- Working comparison:

- not a claim that Tolkien simply derived the forms from Lewis Carroll;
- not an influence proceeding;
- nobody has subpoenaed Carroll.

- The adjacency is in **play style**:

> take English seriously enough to notice that its rules are peculiar, then mischievously extend or manipulate one of those rules.

- Carroll often uses linguistic logic as a toy.

- Tolkien does too — except Tolkien has a pathological tendency to give the joke **infrastructure**.

### 🏛️ Oxford town / gown as social substrate

- A useful lens to keep open:

> Tolkien lived inside a place where multiple social worlds occupied the same geography while possessing radically different status, horizons and forms of knowledge.

- Oxford is simultaneously:

- university;
- city;
- colleges;
- workers;
- pubs;
- neighbourhoods;
- local politics;
- international prestige;
- ordinary life.

- An outsider can interact extensively with **Oxford University** and sincerely imagine they have experienced *Oxford*.

- Town:

> **hello.**

- This does not require:

> Shire = Oxford town  
> Elves = Oxford gowns

- That would flatten the text into precisely the kind of one-to-one allegory Tolkien warns against.

- The more useful proposition is:

> **Tolkien knew intimately what it was like for prestige knowledge and ordinary local knowledge to coexist without being the same social world.**

- Keep this lens available as the actual narrative begins.

### 💍 Elves: suspiciously gentleman-coded

- Emerging class thought:

- are the Elves partly coded through the British social vocabulary of the **cultivated gentleman / gentry class**?

- Very:

- ancient lineage;
- cultivated speech;
- inherited cultural capital;
- excellent education;
- beautiful things;
- artistic competence;
- elaborate manners;
- apparently enough leisure to become good at absolutely fucking everything.

- Essentially:

> **people Austen would immediately start observing for defects.**

- Tolkien seems genuinely to admire:

- learning;
- beauty;
- refinement;
- tradition;
- craftsmanship.

- But none of those automatically establish **moral superiority**.

- Possible Austen-adjacent test:

> “Yes, he has breeding, manners, education and a lovely house.”

- **But what is he actually like?**

- Tolkien edition:

> “Yes, you are an immortal linguistic aristocrat glowing faintly beneath the stars.”

- **But are you a dick?**

- Hobbits therefore make an excellent class counterweight:

- Elves → enormous inherited cultural capital.
- Hobbits → enormous opinions about dinner.
- Tolkien → **both of these people are morally serious.**

- Watch whether the book repeatedly separates:

- rank from virtue;
- refinement from goodness;
- education from wisdom;
- cultural prestige from moral authority.

- Provisional:

> Tolkien may not be anti-elite at all. He may simply be extremely British about demanding that **the gentleman demonstrate he is actually a gentleman**.

- Jane Austen has entered the fantasy department.

### 🧠 Knowledge can be the objective

- A major Hobbit / Elf translation problem:

> **some people experience knowing things as intrinsically rewarding.**

- The activity can be:

- information → understanding → classification → writing → satisfaction because now it is set out properly.

- There need not be:

- monetisation;
- career advancement;
- leadership;
- influence;
- strategic deployment;
- a Whole Thing.

- Sometimes:

> “But what are you going to do with it?”

- **Read it, probably.**

- Tolkien himself is an almost comically strong demonstration of the problem:

- learns enormous amounts of mythology, history and philology;
- invents languages;
- gives the languages histories;
- gives the histories peoples;
- gives the peoples migrations;
- gives the migrations geography;
- gives the geography maps;
- gives the societies calendars.

- Imagined behavioural analytics:

> **EXTREME STRATEGIC CAPABILITY ACCUMULATION**

- John:

> **I LIKE WORDS.**

- Normal Hobbit behaviour.

### 🪜 Leisure is not failed productivity

- Another working proposition:

> **Leisure has positive utility.**

- Not:

> leisure = recovery period required to restore worker productivity.

- But:

> **leisure = part of the thing the human is alive to experience.**

- Hobbit leisure can mean:

- cider in the garden;
- cricket somewhere across the field;
- reading;
- writing;
- collecting;
- gardening;
- making;
- learning;
- spending six hours on a question because it became interesting.

- High-effort leisure remains leisure.

> **I HAVE ACCIDENTALLY READ FOUR BOOKS ABOUT MEDIEVAL BOUNDARY CUSTOMS AND IT IS 3 A.M.**

- This is perfectly normal fucking behaviour.

- The absence of an external objective is not missing data.

- It is the answer.
