# 🧙 Tolkien Intermission — Catholicism, Anglo-Saxon, Ireland & Changing England

- Intermission notes arising while reading *The Fellowship of the Ring*.
- Actual origin of this entire research detour:

> **Some of this is way too specifically Irish Catholic coded, bro. You met us.**

- This then produced a series of increasingly specific questions:
  - Was Tolkien's familiarity primarily literary?
  - Was it acquired through the Army?
  - How deeply did he actually know medieval Irish material?
  - What Catholic communities did he grow up within?
  - What kind of Catholic community did he choose as an adult?
  - Did those communities give him sustained contact with living Irish / Irish-descended Catholic culture?
  - How does this intersect with his professional specialism in Anglo-Saxon and his wider philological knowledge?
- The point is **not currently to establish a direct line of influence**.
- It is to establish the information and cultural environments Tolkien actually inhabited before deciding what any resemblance in *The Lord of the Rings* means.

---

## 🕯️ Newman, Neri & the Birmingham Oratory

- Tolkien's childhood Catholic formation brings us into the Birmingham Oratory and therefore into the tradition of:
  - St Philip Neri;
  - John Henry Newman;
  - the Congregation of the Oratory;
  - eventually the Confederation of Oratories of St Philip Neri.
- Philip Neri's Oratory is an interesting institutional form:
  - secular priests and brothers living communally;
  - no conventional religious vows;
  - stability within a particular local Oratory;
  - considerable autonomy between individual Oratories;
  - personal property can be retained;
  - communal prayer and meals alongside substantial individual activity;
  - members work in ordinary social environments: parishes, education, hospitals, spiritual direction, administration, etc.
- Frederick William Faber described the Oratorian charism as:
  - **“a spirituality of everyday life.”**
- Philip Neri himself emphasised engagement with ordinary people rather than religious withdrawal.
- This produces a useful Tolkien joke/model:
  - **the Oratorians are not the Hobbits;**
  - they are potentially closer to **a collection of Gandalf the Greys who have concluded that the spirituality of the Hobbits is extremely important.**
- The specialist religious vocation does not require everyone else to adopt the specialist vocation.
- Ordinary domestic/community life can itself be something worth sustaining.

---

## 🏡 Adult Tolkien's Catholic practice

- Important not to assume that Tolkien remained institutionally Oratorian simply because the Birmingham Oratory was formative.
- The more interesting question:
  - **when adult Tolkien had choices about Catholic practice, what kind of Catholic environment did he actually choose?**
- Oxford gave him plenty of possibilities:
  - university Catholicism;
  - Jesuits;
  - Dominicans;
  - grander and more intellectually specialised religious environments;
  - ordinary neighbourhood parishes.
- The available evidence points substantially towards **ordinary geographically embedded parish Catholicism**.
- His later Oxford/Headington life included local parish participation rather than exclusively seeking prestigious or unusually elaborate Catholic institutions.
- There is evidence of Tolkien serving as a **reader** in his local Catholic environment.
- This is potentially interesting alongside the recurring importance in Tolkien of:
  - household;
  - locality;
  - ordinary life;
  - food;
  - neighbours;
  - continuity;
  - belonging somewhere.

## ☘️ The Irish-Catholic question

- A separate research question emerged:
  - **where did Tolkien have sustained contact with living Irish / Irish-descended Catholic communities?**
- This matters because some things appearing in the *Fellowship* reading are prompting comparisons with Irish heroic/storytelling traditions, particularly material around the *Táin Bó Cúailnge*.
- The initial reaction was not:
  - **“Tolkien borrowed this from the *Táin*.”**
- It was much closer to:
  - **“This is culturally specific enough that I want to know whether you actually knew Irish Catholics, Professor.”**
- Some resonances feel sufficiently culturally literate that they raise the question of **sustained familiarity**, rather than anecdotal exposure.
- The point is NOT:
  - Tolkien heard one Irish story from somebody over a pint and copied it.
- Tolkien already had substantial scholarly access to Irish material.
- He explicitly said that he knew “Celtic things”, including material in **Irish and Welsh in their original languages**.
- Therefore there are potentially several overlapping routes:
  - formal philological study;
  - medieval Irish literature;
  - Catholic social environments;
  - Birmingham's substantial Irish population;
  - wartime encounters;
  - friendships and professional networks.
- These should not be collapsed into one another.
- The research question is therefore:
  - **where did Tolkien's deep textual knowledge of medieval Irish culture meet living Irish Catholic culture?**

### ☘️ St Anne's / Birmingham

- Tolkien's early Catholic life included **St Anne's in Birmingham** before the family's deeper relationship with the Birmingham Oratory.
- Digbeth and surrounding inner-Birmingham districts had substantial Irish Catholic populations.
- This therefore gives us a possible route for **living Irish Catholic social contact from childhood**, entirely independently of Tolkien's later Army service.
- Needs more demographic/parish research before making a strong claim about the precise composition of Tolkien's congregation.
- But it means the Army is not required as the mechanism by which Tolkien could first have encountered Irish Catholic people and culture.

## 📚 Old English is not “Anglo-Saxon”

- Important distinction arising during the rabbit hole:
  - **Old English = a language.**
  - **Anglo-Saxon = a substantially broader historical/cultural field and referent.**
- Tolkien knew and worked extensively with Old English.
- Separately, in 1925 he became:
  - **Rawlinson and Bosworth Professor of Anglo-Saxon at Oxford.**
- The latter fact is interesting in its own right.
- His scholarly world therefore encompassed much more than simply knowing Old English:
  - early English texts;
  - literature;
  - philology;
  - Germanic linguistic relationships;
  - Old Norse/Icelandic material;
  - interactions among peoples, languages and traditions across the islands and northern Europe.
- Tolkien also possessed serious knowledge of Welsh and Irish material.
- Consequently his intellectual picture of these islands could hardly consist of:
  - “Angles and Saxons arrive → Normans arrive → England happens.”
- Serious philology keeps exposing:
  - migration;
  - borrowing;
  - displacement;
  - multilingual contact;
  - renamed places;
  - inherited stories;
  - cultural strata;
  - texts surviving political rupture;
  - movement between the islands.

## 🎓 Oxford appointing a Catholic Tolkien

- Another contextual point which is easy to miss from the twenty-first century:
  - **Tolkien being a conspicuously practising Roman Catholic Oxford professor was not historically unremarkable.**
- Oxford had previously enforced Anglican religious tests.
- The Universities Tests Act **1871** removed major remaining religious barriers to university positions.
- Restrictions affecting Catholic participation at Oxford therefore belonged to extremely recent institutional history when Tolkien was young.
- Tolkien received the Rawlinson and Bosworth chair in **1925**:
  - only 54 years after the 1871 Act.
- We should NOT claim without evidence that Oxford selected Tolkien *because* his Catholic background would produce a more plural understanding of early England.
- There is no need for that claim.
- The interesting fact is simpler:
  - Oxford had a chair in Anglo-Saxon;
  - an earlier Oxford would have formally excluded Catholics from much of its institutional life;
  - the Oxford of 1925 appointed the scholar it judged suitable despite Tolkien being conspicuously Catholic.
- Joking description:
  - **proto-DEI Oxford: “Apparently we are simply going to appoint the Catholic because the bastard is extremely good at Anglo-Saxon.”**
- This is genuinely a marker of institutional change.
- It also matters that Catholic identity in England could overlap socially with anti-Irish prejudice and anxieties about national loyalty.
- Catholicism and Irishness should **not** be treated as synonymous.
- But neither should we erase the extent to which English anti-Catholic and anti-Irish prejudice historically interacted.
- A Catholic scholar taking a prestigious Oxford chair concerned with early English culture therefore sits within a much larger history of changing definitions of who was allowed to count as an authoritative interpreter of England.

## 🪖 World War I & the changing social system

- This needs to sit inside the much larger transformation of British society across the First World War.
- Pre-1914 English society could be **profoundly restrictive**.
- Access and belonging could be regulated through:
  - religion;
  - class;
  - sex;
  - family;
  - school;
  - accent;
  - property;
  - profession;
  - institutional networks;
  - the judgement that somebody simply wasn't “the right sort”.
- Some exclusions were statutory.
- Others were maintained socially and institutionally without needing legislation.
- The First World War did not magically make Britain tolerant.
- It did, however, **break or destabilise mechanisms through which exclusion had previously reproduced itself**.
- Enormous death and disability among a generation of young men mattered.
- So did:
  - mass mobilisation;
  - movement between regions and classes;
  - women's wartime labour;
  - new institutional responsibilities;
  - the simple practical requirement to use capable people whom an earlier system might have excluded.
- Tolkien belongs almost perfectly to this hinge generation:
  - born **1892**;
  - raised inside late-Victorian/Edwardian structures;
  - fought in the First World War;
  - survived the Somme;
  - entered his mature academic career in the society produced by that rupture;
  - became an Oxford professor in **1925**.
- Important:
  - this does **not** mean Tolkien got his professorship because other men died.
  - His scholarship stands independently.
- But the Oxford making appointments in 1925 existed in a profoundly different demographic and social environment from the Oxford of 1905.
- Another enormous transformation follows the Second World War.
- Therefore it is misleading to imagine modern British social hierarchy projected backwards with slightly fancier clothes.
- Earlier versions could be **much more formally and informally exclusionary**.
- Useful shorthand:
  - **If you think we're stuffy now, you should have seen the previous build.**

## 🇪🇸 Tolkien's politics & the Franco problem

- Tolkien's political comments produce an interesting contradiction.
- He was deeply suspicious of concentrated political authority.
- He wrote approvingly of philosophical anarchism understood as the **abolition of control**, rather than political violence.
- He was sceptical about people seeking authority over other people.
- Power and its capacity to corrupt are obviously major concerns within his fiction.
- At the same time:
  - he was intensely anti-communist;
  - he was sympathetic towards Franco's Nationalists during the Spanish Civil War;
  - he believed some criticism of Franco amounted to “Red Propaganda”.
- His Catholic information environment matters here.
- Anti-Catholic violence in Republican territory was real.
- Clergy and religious communities were murdered.
- Tolkien received testimony concerning this violence from people he trusted.
- There was also genuine anti-Catholic sentiment within parts of the wider political environment discussing Spain.
- It is therefore understandable how Tolkien could develop a defensive prior:
  - Catholics really are being persecuted;
  - some observers really are minimising that persecution;
  - therefore information from those observers about Franco becomes suspect.
- But:
  - **the validity of the first propositions does not establish the validity of the last one.**
- Franco's regime itself exercised enormous information control.
- Evidence of repression was hidden, destroyed, dispersed or simply never created.
- Spain is still dealing with:
  - mass graves;
  - archival reconstruction;
  - missing people;
  - suppressed documentation;
  - contested historical memory.
- Tolkien died in **1973**.
- Franco died in **1975**.
- Portugal's neighbouring Estado Novo dictatorship also survived Tolkien:
  - Tolkien died September 1973;
  - the Carnation Revolution occurred April 1974.
- Tolkien therefore never lived in the later European information environment in which the dictatorships had fallen and their records and victims could progressively become more visible.
- We should NOT assume:
  - Tolkien knew everything now documented about Francoism and approved anyway.
- Nor should we assume:
  - Tolkien knew nothing about Nationalist violence.
- Better historical question:
  - **what information was available to Tolkien, through which networks, at which time, and what did his existing beliefs cause him to trust or distrust?**
- Particularly interesting because Tolkien himself understood propaganda as a problem.
- His mistake may therefore illustrate something nastier:
  - **good principles do not protect somebody from a distorted information environment.**
  - A false model can become particularly resistant to correction when it has attached itself to something the observer is *right* to care about.

## 🕸️ Why any of this may matter for *Fellowship*

- None of this should become:
  - **Tolkien biography explains Tolkien novel.**
- It gives us additional environments in which to understand what Tolkien knew and experienced.
- Particularly worth holding while reading:
  - his professional understanding of cultural transmission;
  - his deep knowledge of language;
  - his exposure to multiple traditions across these islands;
  - his Catholic minority identity;
  - possible sustained contact with Irish Catholic communities;
  - his attachment to ordinary parish life;
  - his suspicion of concentrated authority;
  - his experience of WWI;
  - his position across enormous changes in British social organisation.
- The emerging *Táin* question should therefore remain open:
  - not merely **“Did Tolkien borrow from Irish mythology?”**
  - but **“What did Tolkien actually understand about Irish storytelling, social behaviour and cultural forms — textually and through living communities?”**
- Worth returning to the particular *Fellowship* passages that triggered the Irish-recognition response and testing them individually.
- Do not prematurely convert resemblance into influence.
- But equally:
  - **do not underestimate the professor's cultural literacy.**
