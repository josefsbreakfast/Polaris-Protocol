# 🌳 FotR — Intermission 01

*Working notes on Edith's bright eyes, Tír na nÓg, Irish and Catholic cultural transmission, Saint Sam, the Machine, empire, provenance, and what happens when the metadata falls off.*

## 🧭 Orientation — this is a reading from somewhere

- This is **not** an attempt to establish the One Correct Tolkien Interpretation.

- These are reflections produced while I am reading *The Fellowship of the Ring* from the particular place where I actually stand:
  - as a woman;
  - as somebody embodying more than one identity;
  - as somebody carrying particular British, Irish-adjacent, minority, regional, gendered and historical lookup tables;
  - as somebody already interested in information environments, cultural transmission, institutions, power and the ways people carry knowledge between systems.

- I am not an abstract eyeball floating six feet above the text.

- I have a body.
- I have a history.
- I have cultural memories and associations.
- I notice some things because of those things.
- Another reader will notice other things because they arrive carrying different equipment.

- Therefore:
  - **“this is what I recognise”** is not the same claim as **“this is what Tolkien consciously intended”**;
  - **“this reminds me of X”** is not the same claim as **“Tolkien directly sourced this from X”**;
  - **“this text can function this way for me”** is not the same claim as **“this is the only legitimate meaning of the text”**;
  - **explicitly labelled head canon remains head canon and does not require the robot to call Scotland Yard.**

- There are several different layers available here:
  1. Tolkien's conscious intentions.
  2. Demonstrable textual sources.
  3. The cultural environments available to Tolkien.
  4. Tacit / social / vernacular knowledge circulating inside those environments.
  5. Structures that ended up in the finished text.
  6. What those structures make available to readers.
  7. What a particular reader recognises because of the cultural equipment they bring.
  8. What happens to all of that when the text travels internationally.

- These layers can interact without being collapsed into one another.

- Most importantly:

> **AUTHORIAL INTENT IS NOT THE SAME QUESTION AS TEXTUAL FUNCTION.**

- Approximately ten times during the original conversation:

> **LAWYER LAWYER LAWYER.**
>
> **I AM TALKING ABOUT ITS FUNCTION AS A TEXT BRO.**

- Methodological instruction for Future Robit:

> Do not mistake interpretive analysis for a factual allegation requiring proof of authorial intent.
>
> Do not mistake cultural pattern-recognition for a claim of direct derivation.
>
> Do not mistake explicitly labelled head canon for historical reconstruction.
>
> **The human knows. Keep up.**

## 👁️ Edith's bright eyes — before Saint Sam, there was an actual woman

- One of the bits that absolutely must not get flattened out of these notes is Tolkien remembering young Edith.

- The image is intensely sensory:
  - dark/raven hair;
  - clear skin;
  - bright eyes;
  - singing;
  - dancing;
  - woodland;
  - hemlocks;
  - a young woman visibly and physically **alive**.

- What struck me wasn't merely “Edith inspired Lúthien.”

- It was the way Tolkien remembers **her**.

- This is not an abstract memorial to Wife™.

- He remembers a particular young woman moving in front of him.

- Singing.

- Dancing.

- Her eyes bright.

- And the later recollection carries the extraordinary emotional information that **her eyes were brighter than Christopher had known them**.

- Christopher knew his mother.

- Ronald is remembering the young Edith whom *he* knew before Christopher existed.

- There is something extremely tender in:

> **You knew her.**
>
> **But I remember her eyes brighter than you ever saw them.**

- And reading that **as a woman** does something.

- “Bright eyes” are ordinary language, but ordinary language can carry enormous embodied information:
  - animation;
  - pleasure;
  - excitement;
  - attraction;
  - youth;
  - mischief;
  - delight;
  - somebody being visibly more alive.

- It sits in the broader literary neighbourhood of women's **fine eyes** — not because we need to establish Tolkien was doing an Austen bit, but because English literary culture has long known that a woman's eyes can communicate rather a lot without anybody having to write a technical memorandum concerning why.

- The scholarly interpretation:

> **Edith appears to have been experiencing considerable animation.**

- The Survivor's Voice interpretation:

> **BRO HAVE YOU SEEN THIS BITCH'S EYES.**

- Tolkien, approximately:

> **LAD.**
>
> **HER EYES.**

- And this matters because the later **Women's Eye-Brightening Studies** joke did not descend randomly from the horny heavens.

- Edith is the upstream metadata.

## 🗣️ Vernacular knowledge — the serious thing underneath Saint Sam

- Saint Sam is funny because the mechanism is real.

- Perfectly ordinary knowledge can circulate through **folk-like / vernacular transmission** without being folklore in the sense of myth.

- Folk-like here describes the **network**:
  - socially distributed;
  - unevenly held;
  - often oral;
  - often practical;
  - adapted to circumstances;
  - dependent on relationships and trust;
  - poorly represented in formal documentary records.

- Formal culture might tell you what respectable people are **supposed** to know.

- Vernacular culture is frequently how somebody actually tells you what you **need** to know.

- Knowledge can move:
  - friend → friend;
  - soldier → soldier;
  - sibling → sibling;
  - older person → younger person;
  - servant/batman → officer;
  - colleague → colleague;
  - woman → woman;
  - man → man;
  - pub → brain;
  - barracks → brain;
  - joke → brain;
  - demonstration → brain.

- Somebody knows something.

- Somebody else does not.

- Social proximity creates an opportunity.

- Knowledge transfers.

- Recipient incorporates it.

- Forty years later:

> **HISTORIAN:** I can find no evidence this was taught.
>
> **SAINT SAM:** bro.

- Future historian searches the official curriculum.

- **Absolutely fuck all.**

- This is not a trivial methodological problem.

- Historical archives structurally preserve some forms of knowledge better than others.

- People inhabit **information ecologies**, not syllabuses.

## ✝️ Irish myth and Catholic culture are not sealed laboratory samples

- Do not reproduce a modern categorical fantasy in which:
  - pre-Christian Irish mythology lives in Box A;
  - Christianity arrives;
  - Box A is sealed;
  - Catholic culture begins in Box B;
  - nobody ever carries information between them again.

- Cultural transmission is messier.

- Older stories can survive through Christian manuscript cultures.

- Traditions can be transformed.

- Stories can be retold.

- Older imaginative structures can remain culturally available inside later religious worlds.

- People do not necessarily experience this as:
  
  **TODAY I SHALL ACCESS THE PAGAN DATABASE.**

- They may experience it as:
  
  **a story people know.**

- Which is exactly why provenance can become difficult later.

## 🏛️ The British Empire can be caught by the machinery too

- This is central.

> **Functionally, *The Lord of the Rings* can serve for this reader as a criticism of the British Empire from within the cultural and institutional apparatus of the British Empire itself.**

- That is more interesting than:

> **Mordor = Britain.**

- One-to-one substitution can actually make political reading **safer**.

- If Mordor = designated historical enemy, the reader can say:

> **Yes. Those people are terrible. Very good.**

- End of moral labour.

- If the text instead models mechanisms:
  - domination;
  - extraction;
  - instrumentalisation;
  - coercive benevolence;
  - superior knowledge becoming political entitlement;
  - classification by usefulness;
  - landscapes subordinated to production;
  - good people adopting the enemy's dominating tools;

  then the reader can ask:

> **Where is this happening here?**

- And Britain does not get an exemption merely because Tolkien is British.

- The text emerged from a man who lived substantially **inside British institutional life**.

- Army.

- Oxford.

- Respectable professional culture.

## 🫥 Successful forgetting does not require destroying the thing

- This is where the Tolkien ramble connects to something much larger.

- Cultural erasure does not always look like:
  - banning;
  - burning;
  - destroying;
  - nobody knowing the material anymore.

- Sometimes the material survives **brilliantly**.

- Everyone loves it.

- Everyone knows it.

- Everyone copies it.

- It becomes foundational.

- What disappears is knowledge of some of the ancestry.

- That can happen through:
  - canon formation;
  - publishing;
  - education;
  - migration;
  - linguistic dominance;
  - cultural prestige;
  - repeated simplification;
  - international export;
  - ordinary metadata loss.

- No villain necessarily needs to press the giant **ERASE CULTURE** button.

- Which makes the end state more disturbing:

> **Everyone knows the descendant.**
>
> **Almost nobody knows one of its ancestors.**

- Hence:

> **WE KNOW WHAT SUCCESSFUL FORGETTING LOOKS LIKE AFTER IT SUCCEEDS.**

- And:

> **People can preserve the fruit extraordinarily well while gradually forgetting some of the trees it grew on.**

- Cultural survival and cultural silencing are therefore not necessarily opposites.

- A culture can survive **inside its descendants** while becoming progressively harder to identify as an ancestor.
