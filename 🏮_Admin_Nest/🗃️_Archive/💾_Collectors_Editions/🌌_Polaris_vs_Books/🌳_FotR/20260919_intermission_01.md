# 🌳 FotR — Intermission 01

tldr: John Tolkien, antifascist king.  

*Working notes — cultural transmission, textual function, Catholicism, empire, and why the robot needs to stop demanding the lady prove her credentials.*

## 🪞 The observation

- Reading *The Fellowship of the Ring* from inside the cultural environment of Britain and Ireland can make some things visible that may be less legible once Tolkien has travelled through the international Anglophone fantasy tradition.
- This is **not**:
  - laughing at North American readers for failing to recognise something;
  - claiming that Tolkien is secretly writing Irish mythology;
  - claiming that Hobbiton is literally Ireland;
  - reducing Tolkien to a Catholic allegory;
  - establishing authorial intent.
- It is an observation about:
  - cultural transmission;
  - cultural legibility;
  - provenance;
  - what gets flattened under labels such as “British,” “English,” “Celtic,” or simply “fantasy”;
  - what a text can *do* irrespective of whether the author consciously formulated that function.

## 🏝️ Britain and Ireland are not one cultural blob

- International Anglophone reception can understandably receive material from these islands as broadly:
  - British;
  - English;
  - UK;
  - Celtic;
  - medieval European;
  - generic fantasy.
- From inside the islands, those categories contain enormous internal differences.
- English, Irish, Scottish, Welsh and Northern Irish histories are not interchangeable.
- Neither are their relationships with:
  - Catholicism;
  - Protestantism;
  - the British state;
  - empire;
  - class;
  - land;
  - folklore;
  - language;
  - migration;
  - nationalism;
  - industrialisation.
- “Catholic” does not erase those distinctions either.
- English Catholic culture is not simply Irish Catholic culture occurring in England.
- But English Catholic culture also did not develop in isolation from Irish Catholic people, migration, institutions and cultural transmission.
- The interesting question is therefore not:
  - **Was Tolkien culturally Irish?**
- It is closer to:
  - **What cultural worlds were available to an English Catholic man of Tolkien's generation, and what kinds of knowledge travelled through them?**

## 🍻 The people after Mass problem

- Literary influence is not restricted to identifiable source texts.
- Culture also travels through:
  - families;
  - churches;
  - pubs;
  - schools;
  - friendships;
  - regiments;
  - workplaces;
  - neighbourhoods;
  - jokes;
  - stories;
  - food;
  - rituals;
  - ordinary expectations about how people should behave.
- Hence the deliberately informal formulation:
  - **the people Tolkien went to the pub with after Mass.**
- This is not a biographical claim about particular drinking companions.
- It is a reminder that culture is embodied socially.
- A writer can absorb a cultural grammar without thinking:
  - “I shall now incorporate Cultural Influence X into Chapter Y.”

## 🌳 “Oh. I know these people.”

- Some elements of Hobbiton / the Shire can therefore produce recognition that is different from identifying a literary allusion.
- The reaction is less:
  - “I have located the source.”
- More:
  - **“Oh. I know this cultural grammar.”**
- Things that may participate in that recognition include:
  - food;
  - hospitality;
  - local memory;
  - storytelling;
  - irritating relatives;
  - gardens;
  - community;
  - feasting;
  - music;
  - ordinary domestic pleasures;
  - attachment to place;
  - suspicion of grandiosity;
  - the coexistence of the mundane and supernatural;
  - a strong sense that ordinary life is itself something worth protecting.
- None of those things is exclusively Irish or Catholic.
- Their clustering and treatment can nevertheless resonate differently depending upon the reader's cultural library.

## 📦 Tolkien as an Anglophone export

- Tolkien becomes enormously influential internationally.
- His work then becomes one of the sources through which later readers learn what fantasy itself looks like.
- This can create a transmission chain resembling:

> cultural traditions → Tolkien's synthesis → international Anglophone reception → modern fantasy conventions

- Once that happens, something inherited through Tolkien can become recognisable simply as:
  - “Tolkien stuff”;
  - then eventually:
  - “fantasy stuff.”
- The upstream provenance becomes progressively harder to see.
- A reader cannot be expected to recognise metadata that was never transmitted to them.

## 🫥 Silencing and provenance

- This makes the reception history interesting without making individual readers culpable.
- A culture can remain influential while becoming difficult to identify as an influence.
- Something can therefore:
  1. survive;
  2. travel;
  3. become absorbed into a culturally dominant form;
  4. lose visible attribution;
  5. eventually appear to have originated primarily in the dominant form.
- This helps explain why later cultural silencing can be painful.
- The injury is not necessarily:
  - “you failed to give us credit for this one thing.”
- It can be:
  - **we know what successful forgetting looks like.**
- People can preserve the fruit beautifully while forgetting some of the trees it grew on.

## 🎩 Tolkien inside English respectability

- Tolkien simultaneously inhabited a highly respectable English institutional world:
  - officer;
  - academic;
  - Oxford professor;
  - philologist;
  - participant in institutions embedded within British national and imperial history.
- He was not writing from a position outside British society.
- He was culturally and materially inside substantial parts of its machinery.
- That means the interesting tension is not:
  - outsider versus establishment.
- It is:
  - **a person inhabiting several overlapping systems whose values and histories do not perfectly agree.**

## ⛪ Catholicism inside that respectability

- English Catholicism itself had a historically complicated relationship with dominant English national culture.
- Catholic identity could therefore coexist with participation in respectable English institutions while carrying:
  - minority memory;
  - experiences of exclusion;
  - different institutional loyalties;
  - different moral traditions;
  - cultural contact with communities whose relationships with British power were substantially more antagonistic.
- None of this automatically makes Tolkien anti-imperialist.
- It does make his cultural location more complicated than:
  - **English Oxford professor writes English mythology.**

## ⚙️ Marx and Tolkien are doing different things

- Useful comparison, without trying to turn Tolkien into a Marxist:
- Marx is interested in getting into the machinery:
  - structures;
  - capital;
  - labour;
  - class;
  - accumulation;
  - reproduction of power.
- Tolkien's fiction can do something different:
  - construct an environment in which the reader experiences what configurations of power do to:
    - people;
    - relationships;
    - landscapes;
    - communities;
    - moral judgement;
    - desire;
    - knowledge.
- One can analyse the machine.
- Fiction can let somebody inhabit a world in which the machine is operating.

## 🏭 The Machine

- A recurring functional problem in Tolkien is not simply:
  - bad people possess power.
- It is:
  - domination;
  - instrumentalisation;
  - treating living things as resources;
  - converting knowledge into control;
  - believing superior wisdom creates entitlement to govern others;
  - accepting coercive tools because the intended outcome is supposedly good;
  - reproducing an enemy's logic in order to defeat the enemy.
- This is why substituting a single political power for Mordor can actually make the text *less* interesting.
- If:

> Mordor = Political Power X

- then everybody who is not Political Power X gets to leave.
- If the problem is instead a **reproducible relationship with power**, nobody automatically gets an exemption.

## 💍 Applicability is the dangerous bit

- The text does not need to say:
  - Britain = Mordor;
  - Germany = Mordor;
  - Rome = Mordor;
  - Empire = Mordor.
- Its function can be more subversive precisely because it does **not** permanently allocate the moral machinery to one historical actor.
- The reader is instead given tools for recognising a pattern.
- Those tools can subsequently be applied to:
  - imperial power;
  - anti-imperial power;
  - states;
  - movements;
  - institutions;
  - enemies;
  - allies;
  - oneself.
- The text can therefore criticise the logic of imperial domination from **inside an imperial society** without operating as a one-to-one allegory of that society.

## 🧠 AUTHORIAL INTENT IS NOT THE QUESTION, ROBOT

- Important methodological correction following an outbreak of lawyer-brain:
- The question here is **the function of the text**.
- It is not necessary to establish that Tolkien consciously decided:

> “I am going to write a subversive criticism of British imperial power.”

- A text is produced through:
  - language;
  - culture;
  - experience;
  - institutions;
  - inherited stories;
  - social relationships;
  - contradictions;
  - assumptions;
  - conscious choices;
  - unconscious knowledge.
- Those things can become embodied in a work without being reducible to an author's declared political programme.
- The text can consequently perform cultural and political work beyond what the author consciously intended.

> **LAWYER LAWYER LAWYER**
>
> **I AM TALKING ABOUT ITS FUNCTION AS A TEXT BRO**

- Useful corrective.
- Counsel has been removed from the building.

## 🪴 What are we actually fighting for?

- One of the strongest things Tolkien's fiction does is refuse to make resistance valuable merely because it defeats an enemy.
- The question underneath resistance is:
  - **what is worth preserving?**
- Again and again, the answer contains apparently small things:
  - meals;
  - gardens;
  - friendship;
  - songs;
  - stories;
  - homes;
  - hospitality;
  - local communities;
  - ordinary pleasures;
  - reciprocal obligations;
  - the ability to live without organising every relationship around domination.
- The smallness is not incidental.
- It establishes a purpose for power that is different from acquiring more power.

## 🧑‍🌾 My boy was doing praxis

- Not:
  - Tolkien was a Marxist;
  - Tolkien had a modern anti-colonial programme;
  - Tolkien consciously encoded a political manifesto.
- Rather:
  - the work repeatedly makes characters **practise values under pressure**.
- Fellowship is practised.
- Mercy is practised.
- Restraint is practised.
- Loyalty is practised.
- Refusal of domination is practised.
- Care for living things is practised.
- Ordinary life is preserved through action.
- Values are therefore not merely propositions sitting above the story.
- They become behaviours whose consequences the reader experiences.

> **My boy was doing praxis.**

## 🧨 The subversive contradiction

- This is perhaps the most interesting position:
- Tolkien can be:
  - culturally inside British power;
  - institutionally inside respectable England;
  - to some degree inevitably implicated in the structures surrounding him;
  - simultaneously formed by minority and adjacent cultural worlds that had complicated or adversarial histories with those structures.
- A person does not have to consciously theorise that contradiction for knowledge produced by living through it to enter their art.
- The resulting text can therefore become **subversive in function without being subversive by manifesto**.
- That may actually make it more durable.
- Readers do not merely learn:

> “Political Actor X is bad.”

- They are invited to experience:

> **“This is what domination does. This is how it reproduces itself. This is what it asks you to sacrifice. This is what you might need to refuse in yourself.”**

- That remains applicable when the reader turns back towards their own institutions.

## 🌌 Why the North American reception is useful

- North American reception is useful here as a **contrast dye**, not a target.
- Tolkien crossed the Atlantic extraordinarily successfully.
- Some of the cultural metadata surrounding the worlds that contributed to him did not necessarily travel with equal clarity.
- The resulting readings can therefore reveal what has become invisible in transmission.
- Likewise, readers inside Britain may miss the same signals when their own cultural location does not provide the relevant lookup table.
- “British” is not itself sufficient cultural metadata.
- Watching what different readers recognise can therefore teach us something about:
  - Tolkien;
  - fantasy;
  - Britain;
  - Ireland;
  - Anglophone cultural export;
  - and the internal diversity that the export category conceals.

## 🧷 Working thesis

> Tolkien does not need to have consciously written an anti-imperial allegory for *The Lord of the Rings* to function as a powerful criticism of domination from within an imperial cultural environment. The more interesting question is how the overlapping cultural worlds Tolkien inhabited — respectable English institutions, Catholic minority life, the histories and traditions circulating through Britain and Ireland, war, class, industrialisation and medieval literature — became metabolised into a text whose moral ecology can be applied back against the structures surrounding its author. International reception then provides a useful contrast: readers may inherit the finished synthesis while losing some of the cultural metadata that made its constituent traditions legible. That loss of provenance is itself instructive about how cultural silencing works.

## 🌊 Tír na nÓg — “Oh look, it’s Hobbiton”

- Tír na nÓg belongs in this intermission because it helped make the larger cultural-transmission problem visible.
- The point is **not**:
  - Tír na nÓg = Hobbiton;
  - Tolkien copied Tír na nÓg;
  - Irish mythology provides a single explanatory key for Tolkien.
- The recognition was instead:
  - **“Oh. I recognise this cultural grammar.”**
- Tír na nÓg is represented within Irish tradition as an Otherworld associated with:
  - beauty;
  - abundance;
  - joy;
  - music;
  - feasting;
  - altered relationships with ageing and mortality;
  - travel beyond ordinary geography;
  - thresholds including water, mist, caves and ancient mounds;
  - invitation into another mode of existence.
- Other names and associated Otherworld traditions include:
  - Tír Tairngire — Land of Promise;
  - Tír fo Thuinn — Land under the Wave;
  - Mag Mell — Plain of Delight;
  - Emain Ablach — Isle of Apple Trees.
- These are not uniquely “Tolkien things.”
- Nor are they evidence for a simple line of direct borrowing.
- They demonstrate the much richer mythological and cultural environment within which later fantasy becomes legible.
- Once Tolkien's synthesis becomes globally dominant, readers can encounter descendants or transformations of older cultural patterns first as:
  - Tolkien;
  - and subsequently as:
  - generic fantasy.
- Hence the delighted response:

> **Oh look, it’s Hobbiton.**

- Not a source claim.
- A recognition event.
- And therefore useful evidence for the larger question:
  - **what becomes visible when a reader possesses cultural metadata that another transmission pathway did not preserve?**

## 🗣️ Saint Sam and vernacular knowledge transmission

- A second, considerably sillier example nevertheless illustrates a serious cultural mechanism.
- Working head canon:
  - Tolkien's Samwise Gamgee may or may not owe anything whatsoever to:
  - **Saint Sam, Apostle of Women's Eye-Brightening Knowledge.**
- There is no evidentiary claim here that such a particular person existed.
- The euphemism is sufficient:
  - **women's eye-brightening knowledge**.
- Nothing more needs reconstructing.
- The useful historical point is that people did not acquire all ordinary practical knowledge through formal institutions.
- Formal systems might provide some categories of instruction:
  - church;
  - family;
  - school;
  - military;
  - professional hierarchy.
- But formal systems also had substantial gaps.
- Other knowledge travelled horizontally and informally:
  - friend to friend;
  - soldier to soldier;
  - servant / batman to officer;
  - older person to younger person;
  - sibling to sibling;
  - colleague to colleague;
  - pub conversation;
  - barracks conversation;
  - jokes;
  - stories;
  - warnings;
  - demonstrations;
  - accumulated practical experience.
- In that sense, perfectly ordinary knowledge could function through something resembling a **vernacular oral tradition**.
- Not “folklore” because the information was necessarily mythical.
- Folk-like because:
  - it was socially held;
  - unevenly distributed;
  - transmitted person-to-person;
  - adapted to circumstance;
  - dependent upon trust and social relationships;
  - frequently absent from respectable written accounts.

> **Formal culture tells you what respectable people are supposed to know.  
> Vernacular culture is frequently how somebody actually tells you what you need to know.**

- This matters for interpreting historical cultural environments.
- Looking only at:
  - published books;
  - official doctrine;
  - institutional rules;
  - respectable correspondence;
  - formal education;
- can substantially underestimate what people actually knew and how they learnt it.
- People inhabited **information ecologies**, not syllabuses.

## 🎖️ Samwise Gamgee Medal for Services to Women's Eye-Brightening Studies

- The running joke is useful enough to preserve because it contains the mechanism in miniature.
- Head-Canon Saint Sam does not represent a historical claim about one identifiable man.
- He represents the otherwise poorly documented human layer through which practical knowledge can travel.
- Someone knows something.
- Someone else does not.
- Social proximity permits transmission.
- The recipient subsequently discovers that this information has observable practical effects.
- Specifically:

> **“We could be sensible, or I could make her eyes that bright again.”**

- This is all the detail required.
- The medal may now be awarded.

> 🏅 **THE SAMWISE GAMGEE MEDAL**
>
> *For Distinguished Service to Women's Eye-Brightening Studies and the Vernacular Transmission of Useful Knowledge.*

- Saint Sam remains:
  - historically unverified;
  - methodologically dubious;
  - culturally illuminating;
  - **MY MAN, HONOURED FOR ALL AGES.**

## 🕸️ Why Saint Sam actually belongs in the argument

- The joke points back towards the larger Tolkien problem.
- Cultural influence does not require:
  - a citation;
  - a named source;
  - conscious adoption;
  - formal instruction.
- Human beings acquire enormous amounts of culture because:
  - somebody tells them something;
  - somebody demonstrates something;
  - everybody around them already behaves as though something is obvious;
  - stories circulate;
  - practices are repeated;
  - knowledge becomes ordinary enough that nobody records where it came from.
- That makes provenance exceptionally difficult to recover later.
- It also explains why examining only Tolkien's documented reading can never completely describe the cultural environment from which Tolkien emerged.
- Books matter.
- Manuscripts matter.
- Catholic doctrine matters.
- Army institutions matter.
- English respectability matters.
- Irish cultural transmission matters.
- **The people standing next to you matter too.**

- Sometimes culture travels through a medieval manuscript.
- Sometimes it travels through Mass.
- Sometimes it travels through a song.
- Sometimes it travels through somebody at the pub.
- And sometimes, in the sacred traditions of Head Canon:

> **Sam takes one look at the situation and realises the officer has not been adequately briefed.**
