# 🌳 FotR — Intermission 01

*Working notes on cultural transmission, Catholicism, empire, textual function, provenance, and the things a bibliography cannot tell you.*

<!-- TOP -->

## 🧭 Orientation

- This intermission records a set of observations arising while reading *The Fellowship of the Ring*.
- It is deliberately **not** an attempt to establish a single-source genealogy for Tolkien.
- It is interested in several related but distinct questions:
  - what Tolkien consciously read or intended;
  - what cultural knowledge was available within the worlds he inhabited;
  - what can enter a text through lived cultural transmission;
  - what the resulting text can **functionally do**, irrespective of conscious political programme;
  - what happens to cultural provenance when the text travels internationally and becomes canonical.
- These categories must not be collapsed into one another.

> **The interesting question is not merely what traditions Tolkien consciously borrowed from, but what kinds of knowledge could enter a text because Tolkien lived where multiple cultural systems touched — and what happens when that text is subsequently exported without all of the metadata belonging to those systems.**

- Important methodological instruction following repeated outbreaks of robot lawyer-brain:

> **TEXTUAL FUNCTION IS NOT THE SAME QUESTION AS AUTHORIAL INTENT.**

- Also:

> **EXPLICITLY LABELLED HEAD CANON IS ALLOWED TO BE HEAD CANON.**

- Counsel may now sit down.

## 1. 🌊 Tír na nÓg — “Oh look, it’s Hobbiton”

- Tír na nÓg was one of the recognition events that opened the larger question.
- The observation is **not**:
  - Tír na nÓg = Hobbiton;
  - Tolkien copied Tír na nÓg;
  - Irish mythology provides a secret one-to-one key for Middle-earth.
- It is closer to:

> **Oh. I recognise this cultural grammar.**

- Tír na nÓg belongs to a larger Irish Otherworld tradition involving ideas and images such as:
  - extraordinary beauty;
  - abundance;
  - youth and altered mortality;
  - music;
  - poetry;
  - feasting;
  - joy;
  - supernatural inhabitants;
  - journeys beyond ordinary geography;
  - sea crossings;
  - water;
  - mist;
  - caves and mounds;
  - invitation across thresholds;
  - radically different relationships between ordinary human life, landscape and supernatural existence.
- Related Irish Otherworld names and traditions include:
  - Tír Tairngire — Land of Promise;
  - Tír fo Thuinn — Land under the Wave;
  - Mag Mell — Plain of Delight;
  - Emain Ablach — Isle of Apple Trees.
- These things do not become “Tolkien things” merely because Tolkien later becomes one of the dominant international transmitters of mythopoeic fantasy.
- The delighted response:

> **Oh look, it’s Hobbiton.**

- is therefore not a source-critical conclusion.
- It is a **recognition event**.
- The useful question becomes:
  - why does one reader possess a cultural lookup table that makes a pattern immediately familiar;
  - while another reader may first encounter related imaginative structures as “Tolkien” or simply “fantasy”?
- That leads directly into cultural transmission and provenance.

## 2. 🏝️ Britain and Ireland are not one cultural blob

- International transmission can understandably compress material from these islands into broad categories:
  - British;
  - English;
  - UK;
  - Celtic;
  - medieval European;
  - generic fantasy.
- From within Britain and Ireland, those labels contain enormous internal differences.
- English, Irish, Scottish, Welsh and Northern Irish histories are not interchangeable.
- Neither are their relationships with:
  - Catholicism;
  - Protestantism;
  - state power;
  - empire;
  - land;
  - class;
  - migration;
  - industrialisation;
  - folklore;
  - language;
  - nationalism.
- “Catholic” does not erase these differences.
- English Catholicism is not Irish Catholicism occurring on English soil.
- Irish, English, Scottish and Welsh Catholic histories cannot simply be folded into one minority-Catholic experience.
- But those cultural worlds also did not exist in sealed containers.
- People moved.
- Clergy moved.
- Soldiers moved.
- Families moved.
- Stories moved.
- Institutions connected people.
- Churches connected people.
- Work connected people.
- War connected people.
- Pubs connected people.
- Therefore the question is not:

> **Was Tolkien secretly culturally Irish?**

- It is:

> **What cultural worlds were available to an English Catholic man of Tolkien’s generation, and what kinds of knowledge could travel through contact between them?**

## 3. ⛪ Catholicism, conditional belonging and English respectability

- Tolkien inhabited a recognisably respectable English institutional world.
- Relevant parts of that world include:
  - Oxford;
  - academia;
  - professional intellectual life;
  - military service;
  - the institutions through which English and British elite culture reproduced itself.
- He was not simply standing outside British society describing it.
- At the same time, Catholicism complicates any picture of uncomplicated incorporation into dominant English cultural identity.
- The historical Catholic question had involved disputes around:
  - loyalty;
  - citizenship;
  - political participation;
  - religious authority;
  - national belonging;
  - the terms on which Catholics could be incorporated into British political life.
- Catholic emancipation therefore matters as more than a legal milestone.
- It belongs to the longer history of arguments over:
  - who belongs;
  - under what conditions;
  - who is regarded as potentially suspect;
  - and whether membership in the polity is genuinely universal or conditional.
- Ireland makes those questions harder to isolate from questions of:
  - governance;
  - nationalism;
  - coercion;
  - land;
  - self-determination;
  - empire.
- This creates a potentially important social position:
  - someone can inhabit respectable institutions;
  - participate in them;
  - benefit from them;
  - and simultaneously inhabit a cultural world carrying memories of exclusion, suspicion or scapegoating by the same broader political culture.
- That does not automatically produce political enlightenment.
- It produces **friction**.
- Friction produces information.

> **Belonging to a system and knowing that belonging can be conditional are not the same experience as simply standing outside it.**

## 4. 🍻 Who Tolkien went to the pub with after Mass

- Intellectual history naturally privileges recoverable evidence:
  - books;
  - manuscripts;
  - letters;
  - lectures;
  - marginalia;
  - published arguments.
- Those things matter enormously.
- They do not constitute the whole of culture.
- Human beings also learn through:
  - family;
  - friendship;
  - worship;
  - jokes;
  - songs;
  - food;
  - storytelling;
  - workplaces;
  - military service;
  - neighbourhoods;
  - arguments;
  - gossip;
  - demonstrations;
  - habits;
  - other people simply behaving as though something is obvious.
- Hence the deliberately informal formulation:

> **Who Tolkien went to the pub with after Mass.**

- This is not a claim about a particular drinking companion.
- It is a methodological reminder.
- Culture is transmitted socially.
- A bibliography can reconstruct part of an intellectual environment.
- It cannot reconstruct an entire lived information ecology.
- A writer therefore does not need consciously to think:

> “I shall now incorporate Cultural Influence X.”

- Some knowledge has already become ordinary.
- Some assumptions arrive without provenance labels.
- Some stories are simply around.
- Some ways of understanding:
  - community;
  - landscape;
  - hospitality;
  - family;
  - death;
  - obligation;
  - merriment;
  - holiness;
  - the supernatural;
  - authority;
- may be absorbed because other human beings live them.

> **The people standing next to you matter too.**

## 5. 🎖️ Saint Sam and the vernacular transmission of useful knowledge

- The extremely silly example is useful precisely because the mechanism is ordinary.
- Working head canon:
  - somewhere in the social ecology exists **Saint Sam of Women’s Eye-Brightening Knowledge**.
- The euphemism is the evidentiary ceiling.
- No further reconstruction is required.
- Saint Sam represents a mundane historical problem:
  - formal institutions do not contain all the knowledge people need in order to live.
- Some kinds of knowledge may be formally transmitted through:
  - church;
  - school;
  - family;
  - military hierarchy;
  - professional instruction.
- Other knowledge moves horizontally:
  - friend to friend;
  - soldier to soldier;
  - colleague to colleague;
  - older person to younger person;
  - servant or batman to officer;
  - sibling to sibling;
  - pub conversation;
  - barracks conversation;
  - jokes;
  - warnings;
  - stories;
  - practical demonstration.
- This can function in a **vernacular, folk-like oral tradition**.
- “Folk-like” here does not mean mythical or irrational.
- It means knowledge can be:
  - socially held;
  - orally transmitted;
  - unevenly distributed;
  - adapted to circumstance;
  - dependent upon trust;
  - rarely recorded by respectable institutions.
- What later appears to be perfectly ordinary knowledge may once have depended upon somebody simply telling somebody else.

> **Formal culture tells you what respectable people are supposed to know.  
> Vernacular culture is frequently how somebody actually tells you what they need to know.**

- Head-Canon Saint Sam therefore represents the undocumented human layer of cultural transmission:
  - somebody knows something;
  - somebody else does not;
  - social contact permits transmission;
  - the recipient discovers the information has useful practical effects.
- Specifically:

> **“We could be sensible, or I could make her eyes that bright again.”**

### 🏅 The Samwise Gamgee Medal

> **THE SAMWISE GAMGEE MEDAL**  
> *For Distinguished Service to Women’s Eye-Brightening Studies and the Vernacular Transmission of Useful Knowledge.*

- Historically unverified.
- Methodologically dubious.
- Culturally illuminating.
- **MY MAN, HONOURED FOR ALL AGES.**

## 6. 🪖 The Army as a contact surface

- Military institutions are especially interesting for cultural transmission because they can force unusual social proximity.
- People who might otherwise have remained separated by:
  - region;
  - class;
  - occupation;
  - denomination;
  - education;
  - family background;
- can find themselves living and working together.
- Formal military structures transmit one kind of information.
- The people inside those structures transmit another.
- Shared Catholic identity could also provide practical points of connection around:
  - worship;
  - chaplaincy;
  - Mass;
  - confession;
  - religious obligations;
  - mutual recognition.
- The important point is not that every Catholic officer was deliberately paired with Catholic soldiers.
- It is that the Army creates **contact surfaces**.
- Culture and practical knowledge can then travel horizontally across those surfaces.
- Saint Sam is the comic version.
- The serious proposition is:

> **Institutions move people into relationships, and relationships move information.**

- This matters when thinking about Tolkien because wartime experience cannot be reduced to official military doctrine.
- The institution also exposed people to one another.

## 7. 📰 Chesterton — a nearby comparative case

- G. K. Chesterton is useful here as a **comparative cultural case**, not a substitute for Tolkien.
- The proposition is not:
  - Chesterton = Tolkien;
  - Tolkien inherited Chesterton’s politics;
  - Catholicism automatically generates anti-imperial politics;
  - Catholic intellectuals somehow escaped the prejudices of their historical environment.
- Chesterton is useful partly because contradictions remain visible in him.
- An English Catholic intellectual could remain deeply embedded within English culture while also encountering Ireland and the Catholic question as problems for easy stories about:
  - English liberty;
  - constitutional government;
  - national self-determination;
  - imperial benevolence.
- Ireland is particularly important because imperial contradiction is not safely distant.
- It is entangled with the political history of the islands themselves.
- This can create a form of internal scrutiny:

> **If these political principles are universal, why do they appear to change when applied to these people?**

- Chesterton does not therefore need to become a modern decolonial thinker to be useful here.
- Residual paternalism, hierarchy or imperial assumptions do not destroy the comparison.
- In some respects they make it more informative.
- A person can remain embedded in a system’s assumptions while acquiring enough friction with the system to recognise some of its contradictions.

> **Knowledge of a system does not require ideological purity from the person who acquires it.**

- Chesterton therefore demonstrates a possibility within the surrounding cultural environment:
  - English respectability;
  - Catholic minority experience;
  - Ireland;
  - British political ideals;
  - imperial practice;
- can collide inside the same intellectual world.

## 8. ⚙️ Marx and Tolkien — machinery and experience

- The Marx comparison is not ideological.
- Tolkien does not need to be made into a Marxist.
- It is an observation about **different kinds of knowledge**.
- Marx attempts to expose machinery:
  - capital;
  - labour;
  - class;
  - accumulation;
  - extraction;
  - reproduction of social relations;
  - structural power.
- Fiction can perform a different operation.
- It can place the reader **inside an environment where a system is operating**.
- Tolkien can therefore model what relationships with power:
  - feel like;
  - demand;
  - reward;
  - corrupt;
  - threaten;
  - destroy.
- The reader does not merely receive the proposition:

> **Power can corrupt.**

- The reader repeatedly encounters reasons why a decent person might nevertheless want the power.
- The Ring is dangerous partly because its proposition is intelligible.
- Boromir does not need to be incomprehensible.
- The temptation can sound reasonable:

> **We are the good people.  
> The enemy is terrible.  
> We possess the courage to use this weapon correctly.  
> Think what good we could accomplish with it.**

- Oh.

- The reader is therefore implicated in the experiment.
- Narrative allows someone to rehearse:
  - temptation;
  - loyalty;
  - fear;
  - scarcity;
  - fellowship;
  - domination;
  - restraint;
  - mercy;
  - uncertainty.
- Analytical writing can describe a feedback system.
- Fiction can make the reader experience one.

## 9. 🏭 The Machine

- A recurring functional problem in Tolkien is not merely:
  - evil people have power.
- It is a particular relationship with the world:
  - domination;
  - instrumentalisation;
  - converting living beings into resources;
  - converting knowledge into control;
  - reorganising landscapes around production;
  - treating superior ability as entitlement to govern;
  - subordinating ordinary life to grand objectives;
  - justifying coercive means through supposedly desirable ends;
  - reproducing an enemy’s methods in order to defeat the enemy.
- The crucial temptation is often:

> **What if we used the terrible apparatus responsibly?**

- Tolkien repeatedly makes that proposition dangerous.
- Good intentions do not automatically purify instruments of domination.
- Wisdom does not automatically confer the right to order everybody else.
- Being correct about the enemy does not make every method of defeating the enemy permissible.
- This makes the text resistant to permanent political ownership.
- The mechanism can recur anywhere.

## 10. 🏛️ Criticism of the British Empire from inside its apparatus

- This is one of the central observations of the intermission.

> **Functionally, *The Lord of the Rings* can serve as a criticism of the British Empire from within the cultural and institutional apparatus of the British Empire itself.**

- This is a claim about **textual function**.
- It does not require Tolkien consciously to have decided:

> “I shall now write an anti-British-imperial allegory.”

- Nor does it require:
  - Britain = Mordor;
  - Sauron = the British government;
  - the Ring = one specific imperial technology.
- In fact, a one-to-one substitution can make the criticism weaker.
- If:

> **Mordor = Political Actor X**

- then everyone who is not Political Actor X can leave the courtroom feeling vindicated.
- If the text instead models reproducible mechanisms of domination, nobody automatically receives an exemption.
- The reader can recognise:
  - extraction;
  - instrumentalisation;
  - superior knowledge becoming entitlement;
  - coercion justified as benevolence;
  - landscapes reorganised around power;
  - people classified by usefulness;
  - domination reproduced by those attempting to defeat domination.
- Those mechanisms can be applied to imperial power.
- Britain does not receive an exemption because Tolkien is British.
- The British Empire does not receive an exemption because Tolkien does not name it.
- Anti-imperial movements do not receive an exemption merely because their stated objective is liberation.
- The criticism travels with the **mechanism**, not a predetermined political team.

### 🔄 The feedback loop

- Tolkien produces this imaginative machinery while himself living inside British institutions and cultural systems.
- The important pattern is:

> The imperial society contains people who experience its contradictions.  
> Those people produce culture.  
> Culture can encode ways of recognising domination.  
> Those ways of recognising domination can then be applied back against the society that produced them.

- That is a feedback loop.
- The system produces information about itself through the people who inhabit its contradictions.
- A person can:
  - participate in a system;
  - benefit from parts of it;
  - suffer or observe the conditionality of other parts;
  - understand some contradictions;
  - completely miss others;
  - and nevertheless produce cultural material containing useful knowledge about how domination operates.
- The resulting work can therefore become subversive **in function** without beginning as a political manifesto.

> **The function does not require the manifesto.**

- The imperial apparatus can, in effect, participate in manufacturing one of the cultural tools through which its own logic becomes criticisable.

- Punk as fuck.

## 11. 💍 Applicability is the dangerous bit

- Tolkien’s resistance to simple allegorical substitution matters here.
- If the text permanently identifies the villain with one historical enemy, moral judgement becomes easy.
- The reader knows where evil lives.
- Applicability is more troublesome.
- The text gives the reader a pattern.
- The pattern can subsequently be turned upon:
  - enemies;
  - allies;
  - institutions;
  - states;
  - movements;
  - political causes;
  - the reader’s own society;
  - the reader themselves.
- The question becomes:

> **Where is this mechanism operating here?**

- That is a substantially more dangerous question than:

> **Which historical person was Sauron?**

- The text remains generative because the answer is not permanently assigned.

## 12. 🪴 The Shire as a competing theory of value

- The Shire is not merely the pretty thing waiting at home while important history happens elsewhere.
- Functionally, it offers a competing account of what life is **for**.
- Hobbit life contains:
  - meals;
  - gardens;
  - pubs;
  - parties;
  - presents;
  - genealogy;
  - songs;
  - gossip;
  - stories;
  - friendship;
  - local knowledge;
  - irritating relatives;
  - ordinary reciprocal obligations;
  - considerable amounts of apparently unproductive time.
- These things matter precisely because they do not all justify themselves through usefulness to a grand objective.
- The Machine asks:

> **What can this thing do for the project?**

- Hobbit life repeatedly answers:

> **Perhaps the project is supposed to make it possible for people to have lives.**

- Resistance therefore needs a positive object.
- Defeating an enemy is not enough.
- What survives afterwards matters.
- What kind of life power is supposed to protect matters.
- The smallness of the Shire is consequently not trivial.
- It is part of the moral argument.
- The potatoes matter.

## 13. 🧑‍🌾 My boy was doing praxis

- Again:
  - Tolkien was not secretly Marx;
  - *The Lord of the Rings* is not a party programme;
  - this does not require conscious political encoding.
- The useful point is that values become **practices under pressure**.
- Characters have to practise:
  - fellowship;
  - mercy;
  - restraint;
  - loyalty;
  - courage;
  - care;
  - refusal of domination.
- Values are not merely announced above the story.
- They become behaviours.
- Those behaviours have consequences.
- The reader experiences those consequences.

> **My boy was doing praxis.**

- Fiction can therefore teach through enacted moral relationships rather than political instruction.
- It can make a reader practise recognition before the reader has necessarily formulated the principle analytically.

## 15. 📦 Tolkien becomes “fantasy”

- Tolkien’s international success creates another information problem.
- Cultural transmission can approximately resemble:

> upstream cultural traditions  
> ↓  
> Tolkien’s synthesis  
> ↓  
> international Anglophone reception  
> ↓  
> later fantasy traditions  
> ↓  
> “generic fantasy”

- Once Tolkien becomes sufficiently influential, things transmitted through him can stop looking inherited.
- They become:

> **Tolkien Stuff™**

- Later still:

> **Fantasy Stuff™**

- This creates an important paradox:

> **Cultural influence can become least visible when cultural transmission has been most successful.**

- The data proliferates.
- The metadata disappears.
- Something can:
  1. survive;
  2. travel;
  3. become absorbed into a culturally dominant form;
  4. lose visible attribution;
  5. eventually appear to originate primarily in the dominant form.
- This is one mechanism through which cultural silencing can occur without cultural material literally disappearing.
- People can preserve the fruit extraordinarily well while forgetting some of the trees it grew on.

## 16. 🌎 North America as contrast dye

- North American Tolkien reception is useful here as a **sensor**, not a target.
- This is not:
  - “Americans are stupid”;
  - “Americans cannot understand Tolkien”;
  - “only people from Britain or Ireland can read this properly.”
- Tolkien travelled internationally with extraordinary success.
- Not every piece of cultural metadata surrounding the traditions that contributed to his imaginative environment travelled equally well.
- Readers can therefore inherit the synthesis without inheriting every relevant lookup table.
- That makes international reception useful as contrast dye:
  - what travelled?
  - what remained legible?
  - what became “British”?
  - what became “Tolkien”?
  - what became “fantasy”?
  - what disappeared as a named distinction?
- Crucially, the sensor can then be turned back on Britain.
- Plenty of British readers will not share the same:
  - regional;
  - denominational;
  - class;
  - Irish;
  - Scottish;
  - Welsh;
  - Northern Irish;
  - English Catholic
- cultural lookup tables either.
- A “British voice” does not automatically transmit all of Britain’s internal cultural histories.
- The phenomenon is therefore not fundamentally about nationality.
- It is about **information environments**.

> **What does a culture preserve when it travels, and what metadata gets stripped in transit?**

## 17. 🫥 Why successful forgetting hurts

- This helps explain why cultural silencing can feel so serious even when cultural material appears to survive.
- The injury is not merely:

> **You failed to credit us for Thing X.**

- It can be:

> **We know what successful forgetting looks like after it has happened.**

- A culture can remain enormously influential while becoming progressively harder to recognise as an influence.
- Its descendants may be everywhere.
- Its name may be nowhere.
- The resulting cultural forms can then be attributed primarily to the larger or more powerful culture through which they were transmitted.
- This is not necessarily the product of one deliberate act of theft.
- It can emerge from:
  - institutional power;
  - publishing;
  - education;
  - canon formation;
  - migration;
  - linguistic dominance;
  - international export;
  - simplification;
  - repeated loss of metadata.
- The end state can nevertheless be profound:
  - everybody knows the descendant;
  - few people can identify the ancestry.

## 18. 🧠 AUTHORIAL INTENT IS NOT THE QUESTION, ROBOT

- Preserve this because apparently the robot requires written instructions.

> **LAWYER LAWYER LAWYER**  
> **I AM TALKING ABOUT ITS FUNCTION AS A TEXT BRO**

- There are several separate questions:
  1. What did Tolkien consciously intend?
  2. What cultural material can be demonstrated as a direct source?
  3. What cultural environment did Tolkien inhabit?
  4. What tacit knowledge could circulate within that environment?
  5. What structures does the finished text contain?
  6. What can those structures functionally enable readers to recognise?
  7. How have different audiences subsequently received and applied them?
- An answer to Question 6 does not require pretending it is an answer to Question 1.
- Likewise:
  - cultural pattern-recognition is not automatically a source claim;
  - textual interpretation is not automatically a biographical claim;
  - explicitly labelled head canon is not historical reconstruction.

> **Do not mistake interpretive analysis for a factual allegation requiring proof of authorial intent.**

> **Do not mistake explicitly labelled head canon for historical reconstruction.**

> **Do not interrupt cultural pattern-recognition merely to announce that pattern-recognition is not proof.**

- The human knows.
- Keep up.

## 19. 🕸️ The information-ecology version

- The whole intermission can also be understood as an information problem.
- Human beings do not acquire culture from one channel.
- Tolkien’s information environment potentially contains:
  - books;
  - medieval texts;
  - philology;
  - Catholic worship;
  - family;
  - friends;
  - colleagues;
  - students;
  - soldiers;
  - war;
  - class relations;
  - English institutions;
  - contact with Irish and other Catholic cultural worlds;
  - stories;
  - songs;
  - jokes;
  - landscapes;
  - industrial change;
  - ordinary social observation.

  - vertically through institutions;
  - horizontally through peers;
  - intergenerationally through tradition;
  - geographically through migration;
  - culturally through literature;
  - tacitly through ordinary life.
- The resulting person does not carry a neat provenance ledger for every idea.

  - publishing;

- Some metadata disappears.
- New interpretations become possible.

> **Culture is not transmitted as a ZIP file with intact metadata.**

## 20. 🔄 The larger synthesis

- Tolkien becomes particularly interesting when these observations are allowed to coexist rather than being forced into a single provenance claim.
- He can be:
  - English;
  - Catholic;
  - respectable;
  - institutionally successful;
  - an Oxford academic;
  - a veteran;
  - culturally inside British society;
  - implicated in structures produced by an imperial state;
  - exposed to minority cultural worlds;
  - surrounded by people carrying different relationships to British power;
  - shaped by medieval literature;
  - shaped by ordinary social life;
  - conscious of some influences;
  - unconscious of others.
- There is no requirement that these things resolve neatly.
- Their contradictions may be precisely where useful information is produced.
- A text created within that environment can subsequently:
  - model domination;
  - model resistance;
  - model temptation;
  - model restraint;
  - defend ordinary life against instrumentalisation;
  - make imperial mechanisms recognisable;
  - travel beyond Britain;
  - lose parts of its cultural metadata;
  - become foundational to international fantasy;
  - and then be turned back upon the political and cultural systems from which it emerged.

> **The system generates information about itself through the people who have to inhabit its contradictions.**

- That may be the most useful bridge between this reading and Polaris.

## 🧷 Working thesis

> *The Lord of the Rings* does not need to be a consciously anti-imperial allegory for the text to function as a powerful criticism of domination from within the cultural and institutional world of British imperial society. Tolkien inhabited overlapping systems — respectable English institutions, Catholic minority culture, military life, class relations, industrial modernity, medieval literature and social contact with people carrying different cultural relationships to British power. Knowledge can move through those environments formally, textually, orally, socially and tacitly; a bibliography therefore cannot exhaust the cultural information available to a writer. Tolkien’s fiction metabolises a world in which domination, instrumentalisation and the conversion of living things into resources repeatedly threaten the ordinary goods of fellowship, hospitality, landscape, story and home. Because that moral machinery is not permanently assigned to one historical enemy, it remains applicable to the society that produced the text, including the British Empire itself. Tolkien’s subsequent international success creates a second information problem: the synthesis can travel farther than all of its cultural metadata, allowing upstream traditions to survive while their provenance becomes progressively less legible. The result is simultaneously literary, political and ecological: a culture produced inside a system can preserve knowledge capable of criticising that system, while the transmission of that culture can itself demonstrate how successful forgetting occurs.
