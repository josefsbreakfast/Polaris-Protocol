BASIC IDEA

* Working name for the larger proxy battle visible through the Palantir–Met–MOPAC contract dispute:
    * ⚔️ The Battle of Waterloo Place
* This is not the name of the litigation itself.
* It is not a claim that everybody involved is participating in one coordinated conflict.
* It is the narrative container for the larger arguments that are becoming visible through and around the contract case.
* The actual procurement dispute is one engagement / battlefield within it.
* Useful distinction:
    * contract dispute = concrete object
    * Battle of Waterloo Place = wider proxy battle / interpretive theatre
    * Great Kraken Disturbance = still larger teaching ecology


WHY WATERLOO PLACE

* Central London already provides us with absurdly appropriate military scenery.
* Waterloo Place sits in the Westminster / Whitehall political-governmental environment.
* It contains the Guards Crimean War Memorial and associated nineteenth-century military/state memorial landscape.
* Gives us a real geographical and historical anchor rather than inventing “The Great Procurement War”.
* Sounds sufficiently like an actual British battle that somebody could misunderstand it for approximately three seconds.
* Very Bayeux-Tapestry compatible.
* Needs a proper geography/history research pass before the finished node:
    * exact relevant institutional addresses
    * distances
    * what falls inside the useful geographical theatre
    * history of Waterloo Place
    * memorials/statues
    * imperial/military provenance
    * why this particular landscape exists there
* Do not manufacture a precise geographical “centre” unless the mapping actually supports it.


THE PROXY-BATTLE IDEA

* The narrow dispute concerns an actual procurement decision and the legal/institutional decisions surrounding it.
* But the dispute is already capable of carrying much larger arguments.
* Different participants, commentators, politicians, journalists, institutions and publics may understand themselves to be arguing about different things through the same object.
* Important:
    * the existence of proxy arguments does not prove that the original dispute was created in order to conduct those arguments.
    * strategic usefulness ≠ strategic intent.
    * shared interest ≠ coordination.
    * similar position ≠ common faction.
* Better question:
    * What larger argument does each participant or observer understand themselves to be having when they talk about this contract?

COMPETING RISK APPARATUSES

* Particularly important Battle of Waterloo Place concept.
* Actors may not simply disagree about the answer.
* They may be measuring different dangers.
* Possible risk categories:
    * operational failure
    * procurement legality
    * litigation
    * value for money
    * supplier dependency
    * technological failure
    * information security
    * national security
    * political accountability
    * democratic legitimacy
    * reputation
    * institutional responsibility
    * service continuity
* Reducing one actor’s Risk A may increase another actor’s Risk B.
* Once somebody moves in response to their risk apparatus, everybody else has to recalculate.
* Hence:
    * action

WHO CAN SEE THE BATTLE?

* Information asymmetry may be part of the battle itself.
* Different actors possess different views of the terrain.
* Met may possess operational information.
* MOPAC possesses its own governance/oversight information and powers.
* Suppliers possess detailed technical/commercial information.
* Courts see the material properly put before them.
* Parliament can create and seek records through its own mechanisms.
* Journalists/public see the public-domain record.
* Some material may legitimately remain unavailable.
* Need to distinguish exact reasons:
    * confidentiality
    * commercial sensitivity
    * privilege
    * court restrictions
    * personal data
    * national-security restrictions
    * defence/security sensitivities
    * other applicable regimes
* Never assign a secrecy regime to a particular document without evidence.
* Question:
    * Who knows what?
    * Who is entitled to know?
    * Who can compel an explanation?
    * Who can publish it?

2. “What larger argument does each participant think they are having?”
This is excellent and hasn’t quite survived intact:

What larger argument does each participant or observer understand themselves to be having when they talk about this contract?

The README now identifies the possible larger arguments, but that’s not the same analytical exercise. We should preserve a future proxy-position matrix:

| Actor / observer | Concrete issue | Larger issue explicitly invoked | Evidence | Date | Our interpretation |

That lets us distinguish Palantir says this is about X from journalists frame it as Y from Polaris notices Z. Very Kraken-safe.

4. The democratic-control-surfaces investigation.
This is the biggest substantive strand that hasn’t acquired a proper home.

elected government → delegated authority → statutory bodies → officials → procurement → frameworks → suppliers → subcontractors → technical architecture → contracts → courts → oversight

Especially preserve these questions:

8. The original risk question is slightly different from our newer risk-calculator work.
We developed how risk systems struggle. The notes also ask who has authority to privilege one species of risk over another.

For example, hypothetically:

* Met: operational continuity;
* MOPAC: governance/procurement legality;
* supplier: commercial/contractual risk;
* court: litigation/legal error;
* public: legitimacy/accountability.

The really interesting governance question becomes:

When reducing Risk A increases Risk B, who has authority to decide which risk the institution should bear?

That’s worth adding to the README or Surgeon’s Knife. It goes directly to Who governs when government buys capability?
