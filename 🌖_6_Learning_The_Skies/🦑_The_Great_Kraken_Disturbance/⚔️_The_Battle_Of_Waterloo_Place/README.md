# ⚔️ The Battle Of Waterloo Place

**First created:** 2026-09-19 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-19

*An analytical battlefield for studying how public authority, operational capability, commercial technology, law, information and institutional power meet — viewed from Waterloo Place.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

**The Battle of Waterloo Place is not the name of the litigation.**

Nor does it mean that the events examined in this folder physically occurred at Waterloo Place.

**Waterloo Place is where we are looking from.**

The Guards Crimean War Memorial gives the project a real historical and material observation point from which several recurring questions become unusually legible:

- How does formal authority become practical capability?
- Who governs when government buys capability?
- What happens when public institutions depend upon privately supplied technical capacity?
- Where does operational autonomy end and democratic governance begin?
- What happens when institutions disagree about that boundary?
- How does litigation make normally less-visible relationships observable?
- What happens when the dispute moves from decisions into documents, communications and information architecture?
- What can a court actually compel, determine and remedy?
- What remains unknown even after all of that?

The immediate contemporary object is the dispute surrounding the proposed Metropolitan Police **Unified Operational Analytics** capability, MOPAC's refusal of approval, and the resulting Palantir litigation.

But the **Battle** is larger than the lawsuit.

The lawsuit has parties.

The Battle has:

- relationships;
- dependencies;
- authority;
- capability;
- information;
- law;
- money;
- technical infrastructure;
- governance;
- feedback.

> **The lawsuit has parties. The power system has relationships.**

## 🏛️ 1. The Observation Point

Waterloo Place was not chosen because every event happened there.

It was chosen because the physical landscape provides an unusually useful lens.

At the Guards Crimean War Memorial we find:

- soldiers;
- bronze;
- captured cannon;
- military death;
- Victorian state memory;
- institutional failure;
- logistics;
- administration;
- communications;
- later monuments to Florence Nightingale and Sidney Herbert.

The nineteenth-century world makes capability unusually visible.

There are:

- bodies;
- uniforms;
- ships;
- cannon;
- supplies;
- hospitals;
- telegraphs.

Modern institutional capability can be harder to see.

It may reside partly in:

- software;
- data;
- contracts;
- procurement frameworks;
- technical expertise;
- information architecture;
- digital communications;
- organisational relationships.

Hence one of Waterloo Place's recurring questions:

> **Where is power when you can no longer see the cannon?**

And:

> **The cannon did not disappear. It acquired an information system.**

## 🧭 2. The Central Question

The folder repeatedly returns to:

> # **Who governs when government buys capability?**

That question immediately fragments.

Who possesses:

- democratic authority?
- statutory authority?
- operational authority?
- technical capability?
- procurement authority?
- money?
- information?
- specialist knowledge?
- legal authority?
- the power to approve?
- the power to refuse?
- the power to challenge?
- the power to compel evidence?
- the practical ability to build the thing?

Nobody necessarily possesses all of them.

That is the point.

Waterloo Place is not trying to identify:

> **the person or institution who really controls everything.**

It is trying to map how different forms of power become operational through relationships.

## 🧮 3. The Mapping Rule

Use:

> **actor  
> → relationship  
> → resource  
> → authority / capability  
> → pathway  
> → observable consequence**

Do not jump directly from:

> actor

to:

> consequence.

Do not turn:

- proximity into coordination;
- access into use;
- capability into use;
- communication into agreement;
- agreement into action;
- action into causation;
- benefit into intent;
- network position into conspiracy.

Where an arrow is missing:

> **leave it missing.**

This is one of the folder's governing rules.

## 🕰️ 15. Keep The Clocks Separate

Throughout this folder preserve:

### Event time

When did the underlying thing happen?

### Information time

When did a particular actor know or possess information about it?

### Evidence time

When did evidence establishing it become available?

### Court time

When did it enter the litigation?

### Public time

When did the public become able to know it?

A later disclosure can alter our understanding of an earlier event.

It cannot travel backwards and make later knowledge available earlier.

> **Later evidence ≠ earlier knowledge.**

## 🧬 23. Nobody Has All The Powers

One of Waterloo Place's most useful emerging findings is methodological rather than partisan.

Different actors possess different things.

MOPAC may possess authority the Met lacks.

The Met may possess operational capability MOPAC lacks.

A technology supplier may possess technical capability both lack.

The judiciary may possess compulsory legal authority none of the three possesses.

The judiciary may simultaneously lack:

- operational expertise;
- technical capability;
- democratic mandate;
- procurement responsibility.

Therefore:

> **power is not a single ladder.**

Map:

> actor  
> → source of power  
> → object over which it operates  
> → pathway  
> → constraint  
> → consequence.

The pointy hat is powerful.

It is not the Infinity Hat.

## 🔬 25. Research Method

For every proposition worth keeping:

1. Identify the actor.
2. Identify the date.
3. Identify the source.
4. Identify the evidential status.
5. Identify the relationship.
6. Identify the relevant authority/capability/resource.
7. Identify the pathway.
8. Identify the observable consequence.
9. Record alternative explanations.
10. Record what remains unknown.

Where possible prioritise:

1. judgments and court orders;
2. pleadings and formal legal documents;
3. legislation and procedural rules;
4. official MOPAC / Met / GLA records;
5. procurement documentation;
6. parliamentary records;
7. primary institutional statements;
8. reliable court reporting;
9. reliable journalism;
10. commentary.

The hierarchy is contextual rather than mechanical.

A primary source can establish:

> **what an institution says**

without establishing:

> **that what it says is true.**

## ✨ Stardust

Waterloo Place is an observation point.

The contemporary dispute gives us an unusually public interface between:

- democratic governance;
- operational policing;
- private technological capability;
- procurement;
- information;
- law;
- money.

The litigation makes some of those relationships unusually visible.

Disclosure may make more visible.

Judicial intervention may establish some propositions and leave others unanswered.

The resulting information can feed back into:

- institutions;
- Parliament;
- journalism;
- procurement;
- risk management;
- further litigation.

That complexity does not require a hidden conductor.

It requires careful mapping.

The folder therefore keeps different analytical jobs separate:

- history and symbolic landscape;
- institutional bodies;
- chronology;
- legal argument;
- disclosure;
- judicial power;
- institutional anatomy;
- information architecture.

The purpose is not to discover:

> **who secretly controls Britain.**

It is to understand:

> **how different forms of power meet, depend upon one another, constrain one another, and become operational.**

And when the evidence runs out:

> **stop.**

The missing arrow stays missing.
