🍿 Basic presentation idea

* Make the subject actually fucking readable.
* Procurement, public law, disclosure, contracting structures and institutional relationships are consequential but spectacularly capable of boring civilians to death.
* Possible basic format for individual episodes/notes:
    * First half: deliberately entertaining narrative.
        * battles
        * wizard fights
        * military history
        * fantasy

    * Second half: extremely literal evidence ledger.

        * who actually said what
        * documents
        * dates
        * sources
        * allegation vs response
        * documented fact vs interpretation
        * unresolved questions
* Basic bargain with reader:
    * Here is our entertaining interpretation of the evidence.
    * Here is the evidence underneath it.
    * You don’t have to accept the interpretation.

⚔️ The Great Procurement Wars

* Possible story-within-the-larger-Kraken:
    * The Great Procurement Wars
* Deliberately silly headline faction model:
    * 💣 The Politicians
    * 🧨 The Procurers
* But this should explicitly not mean there are literally two organised, permanent factions.
* People/institutions can occupy different positions in different engagements.
* Better conceptual rule:
    * map resources and positions, not teams.
* Someone can be 💣 in one battle and 🧨 somewhere else.
* There are overlapping political, professional, legal, governmental, commercial, defence and media networks.
* We should not turn the existence of networks into an unsupported claim of coordination.
* Nor assume that because something was useful to one participant, somebody deliberately introduced it for that purpose.
* Useful distinction:
    * strategic usefulness ≠ proof of strategic intent.

🦑 Kraken / “deep state”

* This probably needs its own substantial note.
* Important personal/conceptual provenance for Kraken:
    * Kraken already means something specific inside Polaris.
    * networked response
    * distributed load
    * adaptation to pressure
    * cancellous/spongy-bone model
    * responding appropriately in a networked information environment
* Need a short acknowledgement somewhere of the history of antisemitic octopus imagery.
    * Particularly important because material about hidden/networked power can attract exactly the audiences for whom that imagery has conspiratorial meanings.
    * Kraken is not being used to mean a secret ethnic/cabalistic controller.
* We have also previously used Kraken/deep-state satire around Liz Truss and her claims about the “deep state”.
    * Running joke: Liz Truss guards the Kraken because she is the only person who can talk to her.
* More serious teaching opportunity:
    * people use “deep state” to describe several different phenomena.
    * background machinery of government
    * institutional continuity
    * professional networks
    * security/defence institutions
    * informal influence
    * power whose source/pathway isn’t immediately visible
    * and, in stronger/conspiratorial versions, alleged covert networks exercising hidden control.
* The interesting starting experience is:
    * “I can see that this person/institution has power, but I can’t see where all of that power comes from.”
* Rather than either:
    * dismissing that perception automatically, or
    * filling the invisible space with conspiracy,
* Polaris can say:
    * open the bonnet and map it.
* Possible analytical sequence:
    * actor
    * relationship
    * resource
    * authority/capability
    * pathway
    * observable consequence
* Power that is difficult to see is an invitation to map mechanisms, not permission to invent mechanisms.
* Also important:
    * governments and state institutions can conceal wrongdoing.
    * therefore “hidden institutional power never exists” is not a serious answer either.
* Potential historical teaching routes:
    * Hillsborough
    * duties/candour/accountability
    * previous British political scandals
    * procurement scandals
    * cash-for-questions / access and influence
    * VIP procurement
    * historical reasons people reasonably care about invisible relationships and state contracting.

🏛️ Why procurement is actually enormous

* The contract dispute can be the doorway, rather than the entire subject.
* Modern British governance is heavily mediated through contracts and external providers.
* Procurement therefore isn’t merely:
    * “Council needs twelve staplers; obtain staplers.”
* It connects to huge questions about:
    * policing
    * defence
    * healthcare
    * asylum/immigration provision
    * prisons/carceral infrastructure
    * technology
    * public services
    * outsourcing
    * value for money
    * state capacity
    * private profit from public provision
    * what considerations public bodies may legitimately take into account.
* Important not to overclaim that one Palantir dispute will legally determine all these other areas.
* Better teaching question:
    * why do the rules governing public purchasing matter so much when so much state activity is delivered through contracts?
* Particularly resonant in a political/economic environment where people continually ask:
    * Why is everything so fucking expensive?
* Historical procurement/governance scandals become useful context for why people care about:
    * favouritism
    * access
    * conflicts
    * competition
    * value
    * transparency
    * political influence
    * contractors becoming entrenched.

## 🧭 Possible Child Clusters
> **Working question:**  
> **Who governs when government buys capability?**
>
> The contract dispute is the teaching specimen rather than the whole subject.
> It provides a route into several overlapping questions about where power sits,
> how it moves, what constrains it, and how citizens retain meaningful control
> when public functions depend upon complex institutional and commercial systems.
### 🏛️ Where Does Public Power Actually Live?
- Formal constitutional authority versus practical/operational power.
- Ministers, Parliament, mayors, statutory bodies, police governance, courts.
- Procurement teams, frameworks, technical specialists, contractors and infrastructure.
- Which actor possesses which kind of power?
- Through what mechanism?
- At what point in a decision?
- Distinguish visible institutional complexity from claims of hidden control.
---
### 🤝 The Contract State
- Government increasingly performs functions through contracts and purchased capability.
- Procurement as governance rather than administrative housekeeping.
- Accountability when public functions depend upon private suppliers.
- State capacity versus bought capacity.
- What does democratic government mean when important capabilities are purchased rather than internally possessed?
---
### ⚙️ Old Power Meets New Capability
- Traditional institutional power:
  - statutory authority
  - democratic mandate
  - institutional memory
  - public-law duties
  - legal and coercive authority
- Supplier/technical power:
  - expertise
  - products
  - integration capacity
  - technical knowledge
  - difficult-to-reproduce capability
- Formal authority ≠ operational capability.
- Neither automatically determines the outcome.
- What happens when different species of power encounter one another?
---
### 🪤 Dependency, Lock-In and Exit
- Supplier dependency.
- Switching costs.
- Technical interoperability.
- Data portability.
- Internal expertise.
- Replacement capability.
- Contract renewal and future bargaining power.
- Procurement today as a potential constraint on future room for manoeuvre.
- Keep general questions about lock-in separate from contested claims about any particular supplier.
---
### 👁️ Who Can See Enough to Govern?
- Different actors possess different pieces of the information environment.
- Operational knowledge.
- Commercial information.
- Technical knowledge.
- Parliamentary information.
- Court material.
- Audit and oversight information.
- Publicly available information.
- Confidentiality, privilege, security and other legitimate restrictions.
- Authority alone may be insufficient if the decision-maker cannot see enough to exercise it meaningfully.
**Question:**  
Who knows what, who is entitled to know what, and who can compel somebody else to explain?
---
### ⚖️ Law as Infrastructure for Disagreement
- Procurement law.
- Public law.
- Delegated authority.
- Judicial review.
- Disclosure.
- Parliamentary scrutiny.
- Audit and oversight.
- Law as machinery through which powerful actors can be required to:
  - explain
  - disclose
  - justify
  - reconsider
  - defend
Not merely lawyers arguing over technicalities.
The technicalities are part of the infrastructure through which power is contested.
---
### 🧮 Competing Risk Apparatuses
Different actors may be responding to different risks:
- operational failure
- procurement legality
- value for money
- technological dependency
- litigation
- reputation
- democratic legitimacy
- information security
- national security
- institutional responsibility
An intervention intended to reduce **Risk A** may increase **Risk B** for another actor.
That changed risk environment can then change behaviour.
→ strong route back into **The Feedback Environment**.
---
### 🗳️ Control, Legitimacy and the Citizen
- What meaningful control does the citizen retain?
- Voting is only one control surface.
- Delegation creates distance between electoral choice and operational decisions.
- Public bodies, statutory structures, procurement frameworks, contractors and technical architecture may sit between the two.
- Where are the democratic control surfaces?
- How accessible are they?
- Who can activate them?
- Where does the chain become so remote or opaque that citizens struggle to recognise their own agency within it?
- Relationship between political trust, perceived control, institutional legitimacy and populist responses should be examined empirically rather than assumed.
---
### ♻️ Power Under Observation
Once a decision becomes contested, scrutiny becomes part of the environment.
```text
decision
    ↓
record / disclosure
    ↓
interpretation
    ↓
legal / political / media intervention
    ↓
changed risk and information environment
    ↓
institutional response
    ↓
new records / disclosures / decisions
    ↓
further intervention

Possible examples of mechanisms:

* a letter changes litigation risk
* litigation changes disclosure incentives
* a parliamentary question creates a durable public record
* reporting alters reputational conditions
* disclosure changes what another actor knows
* judicial decisions may alter later institutional behaviour

Observation enters the system and becomes part of the system.

🧵 The Contract As Routing Object

The contract case does not need to prove every proposition explored by these
clusters.

Instead, it can operate as a worked example through which the reader encounters:

authority · capability · procurement · dependency · information · law · risk · legitimacy · feedback

The broader object is therefore not:

Who wins the contract dispute?

but:

What does the dispute allow us to see about how governing power actually
operates when public authority, private capability, law, information and
institutional risk meet?

That gives you the **serious skeleton underneath the squid** without yet committing us to whether each heading ultimately becomes one child node, several nodes, or gets absorbed into a larger cluster.
