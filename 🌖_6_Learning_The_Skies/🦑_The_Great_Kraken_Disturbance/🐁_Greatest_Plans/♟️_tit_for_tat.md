# ♟️ Tit For Tat

*Quick notes on responsive strategy, reciprocity, and what happens when everybody thinks the other bastard moved first.*

## ♟️ What is tit for tat?

- **Tit for tat** is a strategy based on reciprocity:
  - begin by cooperating;
  - thereafter, do what the other player did on the previous round.
- Cooperation is therefore answered with cooperation.
- Defection is answered with defection.
- Unlike a strategy of permanent retaliation, it can return immediately to cooperation if the other player does.
- The important feature for Polaris is that the next move is substantially determined by the **observed previous move of another actor**.
- This makes it useful for thinking about systems in which apparently strategic behaviour may be partly **responsive and recursive**, rather than the execution of one enormous pre-existing plan.

## 📚 Brief history

- The phrase **“tit for tat”** is much older than modern game theory and broadly means equivalent retaliation or repayment in kind.
- Its famous technical use comes from work on the **iterated Prisoner's Dilemma**.
- Political scientist **Robert Axelrod** organised computer tournaments around the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma around 1980.
- Anatol Rapoport submitted a remarkably simple strategy:
  - cooperate on the first move;
  - thereafter copy the opponent's previous move.
- Tit for tat performed extremely well in Axelrod's tournaments despite its simplicity.
- Axelrod subsequently used the tournaments to investigate how cooperation might emerge and persist among actors pursuing their own interests.
- This work became particularly influential through *The Evolution of Cooperation* (1984).

## 🧰 Uses

- Useful model for:
  - repeated interactions;
  - reciprocity;
  - retaliation;
  - cooperation;
  - deterrence;
  - escalation and de-escalation;
  - arms and capability competition;
  - institutional responses;
  - repeated diplomatic or commercial encounters.
- Particularly useful where actors repeatedly explain their own behaviour as a **response**:
  - *We did X because they did Y.*
  - *They did Y because we previously did Z.*
- Can help distinguish:
  - **grand strategy** — execution of a longer-term plan;
  - **responsive strategy** — behaviour substantially shaped by another actor's preceding behaviour;
  - **emergent pattern** — a larger configuration produced by repeated interactions without anybody having planned the whole thing.
- Useful question:
  - **Who thinks they moved first?**
- Requires attention to both:
  - **event time** — what actually happened when;
  - **information time** — what each actor could reasonably have known when choosing its response.

## 🪤 Cons and limitations

- Real institutions are not two-player Prisoner's Dilemma simulations.
- Actors may:
  - misunderstand each other's actions;
  - receive information late;
  - interpret the same action differently;
  - have several objectives simultaneously;
  - contain internal factions;
  - respond to several other actors at once.
- An apparent retaliation may have been planned beforehand.
- Similar actions do not establish copying.
- Sequence does not establish motive.
- **B happened after A ≠ B happened because of A.**
- Noise creates a particular problem:
  - accidental or misunderstood “defection” can provoke retaliation;
  - retaliation can then be interpreted as fresh hostility;
  - reciprocal responses can produce an escalation neither side originally intended.
- Simple tit for tat can therefore become brittle when actors cannot reliably distinguish **intentional action from error, accident, constraint, or misperception**.
- Other reciprocal strategies can be more forgiving.
- Do not retrofit every alternating sequence into tit for tat merely because it looks satisfyingly chess-like.

## 🐁 Greatest Plans

- Tit for tat offers a useful corrective to the **chessmaster model of power**.
- A complicated strategic pattern does not necessarily imply that somebody planned the entire pattern.
- Sometimes:
  - A moves;
  - B responds;
  - A responds to B's response;
  - B responds again;
  - eventually everybody is standing somewhere nobody originally intended to stand.
- Each actor may sincerely understand itself as responding rather than initiating.
- The resulting system can nevertheless acquire:
  - direction;
  - momentum;
  - escalation;
  - path dependence;
  - apparently strategic structure.
- **Strategy can emerge from feedback without requiring a chessmaster.**
- Which is where the mouse enters the chessboard:
  - the greatest plans encounter other actors;
  - other actors have plans;
  - everybody changes the position;
  - *gang aft agley*.

## ❓ Questions for the Kraken

- Who thinks they moved first?
- What does each actor identify as the move it was responding to?
- Did the supposed first move itself have an earlier antecedent?
- What information did each actor possess when it moved?
- Was the response reciprocal, escalatory, conciliatory, or merely interpreted as such?
- Could either actor stop reciprocating without accepting unacceptable risk?
- Is the apparent strategy actually a feedback loop?
- At what point does repeated response create a structure that nobody individually designed?

> **Do not infer a chessmaster where feedback is sufficient to explain the board.**
