# 👑 You Best Not Miss

*Notes on challenging powerful actors, failed moves, retaliation, and what remains standing afterwards.*

## 👑 Basic idea

- Powerful actors are not merely difficult opponents.
- They often possess resources that allow them to survive challenges and respond afterwards.
- Relevant resources may include:
    - legal authority;
    - money;
    - institutional continuity;
    - information;
    - technical capability;
    - political access;
    - professional expertise;
    - litigation capacity;
    - public legitimacy;
    - coercive authority;
    - time.
- Therefore a strategic challenge has at least two questions:
    - **Can the challenger make the move?**
    - **What happens if the challenged actor is still standing afterwards?**

## ♟️ Not one king

- Do not turn the metaphor into a hidden hierarchy.
- Different actors can be extremely powerful in different domains.
- One actor may dominate one relationship while depending upon another actor elsewhere.
- A corporation can possess enormous commercial and technical power without possessing governmental authority.
- A public authority can possess legal authority while depending upon commercial infrastructure.
- A court possesses formidable legal powers within its jurisdiction without governing the entire surrounding political system.
- **Power is relational.**
- There may be several kings on several overlapping boards.

## 🎯 Missing matters

- A failed challenge can change the position even when it does not achieve its objective.
- It may:
    - reveal the challenger's intentions;
    - reveal previously hidden relationships;
    - consume resources;
    - trigger litigation;
    - provoke institutional adaptation;
    - harden positions;
    - generate precedent;
    - expose vulnerabilities;
    - create new alliances or conflicts;
    - alter subsequent calculations.
- Therefore:
    - **failure ≠ return to the previous position.**
- Once somebody has come for the king, everybody knows somebody came for the king.

## 🧨 Retaliation is not automatic

- Do not assume that a powerful actor will retaliate.
- Do not infer retaliation merely because something adverse subsequently happens to the challenger.
- Establish:
    - capacity;
    - authority;
    - opportunity;
    - action;
    - attribution;
    - causation.
- A powerful actor may instead:
    - ignore;
    - negotiate;
    - accommodate;
    - litigate;
    - adapt;
    - withdraw;
    - counterattack;
    - allow another institution to respond.
- **Capacity to respond ≠ evidence of response.**

## 🐁 Greatest Plans

- Before challenging a powerful actor, strategy must account for what happens after the first move.
- This connects to:
    - retaliation;
    - deterrence;
    - escalation;
    - bargaining;
    - institutional resilience;
    - tit for tat;
    - unintended consequences.
- The strategic error is not simply:
    - *the plan failed.*
- It can be:
    - *the plan failed while materially changing the position of an actor powerful enough to make another move.*

## ❓ Questions

- Who possesses power here?
- **What kind of power?**
- Over whom?
- Through what mechanism?
- What would constitute successfully constraining that power?
- What survives an unsuccessful challenge?
- What responses are actually available?
- What response was chosen?
- What evidence connects that response to the original challenge?
- Did the challenger anticipate the second move?
- Did everybody mistakenly assume there was only one king?
