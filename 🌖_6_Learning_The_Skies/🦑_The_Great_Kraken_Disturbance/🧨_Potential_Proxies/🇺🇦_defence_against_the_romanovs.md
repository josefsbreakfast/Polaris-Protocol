
Ahhh. Yes. Romanovs can do double duty.

Not just “Russia, but with historical camp.” It can become a deliberately slippery Polaris term for dynastic/oligarchic elite power that captures or inhabits state machinery, irrespective of nationality.

So 🇺🇦_defence_against_the_romanovs.md can carry two meanings simultaneously:

* Literal historical layer: the Romanov state, Crimea, Britain/Russia, imperial power, and the historical landscape from which Waterloo Place’s memorial emerged.
* Analytical layer: “Romanovs” as the camp shorthand for the recurring problem of concentrated elite power becoming entangled with public authority — wealth, patronage, dynastic networks, commercial interests, political access, institutional capture, etc.

That makes “defence against” more interesting too. It doesn’t have to mean merely military defence against Russia. It can ask what institutional defences prevent public capability being captured by concentrated private/elite power, including when the relevant elites are domestic, allied, commercial, transnational, or emphatically not Russian.

We’d just need one very crisp early rule:

Not every Romanov is Russian, and not every Russian is a Romanov.

Because otherwise the metaphor will start silently converting elite capture into Russian influence, which would wreck precisely the distinction you’re trying to preserve.

And that fits the Waterloo Place method rather nicely: map the mechanism of power first; nationality is an evidential question, not the definition of the mechanism.
