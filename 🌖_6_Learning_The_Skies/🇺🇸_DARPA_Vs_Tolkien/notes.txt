YES. And I think this is one of the strongest reasons for keeping Tolkien in this cluster, because he is an absolute nightmare for feature-based ideological classification. 😭

Small historical precision first: “leave Franco alone” is our comic compression rather than quite Tolkien’s documented position. What we have particularly clearly is Tolkien’s 1944 letter after meeting Roy Campbell, who had fought on Franco’s side. Tolkien thought C. S. Lewis was too willing to believe what Tolkien called “Red propaganda” against Franco, and his reaction was bound up heavily with reports of anti-Catholic violence in Republican-held Spain.  

So:

🇺🇸: “Anti-communist.”

🇬🇧: “Yes.”

🇺🇸: “Excellent. We’ve classified him.”

🇬🇧: “Oh no you fucking haven’t.”

Because Tolkien’s anti-communism sits inside a much stranger constellation: Catholicism; hostility to Soviet/Stalinist totalitarianism; horror at attacks on Catholic clergy; profound suspicion of centralised power; hostility towards industrialised mass society; experience of WWI; opposition to Nazi racial ideology; and a broader theological conception of coercive power and human fallibility.

And importantly, agreement at the output level doesn’t establish agreement at the mechanism level.

That’s a fantastic Learning the Skies principle:

Two systems can produce the same answer for entirely different reasons.

Which means:

Tolkien: communism bad

and

US security institution: communism bad

does not imply:

Tolkien's model of communism = US security institution's model of communism

or even:

Tolkien and US institution will agree about what should be done about communism.

In fact, the disagreement can explode immediately downstream.

The same Tolkien who could be fiercely anti-communist could write during WWII about the misery produced by war and recoil from pleasure being taken in German civilian suffering. In January 1945 he described the destruction of Germany—even while saying Germany had brought catastrophe upon itself—as itself an enormous catastrophe and worried about the triumph of “Machines” and the privileged class serving them.   His April 1944 letter similarly treats war as producing enormous material, moral and spiritual waste even while acknowledging that confronting evil can make war necessary.  

So an intelligence analyst who records merely:

ANTI-COMMUNIST = 1

has thrown away most of the information.

And this generalises enormously beyond Tolkien.

🧭 Agreement is not alignment

I think that deserves its own thing inside 🇺🇸_DARPA_Vs_Tolkien/.

You can have:

Person A → Catholic theology → proposition X

Person B → national-security doctrine → proposition X

Person C → economic interests → proposition X

Person D → ethnic nationalism → proposition X

They’re standing together on X.

Move to Y and they may scatter in four directions.

That’s extremely important to intelligence analysis because if you mistake convergent positions for shared models, you’ll make terrible predictions about what those people do next.

And Tolkien gives us a gloriously compact demonstration because an American analyst could repeatedly think they had him:

🇺🇸: “Anti-communist conservative Catholic. Got it.”

Tolkien says something anarchistic about government.

🇺🇸: 😐

🇺🇸: “Okay. Catholic traditionalist suspicious of modernity.”

Tolkien attacks racism and Nazi racial categories.

🇺🇸: “Fine.”

Tolkien becomes appalled by mechanised warfare and civilian destruction.

🇺🇸: 😐

🇺🇸: “But at least he’s anti-industrial.”

🇬🇧: “He’s from Birmingham.”

🇺🇸: “STOP SAYING THAT LIKE IT EXPLAINS THINGS.”

🇬🇧: “It explains quite a lot, actually.”

🤣

And your intelligence-community point can be made without assuming that US intelligence doesn’t already understand this. Good intelligence analysis does try to distinguish motives, interests, ideology, capability and contingent coalition. The useful question for the node is therefore when institutional classification loses that nuance—particularly when complex human interpretation gets compressed into databases, targeting categories, sentiment labels, ideological taxonomies or machine-readable features.

Because the information loss happens here:

“Why does Tolkien believe this?”

↓

“What does Tolkien believe?”

↓

“Which category contains people who believe this?”

↓

SUBJECT CATEGORY: X

Every downward step makes the information easier to operationalise.

And potentially less capable of predicting Tolkien.

That plugs straight back into the accidental-dataset cluster too. Categorisation makes people computationally tractable by throwing context away. Sometimes that’s necessary. The danger begins when the institution forgets that it did the throwing away.
