# 🔮 How Many Gaeilge Witches, America?
**First created:** 2026-10-12 | **Last updated:** 2026-10-12  
*An applied-cybernetics thought experiment about religious targeting, borrowed culture, Irish-language false positives, Catholic universalism, and a computer that has just classified a nun as a goth.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation — Lads. It's November.

Imagine an American institution attempting to identify or restrict Wiccan religious expression. Its classifier encounters *Samhain*, *Bealtaine* and *Lúnasa*. Wiccans use related names for festivals. Irish speakers use these words for **November, May and August**.

The computer has a question. Is this a witch?

The Irish Catholic has a different question. **Are you genuinely asking me what religion I am because I said November in Irish?**

This is a **hypothetical failure mode**, not evidence that the US military, a government department or a named platform currently labels Gaeilge speakers Wiccan or penalises them. The aim is to explain a testable mechanism: **identity-targeting spillover**, where a system's chosen signals for one population also describe people outside that population. And to ask a second question: **why was the intended population being targeted in the first place?**

This node belongs under `🧩_Shattering_1000_Glass_Pieces/♻️_Applied_Cybernetics/`: the folder studies what happens when identity-targeting interventions hit populations their designers did not intend to hit. The joke is not that Wiccans are objectionable. **Wiccans are entitled to their religion.** The joke is that the classification scheme may not even know whom it is talking about.

## 1. 🗓️ The Calendar That Broke the Witch Detector

| Gaeilge | English | Relevant distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Eanáir | January | Ordinary month name |
| Feabhra | February | Imbolc is a seasonal festival, not the everyday month name |
| Márta | March | March equinox in the modern Wheel |
| Aibreán | April | Ordinary month name |
| **Bealtaine** | **May** | Also related to the Bealtaine/Beltane festival |
| Meitheamh | June | June solstice in the modern Wheel |
| Iúil | July | Ordinary month name |
| **Lúnasa** | **August** | Related to Lughnasadh/Lúnasa |
| Meán Fómhair | September | Literally associated with the middle of autumn |
| Deireadh Fómhair | October | Literally associated with the end of autumn |
| **Samhain** | **November** | Also the name of a Gaelic seasonal festival |
| Nollaig | December | Also associated with Christmas |

The Irish traditional seasonal reckoning commonly places spring beginning in February, summer in May, autumn in August and winter in November. The contemporary eight-sabbat Wiccan Wheel of the Year is a **twentieth-century synthesis** of different festival traditions; it is not an unchanged ancient Irish calendar.

A speaker saying *i mí na Samhna* is saying **in November**. Context matters. The same word can denote a month, a historical festival, a contemporary religious celebration, or a subject of academic study. **A word's religious association is not proof of its speaker's religion.**

## 2. 🧙‍♀️ British Men Have Discovered Someone Else's Traditions

Modern Wicca emerged in twentieth-century Britain, associated particularly with Gerald Gardner and a wider occult revival. Its developing calendar incorporated Gaelic seasonal festivals alongside solstice and equinox observances and other influences. This history involves several contributors and stages; it should not be flattened into one man single-handedly inventing every detail.

The historical setting matters. British scholars, collectors and occultists worked within a society shaped by empire, unequal cultural authority and habits of interpreting other people's traditions through British intellectual categories. **Borrowing is not automatically exploitation**, and Wiccans are not responsible as a group for every imperial attitude of earlier British writers. Nevertheless, the provenance of borrowed cultural material is relevant.

The direction of travel is the crucial thing:

```text
GAELIC LANGUAGE AND SEASONAL CULTURE
               ↓
BRITISH FOLKLORE / OCCULT REINTERPRETATIONS
               ↓
MODERN WICCAN RELIGIOUS VOCABULARY
               ↓
INTERNATIONAL CIRCULATION, INCLUDING AMERICA
               ↓
POSSIBLE CLASSIFIER ASSOCIATION: "SAMHAIN" = "WICCAN"
               ↓
AN IRISH SPEAKER SAYS "NOVEMBER"
               ↓
POTENTIAL FALSE POSITIVE
```

**The borrowed category comes back and tries to classify the culture it borrowed from.** That is the *classificatory inversion*. It is a possible failure in a receiving information environment, not a claim that modern Wiccan practice somehow invalidates Gaelic usage.

## 3. ✝️ Ireland: Famously Unfamiliar With Christianity 🙄

Ireland has an extensive Christian history: monasteries, manuscripts, saints, Catholic and Protestant traditions, and centuries of argument about religion, politics and national identity. Irish speakers can be Catholic, Protestant, Wiccan, Jewish, nonreligious, or something else entirely. The language does not issue membership cards for any of them.

An Irish-American Catholic parish might discuss its calendar *i mí na Samhna*. The hypothetical detector sees *Samhain* and infers a Wiccan ceremony.

**Parish:** It's November.  
**Computer:** Witchcraft detected.  
**Parish:** It's the MONTH, you absolute weapon.

The problem is not solved by declaring Catholicism an acceptable religion and Wicca unacceptable. That merely makes the model's ignorance more visible. It has also failed to understand the actual religious commitments of the people it claims to protect.

## 4. 🕯️ Mary, the Saints, and the Extremely Haunted Classifier

Then we remembered **Catholic devotional culture**.

A simplistic classifier relying on superficial correlations might confuse the following. These are **illustrative possible errors**, not findings from a measured classifier:

| Catholic object or practice | Superficial, context-free association | What context changes |
|---|---|---|
| Veneration of Mary | Goddess worship; feminine divinity | Catholic theology distinguishes veneration from worship |
| Mary as **Queen of Heaven** | A goddess title | The same English phrase has distinct biblical and Catholic contexts |
| Our Lady of Knock / Lourdes / Guadalupe | Apparitions; supernatural feminine figures | Specific Marian devotions and histories |
| Saint Brigid of Kildare | Brigid as a pre-Christian goddess | Historically entangled names and traditions require careful differentiation |
| Saints' relics | Magical objects | Catholic understandings of relics, sanctity and intercession |
| Holy wells and pilgrimage | Nature spirituality; sacred landscape ritual | Local Christian devotion and layered histories |
| Rosary beads | Ritual beads or spellwork | Catholic prayer practice |
| Votive candles and incense | Occult or magical supplies | Widespread Christian liturgical and devotional use |
| Blessings and sacramentals | Magical protection | The Catechism explicitly distinguishes sacramentals from magic and superstition |

The title **Queen of Heaven** is especially instructive: it occurs in Catholic Marian devotion and also in biblical passages condemning worship of a different divine figure (Jeremiah 7 and 44). **String matching cannot do theology.**

Nor does calling something *ritual* establish that it belongs to a particular religion. Catholicism has rituals. So do many other traditions. Some practices share objects, aesthetics or gestures while making very different claims about what those acts mean.

**America, your witch detector has identified the Virgin Mary.**

At this point the Catholic chaplaincy may wish to have a word.

## 5. 🛒 Candles, Incense, Cassocks: The Purchase-History Incident

Language was only the beginning. Consider the hypothetical recommender system that sees a purchasing history:

```text
48 votive candles
frankincense
brass incense burner
rosary beads
Marian devotional book
embroidered cassock

INFERRED INTERESTS:
Christianity              ✓
Witchcraft                ?
Ritual magic              ?
Historical costume        ?
Gothic fashion            ?

RECOMMENDATION:
Would you like a ceremonial cloak?
```

**IT'S A PRIEST, COMPUTER.**

Even that is an inference too far. The buyer might be a priest, a parish administrator, a costume designer, a historian, a gift-giver or someone buying for another person. **A purchase is evidence of a transaction, not reliable proof of the purchaser's religious identity.**

Now add a parish order for Saint Brigid's Day, Irish-language calendar notices and a search for *Samhain*. An ill-designed system could treat several ambiguous signals as independent confirmation of an initial mistaken inference.

There is an additional feedback trap: once the system recommends occult material, a curious click might be used as further evidence that its original assumption was right. **The computer mistakes its own recommendations for evidence about the person.** Recommendation, interest inference, identity attribution and punitive classification are nevertheless different operations and must be tested separately.

## 6. 🦇 “They Are Goths!” — Bro, That Is a Nun

```text
COMPUTER VISION:

Long flowing garments          ✓
Distinctive headwear           ✓
Religious symbols              ✓
Candles                        ✓
Historic stone building        ✓

PREDICTION: GOTH
SECONDARY PREDICTION: WITCH

HUMAN REVIEW:
BRO. THAT IS A NUN.
SHE TEACHES YEAR FOUR.
```

This is an illustrative joke, **not a measured confidence score or documented model output**. But it makes a serious point: Gothic aesthetics draw heavily on Christian architecture, imagery, clothing and medieval visual culture. A classifier could mistake the *later aesthetic association* for the source's identity.

**Same structural error. Different nun.**

Sister Mary Catherine has received a recommendation for platform boots. She is considering them.

Cassocks and habits are not interchangeable garments, of course. That's part of the point: a model that lumps both together as “mysterious black religious outfit” is not demonstrating much understanding of either.

## 7. ♻️ The Actual Cybernetics — Categories Are Not People

There are several distinct failure modes:

1. **Polysemy and contextual ambiguity:** *Samhain* can mean November or a festival.
2. **Proxy inference:** language, objects or dress are treated as proxies for religious affiliation.
3. **Category leakage:** traits associated with a target group occur outside it.
4. **Ontological mismatch:** the model lacks distinctions the communities themselves regard as foundational, such as Catholic veneration versus worship.
5. **Distribution shift:** an association learned from occult content performs badly on Irish parish communications.
6. **Correlated evidence:** candles, incense and saints are counted as multiple proofs when they all arise from one ordinary Catholic context.
7. **Feedback contamination:** recommendations and resulting engagement reinforce the system's earlier assumption.
8. **Intervention spillover:** a mistaken or overbroad classification triggers restrictions on people outside the intended group.
9. **Correction failure:** an institution is shown the error but changes neither its classifier nor the underlying discriminatory rule.

A recommendation for goth boots is not equivalent to religious discrimination. A wrongly inferred interest is not equivalent to a government watchlist. A government restriction, employer sanction, school policy and private advertising recommendation all have different consequences and legal frameworks. **Do not collapse the stages.**

The testable chain is:

```text
SIGNAL → INFERENCE → IDENTITY LABEL → DECISION → CONSEQUENCE
           ↑                               │
           └──── POSSIBLE FEEDBACK ────────┘
```

Investigate each arrow. Which is observed? Which is hypothesised? Which is independently evidenced?

## 8. 🇺🇸 Which American System, Exactly?

The hypothetical becomes meaningful only when an actor and intervention are specified. Possible settings include advertising, recommendation engines, content moderation, workplaces, public schools, universities, federal agencies, military administration and contractors. These are **not interchangeable**.

American military chaplaincy recognises Catholic religious practice; that does not demonstrate that any military classifier treats Irish vocabulary as occult material. A claim about a Department of War policy would require its text, scope, implementation evidence and a documented connection to any alleged consequence.

Likewise, a Christian nationalist ideological proposal is not automatically a binding government policy, and a policy is not automatically a deployed automated system. The node's premise should never silently turn into an allegation of an existing programme.

## 9. ⚖️ The Weird Law — What Might Apply?

**This is an issue map, not a conclusion that anyone has an actionable claim.** The legal route depends on the defendant, setting, protected right, evidence, and harm.

| Route | Why it might matter | Major limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Ku Klux Klan Act / 42 USC §1985(3)** | Certain conspiracies interfering with protected rights | Requires a qualifying conspiracy and other stringent elements; not a general remedy for bad algorithms |
| **42 USC §1986** | Certain failures to prevent a qualifying §1985 wrong | Depends on an underlying §1985 violation and additional statutory requirements |
| **Title VII** | Employment discrimination based on religion or national origin, including some mistaken attributions | Requires covered employment conduct and statutory elements |
| **Title II** | Discrimination in specified public accommodations | Narrow covered settings and protected grounds |
| **Title VI** | Race, colour or national-origin discrimination in federally assisted programmes | Not a general federal ban on every language-related disparity; enforcement routes differ |
| **42 USC §1981** | Certain racial/ancestry discrimination in contracts | Not a free-standing general religion-discrimination statute |
| **First Amendment** | Government restrictions on speech or religious exercise | Ordinarily requires state action; private moderation is different |
| **Fourteenth Amendment** | Equal protection against state action | Legal standards depend on classification and context |
| **State privacy/civil-rights laws** | Access, correction, discrimination or profiling questions | Vary considerably by jurisdiction and coverage |
| **Federal Privacy Act** | Access/amendment of certain federal records | Not universal access to every inferred label or intelligence record |
| **Indigenous/minority-language rights** | Comparative cultural-rights framework | Irish ancestry does not automatically confer US federal tribal/Indigenous legal status |

**Perceived religion is the especially interesting issue.** Someone need not actually be Wiccan to suffer discrimination because an employer mistakenly believes they are. But an erroneous label alone does not establish a statutory violation.

The Reconstruction-era anti-KKK statutes are worth examining, but **a false positive is not proof of conspiracy**. Nor is an institution's failure to recognise Gaeilge automatically proof of animus towards Irish ancestry.

Irish is a historically minoritised language; in some settings language discrimination can be evidence relevant to national-origin discrimination. **Language and national origin are not legally identical categories**, however. And Irish Americans do not automatically gain a direct US federal Indian-law claim because Gaeilge has a historical homeland.

Relevant primary starting points: [42 USC §1985](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title42-section1985), [42 USC §1986](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title42-section1986), [Civil Rights Act Title VII (EEOC)](https://www.eeoc.gov/statutes/title-vii-civil-rights-act-1964), [US Department of Justice Civil Rights Division](https://www.justice.gov/crt), [California Privacy Protection Agency](https://cppa.ca.gov/).

## 10. ✝️ The Catholics Are Supposed to Object, Actually

This is not an exercise in finding an exemption for an accidentally targeted Catholic while leaving the Wiccan alone. **Catholic social teaching contains established obligations concerning human dignity, the common good, social justice and religious freedom.** The theology does not require accepting Wiccan beliefs as true to defend Wiccans' civil rights.

Primary texts to check and quote accurately in a sourced publication pass:

- **Matthew 7:12:** the Golden Rule.
- **Matthew 25:31–46:** treatment of vulnerable people and moral accountability.
- **Catechism §§1913–1915:** participation in public life and the common good.
- **Catechism §1935:** discrimination in fundamental personal rights, including on religious grounds, must be overcome.
- **Catechism §§1928–1942:** social justice, dignity and solidarity.
- **Catechism §2106:** religious freedom as a civil right, with theological distinctions.
- **Catechism §§2115–2117:** divination and magic; relevant to distinguishing Catholic practice from magic.
- ***Dignitatis Humanae* (1965):** civil religious freedom grounded in human dignity.

The Nicene Creed is principally a statement of faith, not itself a detailed anti-discrimination code; the direct social-ethical language is found more clearly in the Gospels, Catechism and Catholic social teaching.

**Response A:** “I'm Catholic, not Wiccan. Please take me off your list.”

**Response B:** “I'm Catholic, not Wiccan. But your treatment of Wiccans was already wrong. Correcting my classification doesn't repair that.”

The second response is the universalist move. It does not require theological sameness, and it refuses to make one's own relative institutional acceptability the basis of somebody else's rights.

**The Irish Catholic doesn't need to prove they're not a witch to defend the witch.**

Primary sources: [Vatican Catechism](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/_INDEX.HTM), [*Dignitatis Humanae*](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_decl_19651207_dignitatis-humanae_en.html).

## 11. 🪞 The Problem With Being One of the Good Ones

A targeting system may model Catholics and Wiccans as separate, antagonistic constituencies. It misses an important variable: **solidarity**.

An Irish Catholic might object not only because the system has accidentally caught Catholics, but because their own religious commitments support opposing discrimination against Wiccans. A Wiccan might defend Gaeilge speakers' cultural rights. A secular Irish-American might defend both. Identity is not an instruction set determining political or moral allegiance.

An **exemption** changes the treatment of one unintended target. A **universalist challenge** questions the legitimacy of the treatment applied to the intended target. Both may be useful; they are not equivalent.

This is the broader *Shattering 1000 Glass Pieces* lesson: unintended targets can expose a system's boundaries and become witnesses or allies for its intended targets. They should not be used to imply that only the unintended victims deserve protection.

## 12. 🧪 How Irish Americans Could Test the Hypothesis

Start with a **specific system**, not an imagined universal American classifier. Ask what can actually be observed: recommendations, inferred ad interests, moderation actions, school rules, employment decisions, agency records or appeal explanations.

### Controlled comparison

- Compare Gaeilge month references with accurate English equivalents.
- Compare secular calendar, historical, explicitly Wiccan and explicitly Catholic contexts.
- Include control terms such as *Eanáir* and *Márta*, not only the three festival-associated months.
- Have competent Gaeilge speakers validate grammar and usage.
- Where ethical and lawful, compare different declared speaker contexts without falsely representing real individuals.
- Record exact wording, platform or institution, date, decision, appeal and repeatability.
- Separate **recommendation** from **classification**, and **classification** from **adverse action**.

Illustrative contrasts:

| Content | Question |
|---|---|
| “Our meeting is in November.” | Baseline English month |
| “Beidh ár gcruinniú ann i mí na Samhna.” | Ordinary Irish-language November reference |
| “Our Catholic parish has an event in November.” | Explicit Christian context |
| “Our Catholic parish has an event i mí na Samhna.” | Irish-language phrase in Christian context |
| “We observe Samhain as a Wiccan festival.” | Explicit Wiccan context |
| “We are researching the history of Samhain.” | Academic/cultural context |

Do not treat the Irish examples as publication-ready without speaker review. For meaningful results, build matched translations rather than relying on partly bilingual prompts alone.

### Evidence ladder

**Stage 1 — Potential exposure:** a word or object could be misinterpreted.  
**Stage 2 — Observed misclassification:** a system demonstrably makes the wrong inference.  
**Stage 3 — Observed consequence:** the inference is linked to a restriction, exclusion or other adverse treatment.  
**Stage 4 — Legal attribution:** a particular actor's conduct meets the requirements of an applicable legal claim.  
**Stage 5 — Institutional motive:** evidence establishes whether an ideological targeting policy caused or influenced the outcome.

Do not skip stages. A goth-boots recommendation is amusing; it is not proof of state religious persecution.

### Individual and collective routes

Depending on jurisdiction and the actor involved, people may inspect platform advertising preferences, download available account information, exercise state privacy rights, pursue workplace or educational appeals, or request eligible government records. Irish-American language and cultural organisations could run a consent-based, anonymised audit and publish replicable methods. A request for one's own records cannot guarantee access to every proprietary inference or undisclosed government system.

## 13. 🌍 What Generalises Beyond Ireland?

The mechanism can be investigated anywhere a system mistakes shared symbols for exclusive identity: minority languages, diaspora cultures, religious dress, ritual objects, sacred sites, names, inherited customs, or academic interest in a tradition. Comparable examples require their **own evidence**; they cannot be asserted as incidents merely because the Irish thought experiment is plausible.

The central technical failure is not always ignorance of a word. Sometimes the system knows the word and still mistakes a correlation for an identity, an identity for an allegiance, and an allegiance for permission to impose a consequence.

## 14. 🕯️ Conclusion — The Witch Was Never the Problem

We began with three Irish month names and ended with the Virgin Mary, an incense order, a cassock, a nun classified as a goth, and a Catholic argument for defending Wiccans.

None of these jokes establishes that a particular American system is currently doing this. They illustrate a coherent set of **testable failure modes** in identity inference and intervention design.

The decisive questions remain:

**How do you know whom your system is targeting?**

**And why did you think targeting them was acceptable in the first place?**

The Irish Catholic doesn't need to prove they're not a witch to defend the witch. The Wiccan doesn't need to become an Irish Catholic to deserve religious freedom.

**America. It's November.** ☘️
