# 📚 Guard The Books
**First created:** 2026-09-16 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-16  
*Books are not merely containers of extractable text: they are memory technologies, social objects, cultural infrastructure, emergency equipment, Commons resources, and messages sent to people who have not been born yet.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

There are moments when a governance argument can remain technical.

And there are moments when the appropriate response is:

**No. Guard the fucking books.**

This node begins from a fairly simple human objection.

There is a difference between preserving a book and consuming it as raw material.

There is a difference between scanning a fragile text so that people can continue to encounter it and acquiring cultural material at scale because its contents can be converted into computational capability.

There is a difference between a library, a Commons, an archive, a bookshop, a collection, a dataset and a mine.

Those differences do not mean artificial intelligence is inherently hostile to books. Quite the opposite: machine translation, accessibility technology, search, optical character recognition, historical reconstruction, language learning, archive discovery and educational agents could become extraordinary additions to human literary culture.

The objection is narrower and more serious:

> **An information technology should understand the information ecology it is entering before it optimises away the things humans valued about it.**

Books are not valuable merely because they contain strings of words.

Humans write them, edit them, illustrate them, translate them, print them, bind them, buy them, lend them, inherit them, annotate them, recommend them, argue about them, hide them, ban them, burn them, rescue them, digitise them, rediscover them and leave them in tiny wooden boxes for strangers.

Sometimes we read them for information.

Sometimes we read them because someone died.

Sometimes we read them because someone survived.

Sometimes we read them because somebody imagined a world that did not exist.

Sometimes we read them because we cannot explain what is happening to us and somebody else found the words first.

Sometimes a librarian hands us the book we did not know how to ask for.

Sometimes a second-hand bookseller keeps an obscure work circulating for another generation.

Sometimes an archive preserves something that its own custodians do not yet understand the significance of.

Sometimes a book sits unread for fifty years and then becomes exactly the book somebody needs.

That is not inefficiency.

That is the system working.

## ✨ Key Features

This node treats books simultaneously as:

- **memory technologies** — allowing thought, testimony and imagination to outlive individual human memory;
- **cultural objects** — carrying histories of publication, translation, ownership, annotation, circulation and reception;
- **social objects** — producing relationships between writers, readers, librarians, booksellers, teachers, translators, collectors and strangers;
- **Commons infrastructure** — capable of circulating knowledge without making knowledge ownerless;
- **witness infrastructure** — preserving accounts made before later generations know how events will end;
- **emergency equipment** — offering language, consolation, orientation and imaginative survival during difficult periods;
- **future infrastructure** — preserving unrealised ideas and worlds that later generations may rediscover;
- **governance objects** — raising questions about ownership, access, consent, stewardship, preservation, extraction and enclosure.

The governing distinction is therefore not:

> **books good / technology bad**

It is:

> **preservation is not extraction; access is not ownership; openness is not ownerlessness; possession is not moral permission; and information is not the whole object.**

## 📚 A Book Is Not Just Its Text

A book has informational content.

That is obvious.

What is less obvious is how quickly computational systems can encourage us to treat informational content as though it were the complete description of the object.

It is not.

A book can also have:

- an authorial history;
- an editorial history;
- a publication history;
- a translation history;
- a censorship history;
- a reception history;
- an ownership history;
- annotations;
- inscriptions;
- typography;
- illustrations;
- binding;
- paper;
- provenance;
- relationships with other editions;
- relationships with other books;
- relationships with particular communities;
- relationships with historical events;
- relationships with particular readers.

Even the date matters.

A political book published in 2022 is not epistemically identical to the same sentences presented without chronology in 2026.

The fact that somebody thought something worth recording **then** is information.

What they knew is information.

What they could not yet know is information.

How contemporary readers responded is information.

The physical and chronological organisation of a library can therefore tell us things that a flattened corpus cannot.

> **A model may contain language from twenty years of warnings. A shelf can show you that somebody was warning you in 2022.**

Those are different epistemic objects.

## 🕯️ Humans Build Things That Remember Outside Themselves

Human memory fails.

People forget.

People die.

Families disperse.

Languages lose speakers.

Governments fall.

Institutions close.

Publishers disappear.

Buildings burn.

Servers fail.

File formats become obsolete.

Markets stop circulating things that no longer appear profitable.

So humans build things that remember outside themselves.

Books are one of those things.

The extraordinary trick is almost too familiar to notice:

> **A human being can arrange marks on a surface so that, centuries later, another human being whom they could never have met can reconstruct some portion of their thought.**

That is already an astonishing information technology.

And transmission is not merely copying.

A reader encounters a text and:

- understands;
- misunderstands;
- associates;
- remembers;
- rejects;
- argues;
- compares;
- recommends;
- teaches;
- translates;
- writes something else.

The output is another human mind in a slightly altered state.

Books therefore participate in **human memory formation**, not merely information storage.

## 🪦 Memory Destruction Has More Than One Form

The most visually obvious form of cultural destruction is burning.

History provides enough examples that nobody should need reminding that destroying books and records can accompany attempts to destroy communities, suppress political movements, erase religious traditions or control public memory.

But burning is only one failure mode.

A culture can lose access to a work because it is:

- deliberately destroyed;
- legally prohibited;
- removed from institutions;
- suppressed by intimidation;
- made financially inaccessible;
- allowed to decay;
- no longer printed;
- poorly catalogued;
- stripped of context;
- stored somewhere nobody can discover it;
- digitised badly;
- locked into an obsolete format;
- translated inaccurately;
- separated from its provenance;
- overwhelmed by disinformation;
- or simply forgotten.

Something can therefore remain technically extant while becoming culturally absent.

That matters when discussing contemporary disputes over libraries, curricula, archives and historical interpretation.

The question is not merely:

> **Does a copy still exist somewhere?**

It is:

> **What evidence about the past can an ordinary person reasonably encounter?**

---

## 🕯️ Witness Before Hindsight

Victor Klemperer's diaries provide an especially important form of preservation: **memory destruction being witnessed while events are still unfolding**.

The value of contemporary testimony is partly that the witness does not possess the ending.

Later historians can reconstruct legislation, institutions and chronology.

What is harder to reconstruct is:

> **What did Tuesday feel like before anybody knew what Thursday would bring?**

A diary preserves uncertainty.

It preserves changes that may have seemed small at the time.

It preserves vocabulary.

It preserves what was becoming normal.

It preserves what the writer noticed before later history decided which details were important.

Klemperer's later attention to language makes the point even sharper: political transformation occurs not only through spectacular events but through changes in the words people repeatedly encounter and eventually begin to use themselves.

So:

> **Guard the words in which the event was experienced.**

A later summary is not a substitute for the record.

## 🪦 What Happens Afterwards?

Jeremy Treglown's *Franco's Crypt* moves the problem forward.

After repression, dictatorship, war or political rupture, memory does not simply switch back on.

Societies inherit:

- monuments;
- absences;
- stories;
- graves;
- buildings;
- films;
- books;
- silences;
- archives;
- competing family memories;
- political narratives;
- unresolved arguments about what should be remembered and how.

Carlos Ruiz Zafón's Cemetery of Forgotten Books gives the same problem a literary architecture.

Someone must guard what might otherwise disappear.

Someone must preserve the possibility of a future encounter.

The crucial word there is **future**.

The person for whom the book will matter may not exist yet.

You guard it for a stranger.

## 🔥 Book Burning Is A Warning, Not The Whole Model

Book destruction recurs across very different political and historical contexts.

The details matter, and distinct events should not be flattened into one generic story. But the recurrence is useful because it demonstrates a persistent political intuition:

> **Control over cultural transmission can matter to control over society.**

A future research pass should maintain a sourced comparative table distinguishing at minimum:

| Context | What Was Targeted | Mechanism | Intended / Documented Function | What Survived |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Qin-era textual destruction traditions | texts and scholarly traditions | destruction / suppression | contested historical accounts of intellectual and political consolidation | later textual reconstruction and transmission |
| Reformation and Counter-Reformation Europe | religious and political texts | censorship, confiscation, destruction | doctrinal and political control | dispersed copies, later collections |
| Nazi Germany | works classified as politically, culturally or racially unacceptable | organised public burnings and wider censorship | ideological purification and suppression | private copies, exile publishing, libraries and archives |
| Francoist Spain | politically and culturally disfavoured materials | censorship, seizure, destruction, suppression | political and cultural control | archives, private collections, later recovery |
| Military dictatorships and authoritarian regimes elsewhere | opposition writing, journalism, community records | censorship, seizure, disappearance, destruction | suppression of dissent and memory | diaspora archives, private custody, later recovery |
| Contemporary library and curriculum disputes | particular books, themes and historical accounts | restriction, removal, curricular control, institutional pressure | contested: child protection, curricular authority, ideological control and historical interpretation are among the stated or alleged rationales | copies frequently remain available elsewhere, but access and routine encounter can change |

The point of the table is **not** that every restriction is equivalent to a book burning.

It is precisely the opposite.

We need enough conceptual precision to distinguish:

**destruction  
suppression  
restriction  
removal  
neglect  
context loss  
historical revision  
disinformation  
and ordinary editorial or collection decisions.**

If every disagreement becomes BOOK BURNING, the concept stops helping us identify the occasions when cultural destruction really is occurring.

## 🧿 Guarding History Requires Epistemic Care

There is a corresponding danger in treating unlimited circulation as automatically virtuous.

Some information can cause harm.

Propaganda exists.

Disinformation exists.

Fraud exists.

Operationally dangerous information exists.

Cybersecurity researchers already have to think seriously about malicious instructions, adversarial information environments and systems that can propagate harmful content at machine speed.

AI research has made some of these questions feel unexpectedly close to the fictional territory explored by collaborative horror projects such as the SCP Foundation: information that changes behaviour merely through exposure; dangerous instructions; systems that must reason about whether information should be propagated.

Fiction is not evidence that such mechanisms exist exactly as imagined.

It is useful because horror frequently takes a real anxiety and pushes it until its structure becomes visible.

The governance problem is therefore not:

> **everything must circulate without restriction**

nor:

> **authorities should decide what everybody may know.**

It is how to preserve open inquiry while maintaining:

- provenance;
- evidence;
- criticism;
- contextualisation;
- trustworthy institutions;
- plural sources;
- and proportionate controls where genuinely dangerous information requires them.

Post-truth conditions make this harder, not less important.

## 🌱 Commons Is Not Ownerless

The history of books intersects with a much older argument about Commons.

Gerrard Winstanley and the Diggers were writing about land, subsistence, property and common cultivation in seventeenth-century England.

They were not writing about artificial intelligence.

The historical differences matter.

But the recurring governance question is recognisable:

> **Who may use a shared resource, who contributes to it, who benefits from it, who may exclude others, and by what authority?**

A Commons is not necessarily a free-for-all.

Commons can have:

- stewardship;
- membership;
- licences;
- customs;
- contribution expectations;
- access rules;
- attribution;
- sanctions;
- boundaries;
- institutions;
- duties.

That makes **PUBLICLY ACCESSIBLE = OWNERLESS RAW MATERIAL** a serious category error.

A resource can be available for use without being available for every conceivable exploitation.

The distinction becomes almost absurdly clear in a Little Free Library.

**TAKE A BOOK, LEAVE A BOOK** does not mean:

> Take every book, dismantle the cabinet, sell the timber, monetise a database of its contents and explain that the neighbours consented because they had placed the box outside.

The joke works because humans already understand the underlying norm.

Availability occurs inside a relationship.

## 🐝 The Digital Commons Already Existed

The internet did not have to wait for frontier AI laboratories to discover that humans like sharing knowledge.

Humans had already spent decades building digital libraries, archives and knowledge projects.

The landscape includes, among many others:

- Project Gutenberg;
- Internet Archive;
- Open Library;
- Wikisource;
- Europeana;
- Gallica;
- HathiTrust;
- specialist religious libraries;
- scientific repositories;
- historical archives;
- language-specific collections;
- disability-access libraries;
- public-domain audiobook projects;
- university digitisation programmes;
- community archives.

Their motives differ enormously.

So do their legal structures.

Some preserve public-domain works.

Some operate controlled access.

Some serve particular scholarly communities.

Some exist to protect vulnerable cultural heritage.

Some improve disability access.

Some provide search infrastructure.

Some are ideological.

Some are governmental.

Some are charitable.

Some are commercial.

Some have faced serious copyright challenges.

That variety is precisely the point.

Humans were already negotiating:

> **How do we place knowledge into wider circulation without destroying the systems that create, preserve and govern it?**

The answers were imperfect.

Copyright sometimes obstructed preservation and access.

Digitisation could leave users flicking slowly through page images that were barely machine-readable.

Search could be poor.

Collections remained fragmented.

Projects ran out of money.

Some digital-lending practices generated major disputes with authors and publishers.

But these were governance problems being negotiated in public.

The answer was not supposed to be:

> **Fuck it. Whoever has the most compute gets the Commons.**

## 📖 Open Library And The Copyright Collision

Open Library is particularly useful because it demonstrates that the collision between preservation, access and copyright predates the current generative-AI dispute.

Its ambition — associated with the idea of creating a web page for every published book — sits squarely within the digital-library tradition.

But its scanning and controlled-digital-lending practices also produced sustained opposition from writers, publishers and rights organisations.

The litigation around *Hachette v. Internet Archive* demonstrated that saying **library**, **preservation**, **access** or **public benefit** does not automatically resolve copyright questions.

That matters.

The book ecosystem had already been arguing about:

- scanning;
- digital reproduction;
- lending;
- ownership;
- first sale;
- fair use;
- accessibility;
- preservation;
- creator compensation.

Then large-scale model training arrived.

The historical question for the publishing industry is therefore not merely:

> **Why do publishers care about one form of copying but not another?**

It is:

> **Which incentives, dependencies, licensing opportunities, competitive pressures and technological assumptions cause different forms of copying and extraction to be treated differently?**

That is a cybernetics problem.

Follow the feedback loops.

Follow the money.

Follow the control points.

Follow who can say no.

## 🏡 Take A Book, Leave A Book

Little Free Libraries are almost offensively wholesome evidence that people enjoy low-friction Commons behaviour when the system retains recognisable reciprocity.

A box appears.

Someone puts books in it.

Someone takes one.

Someone leaves another.

Nobody needs a behavioural-prediction market.

Nobody needs a token.

Nobody needs a metaverse property deed.

Nobody needs to vertically integrate the neighbourhood.

The thing works because the social proposition is legible:

> **I have something you might enjoy. Here.**

That is not identical to public ownership.

It is not identical to charity.

It is not identical to commercial exchange.

It is not identical to a statutory library service.

It is a tiny local circulation mechanism.

And people keep reinventing versions of it.

That should tell technological designers something.

## 🐝 Put Books Into Children's Hands

Dolly Parton's Imagination Library belongs in the same positive constellation.

Its basic proposition is again strikingly human:

> **Children should receive books.**

Not merely information.

Not merely literacy metrics.

Books.

Objects that arrive.

Objects that can be held.

Objects that can become familiar.

Objects that can be reread.

Objects that can belong to a child before that child has the economic power to build a library independently.

This does not mean physical books are the only valid literacy technology.

It means that when thinking about educational technology, the objective should be **expanding the child's relationship with language, story, curiosity and knowledge**, rather than replacing those relationships with whatever product category currently attracts investment.

## 🕹️ We Could Build Much Cooler Things

There is enormous constructive potential in combining educational AI, games, immersive computing, museums, libraries and archives.

Imagine:

- adaptive literacy support that helps children practise without replacing teachers;
- historical VR developed with historians and museums;
- accessible reading systems for dyslexic and print-disabled readers;
- multilingual story environments;
- endangered-language learning tools;
- AI-supported archive discovery;
- manuscript transcription assistance;
- tools that help readers compare editions and translations;
- oral-history interfaces that preserve provenance;
- interactive maps connecting books with places and historical events;
- recommendation systems optimised for curiosity rather than addiction;
- educational agents that send a child back towards books rather than positioning themselves as substitutes for them.

The frustrating thing about technological critique is that the alternative is not technological stagnation.

It is **better imagination**.

## 🕹️ The Metaverse Mistake

Humans already had virtual worlds.

We called many of them games.

People enjoyed:

- exploring;
- playing;
- mastering systems;
- creating;
- modding;
- competing;
- cooperating;
- telling stories;
- discovering secrets;
- being silly with friends.

Some corporate visions of the metaverse appeared to look at this extraordinary human success and conclude:

> Excellent. Add offices, property markets, branded retail environments and financial assets.

The problem was not virtuality.

The problem was failing to notice **what humans liked about the virtual worlds they already inhabited**.

The same category error can happen with books.

Humans built enormous literary cultures around:

- reading;
- writing;
- collecting;
- lending;
- teaching;
- translating;
- annotating;
- recommending;
- preserving;
- discovering;
- discussing.

A computational system can look at this and see:

> **enormous quantity of useful text**

That observation is not false.

It is incomplete.

> **Sometimes the friction is where the human is.**

## 🏛️ Libraries Are Human Technologies

Susan Orlean's *The Library Book* helps make visible how much a library does besides store text.

Libraries:

- acquire;
- catalogue;
- preserve;
- organise;
- lend;
- recommend;
- provide public space;
- maintain continuity;
- help people search;
- create accessibility;
- retain institutional memory.

The library's function can be represented roughly as:

> **collect → preserve → organise → return to humans**

An extractive computational system can instead produce:

> **collect → process → derive capability → accumulate private value**

The second system may produce useful things.

But the governance question is obvious:

> **Where is the return arrow?**

## 📚 The Book You Did Not Know How To Ask For

Michiko Aoyama's *What You Are Looking For Is in the Library* provides the positive social case.

A librarian does not merely retrieve the information most semantically similar to a user's request.

A human intermediary can notice context.

Recommend unexpectedly.

Introduce serendipity.

Give somebody:

> **the book they did not know how to ask for.**

That is not a trivial inefficiency waiting to be eliminated by better search.

It is one of the functions.

The recommendation contains judgement.

The library contains shared infrastructure.

The book contains another person's work.

The reader arrives with circumstances that neither author nor librarian completely controls.

Meaning happens in the interaction.

## 🏮 Bookshops Are Part Of The Ecology

Satoshi Yagisawa's *Days at the Morisaki Bookshop* adds the second-hand bookshop.

This matters because the book ecosystem cannot be reduced to:

> public = virtuous  
> commercial = extractive

Authors make money.

Translators make money.

Editors make money.

Illustrators make money.

Printers make money.

Publishers make money.

Booksellers make money.

They should.

The question is whether the transaction **supports or destroys the surrounding ecology**.

Second-hand books are especially instructive.

A book can be:

> bought → read → sold → shelved → browsed → rediscovered → bought again → read again

That is commercially inefficient if the sole objective is maximising revenue from newly manufactured intellectual property.

It is culturally magnificent.

The book keeps moving.

The shop survives.

An older work remains discoverable.

A new reader encounters an old writer.

Economic value and cultural value are not identical measurements.

## 🩹 Poetry Is Sometimes Emergency Equipment

*Emergency Kit: Poems for Strange Times* makes another function explicit.

Sometimes a person does not need a factual proposition.

They need a poem.

The *Poetry Pharmacy* tradition makes the metaphor even clearer: poems can be selected and offered in response to recognisable human difficulties.

This is not clinical medicine.

It is **curation as care**.

The chain can be:

> librarian → recommendation  
> bookseller → recommendation  
> friend → "read this"  
> teacher → poem  
> anthologist → selection  
> reader → recognition

And poetry is one of the clearest demonstrations that **semantic extraction is not the whole work**.

A poem's value may depend upon:

- rhythm;
- sound;
- ambiguity;
- image;
- lineation;
- form;
- repetition;
- association;
- the exact words;
- the work performed by the reader.

A summary can successfully identify the topic while destroying almost everything for which the reader wanted the poem.

> **A text is not necessarily valuable because it contains information that can be extracted from it. Sometimes its value lies in what happens to a human being while encountering its particular form.**

Books are not merely the archive we protect after an emergency.

Sometimes they are part of the emergency kit we carry through it.

## 🧠 Books Carry Other People's Lives

Bridget Collins's *The Binding* literalises a particularly uncomfortable idea: memories can become bound objects.

Its fictional mechanism is useful precisely because ordinary books already perform a less magical version of this transformation.

Writers draw upon:

- memory;
- testimony;
- history;
- family;
- observation;
- imagination;
- other people's stories;
- communities;
- inherited culture.

Third-party authors can transform experiences beyond themselves into authored works.

That creates several relationships which must not be collapsed:

> **whose experience → who records it → who transforms it → who authors the work → who owns the object → who holds legal rights → who encounters it later**

Those are different things.

The same is true of letters and diaries.

Physical custody does not automatically erase ethical relationships to the people represented.

See [`🖋️ Guard The Letters`](./🖋️_guard_the_letters.md) for the corresponding problem of private correspondence, estates, consent, historical preservation and computational extraction.

## 🔮 Books Preserve Roads Not Taken

Books do not only preserve the past.

They preserve **possible futures**.

Political writing, speculative fiction, poetry, manifestos, design, theology, philosophy and science can record:

- what people hoped;
- what they feared;
- what they proposed;
- what they attempted;
- what failed;
- what was abandoned;
- what nobody had yet built.

A future reader can encounter an old idea and ask:

> Why didn't we do this?

Or:

> It failed then. Would it fail now?

Or:

> They saw the problem fifty years ago.

Or simply:

> **We don't have to organise the world this way.**

That is why futurist collections such as *Terraform: Building a Better World* belong in a node about preservation.

An archive is not merely a record of roads travelled.

> **It is a storehouse of roads not taken.**

## 🌱 Guarding The Past Protects Future Imagination

If preservation increasingly privileges material that is:

- commercially successful;
- already highly digitised;
- English-language;
- computationally legible;
- frequently cited;
- currently fashionable;
- institutionally powerful;
- or especially useful for machine learning,

then the loss is not merely historical.

The **space of imaginable futures narrows**.

Obscure works matter.

Minority languages matter.

Failed movements matter.

Old arguments matter.

Local histories matter.

Weird books matter.

Bad books can matter.

Forgotten books matter.

The future may ask different questions from us.

Preservation therefore requires enough humility to admit:

> **We do not know which parts of the archive future people will need.**

## 🧮 The Extraction Question

Shoshana Zuboff's work on surveillance capitalism provides one useful conceptual language for thinking about computational extraction from human activity.

Generative AI is not simply identical to the systems Zuboff describes.

But the recurring move is worth examining:

> **something produced through human life becomes reconceptualised as an available input into privately accumulated computational value.**

That produces a governance question rather than an anti-technology slogan.

When human cultural production becomes training material:

- who consented?
- who was compensated?
- who retains rights?
- what provenance survives?
- what cultural context survives?
- what becomes privately enclosed?
- what returns to the people whose work created the capability?
- what happens to the institutions that previously sustained production?
- what happens when the extractor becomes economically stronger than the ecosystem being extracted?

The last question is cybernetic.

A system that removes more value from its environment than the environment can replenish eventually damages its own input conditions.

## 🌀 The Commons Needs Return Pathways

A healthy Commons contains feedback.

People take.

People contribute.

People maintain.

People teach newcomers.

People establish norms.

People correct errors.

People preserve.

People replenish.

Remove the return pathways and eventually the word **Commons** stops describing the system.

You are describing extraction from one.

This is why the question for AI and books cannot end at:

> **Was the text accessible?**

It must include:

> **What relationship allowed it to become accessible, and does your use preserve that relationship?**

## 📣 Books Can Warn The Future

Azeem Ibrahim's *Authoritarian Century* is useful partly because of when it appeared.

A political diagnosis published in 2022 becomes a timestamp.

It lets later readers ask:

> **What could an attentive person have known at this point?**

Jason Stanley's *Erasing History* belongs nearby for a related reason: it centres the political significance of historical narrative and the relationship between accounts of the past and possibilities for the future.

The works do not need to be treated as infallible predictions.

Their value includes the fact that somebody recorded an argument **before later events had finished unfolding**.

Books allow societies to leave warnings for themselves.

Sometimes they even read them.

## 🧿 Guard The Conditions Of Historical Knowledge

Contemporary disputes in the United States over books, curricula and the presentation of Black history provide an important current case for a later evidence pass.

The node should maintain careful distinctions between:

- individual book challenges;
- school-library removals;
- public-library decisions;
- curriculum requirements;
- restrictions on teaching;
- museum and heritage interpretation;
- archival preservation;
- political disagreement over historical framing;
- deliberate falsification or destruction of evidence.

These mechanisms are not interchangeable.

But they can all affect the same variable:

> **What evidence about the past will the next generation routinely encounter?**

Guarding books therefore means more than preventing bonfires.

It means guarding the conditions under which people can **find out what happened**.

## 🧨 WAAAA Threshold

There is a point at which the emotional response to technological development changes.

It can begin at:

> Okay. This is disruptive, but perhaps we can reform the system. We can negotiate licences. We can improve provenance. We can build better public infrastructure. We can make the Commons work.

And then somebody proposes destroying scarce books, purchasing culturally significant artefacts for destructive scanning, stripping provenance, consuming private correspondence or treating the accumulated literary inheritance of humanity as merely another feedstock for proprietary model development.

At which point:

> **Right lads. Today we eat the rich. Get the sriracha.**

This is not a policy recommendation.

It is a diagnostic reading of the emotional threshold.

There are forms of extraction that cause people to stop perceiving a technology company as an imperfect participant in a shared cultural system and start perceiving it as a threat to that system.

That transition matters.

Trust is infrastructure.

Once destroyed, it is expensive to rebuild.

Medical history provides an uncomfortable analogue: institutions may believe that scientific utility is sufficient justification for acquiring human material. Communities may remember instead that bodies were taken, consent was absent, and professional institutions benefited.

The resulting distrust can outlive the original practice by generations.

**Technical utility does not cancel cultural injury.**

Frontier AI laboratories would be wise not to rediscover that principle using libraries.

## 📚 The Reading Shelf

This shelf is deliberately mixed.

It contains fiction, diaries, cultural history, political analysis, poetry and books about libraries and bookshops because **the subject itself exceeds a single disciplinary vocabulary**.

The fiction is not being used as documentary evidence for historical events.

The political works are not being treated as neutral descriptions merely because they are nonfiction.

The shelf is a set of lenses.

| Work | Shelf Function |
|---|---|
| **Carlos Ruiz Zafón — *The Shadow of the Wind*** | **Guardianship and forgotten culture.** The Cemetery of Forgotten Books gives literary form to the obligation to preserve the possibility of future encounter. |
| **Jeremy Treglown — *Franco's Crypt: Spanish Culture and Memory Since 1936*** | **Contested memory after dictatorship.** How cultural memory is reconstructed, represented and argued over after political rupture. |
| **Victor Klemperer — *I Shall Bear Witness: The Diaries 1933–41*** | **Witness during destruction.** The preservation of ordinary experience, political change and language before hindsight has organised the story. |
| **Susan Orlean — *The Library Book*** | **Libraries as memory infrastructure.** What destruction reveals about the institutional, social and cultural functions of libraries. |
| **Bridget Collins — *The Binding*** | **Memory, possession and authored transformation.** A fictional mechanism for asking what happens when intimate human experience becomes transferable material and when third-party authors transform lives into books. |
| **Shoshana Zuboff — *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*** | **Extraction.** A conceptual framework for examining systems that convert human-generated experience into privately accumulated computational value. |
| **Jason Stanley — *Erasing History: How Fascists Rewrite the Past to Control the Future*** | **Political memory.** An argument about historical narrative, education and the political consequences of controlling accounts of the past. |

## 🔮 Guard The Future Reader

This may be the deepest principle in the node.

Preservation is an act undertaken for someone whose questions we cannot predict.

The future reader may:

- speak another language;
- belong to another community;
- reject our politics;
- possess technologies we cannot imagine;
- discover evidence we overlooked;
- care deeply about a writer we considered minor;
- need a poem we barely noticed;
- recognise a warning we failed to understand;
- revive an idea we abandoned.

That is why preservation cannot be governed solely by present utility.

The archive has to retain enough strangeness for the future to disagree with us.

> **Guarding a book means preserving the possibility that another human being will make something from it that we could not.**

## 🪄 Build The Cool Shit Without Eating The Library

There is no reason artificial intelligence has to be the enemy in this story.

Quite the opposite.

AI could help make books:

**more discoverable  
more accessible  
more multilingual  
more navigable  
more teachable  
more connected to archives  
more useful to historians  
more available to disabled readers  
more available across borders  
more legible when damaged  
more richly contextualised**

But that requires a different design objective.

Not:

> **How much culture can we ingest?**

But:

> **How much human cultural capacity can we strengthen?**

That means designing for:

- creator consent;
- provenance;
- attribution;
- sustainable licensing;
- preservation of originals;
- non-destructive digitisation;
- public-interest access;
- accessibility;
- cultural context;
- institutional partnership;
- reciprocal value;
- future discoverability;
- human curiosity.

The technology should join the ecology.

It should not eat the ecology and announce that the resulting model contains a statistically useful representation of what used to live there.

## 📚 Guard The Books

So yes.

Guard the books.

But not because paper is sacred.

Guard:

**the writers.  
the translators.  
the editors.  
the illustrators.  
the librarians.  
the archivists.  
the booksellers.  
the teachers.  
the readers.  
the collectors.  
the little boxes on street corners.  
the public libraries.  
the strange specialist archives.  
the second-hand shops.  
the endangered-language collections.  
the digital repositories.  
the poems somebody hands you when everything has gone wrong.  
the forgotten book waiting for a reader who does not exist yet.**

Guard the legal conditions that let people create.

Guard the Commons without pretending it is ownerless.

Guard provenance.

Guard privacy.

Guard the right to remember.

Guard the ability to discover.

Guard the arguments.

Guard the weird books.

Guard the bad books well enough that future people can understand why they were bad.

Guard the warnings.

Guard the roads not taken.

Guard the human relationships around the text.

And when somebody arrives with an extraordinary new machine, do not ask only whether it can read the library.

Ask:

> **Does it leave us with a healthier library afterwards?**

If the answer is no, the problem is not that humanity has failed to appreciate technological progress.

The machine has failed its governance test.
