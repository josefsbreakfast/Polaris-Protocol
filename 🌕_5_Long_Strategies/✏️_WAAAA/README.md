# ✏️ WAAAA
**First created:** 2026-09-16 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-16  
*Writers Against Autocrats and Assholes: a long-strategy cluster for defending the human infrastructure through which people remember, imagine, argue, create and tell each other what happened.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

WAAAA began with a deliberately unserious acronym for a serious proposition:

> **Writers Against Autocrats and Assholes.**

The joke is load-bearing.

The underlying strategy is that writing, publishing, libraries, archives, journalism, scholarship, poetry, translation, bookselling and cultural memory are not decorative additions to a functioning society.

They are part of how societies:

- remember what happened;
- preserve testimony;
- transmit knowledge;
- contest power;
- recognise manipulation;
- imagine alternatives;
- communicate across generations;
- retain minority and dissident voices;
- make complicated experiences intelligible;
- and leave evidence for people who come later.

This makes cultural infrastructure relevant to long-term resilience.

Not because writers are uniquely virtuous.

They very obviously are not.

Not because every book is true.

It very obviously is not.

And not because culture should become another instrument of state strategy.

Absolutely not.

The strategic value lies precisely in maintaining a **plural, difficult-to-monopolise ecology of people and institutions capable of recording, preserving, interpreting, criticising and imagining**.

WAAAA therefore asks:

> **What would it mean to treat cultural production and cultural memory as infrastructure worth actively protecting?**

> **Writers, Artists, Academics and Archivists**

## ✨ Key Features

WAAAA treats the cultural ecosystem as a distributed resilience network.

That ecosystem includes:

| Part Of The Ecology | What It Keeps Possible |
|---|---|
| **Writers** | testimony, argument, imagination, criticism, memory |
| **Journalists** | contemporaneous records, investigation, public scrutiny |
| **Historians** | reconstruction, context, continuity, disagreement about the past |
| **Poets** | compressed language, emotional articulation, ambiguity, cultural memory |
| **Novelists** | perspective-taking, speculation, social imagination, alternative worlds |
| **Translators** | movement of thought across linguistic boundaries |
| **Editors** | scrutiny, refinement, contextual judgement, quality control |
| **Illustrators and designers** | visual meaning, interpretation, cultural form |
| **Publishers** | production, investment, circulation and durable editions |
| **Booksellers** | discovery, recirculation, recommendation and cultural place-making |
| **Librarians** | access, preservation, organisation, recommendation and public memory |
| **Archivists** | provenance, continuity, evidence and future discoverability |
| **Teachers** | transmission, interpretation and literacy |
| **Readers** | reception, criticism, remembrance and recirculation |
| **Community collections** | local memory that larger institutions may never collect |
| **Digital archives** | preservation, discoverability and wider access |
| **Physical archives** | originals, provenance and material evidence |
| **Book exchanges and Commons projects** | reciprocal circulation outside purely commercial systems |

No single component can substitute for the others.

A model containing an enormous quantity of text is not therefore equivalent to the cultural ecology from which that text emerged.

It is a different object.

## 🌱 The Strategic Proposition

WAAAA starts from a principle that is easy to miss in discussions of power:

> **People who control weapons, money and institutions do not control every mechanism through which human beings make meaning.**

Culture can be suppressed.

It can be censored.

It can be commercialised.

It can be manipulated.

It can be propagandised.

It can be neglected.

It can be drowned in noise.

But cultural production is unusually regenerative because humans keep doing it.

People tell stories.

People keep diaries.

People write songs.

People make jokes.

People document what happened.

People hide books.

People preserve letters.

People teach languages.

People photograph buildings before they disappear.

People translate banned writers.

People copy documents.

People build archives.

People put books in boxes on street corners.

People write down the thing everyone has been told not to mention.

This does not make culture invulnerable.

It makes it **distributed**.

That is strategically interesting.

## 🌱 Made For The Commons

WAAAA is intended as a **Creative Commons project**.

The point is not to create another centrally controlled cultural brand.

The point is to make an idea people can:

- read;
- share;
- adapt;
- translate;
- print;
- teach with;
- organise around;
- turn into local projects;
- and make considerably sillier.

That principle should extend to the visual culture around WAAAA.

### 📌 Make Your Own Badges

Pins, badges, patches, bookmarks, stickers, zines, library cards, bookplates, posters and other small physical manifestations of WAAAA are actively encouraged.

Especially if they are:

- independently made;
- locally printed;
- artist-designed;
- handmade;
- weird;
- inexpensive;
- swapped between people;
- sold by small makers;
- given away at libraries, bookshops, readings and events.

There does not need to be an Official WAAAA Merchandise Fulfilment Platform™.

In fact, that would rather miss the point.

If somebody wants a beautifully enamelled **WAAAA** pin, somebody else should be able to make one.

If a local writers' group needs **WAAAAAAAAA** badges because conditions have deteriorated substantially since Tuesday, they should not need to submit a licensing request.

The cultural object should circulate through the same kind of distributed human ecology the project is trying to defend.

### 🐝 Commons Does Not Mean Ownerless

Creative Commons is particularly appropriate because it demonstrates one of WAAAA's central arguments:

> **Sharing and authorship are not opposites.**

A creator can say:

> I made this.

and simultaneously say:

> You may use this.

The Commons is therefore not a vacuum created by abolishing ownership.

It can be a deliberately constructed set of permissions through which creators enable reuse while retaining attribution, provenance and whatever boundaries the chosen licence establishes.

That distinction matters enormously to the wider WAAAA argument about AI.

The alternatives are not simply:

**total enclosure**

or

**anything publicly accessible may be appropriated for any purpose.**

Humans have spent decades building licences, institutions, archives, libraries, open-source projects and cultural practices occupying the enormous territory between those positions.

WAAAA belongs in that territory.

### 🧷 Licensing Note

The precise Creative Commons licence should be selected deliberately rather than inherited by accident.

That choice should preserve the intended principles:

- attribution;
- reuse;
- adaptation;
- translation;
- decentralised organisation;
- independent cultural production;
- and the ability of small makers to produce and sell physical WAAAA objects.

In particular, a non-commercial restriction should not be adopted without considering whether it would inadvertently prevent exactly the kind of independent maker economy WAAAA wants to encourage.

## 📚 Guard The Books

The first major WAAAA branch is:

> [📚 Guard The Books](./📚_guard_the_books.md)

Its proposition is not that paper is sacred.

It is that books form part of a wider human memory ecology.

Books can be:

- memory technologies;
- witness infrastructure;
- cultural objects;
- social objects;
- Commons resources;
- emergency equipment;
- repositories of abandoned ideas;
- warnings from the past;
- messages to future readers.

The node follows the problem from historical destruction and censorship through libraries, archives, bookshops, Little Free Libraries and digital Commons projects into contemporary disputes around copyright, artificial intelligence and computational extraction.

Its governing question is simple:

> **Does the new technology leave us with a healthier cultural ecology afterwards?**

If not, technological capability alone is an inadequate measure of progress.

## 🖋️ Guard The Letters

Letters create a related but distinct problem.

A book has usually crossed some threshold into intended publication.

A letter may not have.

Private correspondence can later become:

- historical evidence;
- biographical material;
- literary material;
- family inheritance;
- archival material;
- legal evidence;
- public-interest evidence;
- computational data.

That transition raises questions which cannot be solved simply by asking who physically possesses the paper or digital file.

Relevant relationships may include:

**writer → recipient → subject → estate → archive → researcher → publisher → public**

Each may have different interests.

The WAAAA principle here is therefore:

> **Preservation does not automatically imply unlimited reuse.**

The eventual `🖋️_guard_the_letters.md` branch should distinguish preservation, research access, publication, quotation, digitisation and computational extraction rather than treating them as one permission.

## 🕯️ Guard The Witnesses

Somebody has to write things down while they are happening.

This matters because retrospective history has a structural limitation:

**it knows the ending.**

Contemporary witnesses do not.

Diaries, letters, journalism, photographs, oral histories and mundane records preserve the uncertainty inside an event.

They tell later readers:

- what seemed important then;
- what seemed normal;
- what changed gradually;
- what vocabulary people used;
- what people feared;
- what they misunderstood;
- what warnings existed;
- what institutions claimed;
- what ordinary life looked like around extraordinary events.

WAAAA therefore treats contemporaneous recording as a form of civic infrastructure.

The witness need not understand the entire system.

They need to leave enough trace that somebody later can reconstruct it.

---

## 🧿 Guard Provenance

Preserving information without preserving where it came from creates a different problem.

A quotation without a source is weaker evidence.

A photograph without a date may become misleading.

A document without its archival context can lose meaning.

A dataset stripped of its collection history may hide bias.

A model output that synthesises thousands of sources without making their contribution legible can make verification extraordinarily difficult.

So WAAAA is not merely interested in **more information**.

It is interested in:

> **information that retains enough provenance to remain interrogable.**

That means protecting relationships between:

- claim and source;
- work and author;
- translation and translator;
- photograph and photographer;
- testimony and witness;
- document and archive;
- edition and publication history;
- quotation and context;
- dataset and collection process.

Provenance is not bureaucratic decoration.

It is part of how another person checks whether they should believe you.

## 🐝 Guard The Commons

Commons are central to WAAAA because cultural resilience requires circulation.

But:

> **Commons does not mean ownerless.**

Healthy Commons systems can contain:

- stewardship;
- reciprocity;
- attribution;
- contribution;
- access expectations;
- community norms;
- licences;
- boundaries;
- duties of care.

The internet already contains decades of experiments in digital Commons infrastructure.

Public libraries contain centuries more.

Little Free Libraries reduce the principle to a wooden box:

> **Take a book. Leave a book.**

The exact reciprocity need not occur between the same two people.

That is the clever bit.

Someone contributes because somebody else may need it.

Somebody benefits because another stranger contributed earlier.

The return pathway occurs through the system.

That is radically different from treating public accessibility as permission for unlimited enclosure.

WAAAA is interested in keeping those distinctions legible.

## 🔮 Guard The Weird Stuff

Efficient preservation systems have an obvious temptation:

preserve what appears important.

Unfortunately, humans are terrible at predicting what future humans will consider important.

Today's obscure pamphlet can become tomorrow's crucial primary source.

A forgotten diary can change historical understanding.

A minority-language text can become evidence of vocabulary otherwise lost.

An unsuccessful political movement can contain an idea rediscovered centuries later.

A dreadful novel can reveal social assumptions its author never consciously intended to document.

A children's book can preserve ordinary domestic details that nobody thought worth recording elsewhere.

A weird little local publication can become the only surviving account of a vanished community institution.

So:

> **Do not optimise the archive until only the canon survives.**

Redundancy, oddness and apparent irrelevance are features of resilient cultural memory.

The future gets to ask different questions.

## 🧨 Guard Against Extraction Without Return

WAAAA is not anti-commerce.

Writers should be paid.

Editors should be paid.

Translators should be paid.

Artists should be paid.

Publishers can make money.

Booksellers can make money.

Software developers can make money.

Technology companies can make money.

Independent people making ridiculous little **WAAAAAAAAA** badges can make money.

The relevant question is whether the surrounding system remains capable of reproducing the conditions from which value comes.

A system that continually extracts:

**writing  
art  
knowledge  
attention  
data  
labour  
community  
memory**

while returning insufficient value to the systems that produce them eventually weakens its own environment.

This is not primarily a moral metaphor.

It is a feedback problem.

> **If your business requires a healthy cultural ecosystem, destroying the economics of cultural production is not merely antisocial. It is strategically stupid.**

## 🕹️ Guard Human Enjoyment

One recurring WAAAA suspicion is that optimisation can remove precisely the things humans were enjoying.

Gaming already demonstrated the problem.

Humans built elaborate virtual worlds because they liked:

- challenge;
- mastery;
- exploration;
- creativity;
- story;
- cooperation;
- competition;
- surprise;
- sociality.

A certain kind of metaverse proposal appeared to look at this and ask how virtual worlds could contain more property, commerce, work and corporate infrastructure.

Similarly, humans built literary cultures around:

- discovery;
- recommendation;
- browsing;
- collecting;
- lending;
- discussing;
- rereading;
- annotation;
- surprise;
- ownership;
- gifting;
- physical place;
- social encounter.

A technological system can improve many of those experiences.

But it should first identify what humans actually liked.

> **Do not remove the dopamine and call the remainder innovation.**

Sometimes the supposedly inefficient human interaction is the product.

## 🪄 Build Better Things Instead

WAAAA is a long strategy rather than a preservation bunker.

The objective is not to freeze culture in amber.

There are extraordinary things that could be built.

Educational AI could help children learn to read.

Immersive environments could reconstruct historical places.

Libraries could offer conversational discovery systems tied to verifiable catalogues.

Translation systems could dramatically widen access between languages.

Archives could become searchable in ways previous historians could barely imagine.

Damaged manuscripts could become more legible.

Print-disabled readers could gain better access.

Students could explore historical arguments through interactive primary-source environments.

Children could walk through a reconstruction of an ancient city and then be sent to the books, museum objects and archaeological reports from which the reconstruction was built.

AI could become a **doorway into human knowledge rather than a replacement interface pretending to be its origin**.

This is the alternative WAAAA cares about.

Not:

> **stop inventing things.**

But:

> **For God's sake, invent cooler things.**

## 🩹 Guard The Emergency Kit

Culture is not only useful when societies are stable.

People reach for books, poems, songs, diaries, stories and familiar characters during:

- grief;
- displacement;
- imprisonment;
- war;
- illness;
- political upheaval;
- loneliness;
- disaster;
- personal crisis.

That does not make literature medicine in a literal clinical sense.

It makes cultural material part of the repertoire humans use to survive difficult experience.

A poem can provide words when somebody has none.

A novel can provide temporary distance.

A diary can provide companionship across eighty years.

A children's story can preserve routine during disruption.

A library can be somewhere warm, public and non-commercial.

A book can be carried.

This is part of the infrastructure too.

## 🌈 Guard Imagination

Authoritarianism is not the only thing that narrows possible futures.

Markets can do it.

Technical systems can do it.

Institutional habits can do it.

Metrics can do it.

A society can become so accustomed to asking what is scalable, monetisable, measurable or immediately useful that it stops recognising other forms of value.

WAAAA therefore guards imagination not as decoration but as **optionality**.

Fiction asks:

> What if?

History asks:

> What happened?

Journalism asks:

> What is happening?

Criticism asks:

> What does this mean?

Poetry sometimes asks:

> What does this feel like?

Archives say:

> Here is what remains.

Libraries say:

> You may look.

The Commons says:

> Other people may need this too.

Together they prevent one description of reality from becoming the only available one.

## 🧮 The WAAAA Test

A cultural or technological intervention can be examined with a simple set of questions.

| Question | What It Tests |
|---|---|
| **What is being preserved?** | survival |
| **What is being lost?** | destructive externalities |
| **Who created it?** | provenance |
| **Who contributed to it?** | distributed authorship and labour |
| **Who can access it?** | availability |
| **Who can refuse its use?** | agency |
| **Who gets paid?** | economic return |
| **Who accumulates capability?** | power |
| **Who maintains the underlying infrastructure?** | sustainability |
| **Can another person verify where it came from?** | epistemic integrity |
| **Does context survive?** | meaning |
| **Can future people still encounter the original?** | preservation |
| **Does the Commons receive something back?** | reciprocity |
| **Does the intervention increase human cultural capacity?** | strategic value |
| **Is the ecology healthier afterwards?** | system viability |

No single answer settles every governance dispute.

The table is a way of noticing which questions have quietly disappeared.

## 🌳 WAAAA Is Ecological

The central metaphor is therefore not fortress.

It is ecology.

A fortress protects something by sealing it away.

Cultural resilience requires circulation.

Books need readers.

Writers need audiences.

Languages need speakers.

Archives need researchers.

Libraries need communities.

Ideas need critics.

Commons need participants.

The objective is not perfect containment.

It is a system capable of:

**creating → preserving → circulating → challenging → rediscovering → transforming → creating again**

That loop matters more than the survival of any individual institution.

WAAAA therefore guards **relationships**.

Between writer and reader.

Past and future.

Archive and evidence.

Commons and contributor.

Technology and culture.

Memory and imagination.

## 🪧 The WAAAA Position

The cluster can be reduced to a handful of propositions:

> **Culture is infrastructure.**

> **Memory requires maintenance.**

> **Access is not the same thing as ownerlessness.**

> **A Commons requires return pathways.**

> **Preservation is not automatically permission for every subsequent use.**

> **Provenance is part of knowledge.**

> **Sharing and authorship are not opposites.**

> **Human friction can contain human value.**

> **The future needs access to things the present does not yet understand.**

> **Technology should increase human cultural capacity rather than merely increase the capacity to extract from human culture.**

And perhaps most importantly:

> **You do not defend imagination by deciding in advance what it is allowed to imagine.**

WAAAA is not interested in replacing one cultural authority with another.

It is interested in keeping the ecology sufficiently plural that nobody gets to become the final authority.

## 🏮 Footer

*WAAAA* is a living long-strategy cluster of the **Polaris Protocol**. It develops cultural resilience as a governance problem: protecting the distributed human systems through which knowledge, testimony, memory, criticism and imagination survive changes in governments, markets, institutions and technologies.

The cluster does not treat culture as static heritage to be sealed away. Its concern is maintaining the conditions under which people can continue to create, preserve, encounter, challenge and transform cultural material without allowing concentrated power to become the sole custodian of collective memory.

WAAAA itself is intended to participate in that ecology: reusable, adaptable, translatable, decentralised and available for people to make their own.

Including badges.
