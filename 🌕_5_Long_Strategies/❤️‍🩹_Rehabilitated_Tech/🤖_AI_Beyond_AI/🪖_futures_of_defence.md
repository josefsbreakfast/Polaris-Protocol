# 🪖 Futures of Defence
**First created:** 2026-09-14 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-14  
*Where should Defence put risk, where should it put technology, and what must the human still be able to do when the simulation ends?*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

There is an easy version of the future of Defence.

It has AI.

It has VR.

It has digital twins, synthetic environments, autonomous systems, connected ranges and several extremely expensive demonstrations in rooms with excellent lighting.

Then there is the harder question:

> **What must the human being still be able to do when the simulation ends?**

That is the question this node is interested in.

The immediate British Army collective-training dispute raises a financial problem, but underneath it sits a much older and more useful design problem.

Military training contains risk.

Military operations contain considerably more risk.

Technology can change where some of that risk is encountered, how often people can rehearse before encountering it, how realistically rare situations can be reproduced, and how much information can be carried from one training event into the next.

But technology does not automatically abolish the underlying physical task.

So the useful future question is not:

> **Can AI or VR replace training?**

It is:

> **Which parts of military competence can be generated synthetically, which can be prepared synthetically but must still be validated physically, which risks can technology remove without losing useful learning, and which risks must still be encountered under controlled conditions before somebody meets them for the first time in theatre?**

That is a design problem.

And Britain has already been working on versions of it for more than a century.

---

## 🕰️ We Have Been Here Before

Synthetic military training is not an AI-era invention.

The RAF traces simulated aviation training within the Service back to a model cockpit in **1910**. Computerised simulation followed decades later, and modern Combat Air now combines increasingly sophisticated synthetic environments with live flying.

This matters because aviation gives us a mature example of the argument.

Nobody needs to choose between:

> **simulation is fake**

and:

> **simulation can replace reality.**

The useful question has always been more granular.

What can be rehearsed accurately?

What can be repeated cheaply?

What dangerous or rare event can be practised without having to manufacture the real emergency?

What requires the actual aircraft?

What requires the actual body?

What requires both?

The RAF's modern **Gladiator** system makes the principle explicit: synthetic training complements rather than replaces live flying.

That is not technological timidity.

It is systems engineering.

---

## ✈️ Why Aircraft Are Such A Useful Starting Point

A cockpit is unusually amenable to simulation.

A great deal of the operator's world arrives through:

- instruments;
- controls;
- displays;
- communications;
- visual information;
- warning systems;
- procedural sequences.

That means a synthetic environment can reproduce a substantial amount of the informational problem.

Pilots can rehearse:

- emergencies;
- communications;
- decision-making;
- navigation;
- formation activity;
- rare failures;
- complex tactical scenarios;
- multi-platform interactions.

They can repeat them.

Then repeat them again.

And again.

The RAF's current synthetic infrastructure can also connect platforms and personnel which would be difficult, expensive or impractical to assemble repeatedly in the live environment.

That is an enormous capability.

But the body remains inconveniently real.

Acceleration is real.

Spatial disorientation is real.

Fatigue is real.

Motion is real.

Physical systems fail in physical ways.

Which is why Defence has also built facilities capable of combining simulated cockpit environments with actual physiological exposure.

The interesting lesson is not simply that simulation works.

It is:

> **Different components of competence can be generated in different environments.**

That gives us somewhere much more interesting to go.

---

## 🥾 Gravity Remains Stubbornly Analogue

Land warfare presents a different simulation problem.

A soldier may be:

- wet;
- freezing;
- overheated;
- hungry;
- sleep deprived;
- carrying substantial weight;
- moving across broken ground;
- stuck in mud;
- operating in dust;
- handling equipment with cold or injured hands;
- moving a casualty;
- climbing through a vehicle;
- losing communications;
- trying to understand another exhausted human being;
- making decisions while physically depleted.

A headset can represent a field.

It cannot currently make the field muddy.

It can represent a casualty.

It cannot reproduce the complete physical problem of moving one.

It can represent fatigue cognitively.

It cannot simply manufacture the physiological consequences of several days of inadequate sleep without actually imposing them — at which point we are back inside a real human risk problem.

That does not make virtual technology useless for land forces.

It tells us where its present boundary lies.

And boundaries are useful.

---

## 🧠 The Useful Question Is Not Real Versus Virtual

There are at least three different relationships technology can have with a military task.

### 1. **Substitution**

The synthetic environment reproduces enough of the relevant task that some physical repetitions genuinely do not need to occur.

This may be particularly strong where the important competence is:

- procedural;
- informational;
- cognitive;
- communicative;
- decision-based.

### 2. **Preparation**

The synthetic environment does not replace the physical task.

It allows the human to arrive at the physical task better prepared.

A person can learn:

- the sequence;
- the controls;
- the map;
- the emergency procedure;
- the communications architecture;
- the expected decision points;

before the physical hazard is introduced.

That can make the eventual physical training both safer and more productive.

### 3. **Augmentation**

The technology becomes part of the physical training environment itself.

It may:

- inject information;
- generate adversary behaviour;
- track performance;
- connect geographically separated participants;
- reproduce effects which are impractical to create physically;
- provide rapid after-action analysis;
- vary scenarios faster than a human directing staff can construct them manually.

These are different jobs.

A procurement programme which says **synthetic training** without identifying which job it is performing has not yet answered the capability question.

---

## ⚖️ Where Do We Put The Risk?

This is where the historical duty-of-care argument becomes useful.

Between 2015 and 2017, Parliament examined deaths and serious injuries during military training and selection.

The resulting discussion was not simply:

> **training must be safer.**

Nor was it:

> **war is dangerous, therefore training should be dangerous.**

The Government's response described military training as inherently hazardous and explicitly framed the task as reducing risk **as low as reasonably practicable** while still providing realistic and effective preparation for operational output.

That remains the right problem.

Because risk can move.

Suppose a task has a non-zero physical risk even when performed competently.

We can introduce that hazard during training where:

- supervisors are present;
- medical support is planned;
- equipment can be checked;
- the exercise can be stopped;
- exposure can be progressive;
- mistakes are not being exploited by an adversary.

Or we can remove realistic exposure from training.

The training environment becomes safer.

But if the task remains operationally necessary, somebody may encounter the problem for the first meaningful time:

- overseas;
- under fire;
- exhausted;
- in unfamiliar terrain;
- with constrained medical support;
- while another human being is actively trying to defeat them.

That is not a reason to maximise danger during training.

It is a reason to ask:

> **Did we remove risk, or did we export learning risk into the operational environment?**

---

## ❤️‍🩹 Technology Gives Us A Better Answer Than “Pick One”

This is where emerging technology becomes genuinely interesting.

The choice does not have to be:

> **dangerous realistic training**

versus

> **safe but inadequate training.**

A better system may progressively introduce different dimensions of the problem.

For example:

```text
knowledge
    ↓
synthetic familiarisation
    ↓
repeated procedural rehearsal
    ↓
controlled physical exposure
    ↓
collective application
    ↓
complex / degraded environment
    ↓
live validation
    ↓
operational readiness
```

Not every task needs every stage.

Not every stage needs to happen in that order.

But the architecture matters.

If virtual systems allow somebody to make the first fifty mistakes without physical consequence, the fifty-first physical repetition can be spent learning the things only reality can teach.

That is not replacing training.

That is using technology to make scarce, expensive and hazardous physical training **more valuable**.

---

## 🗺️ Rehearse The Place Before You Enter It

One of the frontier opportunities is not merely simulating *a* military environment.

It is simulating **the environment**.

Increasingly rich geospatial data, reconnaissance, remote sensing, digital mapping, photogrammetry and digital-twin techniques create the possibility of rehearsing against highly specific representations of:

- terrain;
- buildings;
- infrastructure;
- ports;
- airfields;
- industrial sites;
- routes;
- urban areas.

There are obvious limits.

A model is not the territory.

A building changes.

A road is blocked.

Weather changes the ground.

People behave unpredictably.

An adversary deliberately makes yesterday's information wrong.

But that is an argument for teaching the uncertainty of the model, not for throwing the model away.

The future training question becomes:

> **How much useful familiarity can we give somebody before they physically arrive?**

For some missions, even partial familiarity may matter enormously.

---

## 🎲 AI Should Make The Exercise Less Predictable

There is another problem with training.

Humans learn the exercise.

If the same patterns recur, personnel can become very good at solving the training environment rather than the underlying problem.

Generative systems create interesting possibilities here.

An AI-enabled training architecture could potentially vary:

- adversary behaviour;
- communications failures;
- civilian patterns;
- equipment degradation;
- logistics problems;
- weather;
- timing;
- information quality;
- deception;
- casualty patterns;
- command dilemmas.

The purpose is not to replace directing staff.

It is to increase the combinatorial space through which instructors can make people think.

That distinction matters.

The machine can generate variation.

The human training system still has to decide:

- what competence is being tested;
- whether the scenario is doctrinally meaningful;
- whether the model behaves credibly;
- whether the trainee learned the right lesson;
- whether the system accidentally rewards behaviour which would fail outside the simulation.

A synthetic adversary which teaches nonsense very efficiently is not progress.

---

## 🪞 Digital Twins Need A Reality Check

Digital twins are attractive because they promise a persistent representation of a physical system.

In Defence, that could potentially mean models of:

- equipment;
- platforms;
- infrastructure;
- logistics networks;
- training estates;
- operational environments.

Used well, that can support:

- maintenance rehearsal;
- fault diagnosis;
- mission planning;
- logistics;
- training;
- design;
- testing;
- after-action analysis.

But a digital twin is only as useful as the relationship between model and reality.

That means asking:

- How current is the data?
- What is not represented?
- Which assumptions are embedded?
- What happens at the edge of the model?
- Who validates it?
- How quickly does operational evidence update it?
- Can the people using it tell when the representation has become stale?

The better the simulation looks, the more important this becomes.

A crude model reminds you that it is a model.

A beautiful model can tempt you to forget.

---

## 🧍 The Human Is Not A Peripheral Device

This is the part technology programmes repeatedly risk getting backwards.

The human is not the inconvenient analogue component attached to the clever system.

The human is part of the capability.

The people who:

- operate;
- instruct;
- maintain;
- repair;
- command;
- survive;
- recover;

possess information about the system which may not appear in procurement metrics.

That does not mean every operator gets a veto.

It means operator and instructor evidence belongs inside validation.

Ask:

- What does this genuinely help you practise?
- What does it fail to reproduce?
- What mistakes appear when people move from synthetic to live activity?
- Which skills transfer?
- Which do not?
- What became safer?
- What merely became less visible?
- What would you still refuse to let somebody encounter for the first time on operations?

If the same failure repeatedly appears when personnel transition from synthetic to live activity, that is not merely a UX complaint.

It may be hazard information.

The feedback needs to move like fucking butter.

---

## 🩺 Design Around Morbidity As Well As Mortality

Defence risk is often discussed in the most dramatic unit:

death.

But future force design should also care about morbidity.

Training and operations can produce:

- musculoskeletal injury;
- hearing damage;
- traumatic injury;
- heat illness;
- cold injury;
- repetitive strain;
- psychological injury;
- chronic disability;
- medical discharge.

Technology may be able to reduce some exposure without degrading competence.

If a procedural task can be repeated synthetically 100 times before the person handles the dangerous physical system, that may be useful.

If a heavy physical task can be instrumented so technique problems are identified before they become injuries, that may be useful.

If augmented training can identify fatigue-related degradation before somebody crosses a dangerous threshold, that may be useful.

If a virtual environment can reduce unnecessary exposure to blast, noise, vehicle hours or repetitive physical load while preserving the required learning, that may be useful.

The test is not:

> **Was training made less physical?**

It is:

> **Did we preserve the required competence while reducing exposure which was not itself producing useful learning?**

That is a much better safety metric.

---

## 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 The Future Force Is Not Only The Full-Time Regular Force

Future Defence planning also has to ask who needs access to training.

The system may include:

- Regular personnel;
- Reservists;
- specialists;
- civil servants;
- industry;
- allied personnel;
- recruits;
- people moving between civilian and military roles.

And, further upstream, cadet organisations and other youth-facing environments may influence familiarity, confidence, skills and eventual recruitment — while operating under importantly different safeguarding and risk rules.

This matters because physical assembly is expensive.

Reservists cannot necessarily spend the same amount of continuous time in military environments as Regular personnel.

Allies are geographically dispersed.

Civilian specialists may be needed for capabilities which do not map neatly onto conventional military careers.

Synthetic infrastructure can potentially increase access to useful rehearsal without pretending that remote participation creates every form of military competence.

That could change force generation.

Not by making everyone a soldier through VR.

By allowing some forms of knowledge, integration and rehearsal to occur **before scarce people have to be physically co-located**.

---

## 🌐 Distributed Training Could Become Strategic Infrastructure

The RAF's Gladiator system already points toward something larger than a simulator.

It connects different platforms and can support collective synthetic activity across services and with allies.

The 2025 Strategic Defence Review goes further, recommending a single virtual training environment integrated into Defence's common digital architecture and synthetic capability good enough to reduce reliance on live training where appropriate.

That suggests a future in which synthetic training is not merely equipment sitting in individual schools.

It becomes infrastructure.

Potentially:

- persistent;
- distributed;
- cross-service;
- allied;
- updateable;
- connected to operational lessons;
- connected to equipment programmes;
- connected to digital representations of real environments.

That creates opportunities.

It also creates dependencies.

A distributed synthetic training architecture now has to be:

- secure;
- resilient;
- interoperable;
- available;
- trustworthy;
- updateable;
- protected against manipulation.

If training becomes software-defined, software assurance becomes readiness.

If the network is unavailable, what training stops?

If the model is poisoned, what behaviour is learned?

If allies cannot connect, what integration disappears?

If a contractor controls a critical layer, what happens when the contract changes?

The clever future creates new boring dependencies.

The boring dependencies are where systems usually live or die.

---

## 🧰 Procurement Should Buy A Training Ecosystem, Not A Demonstration

Technology is particularly persuasive when it is visible.

A minister can put on a headset.

A contractor can demonstrate an AI system.

A synthetic battlefield can look extremely impressive on a screen.

Collective competence is less photogenic.

So procurement needs harder questions.

Before buying another magic bean:

- What military output is this supposed to generate?
- Which existing activity does it augment?
- Which activity does it replace?
- What evidence supports that substitution?
- What remains live?
- What remains embodied?
- What remains collective?
- What new dependencies does the system create?
- How is transfer into real performance tested?
- Who can report that the system is not working?
- Who owns the data?
- Who updates the model?
- Can Defence continue using the capability if a supplier disappears?
- What does it cost after the demonstration phase?
- Does it save money, or move cost into another budget?
- Does it reduce risk, or move risk somewhere less measurable?

The machine should have to prove itself against the capability requirement.

The capability requirement should not quietly reorganise itself around the machine somebody has already bought.

---

## 🇺🇦 Ukraine Is A Warning Against Single-Technology Futures

The war in Ukraine is useful partly because it refuses to cooperate with neat technological narratives.

It contains:

- drones;
- electronic warfare;
- software;
- satellite imagery;
- rapid iteration;
- open-source intelligence;
- increasingly autonomous systems.

It also contains:

- trenches;
- artillery;
- mines;
- mud;
- logistics;
- casualty evacuation;
- industrial production;
- exhausted humans carrying heavy things.

New technology did not replace the physical war.

It changed it.

That distinction should sit at the centre of future Defence planning.

The future force does not need to choose between:

**technology**

and

**mass / people / logistics / physical competence.**

It has to understand how each changes the requirements of the others.

---

## 🪖 Training Capacity Is Itself Strategic Capacity

Operation Interflex makes another part of the future problem visible.

Training Ukrainian personnel is strategically valuable.

It also consumes real British training capacity.

The National Audit Office found that British Army units bidding for training sites in 2023 had bids rejected eight times more often than in 2019 because sites were unavailable.

That is not an argument against Interflex.

It is an argument against pretending useful commitments are free.

Synthetic environments may relieve some pressure.

Distributed systems may relieve some pressure.

Allied training arrangements may relieve some pressure.

Better scheduling and estate investment may relieve some pressure.

None of those answers should be assumed in advance.

The future system needs to ask:

> **What training capacity does Britain require not only for the force it has today, but for mobilisation, allies, reserves, expansion and contingencies it may need tomorrow?**

A training estate is not spare land.

A simulator is not automatically spare capacity.

An instructor is not overhead.

They are parts of the force-generation system.

---

## 🔭 The Frontier Is Not One Technology

The interesting future is not:

> **VR Army.**

It is the interaction between multiple technologies and the human system.

Potentially:

- AI-generated scenarios;
- virtual and mixed reality;
- augmented reality;
- digital twins;
- instrumented ranges;
- wearable sensing;
- autonomous opposing forces;
- robotics;
- synthetic communications environments;
- electronic-warfare simulation;
- connected allied training;
- high-fidelity geospatial models;
- automated after-action analysis;
- adaptive learning systems.

Some will mature.

Some will disappoint.

Some will solve problems we currently tolerate because there is no better option.

Some will create new risks.

Some will be excellent for one Service and mediocre for another.

Some will be transformative for a narrow task and almost useless outside it.

That is fine.

Rehabilitating technology means allowing it to be **specific**.

A technology does not need to revolutionise warfare to be worth having.

It needs to do its actual job well.

## 🧭 A Better Future-Defence Test

For any proposed training technology, ask five things.

### **1. What human capability are we trying to produce?**

Not what product are we buying.

Not what technology is fashionable.

What must the person or formation actually be able to do?

### **2. Which parts of that capability are informational, procedural, physical, relational and collective?**

Different components may require different training environments.

### **3. Where is the safest place to learn each component without deleting necessary realism?**

This is the risk-placement question.

### **4. How do we prove that competence transfers between environments?**

Synthetic performance is not automatically operational performance.

### **5. What happens when the technology fails?**

A resilient military system must understand its degraded mode.

If the answer to number five is:

> **then nobody can train**

we have created a new readiness vulnerability.

## 🛒 Mother Is Still Doing The Grocery List

The future of Defence still begins with the boring questions.

What are we trying to do?

Where?

With whom?

Against what?

For how long?

How many people does that require?

How often must they practise?

Which skills fade?

Which skills are dangerous to acquire?

Which can be rehearsed virtually?

Which require a body?

Which require several bodies working together?

Which require live equipment?

Which require allies?

Which require land, sea or airspace?

What happens when somebody gets injured?

What happens when the network goes down?

What happens when the contractor disappears?

What happens when the model is wrong?

And:

> **Which risks are we willing to let a human discover for the first time in war?**

That last list should be very short.

---

## 🧿 Futures Assessment

The strongest case for emerging training technology is not that it can make Defence virtual.

It is that it can let Defence become **more deliberate about where reality is necessary**.

Used well, synthetic and AI-enabled systems could:

- increase repetition;
- expose people to rare scenarios;
- improve procedural familiarity;
- connect dispersed forces;
- improve mission rehearsal;
- vary adversary behaviour;
- shorten feedback loops;
- reduce some unnecessary physical exposure;
- make expensive live training more productive.

But the design constraint remains human.

> **Use technology to remove risk that teaches us nothing.  
> Preserve controlled exposure to risk that teaches something necessary.  
> Never quietly export the remaining learning risk to the battlefield.**

The future force should not be designed around what the technology industry can demonstrate.

Technology should be designed and selected around what the future force needs humans and machines to accomplish together.

And every time the answer is:

> **the simulator says they can do it**

somebody should still ask:

> **Grand. Have we checked?**
