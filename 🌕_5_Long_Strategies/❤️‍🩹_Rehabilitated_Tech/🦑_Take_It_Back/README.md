# 🦑 Take It Back
**First created:** 2026-08-22 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-06  
*Reclaiming technical culture from inherited prestige systems, artificial opacity, and somebody else's definition of what competence is supposed to look like.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

Technology is not a private inheritance.

It does not belong to the loudest founder, the most expensive institution, the most intimidating interview process, the person who learned the right jargon earliest, or the subculture that happened to arrive first and then mistook incumbency for ownership.

**🦑 Take It Back** is the part of **❤️‍🩹 Rehabilitated Tech** concerned with reclaiming technical authority.

Not by pretending expertise does not matter.

Not by replacing one prestige hierarchy with another.

And not by asking marginalised people to prove that they can perform somebody else's version of technical seriousness well enough to be admitted.

The question is more useful:

> **What kinds of technical culture help more people become genuinely capable?**

That means distinguishing difficulty from mystification.

Rigour from ritual.

Expertise from priesthood.

Standards from status games.

And technical inheritance from technical gatekeeping.

Technology is a human inheritance.

The objective is not admission to somebody else's game.

**Build a better one.**

---

## 🧿 Expertise Is Real

Some things are difficult.

Some systems require years of study.

Some mistakes can kill people.

Some forms of technical work require deep specialisation, formal qualification, supervised practice, repeated testing, or demonstrated competence.

None of that is a problem.

Rehabilitated Tech does not require flattening expertise into the comforting fiction that everybody already knows everything.

The point is almost the opposite.

If knowledge matters, then the ability to **transmit knowledge well** matters too.

A technical culture serious about competence should care about:

- teaching;
- documentation;
- mentorship;
- accessible explanation;
- reproducibility;
- inspectability;
- apprenticeship;
- maintenance knowledge;
- error correction;
- and whether somebody can actually use what they have learned.

Expertise should create capability around itself.

Not merely awe.

---

## 🦑 The Priesthood Problem

Technical cultures become priestly when access to knowledge depends unnecessarily on belonging to the right social world.

The signs are familiar:

- jargon used where ordinary language would work;
- undocumented systems whose maintainers gain status from being indispensable;
- interview puzzles weakly related to the actual job;
- prestige institutions treated as proxies for competence;
- obscurity mistaken for intellectual depth;
- explanation treated as evidence that a subject is not difficult enough;
- newcomers expected to know unwritten rules;
- contempt for people who arrive through non-standard routes;
- and technical disagreement converted into social dominance.

None of these practices prove that a field is rigorous.

Some actively interfere with rigour.

A system nobody can question because nobody wants to look stupid is not intellectually strong.

It is socially brittle.

---

## 🧠 Accessibility Is Not Dilution

One of the stranger habits of prestige culture is treating understandable explanation as suspicious.

If an idea can be explained clearly, perhaps it was not sophisticated enough.

If a tool can be used without mastering its internals, perhaps the user is not a “real” technical person.

If documentation lowers the entry cost, perhaps standards are falling.

This confuses **difficulty of access** with **quality of knowledge**.

A good explanation does not make the underlying subject simpler.

It makes the route into it better.

A well-designed interface does not abolish engineering.

A readable paper does not abolish scholarship.

A documented codebase does not abolish expertise.

Accessibility can increase the number of people capable of reaching the difficult part.

That is not dilution.

That is infrastructure.

---

## 🪜 There Is More Than One Way In

Technical ability develops through many routes.

People learn through:

- formal education;
- apprenticeships;
- hobbies;
- modding;
- fandom;
- games;
- community organising;
- repair;
- laboratories;
- libraries;
- online communities;
- open-source projects;
- professional work;
- caregiving;
- accessibility problem-solving;
- reverse engineering;
- making art;
- running servers;
- fixing something because nobody else was going to;
- and repeatedly asking what the machine is actually doing.

Those routes do not produce identical knowledge.

They do produce real knowledge.

A healthy technical culture should be capable of recognising competence wherever it appears while remaining precise about what a person does and does not know.

That is more demanding than prestige sorting.

It requires actually looking.

---

## 🧨 Prestige Is A Proxy

Prestige metrics are attractive because they compress difficult judgements.

Instead of asking:

> Can this person do the work?

an institution can ask:

> Where did they study?

Instead of:

> Is this argument correct?

it can ask:

> Who published it?

Instead of:

> Does this system work?

it can ask:

> Who funded it?

Instead of:

> Can this person explain the mechanism?

it can ask:

> Do they sound like the people we already recognise as experts?

Sometimes those proxies contain useful information.

The problem begins when the proxy becomes the thing being measured.

Then technical cultures start optimising for **recognisability** rather than capability.

---

## 🧭 Imperial Metrics

Technical standards do not emerge outside history.

Institutions inherit assumptions about:

- whose education counts;
- which accents sound authoritative;
- which countries produce expertise;
- which disciplines deserve status;
- which forms of labour are intellectual;
- whose informal knowledge is treated as anecdotal;
- and who is expected to demonstrate competence repeatedly while others receive it presumptively.

That does not mean every standard is illegitimate.

It means standards should be interrogable.

Ask:

- What capability is this requirement actually measuring?
- Is that capability necessary?
- Could it be demonstrated another way?
- Who had access to the route we are treating as normal?
- Does the metric predict performance, or merely familiarity?
- Which useful forms of knowledge disappear because the institution cannot easily credential them?

A standard that survives those questions becomes stronger.

One that cannot may have been a status marker wearing a lab coat.

---

## 🛠️ Competence Should Be Demonstrable

Reclaiming technical culture does not mean replacing standards with vibes.

It means preferring standards that connect visibly to the capability being claimed.

Where possible:

```text
claimed competence
→ observable task
→ inspectable method
→ reproducible result
→ feedback
→ improvement
```

That is healthier than:

```text
claimed competence
→ prestigious signal
→ social recognition
→ assumed authority
```

Different fields will require different forms of evidence.

The principle is simply that **technical authority should remain connected to technical reality**.

---

## 🧩 Make The Difficult Thing Teachable

A strong technical culture does not prove its seriousness by keeping difficult knowledge difficult to enter.

It builds ladders.

That can mean:

- introductory explanations that do not lie;
- glossaries;
- diagrams;
- worked examples;
- annotated code;
- reproducible demonstrations;
- accessible interfaces;
- staged learning;
- sandboxes;
- good error messages;
- mentorship;
- multiple explanatory registers;
- and explicit routes from beginner understanding to specialist depth.

The difficult thing remains difficult.

More people become capable of reaching it.

That is success.

---

## 🌷 Open The Source, Not The Trapdoor

Open systems can help distribute technical authority.

But publishing code without documentation, governance, onboarding, or maintainability can preserve the same hierarchy under an open licence.

The people who already understand the system remain the people with practical control.

So reclamation and openness have to meet.

**🦑 Take It Back** asks who gets to count as technically capable.

**🌷 Opening The Source** asks whether the system itself allows that capability to become meaningful participation.

Together:

```text
accessible knowledge
→ demonstrable competence
→ legible systems
→ meaningful participation
→ distributed technical authority
```

Opening the door matters.

So does removing the trapdoor immediately behind it.

---

## 💾 Informal Technical Cultures Count

Some of the internet's most technically inventive spaces have never looked like official technical institutions.

Communities have learned to:

- customise interfaces;
- manipulate platform affordances;
- preserve archives;
- build tagging systems;
- moderate large social spaces;
- route around platform failures;
- teach one another tools;
- create informal standards;
- document workarounds;
- coordinate distributed projects;
- and build knowledge infrastructures around needs commercial systems did not consider important.

Those practices are easy to dismiss when technical culture is defined primarily through credentialed production.

They become harder to dismiss when the question is:

> **What did people actually learn to make the system do?**

Technical culture is larger than the institutions authorised to describe it.

---

## 🧿 Legibility Changes Power

Opacity concentrates authority.

If only a small group can understand a system, that group acquires power whether or not anyone formally assigned it.

Legibility does not eliminate expertise.

It changes the relationship between expert and non-expert.

A legible system makes it easier to:

- ask better questions;
- detect contradiction;
- contest decisions;
- learn;
- contribute;
- audit;
- repair;
- and recognise when somebody is using technical language to obscure rather than explain.

That is why legibility is not merely a usability preference.

It is part of governance.

---

## 🪖 Reality Is The Final Examiner

Prestige cannot negotiate with physics.

A beautiful theory still has to work.

A prestigious engineer's bridge still has to stand.

An expensive model still has to produce useful results.

A celebrated system still has to survive maintenance.

An authoritative explanation still has to match the mechanism.

This is where **🪖 Touch Grass** becomes the useful corrective.

Technical culture can construct elaborate status systems.

Reality remains inconveniently unimpressed.

That is healthy.

---

## ♻️ Reclaiming The Culture

The objective is not to make everybody a software engineer.

It is not to abolish specialisation.

It is not to require every technical system to be understandable in full by every person who encounters it.

It is to build a culture in which:

- expertise can be respected without becoming mystical;
- newcomers can become competent without ritual humiliation;
- explanation is treated as a technical skill;
- documentation is valued;
- informal knowledge can be recognised;
- standards connect to real capabilities;
- systems can be inspected;
- authority can be challenged;
- maintenance counts as technical work;
- multiple routes into competence remain possible;
- and people are not required to become socially identical to existing experts before their knowledge becomes visible.

That is reclamation.

---

## 🦑 Take It Back

Technology was made by people.

Its languages were made by people.

Its standards were made by people.

Its institutions were made by people.

Its prestige systems were made by people.

Its definitions of seriousness were made by people.

That means they can be examined.

They can be repaired.

They can be opened.

They can be taught differently.

They can be governed differently.

And where necessary, they can be replaced.

You do not have to win somebody else's game before you are allowed to notice that the game is badly designed.

**Take it back.**
