# 🪖 Touch Grass: Re-Embedding Technology In The Physical World
**First created:** 2025-12-14 | **Last updated:** 2026-09-05  
*Why technological practice must remain answerable to material limits, ecological reality, maintenance, and consequence.*

---

## 🛰️ Orientation

This is a **repair node**.

After mapping failure modes — abstraction without limits, magical thinking, protagonist governance, innovation-as-distraction — this file asks a forward question:

> **What does technology look like when it is re-embedded in the physical world it depends on?**

“Touch grass” is not a dismissal.

It is a directive:

**return technological decision-making to contact with matter, maintenance, ecology, bodies, infrastructure, and consequence.**

This node sits inside **❤️‍🩹 Rehabilitated Tech** because it is not anti-technology.

It is **anti-disembodied technology**.

Digital systems are still physical systems.

They depend on energy, minerals, fabrication, logistics, cooling, maintenance, workers, buildings, cables, land, water, institutions, and bodies.

No amount of abstraction makes those dependencies disappear.

---

## 🧿 The Epistemic Correction

Technological culture can create environments in which:

- limits feel optional;
- resources become abstractions;
- failure appears reversible;
- maintenance becomes somebody else's problem;
- consequences are deferred;
- physical dependencies disappear behind interfaces;
- and scale itself becomes evidence of success.

That encourages systems optimised for:

- engagement;
- growth;
- speed;
- valuation;
- deployment;
- and novelty.

Rather than necessarily for:

- durability;
- safety;
- sufficiency;
- repairability;
- resilience;
- maintainability;
- and ecological compatibility.

The problem is not abstraction itself.

Engineering requires abstraction.

The problem begins when the abstraction stops being checked against the system it represents.

**Touching grass is the check.**

---

## 🌱 Matter Gets A Vote

To “touch grass” is to reintroduce:

- friction;
- cost;
- weight;
- time;
- decay;
- scarcity;
- maintenance;
- irreversibility;
- and physical consequence.

Grass grows slowly.

Soil depletes.

Infrastructure ages.

Magnets quench.

Mines exhaust.

Batteries degrade.

Servers produce heat.

Bodies break.

Components fail.

These realities are not embarrassing exceptions to the technological model.

They are **the operating conditions**.

A technology that does not account for them is not advanced because it has successfully ignored them.

It has an incomplete model of its own environment.

---

## 🛠️ Repair Is Technical Excellence

Repair culture is often less glamorous than innovation culture.

Repair is:

- slow;
- contextual;
- knowledge-intensive;
- dependent on accumulated experience;
- difficult to package as spectacle;
- and frequently performed by people whose expertise becomes visible only when something stops working.

But repair requires serious technical intelligence.

A repairable system needs:

- understandable components;
- accessible diagnostics;
- replaceable parts;
- documentation;
- maintenance knowledge;
- realistic service intervals;
- supply chains;
- institutional memory;
- and somebody responsible for keeping the thing alive after launch.

A civilisation that cannot repair its tools does not need more innovation.

It needs fewer delusions.

---

## 🧰 Maintenance Is Infrastructure

Maintenance is not what happens after the interesting technical work is finished.

Maintenance **is technical work**.

Systems survive because somebody:

- inspects them;
- cleans them;
- patches them;
- calibrates them;
- lubricates them;
- replaces worn parts;
- monitors deterioration;
- remembers previous failures;
- keeps documentation current;
- trains the next person;
- and notices when reality no longer matches the model.

This applies to bridges.

It applies to aircraft.

It applies to hospitals.

It applies to power systems.

It applies to software.

It applies to datasets.

It applies to AI infrastructure.

A system without a maintenance model is not complete.

It is merely launched.

---

## 🧨 Technology Does Not Get An Exception

Re-embedding technology in the physical world means technical decisions being constrained by the systems they actually inhabit.

That can mean:

- engineers working with ecologists, medics, physicists, maintainers, operators, trades, communities, and other domain specialists;
- design decisions tested against material throughput rather than only market demand;
- success measured partly through uptime, safety, continuity, repairability, and useful lifespan;
- long-term maintenance funded as core infrastructure;
- end-of-life treated as part of design;
- and failure modes treated as first-class engineering concerns.

Many safety-critical fields already understand this.

Aviation cannot persuade gravity to improve its user experience.

Medicine cannot A/B test away physiology.

Civil engineering eventually meets load.

A magnet does not care about the pitch deck.

**Tech is not special. It does not get to opt out.**

## ♻️ Rehabilitated Tech Versus Extractive Tech

The distinction is not that one category contains morally pure technology and the other contains evil machines.

It is about system behaviour.

### Extractive tendencies

An extractive technical system may:

- treat the physical world primarily as substrate;
- externalise environmental or maintenance costs;
- reward throughput independently of usefulness;
- design around replacement rather than repair;
- scale before stewardship exists;
- hide physical dependencies behind frictionless interfaces;
- and treat exhaustion elsewhere in the system as somebody else's accounting problem.

### Rehabilitative tendencies

A rehabilitative technical system instead asks:

- What does this consume?
- Who maintains it?
- What happens when it fails?
- Can it be repaired?
- How long should it last?
- What physical infrastructure does it require?
- What happens at end-of-life?
- Which costs are being displaced onto workers, communities, ecosystems, or future maintainers?
- Is scale actually improving the system?
- Would less technology sometimes solve the problem better?

The point is not technological asceticism.

It is technological accountability.

---

## 🌍 Ecological Capacity Is A Design Constraint

Ecology should not enter the design process only after a product exists and somebody calculates its footprint.

Material and ecological capacity belong upstream.

That means asking about:

- energy demand;
- water use;
- mineral extraction;
- land;
- manufacturing;
- transport;
- replacement cycles;
- waste;
- repair;
- reuse;
- and what happens when a system is multiplied across millions or billions of users.

A locally efficient device can still participate in an inefficient system.

A technically elegant model can still depend on infrastructure whose material costs are poorly distributed.

A product can reduce one form of consumption while increasing another.

Systems analysis therefore has to extend beyond the interface.

---

## 🌿 Sufficiency, Degrowth, And Technological Maturity

Degrowth offers one useful challenge to technological culture: **more throughput is not automatically evidence of greater human welfare or better system performance**.

Rehabilitated Tech does not require every reader to adopt one economic programme wholesale.

The underlying engineering questions remain useful regardless:

- How much is enough?
- What level of throughput can the surrounding system sustain?
- Does increased scale produce proportional social value?
- Could durability outperform churn?
- Could maintenance outperform replacement?
- Could a smaller system perform the useful function more reliably?
- Are we optimising a proxy for success rather than success itself?

Sometimes technological maturity means increasing capability.

Sometimes it means increasing efficiency.

Sometimes it means building less.

Sometimes it means keeping something working for another twenty years.

**Sufficiency is a technical parameter too.**

---

## 🧠 Embodied Knowledge Matters

Not all system knowledge exists in formal models.

People who operate and maintain physical systems accumulate knowledge through:

- sound;
- vibration;
- smell;
- wear;
- timing;
- repeated failure;
- bodily effort;
- environmental change;
- and familiarity with what “normal” feels like.

That knowledge can be difficult to compress into dashboards or datasets.

It is still knowledge.

A rehabilitated technical culture should therefore be suspicious of systems that remove maintainers, operators, patients, users, or local communities from decision-making merely because their knowledge is harder to quantify.

Legibility runs both ways.

Humans should be able to inspect technical systems.

Technical institutions should also remain capable of hearing what the physical world and the people inside it are telling them.

---

## 🦾 AI Still Has A Body

Artificial intelligence is often discussed as though it exists in an abstract computational elsewhere.

It does not.

AI systems depend on physical infrastructure.

They require hardware, fabrication, electricity, cooling, networks, storage, maintenance, and replacement.

They also enter physical and institutional environments through the decisions people make with them.

So questions about AI capability should eventually meet questions such as:

- What infrastructure supports this capability?
- What does deployment require at scale?
- How resilient is that infrastructure?
- Who maintains it?
- What happens during outages or degraded operation?
- What material costs are hidden by the interface?
- Is computation being used because it improves the system, or because computation is currently fashionable?

AI Beyond AI expands the technological imagination.

Touch Grass supplies the constraint:

**whatever we imagine still has to exist somewhere.**

---

## 🧪 Build For The Lifecycle

A useful design should survive more than launch day.

The lifecycle includes:

```text
need
→ design
→ sourcing
→ manufacture
→ deployment
→ operation
→ maintenance
→ failure
→ repair
→ adaptation
→ retirement / reuse / disposal
```

A system designed only for the first half of that sequence exports its complexity downstream.

The person repairing it later still has to solve the rest.

---

## 🧿 Questions For Re-Embedding

When evaluating a technical proposal, ask:

- What physical systems make this possible?
- What resources does it consume?
- Who maintains it?
- Who repairs it?
- What fails first?
- What happens when connectivity disappears?
- What happens when funding disappears?
- What happens when a supplier disappears?
- Can components be replaced?
- Can people understand the failure?
- What happens at end-of-life?
- Which costs are currently invisible?
- Which domain experts have not been invited into the model?
- What knowledge exists among operators or users that the formal system does not capture?
- Is the technology solving the problem, or relocating it?
- Would a simpler intervention work?

And finally:

> **Does this survive contact with reality?**

---

## 🪖 The Injunction

“Touch grass” is not anti-intellectual.

It is anti-disembodiment.

It means:

- leave the silo;
- test assumptions against reality;
- involve people who maintain systems;
- listen to fields that say “no”;
- account for physical dependency;
- budget for repair;
- remember the lifecycle;
- and design as if consequences matter.

Because they do.
